The balance sheet: a snapshot of one day.

The income statement tells you what happened over a year. The balance sheet tells you where the company stands on its last day: everything it owns, everything it owes, and what is left over for the owners. Here is how the two sides fit, and why they always balance.

On this page
  1. A photo, not a movie
  2. The equation that always holds
  3. The two sides, line by line
  4. What to look for
  5. Read a real one: Apple

01A photo, not a movie

If the income statement is a movie of the year, the balance sheet is a photograph taken at the end of it. It lists the company's position on one date: cash in the bank, money customers still owe, stock on the shelves, buildings and machines, and on the other side, bills to pay, loans outstanding and the owners' stake.

Some call it the statement of financial position. Because it's a snapshot, compare it with the one from a year earlier: the changes between the two photos are where the story is.

02The equation that always holds

The balance sheet equationassets = liabilities + equity

Every dollar a business has came from somewhere: either someone lent it (a liability) or the owners put it in or left their profits in the business (equity). So what it owns must equal the claims on it. Rearranged, equity = assets − liabilities: the owners' share is whatever would be left if the company sold everything at the values on the books and paid off everyone it owes.

The pizza shop at the end of year one
What it owns (assets)What it owes, and the owners' stake
Cash$41,500Bills owed to suppliers$8,000
Invoices customers still owe$10,000Bank loan$40,000
Ingredients in stock$6,000Owners' money put in$30,000
Oven and fit-out, after depreciation$48,000Profits kept in the business$27,500
Total$105,500Total$105,500

The owners' stake, $57,500, is the $30,000 they put in plus the $27,500 of the year's $37,500 profit they didn't pay out as dividends.

03The two sides, line by line

Assets

  • Current assets turn into cash within a year: cash itself, receivables (money customers owe), inventory (goods waiting to be sold).
  • Long-term assets last longer: buildings, equipment, and intangibles such as patents, brands and goodwill, the premium paid when one company buys another.

Liabilities

  • Current liabilities are due within a year: bills to suppliers (payables), wages owed, the part of any loan due this year.
  • Long-term liabilities are due later: bonds and long loans, lease obligations, pensions owed.

Equity

  • Paid-in capital: what shareholders put in.
  • Retained earnings: every profit the company has ever made, less every dividend it has paid. This is the line that ties the balance sheet to the income statement.
Try it: equity is what's left

With JavaScript on, this box stacks what the pizza shop owns against what it owes, and shows the owners' equity as whatever is left.

Raise the loan to $120,000 and the owners' column vanishes: the shop owes more than it owns. That is what being insolvent on paper means.

04What to look for

  • Can it pay its bills? Compare current assets with current liabilities, the current ratio. The pizza shop's is $57,500 ÷ $8,000, about 7.2.
  • How much does it borrow? Debt-to-equity compares borrowing with the owners' stake: $40,000 ÷ $57,500 = 0.70 for the shop.
  • Book value is not market value. Equity is measured at accounting values. A brand like Coca-Cola's is worth far more than the books say, which is why most companies' shares trade well above their book value.
  • Watch the soft assets. Goodwill and other intangibles can be written down in a bad year, cutting equity at a stroke.
At the table

The balance sheet is a count of your bankroll at the end of the night: the chips in front of you, the IOUs other players owe you, and against them, the marker you signed at the cage. What's left after paying the marker is really yours. A big stack built on a big marker isn't much of a bankroll.
