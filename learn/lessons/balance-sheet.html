The balance sheet: a snapshot of one day.

The income statement tells you what happened over a year. The balance sheet tells you where the company stands on its last day: everything it owns, everything it owes, and what is left over for the owners. Here is how the two sides fit, and why they always balance.

01A photo, not a movie

If the income statement is a movie of the year, the balance sheet is a photograph taken at the end of it. It lists the company's position on one date: cash in the bank, money customers still owe, stock on the shelves, buildings and machines, and on the other side, bills to pay, loans outstanding and the owners' stake.

Some call it the statement of financial position. Because it's a snapshot, compare it with the one from a year earlier: the changes between the two photos are where the story is.

02The equation that always holds

The balance sheet equationassets = liabilities + equity

Every dollar a business has came from somewhere: either someone lent it (a liability) or the owners put it in or left their profits in the business (equityA company's assets minus its liabilities on the balance sheet: the owners' share of the books.). So what it owns must equal the claims on it. Rearranged, equity = assets − liabilities: the owners' share is whatever would be left if the company sold everything at the values on the books and paid off everyone it owes.

The pizza shop at the end of year one

The owners' stake, $57,500, is the $30,000 they put in plus the $27,500 of the year's $37,500 profit they didn't pay out as dividends.

03The two sides, line by line

Assets

  • Current assets turn into cash within a year: cash itself, receivables (money customers owe), inventory (goods waiting to be sold).
  • Long-term assets last longer: buildings, equipment, and intangibles such as patents, brands and goodwill, the part of a takeover price above the value of the assets bought, less the debts taken on.

Liabilities

  • Current liabilities are due within a year: bills to suppliers (payables), wages owed, the part of any loan due this year. (The pizza shop's loan isn't due for several years, so none of it counts here.)
  • Long-term liabilities are due later: bonds and long loans, lease obligations, pensions owed.

Equity

  • Paid-in capital: what shareholders put in.
  • Retained earnings: every profit the company has ever made, less every dividend it has paid and, at many companies, the money spent buying back shares. This is the line that ties the balance sheet to the income statement.
Try it: equity is what's left

With JavaScript on, this box stacks what the pizza shop owns against what it owes, and shows the owners' equity as whatever is left.

Leave the assets as they are and raise the loan to $120,000, and the owners' column vanishes: the shop owes more than it owns. That is what being insolvent on paper means.

Equity can also shrink for the opposite reason: the owners taking money out through dividends and buybacks. Losses are the warning; payouts are a choice. The balance sheet shows the size of the stake, not why it got there; the financing section of the cash flow statement says which it was.

04What to look for

  • Can it pay its bills? Compare current assets with current liabilities, the current ratioAssets that turn into cash within a year against bills due within a year: a test of short-term liquidity.. The pizza shop's is $57,500 ÷ $8,000, about 7.2.
  • How much does it borrow? Debt-to-equityBorrowings compared with the shareholders' stake in the company. compares borrowing with the owners' stake: $40,000 ÷ $57,500 = 0.70 for the shop.
  • Book value is not market value. Equity is measured at accounting values. A brand like Coca-Cola's is worth far more than the books say, which is why most companies' shares trade well above their book value.
  • Watch the soft assets. Goodwill and other intangibles can be written down in a bad year, cutting equity at a stroke.
At the table

The balance sheet is a count of your bankroll at the end of the night: the chips in front of you, the IOUs other players owe you, and against them, the marker you signed at the cage. What's left after paying the marker is really yours. A big stack built on a big marker isn't much of a bankroll. A small stack after you've cashed out half your winnings is a different story.

05Read a real one: Apple

Apple, September 27, 2025
Cash and marketable securities (current and long-term)$132.4B
Receivables: customers $39.8B, plus $33.2B from the factories that buy parts from Apple to build its products$73.0B
Inventories$5.7B
Property, plant and equipment$49.8B
Everything else (other assets, mostly long-term)$98.3B
Total assets$359.2B
Accounts payable$69.9B
Debt (commercial paper and term debt)$98.7B
Everything else (taxes owed, deferred revenue, other)$117.0B
Total liabilities$285.5B
Shareholders' equity$73.7B
  • A current ratio under 1. Current assets of $148.0B against current liabilities of $165.6B gives 0.89. For a weaker company that would be a warning. For Apple it reflects two things: most of its $132.4B of securities mature in more than a year, so they don't count as current assets (with them, the ratio would be 1.36); and it owes suppliers $69.9B, more than its customers owe it ($39.8B), while turning over its small $5.7B of inventory quickly.
  • More cash than debt. $132.4B of cash and securities against $98.7B of debt: about $33.8B of net cash.
  • Tiny equity for a giant. Equity is $73.7B, a fifth of assets, and retained earnings are below zero (an accumulated deficit of $14.3B) even though Apple earned $112B in fiscal 2025 alone. That is the choice, not the warning: over the years Apple has handed back more in buybacks and dividends than it has earned, and each buyback is charged against this line. Retained earnings is a running tally of profits kept, not a pile of cash: Apple's cash is on the other side of the page, $132.4B of it. The small equity is also why its return on equity is above 100%, a number that says more about the buybacks than about the business.

Source: Apple Inc., Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended September 27, 2025, filed October 31, 2025, Item 8 (Financial Statements and Supplementary Data). Dollar figures in billions, rounded from the reported millions. Find it on SEC EDGAR.
