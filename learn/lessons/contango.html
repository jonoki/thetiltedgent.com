Contango and backwardation: the price of later.

Oil for delivery next month and oil for delivery next year are two different prices. Usually later costs more; sometimes it costs less. Which way the gap runs says a lot about the market for the thing itself, and it quietly decides whether anyone who holds futures for a long time gets the price they think they're getting. Here is why the gap exists, what flips it, and who wins and loses either way.

01Oil below zero

On April 20, 2020, the US oil futures contract for May delivery settled at minus $37.63 a barrel. People holding the contract paid others to take it off their hands. The same day, the contract for June delivery, the same oil one month later, settled at $20.43.

The world hadn't stopped needing oil. It had run out of room for it. With much of the world in lockdown, US refineries were running 24% below the year before, and the tanks at Cushing, Oklahoma, where that contract is delivered, were about three-quarters full and filling fast. A barrel in May was a problem; a barrel in June was still worth something.

That gap between two months of the same oil has a name: contango. Usually it is a few cents or dollars and nobody outside the oil market notices. This lesson is about what sets that gap, what flips it the other way, and who pays when it gets wide.

02The futures curve

A futures contract fixes today the price for something delivered later. Oil, wheat, gold and stock indexes all trade for several delivery months at once, each at its own price. Line those prices up by month and you have the futures curve. The price for immediate delivery is the spot price.

The curve has two basic shapes. When later months cost more than nearer ones, the market is in contango. When later months cost less, it is in backwardation. Neither is a mistake or a free lunch. Each is the price of holding the thing over time.

03Contango: later costs more

Holding a physical thing costs money. Oil needs a tank, wheat a silo, gold a vault and insurance. And the money tied up in it could have been earning interest. A buyer who wants oil in six months can either buy it now and pay to keep it, or buy a six-month future. The future should cost about the same as buying now and paying the carrying costs.

Worked example: the most contango can be

Oil is $70 a barrel today. Storage costs $0.50 a barrel a month, and cash earns 4% a year.

Buy a barrel now and keep it six months: $70 + $3.00 of storage + $1.40 of lost interest = $74.40.

If the six-month future traded at $78, a trader could buy oil at $70, store it, sell the future at $78 and pocket the $3.60 difference with no risk. Traders doing exactly that pull the future back down. So in normal times contango is capped at about the full cost of carry.

Contango is the normal state for things that are plentiful and costly to keep. It gets steep when there is too much of the thing and storage is running short, because then the cost of carry rises: a tank costs more when every tank is full.

04Backwardation: now costs more

Sometimes the thing is wanted now. A refinery that runs out of crude has to shut down; a power plant short of gas pays whatever it takes. When supply is tight, holding the physical barrel is worth more than its storage bill. Traders call that extra value the convenience yield. When it outweighs the cost of carry, the spot price rises above the later months, and the curve slopes down.

Two years after oil went below zero, the curve flipped hard the other way. After Russia invaded Ukraine, buyers scrambled for oil they could get now. On March 8, 2022, the front US oil contract settled at $123.70 a barrel, while the contract for delivery three months later settled at $110.83: $12.87 less for the same oil, a little later.

There is no cap on backwardation the way there is on contango. To profit from a steep contango you buy now and store. To profit from a steep backwardation you would need to sell oil you don't have today and buy it back cheaper later, and you can't borrow a barrel from next year. The only people who can sell into the high spot price are those already holding inventory, and in a shortage they tend to keep it.

05Is the curve a forecast?

Partly, but less than it looks. A curve in contango does not mean traders expect prices to rise; mostly it reflects the cost of carrying the thing until then. A curve in backwardation says the thing is scarce today, and that the market expects the shortage to ease as supply responds. The curve does carry information, but read it as "how tight is the market now?" before "where is the price going?"

06The roll: where holders win or lose

A future expires. Anyone who wants to keep a position for longer than a month or two, such as a commodity fund, has to roll: sell the expiring contract and buy the next one. And a future converges to the spot price as it nears expiry, because at expiry they are the same thing.

Worked example: a year of rolling in contango

Oil sits at $70 all year. The one-month future is 1.5% higher, at $71.05.

Each month you buy the next future at $71.05. By expiry it has converged to $70, and you roll again at $71.05. You lose about 1.5% every month while oil goes nowhere.

After twelve months, $1,000 has become $836. The cash behind the futures earns interest, about 4% a year, which brings it to $870. Oil itself: flat.

That drag is called negative roll yield, and it's why a fund that holds oil futures can lose money in a year when the oil price doesn't fall. In backwardation it runs the other way: each new contract is bought below spot and drifts up toward it, and a holder can earn more than the spot price's move.

07April 2020, explained

Put the pieces together. A futures contract that reaches expiry has to be settled: whoever still holds the May contract at the end takes delivery of 1,000 barrels a contract at Cushing. On April 20, the day before that contract's last day of trading, plenty of holders were funds and traders with no tank to put oil in. They had to sell, and the few buyers with storage could name their price. In the US Energy Information Administration's words, holders were "in effect paying a counterparty to allow them to exit."

That was contango pushed to its limit. Section 03 said the gap between months is capped by the cost of storing the thing; in April 2020 storage was close to running out, so that cost, and the gap, had no ceiling. The next day the May contract expired at $10.01, with June at $11.57.

The biggest US oil fund, the United States Oil Fund (USO), learned what the roll costs. It had held mostly front-month contracts. As the curve steepened it spread its money into later months, the exchange capped how many June contracts it could hold, and its own futures broker barred it from June altogether. In the first half of 2020 USO's value per share fell 72.6%; the oil futures benchmark it aims to track fell 35.7%. On April 28 it did a 1-for-8 reverse split, after its value per share had fallen to $2.04.

10Common misreads

  • "Contango means the market expects higher prices." Mostly it means holding the thing costs money.
  • "An oil fund tracks the oil price." A fund that holds futures tracks the futures, roll included. Over a year the two can be far apart.
  • "Backwardation is a free gain." The roll pays the holder only while the shortage lasts, and shortages end, sometimes in a crash.
  • "A negative oil price means oil was worthless." One contract, for one month's delivery at one place, went below zero for a day. Oil itself never did.
