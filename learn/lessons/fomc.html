The FOMC: twelve votes, one rate.

Eight times a year, a committee in Washington decides the price of overnight money in the US, and markets around the world stop to listen. Here is who sits on it, what it publishes, how traders read every word, and why the decision itself is usually the least surprising part of the day.

03What it decides

The committee's headline decision is a target range for the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The rates lesson follows that one rate through mortgages, bonds, stocks and savings. The committee also decides what to do with the Fed's own holdings of bonds: buy more (quantitative easing), or let them mature without replacing them (quantitative tightening). And it decides what to say, which often matters more than either.

05Words that move markets

The statement is short, and traders compare it with the last one word by word. A phrase added, dropped or softened can move bond yields within seconds, because it changes what the market expects at the meetings ahead. That is the point: the committee uses its words, called forward guidance, to steer longer-term rates without moving the overnight rate at all.

The language traders use for it is simple. Hawkish means leaning toward higher rates to fight inflation; dovish means leaning toward lower rates to support jobs and growth. A hike delivered with dovish words can send yields down; a hold delivered with hawkish words can send them up.

TBD
At the table

Forward guidance is a player announcing how they plan to bet before the cards come, so nobody panics when the chips go in. It works as long as the table believes it. The taper tantrum was the table hearing a raise that hadn't been made yet, and folding all at once.

07What the market expects: fed funds futures

Before each meeting, the market has already placed its bet, and you can read it. Fed funds futures settle on the average overnight rate for a month: a price of 96.00 means an average of 4.00% (100 minus the price). If a meeting falls partway through the month, that average is a blend of the rate before the meeting and the rate after it, so the price tells you what rate the market expects after the meeting.

Worked example: reading the odds

The overnight rate is 4.00%. The meeting is on day 17 of a 30-day month, so 17 days at the old rate and 13 at the new one. The month's futures trade at 95.95: an average of 4.05%.

Rate after the meeting = (30 × 4.05 − 17 × 4.00) ÷ 13 = 4.115%, about 0.115 points above today.

A quarter-point hike would add 0.25 points. The market is pricing 0.115 of it: about a 46% chance of a hike, 54% of no change. A price of 95.89 or so would mean a hike is fully priced.

This is the arithmetic behind the probabilities quoted in the news before every meetingTBD. It is also why the decision itself rarely surprises: by the day of the meeting the odds are usually close to 0% or 100%, and the committee seldom wants to deliver a shock.

09Hawkish and dovish surprises

What moves markets is the gap between what the committee does or says and what was priced. The usual direction of the first reaction:

WhoHawkish surpriseDovish surprise
2-year Treasury yieldUp most, since it tracks the next few meetingsDown
10-year Treasury yieldUsually up, by less; can fall if the market reads the hike as beating inflationUsually down; can rise if the market fears inflation
US dollarTends to strengthenTends to weaken
StocksUsually fall, growth stocks mostUsually rise
GoldTends to fallTends to rise
Mortgage ratesFollow the 10-year yield, not the decision itself

These are first reactions, often reversed within the day as traders digest the press conference. The rates lesson has what higher and lower rates do over the months that follow.

10Common misreads

  • "The decision is the news." It is usually priced in days ahead. The news is the statement, the dots and the press conference.
  • "The chair decides." The chair leads and usually carries the vote, but there are twelve votes, and dissents are public.
  • "The dots are a plan." They are individual forecasts, and they change with the data.
  • "The Fed sets mortgage rates." It sets an overnight rate. Longer rates are set by the market, as the rates lesson shows.
  • "A hike is bad for stocks." A hike the market expected can leave stocks higher. A surprise is what hurts.
