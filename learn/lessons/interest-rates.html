Rate hikes and cuts: the price of money.

A central bank changes one interest rate, on overnight loans between banks, and within months mortgages, bond prices, stock prices, savings accounts and the currency all move. Here is what the central bank actually sets, why it raises or cuts, how one rate travels through everything else, and who wins and loses either way.

012022: from zero to 4.5% in nine months

For two years the Federal Reserve had held its target rate near zero. On March 16, 2022, with inflation running far above its 2% goal, it raised the range to 0.25–0.50%. Then it kept going: seven hikes that year, four of them three-quarters of a point at a time, ending at 4.25–4.50% in December. It peaked at 5.25–5.50% in July 2023. The Bank of Canada moved in step: its first hike came on March 2, 2022, and it peaked at 5% that same July.

Almost everything with a price felt it. Over 2022:

  • The 10-year US Treasury yield rose from 1.52% to 3.88%.
  • The average 30-year US mortgage rate went from 3.11% to 6.42%, after touching 7.08% in the fall.
  • The broad US investment-grade bond market lost about 13%, its worst year in records going back to 1976.
  • The S&P 500 fell 19.4% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite 33.1%, before dividends.
  • The US dollar rose: at its peak in late September it was about 11% stronger against a broad basket of currencies than at the start of the year.

And in March 2023 the hikes helped bring down a bank (section 09). This lesson is about how one rate, on overnight loans between banks, reaches that far.

02What the central bank actually sets

A central bank sets one short-term rate, its policy rate: the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. In the US it is the Federal Reserve's target range for the federal funds rate; in Canada, the Bank of Canada's overnight rate. It steers that rate by setting what it pays banks on their deposits with it, and what it charges them to borrow.

Other short-term rates follow almost one for one: Treasury bills, savings accounts (more slowly), credit cards, lines of credit, variable-rate mortgages. Our Fed target rate and Bank of Canada rate pages track both, with their history.

Long-term rates, like the 10-year government bond yield and the fixed mortgage rates tied to it, are set by the market, not the central bank. They reflect where investors expect the policy rate to go over the years ahead, plus a little extra for locking money up that long. The central bank moves them mostly by changing those expectations.

03Why it raises or cuts

Central banks are balancing two things: prices and jobs. The Federal Reserve's mandate from Congress is maximum employment and stable prices, which it reads as 2% inflation over the longer run, measured by the PCE price index. The Bank of Canada targets 2% inflation, the middle of a 1% to 3% range.

  • Hike when inflation runs too hot. Dearer borrowing and better rewards for saving cool spending, hiring and price rises.
  • Cut when the economy is weakening and inflation is under control. Cheaper money encourages borrowing, spending and investment.

The effect is slow. A hike today shows up in spending and prices over the following year or two, so central banks are steering by where they think the economy is heading, not where it is.

At the table

The policy rate is the blinds. Raise them and every hand costs more to play: loose players fold, the action slows, and the pots get smaller. Lower them and everyone plays more hands. Nobody at the table controls the cards, but whoever sets the blinds controls the pace of the game.

04How one rate travels through everything else

Worked example: the same house at two rates

A $400,000 mortgage paid off over 30 years costs $1,686 a month at 3%.

At 7% it costs $2,661, $975 a month more for the same house.

That is the channel in one line: a buyer who could afford the house at 3% may not at 7%, so fewer people buy, house prices cool, and builders build less.

The same arithmetic runs through the rest of the economy. A business deciding whether to borrow for a project that returns 6% says yes when borrowing costs 3% and no at 7%. A saver earning 4.5% on $10,000 gets $450 a year instead of $50 at 0.5%, and is a little more inclined to save than spend. Higher rates also tend to strengthen the currency, as foreign money comes in to earn them, which makes imports cheaper and exports harder to sell.

05Bonds: the seesaw

A bond pays a fixed coupon. When rates rise, new bonds pay more, so an old bond's price must fall until its yield matches theirs. When rates fall, old bonds with higher coupons become more valuable. Price and yield sit on opposite ends of a seesaw, as the bonds lesson explains.

The longer the bond, the further its end of the seesaw swings. Start with bonds paying 4% and let rates rise one point: a 2-year bond falls 1.9%, a 10-year bond 7.8%, a 30-year bond 15.5%. The short bond hands your money back soon, to reinvest at the new rate; the long one locks you into the old coupon for decades. That sensitivity is called duration.

06Stocks: the value of later

A stock is worth the cash the business will pay out over its life, each dollar discounted back to today. Higher rates make a dollar received later worth less today, and the further away the dollar, the bigger the hit.

Worked example: $100 one year out and ten years out

At a 3% rate, $100 a year from now is worth $97.09 today; $100 ten years from now, $74.41.

At 5%, those become $95.24 (down 1.9%) and $61.39 (down 17.5%).

Same two-point rise, nine times the damage to the faraway dollar.

That is why growth stocks, whose profits lie mostly in the future, tend to fall harder when rates rise than value stocks, which pay out more today. One simple model makes it concrete: price = this year's payout ÷ (the return investors require − the payout's growth rate). With a 9% required return, a value stock paying $1 that grows 1% a year is worth $12.50; a growth stock paying $1 that grows 4% is worth $20. Raise the required return one point and the value stock falls 11.1%, the growth stock 16.7%.

The model is a sketch. Real stocks also move on what rates say about the economy: a cut because inflation is beaten is good news for profits; a cut because a recession is coming may not be.

07Surprises move markets, not decisions

Markets trade on what they expect, and they expect a lot. Futures on the federal funds rate show what traders think the Fed will do at each meeting, and by decision day the move is usually fully priced in. A widely expected hike can leave stocks up and bonds unchanged; what moves prices is a surprise in the decision, or in what the central bank says about the meetings ahead. That is why investors parse every word of the statement and the press conference.

09When hikes break things: Silicon Valley Bank

Silicon Valley Bank was the bank of the tech industry. In 2020 and 2021, startups flush with cash deposited it there, and the bank put much of the money into long-term bonds while rates were near zero. By the end of 2022, securities made up 55% of its assets; at similar banks the average was 25%.

Then rates rose and those bonds fell, just as section 05 says long bonds do. By the end of 2022 the losses on the bonds it had labeled "held to maturity" came to $15.2 billion, close to the bank's entire $16.0 billion of shareholders' equity. The accounting rules let it carry those bonds at what it paid, as long as it never had to sell them. On paper, nothing was wrong.

But a bank has to pay depositors when they ask, and about 94% of SVB's deposits were above the insurance limit. On March 8, 2023, it sold $21 billion of bonds at a $1.8 billion after-tax loss. Depositors took fright: on March 9 more than $40 billion left in a single day, and more than $100 billion more was lined up to leave the next morning. Regulators closed the bank on March 10.

The lesson is the one in the misreads below: a loss on a bond is only on paper until you have to sell, and the people who decide when you have to sell aren't always you.

11Common misreads

  • "The Fed sets mortgage rates." It sets an overnight rate. US fixed mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury yield, which the market sets.
  • "Rate cuts are good for stocks." Depends why. Cuts into a recession have come with falling stock prices.
  • "Rising rates are good for banks." For their lending margins, at first. Not for the bonds they already own.
  • "Bond losses are only on paper." Only if you never have to sell before the bond matures.
  • "Rates went up, so my bond fund is ruined." The fall in price comes with a higher yield from then on. Hold for about the fund's duration in years and the extra income roughly makes up the loss.
