The Tear-Sheet Glossary

Every number, ratio and acronym on our stock reports, in plain English: what it measures, how to read it, and where it sits on the page. Know what the chips are worth before you sit down.

ABCDEFGHILMNOPQRSTUVWY#

A

Accounts payable

AKA Payables · Trade payables

Bills a company owes its suppliers: cash it has kept, for now.

Money a company owes its suppliers for goods and services already received. It sits in current liabilities. A rise means the company has kept cash for now, which lifts operating cash flow until the bills are paid.

TagsFinancial statements

Related Operating cash flow, Current Ratio, Free Cash Flow, Working capital

Accounts receivable

AKA Receivables · Trade receivables

Covers Days sales outstanding · DSO

Sales already counted as revenue but not yet collected in cash.

Money customers owe a company for goods or services already delivered and counted as revenue. It sits in current assets. A rise uses cash, and receivables growing faster than sales can mean the company is struggling to collect.

TagsFinancial statements

Related Revenue, Operating cash flow, Current Ratio, Working capital, Accrual accounting

Accrual accounting

AKA Accrual basis

Covers Accruals · Accrued expenses · Cash basis

Why profit and cash differ, and why both statements matter.

Recording revenue when it is earned and costs when they are incurred, whatever the timing of the cash. A sale on credit counts as revenue now; an oven's cost is spread over its life as depreciation. Public companies report this way.

TagsFinancial statements

Related GAAP, Depreciation, Operating cash flow, Revenue, Income statement, Cash flow statement

Active management

Covers Active fund · Actively managed · Active ETF · Style drift

Whether a manager's picks, and the higher fee, buy an edge.

A fund manager picking investments to try to beat a benchmark index (as opposed to passive management, which copies the index). Active funds usually charge more, and over long periods most have trailed their benchmarks after fees.

TagsFunds

Related Passive management, Index fund, Expense ratio, MER, Style drift, Mutual fund, ETF

Adjusted (non-GAAP) earnings

AKA Non-GAAP earnings

Covers Non-GAAP · Adjusted EPS

Tells you whether a profit figure follows the rule book or management's own adjustments.

Profit as management chooses to present it, leaving out items such as stock-based pay or one-off charges (as opposed to GAAP earnings, the official figure). Usually higher than GAAP; US rules require companies to reconcile them to the GAAP figure.

ExampleGAAP EPS of $2.00, plus $0.50 of stock-based pay and a $0.30 one-off charge after tax: adjusted EPS of $2.80.

TagsFinancial statementsStock reports

Related GAAP, One-off items, EPS (TTM)

Adjusted funds from operations

AKA AFFO

Covers P/AFFO · AFFO per share

Gets closer than FFO to the cash a REIT can pay out as dividends.

Funds from operations (FFO) minus the recurring capital spending needed to keep properties leased and in shape, such as upkeep and improvements for new tenants. Unlike FFO, which has a standard industry definition, AFFO is defined slightly differently by each company.

FormulaAFFO ≈ FFO − recurring capital spending on properties (each company sets its own adjustments); P/AFFO = share price ÷ AFFO per share

ExampleFFO of $5.00 per share minus $0.80 of recurring upkeep: AFFO of $4.20 per share. At a $63 share price, P/AFFO is 15×.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Funds from operations, REIT, Payout Ratio

ADR

AKA American depositary receipt

Covers ADS · American depositary share

Lets you own a foreign company in dollars without opening a foreign account.

A US-traded security that stands for shares of a foreign company held by a depositary bank. It lets investors buy the company in dollars on a US exchange. One ADR can equal one, several or a fraction of a home-market share.

On the tear sheetThe header gives the conversion, e.g. 'ADR: 1 ADR = 2 ordinary shares'. These reports price the US-listed shares in dollars.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Exchange

After-hours and pre-market

AKA Extended hours · Extended-hours trading

Covers After hours · Pre-market · Premarket

Shows how the market reacted to news that landed after the closing bell.

Trading outside the regular US session of 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern: pre-market before the open, after-hours after the close. Fewer shares change hands, so prices can jump on news such as earnings.

On the tear sheetSome headers show an after-hours or pre-market quote under the close, with its own % change. The headline price is the regular-session close.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Earnings call

Allowed return on equity

AKA Allowed ROE

Sets the return a regulated utility is meant to earn for its shareholders.

The return on shareholders' equity that regulators approve for a regulated utility when they set its prices. It applies to the equity-funded part of the rate base (as opposed to the rate base itself, the network the return is earned on).

ExampleA $10 billion rate base, half funded by equity, at a 10% allowed ROE: about $500 million of allowed profit.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Rate base, ROE

Amortization

The cost of patents and other intangibles, spread over their useful lives.

An expense spreading the cost of a limited-life intangible asset, such as a patent or a license, over its useful life (as opposed to depreciation, for physical assets). Like depreciation, it lowers reported profit without spending any cash that year.

ExampleA $5 million patent with 10 years of protection left, spread evenly: $500,000 of amortization a year.

TagsFinancial statements

Related Depreciation, EBITDA, Operating cash flow

Arbitrage

Covers Arbitrageur · Arbitrageurs

The trading that keeps related prices from drifting apart.

Buying something where it is cheap and selling the same thing, or its equivalent, where it is dear, locking in a profit with little or no risk. Arbitrageurs' trading pushes the two prices back together, which is why futures track fair value.

TagsFuturesOptions

Related Cost of carry and fair value, NAV, Futures contract, Put-call parity, Spot price

Average Price Target

AKA Consensus price target · Mean price target

Covers Upside · Downside · Upside to target

Sums up the move analysts expect from the report's price, usually over 12 months.

The average of the price targets published by the analysts a data provider tracks. The gap between it and the current price is the upside (or downside) analysts expect, not counting dividends.

FormulaUpside = (average price target ÷ current price − 1) × 100

ExampleNetflix, Aug 10, 2026: $94.04 average price target ÷ $76.29 share price − 1 ≈ +23.3% upside.

On the tear sheetShown with the implied move from the banner-date price, e.g. '+4.6% upside from $308.26'.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Price target, Consensus Rating

B

Backlog

AKA Order book

Covers Remaining performance obligations · RPO · Orders

Gives a glimpse of sales still to come before they reach the income statement.

Signed orders or contracts not yet delivered, and so not yet counted as revenue. A growing backlog points to revenue still to come; software companies report a similar figure as remaining performance obligations (RPO).

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Revenue, Guidance

Backwardation

Later delivery costs less than now, often a sign the thing is scarce today.

A futures market is in backwardation when later delivery months cost less than nearer ones, usually because the thing is scarce now and buyers pay up to have it soon (as opposed to contango, when later months cost more).

ExampleOil for delivery next month at $70 and in six months at $66: the curve is in backwardation.

TagsFutures

Related Contango, Convenience yield, Roll (futures), Cost of carry and fair value, Futures contract

Balance sheet

AKA Statement of financial position

Covers Assets and liabilities · Total assets · Total liabilities

A snapshot of what a company owns and owes on one date.

The financial statement that lists what a company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the owners' stake (equity) on one date. It always balances: assets equal liabilities plus equity.

TagsFinancial statements

Related Shareholders' equity, Current Ratio, Debt-to-Equity, Retained earnings, Price / Book, Working capital, Income statement, Cash flow statement

Basis points

AKA bps · bp

The unit for small moves in rates and yields, where tenths of a point matter.

One hundredth of a percentage point, so 100 basis points (bps) make 1 percentage point. Used for interest rates, yields and fees, where moves are small: a yield rising from 4.00% to 4.25% is up 25 bps.

ExampleA fund fee of 0.05% a year is 5 basis points; a rate cut from 4.50% to 4.25% is 25 basis points.

TagsRates & the FedBondsStock reports

Related Percentage points, Yield (bonds), Gross Margin

Bear market

Covers Bull market

Puts a common name on how deep a fall has gone.

By convention, a fall of 20% or more from a recent high in a stock, an index or a whole market (as opposed to a correction, a fall of 10% to 20%). Its opposite, a bull market, is a long stretch of rising prices.

On the tear sheetUsed in the five-year story, e.g. Apple's 'rate-shock bear market low' of June 2022.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Correction, Drawdown, Cyclical stocks, Bull case and bear case

Beat and miss

Covers Earnings beat · Earnings miss · Earnings surprise · EPS surprise · In line

Explains why good results can sink a stock: the market grades against expectations.

A beat is a result above the consensus estimate; a miss is below it. The surprise is the gap, usually in percent. A beat paired with weak guidance can still send the stock down.

FormulaSurprise = (actual − consensus estimate) ÷ consensus estimate × 100

ExampleChevron, second quarter 2026 (report as of Aug 12, 2026): $6.06 adjusted EPS ÷ $5.56 consensus − 1 ≈ +9%, a beat.

On the tear sheetApple's July 2026 results beat on revenue and EPS, but Services missed forecasts and guidance slowed; the stock fell 7.35% the next day.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Consensus estimate, Guidance, Earnings call

Beta

AKA Beta (5Y) · Market beta · Beta vs S&P 500

Covers Correlation · Correlation with S&P 500

Tells you whether the stock has tended to amplify or dampen the market's moves.

How much a stock has tended to move when the market moves: 1 means in step, above 1 bigger swings, below 1 smaller. Correlation, from −1 to +1, measures how consistently the two move together.

FormulaCovariance of the stock's returns with the market's ÷ variance of the market's returns

ExampleBeta 1.5: when the market has moved 10%, the stock has typically moved about 15%.

On the tear sheetMeasured against the S&P 500; where a sheet states its period, it is usually five years. Bitcoin's beta of 1.82 with a correlation of 0.51 means much of its movement is unrelated to stocks.

At the tableA high-beta stock plays like a loose-aggressive opponent: bigger swings both ways, which says nothing about whether it wins.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Volatility, S&P 500

Bid-ask spread

Covers Spread · Bid · Ask

A trading cost on every buy and sell, on top of the fund's fee.

The gap between the highest price a buyer offers (bid) and the lowest a seller accepts (ask). It is a trading cost paid on every purchase and sale, on top of the expense ratio.

TagsFundsStocks

Related NAV, Expense ratio

Black-Scholes model

AKA Black-Scholes · Black-Scholes-Merton

Covers Option-pricing model

The standard formula for an option's fair price, and the source of implied volatility.

The 1973 formula, from Fischer Black, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, for the fair price of a European option from five inputs: the stock price, strike, time to expiry, interest rate and volatility. Traders run it backward to turn market prices into implied volatility.

TagsOptions

Related Implied volatility, The Greeks (delta, gamma, theta, vega), Call option, Volatility, Put-call parity, Risk-free rate

Blockchain

Covers Ledger · Block · Node · Distributed ledger

Makes past entries very hard to rewrite, with no central bookkeeper.

A shared record of transactions kept in copies by many independent computers (nodes). New transactions are grouped into blocks, each linked to the one before, so past entries can't be changed without redoing everything after them.

TagsCrypto

Related Proof of work, Proof of stake, Cryptocurrency

Bond

AKA Bonds

Covers Bondholder · Corporate bond · Fixed income · Debt security

A loan you can buy and sell: fixed payments, then your money back.

A loan to a government or company that pays fixed interest, the coupon, usually twice a year, and repays its face value on a set date, the maturity. Bonds trade after issue, so prices move as interest rates and the borrower's credit change.

TagsBonds

Related Coupon, Yield (bonds), Yield to maturity, Duration, Credit rating, Face value (par), Maturity, Credit spread, Credit risk and default

Breakeven inflation

AKA Breakeven · Breakeven rate

Covers Inflation expectations

The bond market's line on inflation, read straight from Treasury prices.

An ordinary Treasury yield minus the TIPS real yield of the same maturity: the average yearly inflation at which the two pay the same. Markets read it as expected inflation.

FormulaNominal yield − real (TIPS) yield

Example10-year Treasury, Sep 23, 2026: 5.11% nominal yield − 2.76% TIPS real yield = 2.35% breakeven inflation.

At the tableThe market's line on inflation: TIPS come out ahead if inflation runs over it, ordinary Treasuries if it runs under.

TagsBondsRates & the Fed

Related TIPS, Yield (bonds)

Brent crude

AKA Brent

The international oil price the news quotes, often next to WTI.

The international benchmark crude oil price, named for a North Sea oil field and traded as futures on ICE (as opposed to WTI, the US benchmark, delivered in Oklahoma).

TagsFutures

Related WTI crude, Futures contract, Contango

Bull case and bear case

Covers Bull Case · Bear Case · Bull · Bear · Bullish · Bearish · Top 3 Risks

Sets the strongest argument for the stock beside the strongest against it.

The bull case is the argument for the stock doing well; the bear case, the argument against. A bull expects prices to rise, a bear expects them to fall.

On the tear sheetSection 08 sets '▲ Bull Case' beside '▼ Bear Case', followed by the Top 3 Risks.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Catalyst, Bear market

Butterfly spread

AKA Butterfly

Covers Call butterfly · Iron butterfly

A cheap bet that the price ends near one level, with the loss capped.

Options at three strikes, paying most if the price ends at the middle one (unlike an iron condor, which pays across a range). A call butterfly buys a low-strike call, sells two middle ones, buys a high one; an iron butterfly mixes puts and calls.

ExampleStock at $100: buy the $90 call, sell two $100 calls, buy the $110 call, paying $2 net. At $100 at expiry it is worth $10: an $8 gain.

TagsOptions

Related Iron condor, Vertical spread, Straddle, The Greeks (delta, gamma, theta, vega)

Buyback

AKA Share repurchase · Stock buyback

Covers Repurchase authorization · Buyback yield

Returns cash to owners by shrinking the share count, so each share claims more profit.

A company buying its own shares on the market, which cuts the share count so each remaining share owns more of the profits. An authorization is the board's permission to buy, not a commitment.

FormulaBuyback yield = dollars spent on net share repurchases over 12 months ÷ market cap × 100

ExampleApple announced a new $100 billion repurchase authorization in April 2026.

At the tableThe company buys chips back from the table, so each chip left claims a bigger share of the pot.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Dilution, Dividend, ROE

C

CAGR

AKA Compound annual growth rate

Covers Annualized return · Annualized · Per year

Lets you compare growth across companies and periods on one yearly footing.

The steady yearly growth rate that would turn a starting value into an ending value over a number of years. It turns a bumpy path into one comparable annual figure.

Formula(Ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

Example$100 growing to $200 in five years: 2^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 14.9% a year.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Total return, Revenue Growth (YoY), Industry size (TAM)

Call option

AKA Call · Calls

The right to buy at a set price, for a fee paid up front.

A contract that gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy something at a set price (the strike) until or on a set date. The buyer pays a premium; the seller keeps it and must sell at the strike if asked.

TagsOptions

Related Put option, Strike price, Implied volatility, The Greeks (delta, gamma, theta, vega)

Callable (call feature)

AKA Call provision

Covers Callable bond · Call price · Redeemable

Lets the issuer take a bond or preferred back early, when it suits the issuer.

A bond or preferred share the issuer can buy back early, at a set price after a set date. Issuers tend to call when interest rates have fallen, so the holder's upside is capped near the call price.

TagsBonds

Related Preferred share, Coupon, Yield to maturity

Cap rate (capitalization rate)

AKA Capitalization rate · Cap rate

A property's yield: the real-estate cousin of an earnings yield.

A property's yearly net operating income (rent less operating costs, before loan payments) divided by its value or price. A building earning $5 million a year and worth $100 million has a 5% cap rate.

TagsStocksFunds

Related REIT, Funds from operations

Capex

AKA Capital expenditure · Capital spending · CapEx

Shows how much of today's cash goes into building tomorrow's business.

Money spent on long-lasting assets such as factories, equipment, networks or data centers. It is subtracted from operating cash flow to get free cash flow, and charged against profit gradually as depreciation.

TagsFinancial statementsStock reports

Related Free Cash Flow, EBITDA, Hyperscaler, Rate base

Capital gain (realized and unrealized)

AKA Capital gains

Covers Capital loss · Realized gain · Unrealized gain · Unrealized loss · Paper loss · Capital gains distribution

The profit on a sale, and the gain or loss that exists only on paper.

The rise in an investment's value above what was paid for it. It is unrealized, a gain or loss on paper, until the investment is sold; then it is realized and, in a taxable account, usually taxed. Funds pass realized gains to holders as distributions.

TagsStocksFunds

Related Total return, Distribution yield, Yield (bonds), Mutual fund

Cash flow statement

AKA Statement of cash flows

Covers Investing activities · Financing activities

Follows the actual cash, which profit alone can hide.

The financial statement that shows the cash a company took in and paid out over a period, in three sections: operating activities (running the business), investing activities (equipment, acquisitions, securities) and financing activities (borrowing, repaying, issuing shares, dividends and buybacks).

TagsFinancial statements

Related Operating cash flow, Free Cash Flow, Capex, Buyback, Working capital, Income statement, Balance sheet

Catalyst

Covers Upcoming catalysts · Event · Catalysts & News

Tells you which dated events on the calendar could move the price next.

A known or expected event that could move the share price, such as an earnings report, product launch, court ruling, regulatory decision or index change.

On the tear sheetSection 06 dates each event and tags it, e.g. 'Potential upside catalyst', 'Key near-term event' or 'Negative reaction (already occurred)'.

At the tableLike the turn or the river: a card still to come that can change what the hand is worth.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Guidance, Bull case and bear case

CET1 Ratio

Covers Common equity tier 1 · CET1 · Capital ratio

Tells you how much loss a bank can absorb before nearing the minimums regulators set.

A bank's highest-quality capital, mainly common equity and retained earnings, as a percentage of its risk-weighted assets: loans and other holdings, each scaled by how risky it is. Regulators set minimums; a higher ratio means a thicker cushion against losses.

FormulaCommon equity tier 1 capital ÷ risk-weighted assets × 100

TagsStocksStock reports

Related ROTCE, Net Interest Margin, Leverage

Circulating supply, max supply and crypto market cap

Covers Circulating supply · Max supply · Total supply · Annual issuance · Burn · Network fees · Crypto market cap

Tells you how many coins exist and whether more can ever be made.

Circulating supply is the coins available to trade now, leaving out any locked or held in reserve; max supply is the protocol's hard limit, if any: 21 million for bitcoin, none for ETH. Annual issuance is new coins created in a year. Crypto market cap is price × circulating supply.

FormulaMarket cap = price × circulating supply

ExampleBitcoin, Sep 22, 2026: $86,172.28 price × 20,088,131 coins in circulation ≈ $1.731 trillion market cap.

TagsCrypto

Related Cryptocurrency, Proof of work, Market cap

Combined ratio

Covers Loss ratio · Catastrophe loss ratio · Underwriting profit

Shows whether an insurer profits from the policies it writes, before investment income.

An insurer's claims plus expenses as a percentage of the premiums it earned. Under 100% means the insurance itself made money before investment income. The catastrophe loss ratio is the slice caused by disasters such as hurricanes.

Formula(Claims + claim-handling costs + underwriting expenses) ÷ earned premiums × 100

ExampleProgressive, 2025 (report as of Aug 18, 2026): 66.0 claims-and-claim-handling ratio + 21.4 expense ratio = 87.4 combined ratio, per $100 of earned premium.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related ROE, Price / Book

Consensus estimate

AKA Street estimate

Covers Analyst estimates · Expectations · Consensus EPS · Forecasts

The bar results are judged against: it decides whether a quarter counts as a beat.

The average forecast of the analysts covering a company, for revenue, EPS or another figure, for a coming quarter or year. Results are judged against it.

At the tableThe line set before the hand: the stock reacts to the result against the line, not to the result alone.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Beat and miss, Forward P/E, Sell-side analyst

Consensus Rating

AKA Analyst consensus · Average rating

Covers Moderate Buy

The Street's view in one word; the breakdown shows how united that view is.

The average of the ratings given by the analysts who cover the stock, expressed as one word on a scale from Strong Sell to Strong Buy. Some data providers add in-between labels such as Moderate Buy.

On the tear sheetThe first card in section 05, with the number of analysts and the data provider, e.g. Apple's 'Buy' from 46 analysts polled by S&P Global.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Strong Buy, Buy, Hold, Sell, Strong Sell, Sell-side analyst

Constant currency

AKA Currency-neutral

Separates a company's sales growth from swings in exchange rates.

Growth restated as if exchange rates had stayed at last year's levels, so a stronger or weaker dollar doesn't flatter or hide the trend. It removes only currency effects, as opposed to organic growth, which strips out acquisitions and businesses sold.

ExampleSales up 3% as reported, but a stronger dollar cut 2 points from overseas sales when converted: up 5% in constant currency.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Organic growth, Revenue Growth (YoY), US dollar index (DXY)

Consumer price index (CPI)

AKA CPI

Covers Core CPI · Headline inflation · Core inflation

The monthly inflation number that moves rates, bonds and the Fed.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' monthly measure of the prices urban consumers pay for a basket of goods and services (as opposed to the PCE price index, the Fed's chosen measure). Core CPI leaves out food and energy; the headline number includes them.

TagsRates & the Fed

Related PCE price index, TIPS, Breakeven inflation, Policy rate (fed funds rate), FOMC (Federal Open Market Committee), Real return and real interest rate

Contango

Covers Futures curve · Term structure

Later delivery costs more than now, so rolling a long position forward costs money.

A futures market is in contango when later delivery months cost more than nearer ones, usually because holding the thing until then costs money (as opposed to backwardation, when later months cost less).

ExampleOil for delivery next month at $70 and in six months at $73: the curve is in contango.

TagsFutures

Related Backwardation, Roll (futures), Cost of carry and fair value, Futures contract

Convenience yield

Why a commodity in hand can be worth more than one promised later.

The benefit of holding a physical commodity rather than a futures contract on it, such as keeping a refinery running when supply is short. When it outweighs storage and interest costs, the spot price rises above later months: backwardation.

TagsFutures

Related Contango, Cost of carry and fair value, Roll (futures), Spot price

Convertible bond

AKA Convertible · Convertibles · Convertible note · Convertible debt

Covers Conversion price

Debt that can turn into shares: dilution waiting to happen.

A bond the holder can exchange for a set number of the company's shares, usually paying a lower coupon than a plain bond in return. Converted, it becomes new shares, which is why the diluted share count counts them in advance when they would dilute.

TagsBondsStocks

Related Dilution, EPS (TTM), Coupon, Leverage, Bond, Rights issue

Convexity

Explains why bond prices gain more when yields fall than they lose when yields rise.

The curve in a bond's price-yield relationship that duration, a straight-line estimate, misses. For an ordinary bond, prices rise a little more when yields fall than they drop when yields rise, and the gap grows with bigger moves.

Formula% price change ≈ −duration × Δyield + ½ × convexity × Δyield²

Example10-year Treasury note, Sep 23, 2026: if yields rise 1 point, −(7.77 duration × 1%) + ½ × 73.0 convexity × (1%)² ≈ −7.40% price change.

On the tear sheetBond reports give the estimated price change for a 1-point fall and rise in yield; on the 10-year note, Sep 23, 2026, +8.13% against −7.40%. The gap is convexity.

TagsBondsRates & the Fed

Related Duration, Yield (bonds)

Correction

AKA Market correction

Covers Pullback · Sell-off

Names a fall that is sharp but short of a bear market.

By convention, a fall of at least 10% but less than 20% from a recent high (as opposed to a bear market, a fall of 20% or more). A smaller dip is often called a pullback; a sell-off is any sharp run of selling.

ExampleAn index that peaks at 5,000 and falls to 4,400 is down 12%: a correction. A fall to 4,000 or below would be a bear market.

TagsStocksStock reports

Related Bear market, Drawdown

Cost of carry and fair value

Covers Fair value · Carry · Carrying cost · Basis

Why a future's price differs from today's price without being a forecast.

The cost of holding something over time: interest on the money tied up, plus storage and insurance, less income such as dividends. A future's fair value is today's price adjusted by that cost; the gap between today's price and the futures price is the basis.

TagsFutures

Related Futures contract, Contango, Roll (futures)

Cost of goods sold (cost of sales)

AKA COGS · Cost of sales · Cost of revenue

Covers Direct costs

The direct cost of what was sold, the first cut out of revenue.

The direct cost of the goods or services a company sold in a period, such as materials, parts and factory labor. Revenue minus it is gross profit; overheads such as marketing and research sit below it, in operating expenses.

TagsFinancial statements

Related Revenue, Gross Margin, Operating Margin, Operating expenses (opex), Income statement

Coupon

AKA Coupon rate

Covers Bond interest

Tells you the cash a bond pays each year, however its price moves.

The fixed yearly interest a bond pays, as a percentage of its face value, usually in two payments a year. It is set when the bond is first sold and doesn't change.

ExampleA 4.625% coupon on $1,000 pays $23.125 every six months.

TagsBonds

Related Yield (bonds), Yield to maturity

Covered call

AKA Covered calls · Buy-write

Covers Covered-call ETF

Income now, in exchange for giving up gains above the strike.

Owning a stock and selling a call option on it. The premium is income now, but any gain above the strike goes to the call's buyer, while the downside stays with the owner. Some ETFs run the strategy across an index to pay higher distributions.

ExampleOwn a $100 stock and sell the $110 call for about $2: you keep the $2, but your gains stop at $110.

TagsOptions

Related Call option, Strike price, Distribution yield, ETF, Protective put, Put-call parity

Credit rating

Covers Bond rating · AAA · Aaa · Aa1 · Investment grade · Junk · High yield bonds · Junk bond · Rating agency

Tells you how the agencies rate a borrower's odds of paying you back.

A rating agency's grade of a borrower's ability to repay, from AAA (Aaa at Moody's) downward. BBB− (Baa3 at Moody's) and above is investment grade; below that is high yield, or junk.

ExampleMoody's has rated the US Aa1, one notch below the top, since May 2025.

TagsBonds

Related Leverage, Treasury note and Treasury bond, Net Debt / EBITDA

Credit risk and default

Covers Default · Default risk · Credit risk · Defaulted · Credit quality

The chance a borrower doesn't pay, which every bond's yield prices in.

The chance a borrower fails to pay interest or repay principal on time. Failing to is a default, and lenders then often recover only part of what they lent. Ratings and credit spreads measure it. A skipped preferred dividend is not a default.

TagsBonds

Related Credit rating, Callable (call feature), Preferred share, Leverage, Credit spread, Bond

Credit spread

AKA Credit spreads · Corporate bond spread · Spread over Treasuries

Covers High-yield spread

The extra yield lenders demand for the risk of not being repaid.

The extra yield a bond pays over a government bond of the same maturity: the market's price for the chance the borrower won't repay in full. Spreads widen when investors grow nervous about defaults and narrow when they relax.

ExampleA 10-year company bond yielding 6% when the 10-year Treasury yields 4% has a credit spread of 2 points, or 200 basis points.
