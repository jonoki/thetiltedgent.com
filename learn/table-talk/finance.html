The Tear-Sheet Glossary

Every number, ratio and acronym on our stock reports, in plain English: what it measures, how to read it, and where it sits on the page. Know what the chips are worth before you sit down.

ABCDEFGHIKLMNOPQRSTUVY#

The header

The top of every tear sheet: what the company is, where it trades, its price on the data date and when it reports next.

Static data as of

Also: Data date · Banner date · As-of date · Edition

The date the page's numbers were taken, usually a market close. Tear sheets are snapshots: prices, ratios and analyst targets stay frozen at that date until the report is refreshed.

On the tear sheetThe amber line at the very top. A refreshed report notes what changed at that edition; a card tagged New results means the company has reported since that date.

See also: Price and day change, Updated (tag), New results (tag)

Ticker

Also: Ticker symbol · Symbol · Share class

The short code a stock trades under, such as AAPL for Apple. A company with more than one class of shares has a ticker for each, such as GOOGL and GOOG for Alphabet.

On the tear sheetThe colored badge beside the company name. It also heads the company's own column in the metrics table.

See also: Exchange, Key Financial Metrics table

Exchange

Also: Stock exchange · NYSE · New York Stock Exchange · Nasdaq · NYSE Arca · TSX · Toronto Stock Exchange · Listing

The marketplace where a stock is listed and traded. Most companies here list on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) or Nasdaq; US ETFs often trade on NYSE Arca, and Canadian funds on the Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX).

On the tear sheet'Exchange:' in the header. Companies based abroad add their home market, e.g. 'NYSE (ADR) · home: Tokyo Stock Exchange'.

See also: ADR, Ticker, Mkt Cap Ranking

ADR

Also: American depositary receipt · ADS · American depositary share

A US-traded security that stands for shares of a foreign company held by a depositary bank. It lets investors buy the company in dollars on a US exchange. One ADR can equal one, several or a fraction of a home-market share.

On the tear sheetThe header gives the conversion, e.g. 'ADR: 1 ADR = 2 ordinary shares'. These reports price the US-listed shares in dollars.

See also: Exchange, Head-office tag, Index badges: S&P 500, NDX, DOW, GLOBAL

Market cap

Also: Market capitalization · Mkt Cap · Market value

What the stock market values the whole company at: the share price times the number of shares. It counts the shareholders' stake only; enterprise value adds the debt.

FormulaShare price × shares outstanding

Example$50 a share × 2 billion shares = $100 billion.

On the tear sheet'Mkt Cap' in the header, at the banner-date price. It changes every trading day with the share price.

See also: Mkt Cap Ranking, Enterprise value (EV), Large-cap, mid-cap, mega-cap, Giant (tag)

Mkt Cap Ranking

Also: Market cap rank · S&P 500 rank · Nasdaq-100 rank · Home-market rank

Where the company stands by market value; number 1 is the most valuable. For a member of the S&P 500 or the Nasdaq-100 it is our own calculation on the report's date: shares times that day's closing price for every member of the index, each company ranked against the others.

On the tear sheetBadges under the ticker, e.g. 'S&P 500: #2 · Nasdaq-100: #2' for Apple. A company in neither index has no index rank. Reports on companies listed abroad show their rank in their home market instead, as it stood when the report was built or last refreshed.

See also: Market cap, Exchange

Price and day change

Also: Share price · Close · Closing price · Settled close · Daily change

The headline price is the stock's closing price on the banner date. The arrow and figures beneath show the change from the previous session's close, in dollars and percent. A settled close is a finished session's final official price.

ExampleApple: '$308.26' and '▼ −$5.07 (−1.62%)', a fall from $313.33 the session before.

On the tear sheetThe line under the price names the session, e.g. 'Close of August 10, 2026'. The last point of the price chart equals the header price.

See also: Static data as of, After-hours and pre-market, Monthly closes

After-hours and pre-market

Also: After hours · Extended hours · Pre-market · Premarket · Extended-hours trading

Trading outside the regular US session of 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern: pre-market before the open, after-hours after the close. Fewer shares change hands, so prices can jump on news such as earnings.

On the tear sheetSome headers show an after-hours or pre-market quote under the close, with its own % change. The headline price is the regular-session close.

See also: Price and day change, Earnings report and call

Next Earnings

Also: Earnings date · Results date · Reporting date

When the company is next due to publish quarterly results, and which quarter they cover. Companies usually confirm the date a few weeks ahead; until then, data providers estimate it from past patterns.

On the tear sheetIn the header line, e.g. '~Oct 29, 2026 (Q4 FY26, est.)'. A ~ or est. means the company had not confirmed the date when the page was built.

See also: Fiscal year (FY) and quarters, Earnings report and call, Guidance, New results (tag)

Fiscal year (FY) and quarters

Also: FY · Fiscal quarter · Q1 · Q2 · Q3 · Q4 · H1 · FY2027 · Q1 FY2027 · Fiscal Year End

A company's 12-month accounting year, which need not match the calendar. Q1 to Q4 are its four fiscal quarters and H1 the first half. 'Q2 FY2027' means the second quarter of the year the company calls fiscal 2027.

ExampleNVIDIA's fiscal 2026 ended January 25, 2026, so its Q2 FY2027 is the quarter that ended July 26, 2026.

On the tear sheetUsed in Next Earnings, on segment cards ('FY25 Revenue') and in catalysts. The overview usually gives the company's year-end, e.g. Apple's fiscal 2025 ended September 27, 2025.

See also: TTM, Next Earnings, YoY and QoQ

Key Financial Metrics

Section 04 sets the company's key ratios beside an industry average and an index average, with a line of context for each row.

Key Financial Metrics table

Also: Metric · Metrics table · Financial metrics

The table in section 04. Each row is one ratio. The column headed by the ticker holds the company's figure, followed by an industry average, an index average and a Context note.

On the tear sheetSome sheets color the company's figures and say what the colors mean, e.g. Apple's: green favorable against both comparisons, red unfavorable against both, amber mixed. On phones the table scrolls sideways inside the page.

See also: Industry Avg, S&P 500, Context, ~, est., ×, n/m, n/v and —

Industry Avg

Also: Industry average · Peer average · Peer median

A typical value of the same ratio among companies in the same line of business, used as a yardstick. It is approximate, and each sheet chooses its own peer group.

On the tear sheetThird column of the metrics table. Some headers name the peers ('Railroad Avg', 'Peer Avg (KO / PEP)') or add (est.); a dash means no comparable figure.

See also: Key Financial Metrics table, S&P 500, Context

S&P 500

Also: S&P · SPX · Standard & Poor's 500

An index of about 500 large US companies, weighted by market value and chosen by a committee at S&P Dow Jones Indices. It is the benchmark most people mean by 'the market'.

On the tear sheetMost metrics tables compare with the S&P 500 in the fourth column; those index figures are approximate averages. Cards carry an S&P 500 badge with the year the company joined.

See also: Nasdaq-100, Dow Jones Industrial Average, Index badges: S&P 500, NDX, DOW, GLOBAL, Index fund

Nasdaq-100

Also: NASDAQ-100 · NDX

An index of the 100 largest companies listed on the Nasdaq stock exchange, leaving out banks and other financial companies. Technology companies make up much of it.

On the tear sheetSome metrics tables, mostly for Nasdaq-100 members, use the NASDAQ-100 as the index column instead of the S&P 500. Cards show membership as the NDX badge.

See also: S&P 500, Dow Jones Industrial Average, Index badges: S&P 500, NDX, DOW, GLOBAL

Context

Also: Context column

The last column of the metrics table: a sentence on what drives the figure, what distorts it and how it compares. When a standard ratio doesn't fit the business, it often gives the measure that does.

On the tear sheetRead it before comparing numbers. Apple's ROE of 148.8% comes with the note that buybacks have shrunk its equity, which inflates the ratio.

See also: Key Financial Metrics table, ~, est., ×, n/m, n/v and —, Notable One-Off Items

~, est., ×, n/m, n/v and —

Also: Approximately · Estimate · est. · Not meaningful · n/m · n/v · Times · Dash

Marks on the figures. ~ or ≈ means approximate; est. an estimate; × means times, as in a multiple of 20×; n/m means not meaningful, where the ratio exists but misleads for this business; n/v means no verified value; — means no figure.

On the tear sheetBank sheets show gross margin and current ratio as n/m or —, and blank ROIC or replace it with bank measures such as ROTCE; REIT sheets flag P/E and ROE in the Context column.

See also: Key Financial Metrics table, Context, Multiple, re-rating and multiple compression

52-Week Range

Also: 52-week high · 52-week low · Annual range

The lowest and highest prices over the past year. Where the current price sits between them shows how far it has come off its high or recovered from its low.

ExampleRange $40–$80, price $50: 25% above the low and 37.5% below the high.

On the tear sheetLast row of most metrics tables; Context places the price, e.g. 'Trading 5.6% off the 52-week low and 48.8% below the high' (NIKE). Fund reports may use closing prices or NAV.

See also: Beaten down (tag), All-time high, Drawdown

Dividend Yield

Also: Yield · Dividend yield %

A year's dividends per share as a percentage of the share price: the cash income each dollar invested brings in before tax, if the dividend stays the same.

FormulaAnnual dividends per share ÷ share price × 100

ExampleApple pays $1.08 a year on a $308.26 share: 1.08 ÷ 308.26 ≈ 0.35%.

On the tear sheetContext gives the dollar dividend, how often it is paid and recent raises. A falling share price lifts the yield even when the dividend is unchanged.

See also: Dividend, Payout Ratio, Income (tag), Dividend and No dividend (tags)

Trailing P/E

Also: P/E · P/E ratio · Price-to-earnings ratio · PE · Earnings multiple

How many dollars investors pay for each dollar of the company's profit over the last 12 months. A high P/E usually signals that the market expects strong growth; a low one, slower growth or doubts about the profit.

FormulaShare price ÷ EPS (TTM)

ExamplePrice $100, earnings per share over the last year $5: P/E = 100 ÷ 5 = 20, written 20×.

On the tear sheetA loss-making company has no meaningful P/E. For REITs, property depreciation depresses earnings and inflates the P/E, so their sheets point to price ÷ FFO instead.

At the tableLike valuing a player on last year's winnings: it prices the record, not the next session.

See also: EPS (TTM), Forward P/E, PEG Ratio, Multiple, re-rating and multiple compression, Value (tag)

EPS (TTM)

Also: Earnings per share · EPS · Diluted EPS

The company's profit over the last 12 months divided by its number of shares: the year's profit that belongs to each share. Diluted EPS also counts shares that options and convertible securities could add.

FormulaNet income to common shareholders ÷ weighted average diluted shares, over the last four quarters

ExampleNet income $10 billion, 2 billion shares: EPS = $5.

On the tear sheetContext gives the change from a year earlier and any one-off items inside it. A negative EPS means a loss and earns the card tag Not yet profitable.

See also: Trailing P/E, TTM, GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP), Dilution

Forward P/E

Also: Forward price-to-earnings · Forward multiple

The share price divided by the earnings per share analysts expect over the coming year. Forward below trailing means profits are expected to grow; above trailing, to shrink.

FormulaShare price ÷ consensus EPS estimate for the next fiscal year (or next 12 months)

ExamplePrice $100, expected EPS $6.25: forward P/E = 16.

On the tear sheetNIKE's forward 23.96 sat above its trailing 19.55 because analysts expected lower earnings. Forecasts are often on an adjusted basis, so the two P/Es can also differ in basis.

At the tableTrailing P/E prices the hand already shown; forward P/E prices the hand the table expects next.

See also: Trailing P/E, Consensus estimate, GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP), PEG Ratio

PEG Ratio

Also: PEG · Price/earnings-to-growth

The P/E divided by the expected yearly growth rate of earnings, in percent. It asks whether a high P/E is matched by fast growth: near 1, the two roughly match; well above 1, investors pay more per point of growth.

FormulaP/E ÷ expected annual EPS growth rate (%)

ExampleP/E 30 with earnings expected to grow 15% a year: PEG = 30 ÷ 15 = 2.0.

On the tear sheetApple's 3.15 pairs a P/E near 36 with forecast growth of about 11–13%. Growth forecasts differ between data providers, and the ratio means little when growth is near zero or negative.

See also: Trailing P/E, Forward P/E, Consensus estimate

Debt-to-Equity

Also: D/E · Debt/equity ratio · Gearing

Borrowings compared with the shareholders' stake in the company. A higher figure means more of the business is financed with debt, which magnifies both returns and risk.

FormulaTotal debt ÷ shareholders' equity (some data providers use total liabilities)

Example$50 billion of debt against $100 billion of equity: D/E = 0.5.

On the tear sheetBuybacks shrink equity and can inflate the ratio, as Apple's Context notes; negative equity makes it meaningless. Banks run high ratios by design, and their sheets point to CET1.

See also: Leverage and net debt, Net Debt / EBITDA, CET1 Ratio, Quality (tag)

Current Ratio

Also: Liquidity ratio · Quick ratio · Acid-test ratio

Assets that turn into cash within a year against bills due within a year: a test of short-term liquidity. Above 1, short-term assets exceed short-term debts. The quick ratio is stricter and leaves out inventory.

FormulaCurrent assets ÷ current liabilities; quick ratio = (cash + marketable securities + receivables) ÷ current liabilities

On the tear sheetMarked not meaningful for banks and REITs. NVIDIA's Context sets its current ratio of 4.59 beside a quick ratio of 2.92; the gap is inventory.

See also: Debt-to-Equity, Free Cash Flow

Revenue Growth (YoY)

Also: Sales growth · Top-line growth · Revenue growth

How much more, or less, the company sold than a year earlier, in percent. It is the plainest measure of whether the business is getting bigger.

Formula(Revenue this period ÷ revenue in the same period a year earlier − 1) × 100

Example$110 billion this year against $100 billion last year: +10%.

On the tear sheetThe period varies: the trailing 12 months, the last fiscal year or the latest quarter. Context says which; Apple's +14.2% is trailing 12 months.

See also: Revenue, YoY and QoQ, Organic growth and constant currency, Growth (tag)

Gross Margin

Also: Gross profit margin · Gross profit

The share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making or buying what was sold. High gross margins usually point to pricing power or software-like products; low ones to manufacturing, retail or commodities.

Formula(Revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue × 100

ExampleSales $100, cost of goods $60: gross margin 40%.

On the tear sheetNot meaningful for banks. Apple's 48.7% sits far above its hardware peers' ~35%.

See also: Operating Margin, Net Profit Margin, Margin expansion and operating leverage

Operating Margin

Also: Operating profit margin · EBIT margin · Operating income

The share of sales left after all running costs, including research, marketing and administration, but before interest and taxes. It shows how profitable the core business is.

FormulaOperating income ÷ revenue × 100

ExampleSales $100, operating income $20: operating margin 20%.

On the tear sheetA row on some sheets. 'Adj. Operating Margin' leaves out items management treats as one-off.

See also: Gross Margin, Net Profit Margin, Margin expansion and operating leverage, GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP)

Net Profit Margin

Also: Net margin · Profit margin · Bottom line

The share of sales that ends up as profit after every cost, interest payment and tax: the bottom line as a percentage of the top line.

FormulaNet income ÷ revenue × 100

ExampleApple: $128.9 billion of net income on $466.8 billion of revenue ≈ 27.6%.

On the tear sheetOne-off gains or charges can swing it; the Notable One-Off Items note under the table flags them.

See also: Gross Margin, Operating Margin, Notable One-Off Items

ROE

Also: Return on equity · ROA · Return on assets

Profit as a percentage of the shareholders' stake (equity): how hard the owners' money is working. Return on assets (ROA) measures profit against everything the company owns instead, which suits banks.

FormulaROE = net income ÷ average shareholders' equity × 100; ROA = net income ÷ average total assets × 100

On the tear sheetBuybacks and debt shrink equity and inflate ROE: Apple's 148.8% is flagged as a product of its capital structure. Bank sheets point to ROTCE in Context.

See also: ROIC, ROTCE, Debt-to-Equity, Buyback

ROIC

Also: Return on invested capital

After-tax operating profit as a percentage of all the money invested in the business by lenders and shareholders alike. It shows what each invested dollar earns, and is harder to inflate with debt than ROE.

FormulaOperating income × (1 − tax rate) ÷ (debt + equity − cash); data providers define invested capital slightly differently

ExampleAfter-tax operating profit $15 billion on $100 billion invested: ROIC 15%.

On the tear sheetNot meaningful for banks, where JPMorgan's sheet uses ROTCE. A high ROIC with little debt earns the card tag Quality.

See also: ROE, Quality (tag), ROTCE

Beta

Also: Beta (5Y) · Market beta · Correlation · Correlation with S&P 500 · Beta vs S&P 500

How much a stock has tended to move when the market moves: 1 means in step, above 1 bigger swings, below 1 smaller. Correlation, from −1 to +1, measures how consistently the two move together.

FormulaCovariance of the stock's returns with the market's ÷ variance of the market's returns

ExampleBeta 1.5: when the market has moved 10%, the stock has typically moved about 15%.

On the tear sheetMeasured against the S&P 500; where a sheet states its period, it is usually five years. Bitcoin's beta of 1.82 with a correlation of 0.51 means much of its movement is unrelated to stocks.

At the tableA high-beta stock plays like a loose-aggressive opponent: bigger swings both ways, which says nothing about whether it wins.

See also: Volatility, Steady and Rollercoaster (tags), S&P 500

Free Cash Flow

Also: FCF · FCF yield · FCF margin · Free cash flow yield · Operating cash flow

The cash a business generated after paying its running costs and its investment in equipment, buildings and the like. It is what's left for dividends, buybacks, paying down debt or acquisitions. FCF yield compares it with market cap.

FormulaOperating cash flow − capital expenditures; FCF yield = FCF ÷ market cap × 100

ExampleApple: $136.7 billion of free cash flow over 12 months, about 29% of revenue.

On the tear sheetNot meaningful for banks. A REIT's can be negative by design when property purchases count as investment, as Realty Income's Context explains.

See also: Capex, Cash machine (tag), EBITDA

Short Interest

Also: Short float · Short selling · Shares short · Days to cover · Short squeeze

Shares that investors have sold short, a bet that the price will fall, as a percentage of the shares available to trade (or of all shares). Days to cover divides shares short by average daily trading volume.

FormulaShares sold short ÷ float × 100; days to cover = shares short ÷ average daily volume

ExampleApple: about 147 million shares short on a 14.6 billion share float ≈ 1.0%.

On the tear sheetThe label or its tooltip gives the basis, % of float or % of shares. FINRA publishes it twice a month, so some sheets date it to a settlement date. Double digits means heavy betting against the stock.

At the tableThe share of the table betting against the hand. When a crowded short is forced to fold at once, the price can jump.

See also: Float and share count, Beta

EV/EBITDA

Also: Enterprise value to EBITDA · EV / EBITDA · Enterprise multiple

The value of the whole business, debt included, divided by its yearly operating earnings before depreciation. Because it counts debt, it compares companies that borrow different amounts more evenly than P/E does.

FormulaEnterprise value ÷ EBITDA (trailing 12 months)

ExampleEnterprise value $120 billion, EBITDA $10 billion: 12×.

See also: Enterprise value (EV), EBITDA, Multiple, re-rating and multiple compression

Price / Book

Also: P/B · Price-to-book · Book value · Book value per share · Tangible book value · Shareholders' equity

The share price against book value per share, the accounting value of what shareholders own (assets minus liabilities). Tangible book also leaves out intangibles such as goodwill, the premium paid for past acquisitions. Most used for banks and insurers, whose assets are mostly financial.

FormulaShare price ÷ (shareholders' equity ÷ shares outstanding)

ExamplePrice $60, book value $40 a share: P/B = 1.5.

See also: ROE, ROTCE, Impairment and goodwill

Net Debt / EBITDA

Also: Leverage ratio · Debt / EBITDA · Net leverage · EBITDAre

How many years of operating earnings it would take to repay debt net of cash. Lenders and rating agencies watch it; a higher figure means heavier borrowing.

Formula(Total debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA

ExampleDebt $30 billion, cash $5 billion, EBITDA $10 billion: 2.5×.

On the tear sheetSome REIT sheets, such as Realty Income's, use EBITDAre, the real-estate version of EBITDA defined by Nareit, the REIT industry body.

See also: Leverage and net debt, EBITDA, Debt-to-Equity, Credit rating

Payout Ratio

Also: Dividend payout ratio · Dividend cover

The share of profit paid out as dividends. A low ratio leaves room to raise the dividend; one above 100% means paying out more than the company earns.

FormulaDividends per share ÷ EPS × 100 (REITs: dividends ÷ FFO or AFFO)

ExampleEPS $4, dividend $1.20: payout ratio 30%.

See also: Dividend, Dividend Yield, FFO and AFFO

Notable One-Off Items

Also: One-time items · Non-recurring items · Special items

Gains or charges that won't repeat, such as a legal fine, an asset sale or a write-down. They can make one period's profit look much better or worse than the business's normal run.

On the tear sheetA note under the metrics table lists them and the figures they distort, e.g. tariff refunds adding about 2 percentage points to Apple's gross margin in one quarter.

See also: GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP), Impairment and goodwill, Net Profit Margin

Net Interest Margin

Also: NIM · Net interest income · NII

A bank's core spread: interest earned on loans and securities minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings, as a share of the assets that earn interest. Net interest income (NII) is the same spread in dollars.

Formula(Interest income − interest expense) ÷ average interest-earning assets × 100

ExampleA NIM of 2.6% means about $2.60 of net interest a year on each $100 of loans and securities.

On the tear sheetBank sheets use it in place of gross margin, which doesn't apply to a bank; JPMorgan's Context gives a NIM of about 2.6%.

See also: ROTCE, CET1 Ratio, Efficiency Ratio

ROTCE

Also: Return on tangible common equity

A bank's profit as a percentage of its tangible common equity: shareholders' equity minus goodwill, other intangibles and preferred stock. Stripping out acquisition accounting lets banks be compared on the capital that absorbs losses.

FormulaNet income to common shareholders ÷ average tangible common equity × 100

See also: ROE, Price / Book, CET1 Ratio

CET1 Ratio

Also: Common equity tier 1 · CET1 · Capital ratio

A bank's highest-quality capital, mainly common equity and retained earnings, as a percentage of its risk-weighted assets: loans and other holdings, each scaled by how risky it is. Regulators set minimums; a higher ratio means a thicker cushion against losses.

FormulaCommon equity tier 1 capital ÷ risk-weighted assets × 100

See also: ROTCE, Net Interest Margin, Leverage and net debt

Efficiency Ratio

Also: Cost-to-income ratio · Net charge-off rate · Charge-offs

A bank's operating costs as a share of its revenue; lower means leaner. Banks also report the net charge-off rate: loans written off as uncollectable in a year, net of recoveries, as a share of loans.

FormulaNon-interest expense ÷ total revenue × 100; net charge-off rate = net charge-offs ÷ average loans × 100

ExampleCosts of $55 on revenue of $100: efficiency ratio 55%.

See also: Net Interest Margin, ROTCE

FFO and AFFO

Also: Funds from operations · Adjusted funds from operations · P/FFO · P/AFFO · AFFO per share

The REIT version of earnings. FFO adds back property depreciation, which cuts net income while buildings often hold their value, and removes gains on property sales. AFFO also subtracts recurring upkeep spending; each company defines it slightly differently.

FormulaFFO = net income + real-estate depreciation and amortization − gains (or + losses) on property sales + impairments of depreciable real estate; P/FFO = share price ÷ FFO per share

ExampleRealty Income: $63.17 share price ÷ $4.445 guided AFFO a share ≈ 14.2× forward P/AFFO.

On the tear sheetREIT sheets keep the standard rows but flag P/E, and often ROE, as distorted by depreciation, and give FFO or AFFO figures in Context.

See also: REIT, Payout Ratio, Trailing P/E

Combined ratio

Also: Loss ratio · Catastrophe loss ratio · Underwriting profit

An insurer's claims plus expenses as a percentage of the premiums it earned. Under 100% means the insurance itself made money before investment income. The catastrophe loss ratio is the slice caused by disasters such as hurricanes.

Formula(Claims + claim-handling costs + underwriting expenses) ÷ earned premiums × 100

ExamplePremiums $100, claims $65, expenses $28: combined ratio 93%.

See also: ROE, Price / Book

Rate base and allowed ROE

Also: Rate base · Allowed ROE · Regulated utility

For a regulated utility, the rate base is the value of its network (plants, wires, pipes) on which regulators let it earn a return. The allowed ROE is that approved return on equity, so profit grows mainly as the rate base grows.

ExampleA $10 billion rate base, half funded by equity, at a 10% allowed ROE: about $500 million of allowed profit.

See also: ROE, Capex

Analyst Consensus

Section 05 sums up what Wall Street analysts say about the stock: their ratings, where they think the price is heading, and who owns the shares.

Consensus Rating

Also: Analyst consensus · Average rating · Moderate Buy

The average of the ratings given by the analysts who cover the stock, expressed as one word on a scale from Strong Sell to Strong Buy. Some data providers add in-between labels such as Moderate Buy.

On the tear sheetThe first card in section 05, with the number of analysts and the data provider, e.g. Apple's 'Buy' from 46 analysts polled by S&P Global.

See also: Strong Buy, Buy, Hold, Sell, Strong Sell, Rating Breakdown, Sell-side analyst

Strong Buy, Buy, Hold, Sell, Strong Sell

Also: Analyst rating · Stock rating · Recommendation

The common five-step scale for analysts' ratings. Buy means the analyst expects the stock to do better than the market or its sector over the next year or so; Hold, to keep pace; Sell, to lag.

On the tear sheetRating Breakdown counts the analysts on each step. Firms use their own words, which data providers map onto this scale.

See also: Overweight, Outperform, Neutral and other house ratings, Consensus Rating, Rating Breakdown

Overweight, Outperform, Neutral and other house ratings

Also: Overweight · Equal Weight · Underweight · Outperform · Market Perform · Sector Perform · Peer Perform · Underperform · Neutral

Firms' own rating words, which US rules require each firm to sort into Buy, Hold and Sell. Overweight and Outperform usually count as Buy; Equal Weight, Market Perform, Sector Perform, Peer Perform and Neutral as Hold; Underweight and Underperform as Sell.

On the tear sheetThey appear in Key Recent Analyst Calls as each firm published them, e.g. Jefferies' downgrade of Apple to Underperform.

See also: Strong Buy, Buy, Hold, Sell, Strong Sell, Upgrade, downgrade, reiterate, initiate

Rating Breakdown

Also: Analyst ratings distribution

How many analysts rate the stock at each step of the scale. A lopsided count and a split one can produce the same consensus word, so the counts say more.

On the tear sheetCounts and a bar, e.g. Apple's '27 Buy · 14 Hold · 5 Sell', split further as 21 Strong Buy / 6 Buy / 14 Hold / 2 Sell / 3 Strong Sell.

See also: Consensus Rating, Strong Buy, Buy, Hold, Sell, Strong Sell

Price target

Also: PT · Target price · Analyst price target

An analyst's estimate of where the share price will be, usually 12 months out. It comes from a valuation model, often expected earnings times a chosen multiple.

ExampleExpected EPS of $10 next year × a P/E of 25 = a $250 target.

On the tear sheetAnalyst calls quote targets with the change, e.g. 'PT cut $286 → $264'.

See also: Average Price Target, Target Range, Multiple, re-rating and multiple compression

Average Price Target

Also: Consensus price target · Mean price target · Upside · Downside · Upside to target

The average of the price targets published by the analysts a data provider tracks. The gap between it and the current price is the upside (or downside) analysts expect, not counting dividends.

FormulaUpside = (average price target ÷ current price − 1) × 100

Target Range

The lowest and highest individual price targets among the analysts covering the stock. A wide range means analysts disagree sharply about the company's prospects.

On the tear sheetEach end is shown as a percentage from the price, e.g. Apple's '$215 – $400', from −30.3% to +29.8%.

Monthly closes

Also: Month-end close · Price chart · Five-year chart

The closing price on the last trading day of each month. Sixty of them make a five-year chart that shows the long trend without the day-to-day noise.

On the tear sheetThe section 07 chart usually runs 60 month-end closes up to the banner-date close, which equals the header price. Companies listed for less than five years are charted from their first trading month.

Stock split and split-adjusted prices

Also: Split · Split-adjusted · Reverse split · Share split

A split divides each share into several, lowering the price per share without changing the company's value; a reverse split combines shares. Split-adjusted charts rescale older prices so a split doesn't look like a crash.

Example4-for-1 split: one $400 share becomes four $100 shares, and earlier prices are divided by 4.

On the tear sheetChart titles and notes state the basis, e.g. '(SPLIT-ADJUSTED)'. Stock charts are not adjusted for dividends unless the chart says so.

Total return

Also: Price return · Dividend-adjusted · Dividends reinvested · Adjusted close · Rebased

The gain from the price change plus dividends, assuming each dividend is reinvested. Price return counts the price alone, so for a dividend payer total return is higher.

Formula(Ending value with dividends reinvested ÷ starting value − 1) × 100

ExampleApple, five years to August 2026: price return about +103%, total return about +107%.

On the tear sheetMost stock charts plot price only, and the chart note says when one is dividend-adjusted. The VOO, VFV, XEQT and BND charts add a total-return line, rebased to start at the same price as the price line.

See also: CAGR, Dividend, Monthly closes

Moving average (MA)

Also: MA · SMA · Simple moving average · 20-Day MA · 1-Month MA

The average of the last several closing prices, recalculated each period, so the line smooths out short-term swings and shows the trend. A price above a rising average is usually read as an uptrend.

FormulaSum of the last n closes ÷ n

On the tear sheetDashed lines on the price chart. Because most charts use month-end closes, their 1-month line (labeled '1-Month MA' or '20-Day MA') is the month-end price itself and sits on the price line.

See also: 50-day moving average, 200-day moving average, RSI (Relative Strength Index)

50-day moving average

Also: 50-day · 50-Day MA · 3-Month MA · 50 DMA

The average closing price over the last 50 trading days, about ten weeks. Traders use it as a gauge of the medium-term trend.

On the tear sheetOn the five-year chart it is approximated by the average of the last three month-end closes, labeled '3-Month MA' or '50-Day MA'.

See also: Moving average (MA), 200-day moving average

200-day moving average

Also: 200-day · 200-Day MA · 10-Month MA · 200 DMA

The average closing price over the last 200 trading days, about ten months. It is the most widely watched long-term trend line, and a price crossing it tends to draw comment.

On the tear sheetOn the five-year chart it is approximated by the average of the last ten month-end closes, labeled '10-Month MA' or '200-Day MA'.

See also: Moving average (MA), 50-day moving average

RSI (Relative Strength Index)

Also: RSI · Relative Strength Index · Overbought · Oversold · Momentum

A 0–100 gauge of how strongly a price has risen versus fallen over recent periods. Above 70 is commonly read as overbought (a hot run), below 30 as oversold (a deep slide). It measures momentum, not value.

FormulaRSI = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + average gain ÷ average loss), over n periods with Wilder's smoothing

On the tear sheetThe panel under the price chart. Most sheets use 6 periods of monthly closes rather than the usual 14 days, so it moves slowly and flags multi-month extremes.

At the tableA read on how hot the run has been, not on the cards: a heater can stay hot well past 70.

See also: Moving average (MA), Monthly closes

Drawdown

Also: Max drawdown · Maximum drawdown · Peak to trough

A fall from a previous high, in percent. The maximum drawdown is the largest such fall over a period, from a peak to the lowest point before a new high.

Formula(Trough price ÷ prior peak price − 1) × 100

ExampleA stock that falls from $200 to $130 has a 35% drawdown.

On the tear sheetThe chart story names each big fall, e.g. Apple's −27% in 2022. Fund and crypto reports give a max drawdown row; month-end data understate falls within a month.

At the tableA downswing: the deepest hole below the bankroll's last high before it recovered.

See also: Bear market and correction, All-time high, Volatility

All-time high

Also: ATH · Record high · Intraday high

The highest price a stock has ever reached. An intraday high is reached during a session and can sit above any closing price.

On the tear sheetTimelines mark milestone highs, e.g. Apple's all-time intraday high of $344.57 in late July 2026.

See also: 52-Week Range, Drawdown

Bear market and correction

Also: Bear market · Correction · Bull market · Pullback · Sell-off

By convention, a correction is a fall of 10% or more from a recent high, and a bear market a fall of 20% or more. A bull market is a long stretch of rising prices.

On the tear sheetUsed in the five-year story, e.g. Apple's 'rate-shock bear market low' of June 2022.

See also: Drawdown, Cyclical, defensive and secular, Bull case and bear case

Volatility

Also: Standard deviation · Annualized volatility · Volatility (annualised) · 5-yr volatility

How widely returns swing around their average, measured as the standard deviation of returns and usually scaled to a yearly figure. Higher volatility means a bumpier ride in both directions.

FormulaStandard deviation of monthly returns × √12

ExampleFive years to August 2026: VOO 15.87% a year; bitcoin 56.3%.

On the tear sheetFund and crypto reports compute it from up to 60 monthly returns (ARTI since its 2024 launch); stock sheets discuss it in the prose and through beta.

At the tableVariance in poker terms: how far results swing around the average, whether or not the player is winning.

Company, moat and earnings

Words that recur in the company sections, earnings coverage and catalysts: how a company makes money, how it reports, and how the market reacts.

Revenue

Also: Sales · Top line · Net sales · Turnover

The money a company takes in from selling its goods and services, before any costs. It sits at the top of the income statement, hence 'top line'.

On the tear sheetSegment cards give revenue for the latest fiscal year or quarter, e.g. 'FY25 Revenue' or 'Q2'26 Revenue'.

See also: Revenue Growth (YoY), Segment and revenue mix, Net Profit Margin

Segment and revenue mix

Also: Business segment · Division · Revenue mix · Rev Mix · Reportable segment

A segment is a business line the company reports separately, such as Apple's iPhone or Services. Revenue mix is each segment's share of total revenue, which shows what the company really depends on.

On the tear sheetSection 02 opens with a bar splitting revenue by segment (Apple: iPhone 50.4%, Services 26.2%), then a card per segment with its revenue, growth, market share and main competitors.

See also: Revenue, Market share, Industry size (TAM)

Market share

Also: Unit share · Mkt Share · Share of market

A company's slice of all sales in its market, counted by revenue or by units sold. The two can differ widely: Apple has about 20% of smartphone units but about 45% of the industry's revenue.

See also: Segment and revenue mix, Industry size (TAM), Moat

Industry size (TAM)

Also: TAM · Total addressable market · Category size · Addressable market

The total yearly spending on a product category: the most a company could sell if it had every customer. It frames how much room a business has to grow.

On the tear sheetA line under each segment card, e.g. 'Industry Size: Global smartphone market ~$520B; ~2–3% CAGR projected through 2030'.

See also: Market share, CAGR, Segment and revenue mix

Moat

Also: Competitive advantage · Economic moat · Switching costs · Network effects · Pricing power

A lasting advantage that keeps competitors from taking a company's profits, like a moat around a castle. Common sources: brand, switching costs, network effects, scale and cost advantages, patents and licenses.

On the tear sheetSection 03 lists 'Sources of Moat' and explains them, then sets out 'Key Vulnerabilities', the ways the moat could be breached.

See also: Market share, Gross Margin, ROIC

Catalyst

Also: Upcoming catalysts · Event · Catalysts & News

A known or expected event that could move the share price, such as an earnings report, product launch, court ruling, regulatory decision or index change.

On the tear sheetSection 06 dates each event and tags it, e.g. 'Potential upside catalyst', 'Key near-term event' or 'Negative reaction (already occurred)'.

At the tableLike the turn or the river: a card still to come that can change what the hand is worth.

See also: Next Earnings, Guidance, Bull case and bear case

Bull case and bear case

Also: Bull Case · Bear Case · Bull · Bear · Bullish · Bearish · Top 3 Risks

The bull case is the argument for the stock doing well; the bear case, the argument against. A bull expects prices to rise, a bear expects them to fall.

On the tear sheetSection 08 sets '▲ Bull Case' beside '▼ Bear Case', followed by the Top 3 Risks.

See also: Catalyst, Bear market and correction

Guidance

Also: Outlook · Company forecast · Raised guidance · Guidance cut

Management's own forecast for coming results, usually revenue, margins or EPS for the next quarter or year, given alongside earnings. A raise or cut often moves the stock more than the results themselves.

On the tear sheetCatalysts and Context notes cite it, e.g. Apple guiding September-quarter growth down to 9–11%, behind a 7.4% one-day drop.

See also: Earnings report and call, Consensus estimate, Beat and miss

Consensus estimate

Also: Street estimate · Analyst estimates · Expectations · Consensus EPS · Forecasts

The average forecast of the analysts covering a company, for revenue, EPS or another figure, for a coming quarter or year. Results are judged against it.

At the tableThe line set before the hand: the stock reacts to the result against the line, not to the result alone.

See also: Beat and miss, Forward P/E, Sell-side analyst

Beat and miss

Also: Earnings beat · Earnings miss · Earnings surprise · EPS surprise · In line

A beat is a result above the consensus estimate; a miss is below it. The surprise is the gap, usually in percent. A beat paired with weak guidance can still send the stock down.

FormulaSurprise = (actual − consensus estimate) ÷ consensus estimate × 100

ExampleConsensus EPS $2.00, actual $2.10: a 5% beat.

On the tear sheetApple's July 2026 results beat on revenue and EPS, but Services missed forecasts and guidance slowed; the stock fell 7.35% the next day.

See also: Consensus estimate, Guidance, Earnings report and call

Earnings report and call

Also: Earnings · Earnings release · Earnings call · Conference call · The print · Results · Earnings season

Each quarter a company publishes its results, the earnings release or 'print', and management takes analysts' questions on a conference call. US companies usually report within a few weeks after a quarter ends.

On the tear sheetNext Earnings in the header gives the date. The card tag New results flags results published after the page's data date.

See also: Next Earnings, Guidance, Beat and miss, SEC filings: 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, New results (tag)

Buyback

Also: Share repurchase · Stock buyback · Repurchase authorization · Buyback yield

A company buying its own shares on the market, which cuts the share count so each remaining share owns more of the profits. An authorization is the board's permission to buy, not a commitment.

FormulaBuyback yield = dollars spent on net share repurchases over 12 months ÷ market cap × 100

ExampleApple announced a new $100 billion repurchase authorization in April 2026.

At the tableThe company buys chips back from the table, so each chip left claims a bigger share of the pot.

See also: Dilution, Dividend, ROE

Dividend

Also: Dividends · Ex-dividend date · Record date · Dividend raise · Quarterly dividend

Cash a company pays its shareholders out of profits, usually every quarter in the US. Shares bought on or after the ex-dividend date don't receive the next payment, and the price typically drops by about the dividend that day.

On the tear sheetContext in the Dividend Yield row gives the yearly amount, the quarterly payment and the latest raise, e.g. Apple's $0.27 a quarter.

See also: Dividend Yield, Payout Ratio, Dividend and No dividend (tags), Total return

EBITDA

Also: Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization · Adjusted EBITDA

Operating profit before the non-cash charges for the wearing out of assets (depreciation and amortization), and before interest and taxes. It approximates cash earnings from operations, whatever the company's borrowing.

FormulaOperating income + depreciation + amortization

See also: EV/EBITDA, Net Debt / EBITDA, Free Cash Flow

Enterprise value (EV)

Also: EV · Firm value

The value of the whole business to everyone with a claim on it: the stock-market value plus debt, minus cash. Roughly what a buyer would pay to take over the company and its debts.

FormulaMarket cap + total debt + preferred stock + minority interests − cash and equivalents

ExampleMarket cap $100 billion, debt $30 billion, cash $10 billion: EV = $120 billion.

See also: Market cap, EV/EBITDA, Leverage and net debt

Multiple, re-rating and multiple compression

Also: Valuation multiple · Re-rating · De-rating · Multiple compression · Multiple expansion · P/S · Price-to-sales · Valuation

A multiple is a price divided by a measure of the business, such as P/E, EV/EBITDA or price-to-sales (market cap ÷ revenue). A re-rating is the market paying a different multiple; compression means a lower one.

ExampleProfits up 10% while the P/E falls from 30 to 24: the share price drops about 12%.

See also: Trailing P/E, EV/EBITDA, Forward P/E

Margin expansion and operating leverage

Also: Margin expansion · Margin compression · Operating leverage

Margin expansion is a rising share of revenue kept as profit. Operating leverage is a common cause: when many costs are fixed, extra sales add mostly profit, so profit grows faster than revenue. It works in reverse when sales fall.

ExampleSales $100, variable costs $50, fixed costs $30: profit $20. Sales up 10% to $110: profit $25, up 25%.

See also: Gross Margin, Operating Margin, Cyclical, defensive and secular

CAGR

Also: Compound annual growth rate · Annualized return · Annualized · Per year

The steady yearly growth rate that would turn a starting value into an ending value over a number of years. It turns a bumpy path into one comparable annual figure.

Formula(Ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

Example$100 growing to $200 in five years: 2^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 14.9% a year.

See also: Total return, Revenue Growth (YoY), Industry size (TAM)

Basis points and percentage points

Also: bps · bp · Basis point · pp · Percentage point · pts

A percentage point (pp) is the plain difference between two percentages; a basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percentage point. A margin rising from 40% to 42% is up 2 pp, or 200 bps, a 5% increase.

See also: Gross Margin, Yield (bonds) and the yield curve

TTM

Also: Trailing twelve months · LTM · Last twelve months

The four most recently reported quarters added together. It gives a full-year figure that is more current than the last fiscal year.

ExampleWith results through June 2026, TTM covers July 2025 to June 2026.

See also: EPS (TTM), Fiscal year (FY) and quarters, YoY and QoQ

YoY and QoQ

Also: Year over year · Quarter over quarter · Sequential · YoY Growth

Year over year (YoY) compares a period with the same period a year earlier, which removes seasonal swings. Quarter over quarter (QoQ, or sequential) compares it with the quarter just before.

ExampleThe June 2026 quarter against June 2025 is YoY; against March 2026 is QoQ.

See also: Revenue Growth (YoY), TTM

GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP)

Also: GAAP · Non-GAAP · Adjusted EPS · Adjusted earnings · Generally Accepted Accounting Principles · IFRS

GAAP are the US accounting rules behind US companies' filings; many companies abroad use IFRS, the international rules, instead. Adjusted (non-GAAP) figures leave out items such as stock-based pay or one-off charges, are usually higher, and must be reconciled to GAAP.

See also: Notable One-Off Items, EPS (TTM), SEC filings: 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K

SEC filings: 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K

Also: SEC · Securities and Exchange Commission · 10-K · 10-Q · 8-K · Annual report · Quarterly report · EDGAR

The SEC regulates US securities markets. Listed companies file an annual report (10-K), quarterly reports for the other three quarters (10-Q) and current reports on major events (8-K), all free on the SEC's EDGAR site.

On the tear sheetMany reports end with a Sources note naming the filings used, e.g. Apple's FY2025 Form 10-K.

See also: GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP), Key Institutional Investors, Insider

Insider

Also: Insider buying · Insider selling · Form 4 · Insider ownership

A company's officers and directors and anyone owning more than 10% of its shares. They must report their trades in the stock to the SEC on Form 4 within two business days.

See also: Key Institutional Investors, Float and share count, SEC filings: 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K

Float and share count

Also: Free float · Public float · Shares outstanding · Share count

Shares outstanding are all the shares a company has issued to investors. The float is the part available for the public to trade, leaving out stakes held by insiders, founders or governments. Index weights and short interest often use the float.

See also: Short Interest, Market cap, Dilution, Insider

Spin-off

Also: Spinoff · Separation · Demerger

A company turns a division into a separate listed company and hands its shares to existing shareholders. The parent's share price drops by roughly the value carved out.

On the tear sheetSome sheets chart only from the spin-off date, e.g. 'Stock Price Since the Exelon Spin-Off'.

See also: IPO, Stock split and split-adjusted prices

IPO

Also: Initial public offering · Listing · Direct listing · Going public

A company's first sale of shares to the public, and the start of trading on an exchange.

On the tear sheetCompanies listed for less than five years get charts that start at the listing, e.g. 'Stock Price Analysis Since IPO (under 5 years)'.

See also: Spin-off, Float and share count

Backlog

Also: Order book · Remaining performance obligations · RPO · Orders

Signed orders or contracts not yet delivered, and so not yet counted as revenue. A growing backlog points to revenue still to come; software companies report a similar figure as remaining performance obligations (RPO).

See also: Revenue, Guidance

Capex

Also: Capital expenditure · Capital spending · CapEx

Money spent on long-lasting assets such as factories, equipment, networks or data centers. It is subtracted from operating cash flow to get free cash flow, and charged against profit gradually as depreciation.

See also: Free Cash Flow, EBITDA, Hyperscaler, Rate base and allowed ROE

Dilution

Also: Dilutive · Accretive · Stock-based compensation · SBC · Diluted shares

When a company issues new shares, through stock-based pay, acquisitions or fundraising, each existing share owns a smaller slice. A deal is accretive if it raises EPS and dilutive if it lowers it.

At the tableNew chips issued into the game make every existing chip a smaller share of the pot.

See also: Buyback, EPS (TTM), Float and share count

Leverage and net debt

Also: Leverage · Borrowing · Net debt · Net cash · Deleveraging · Financial leverage

Leverage is the use of borrowed money. Net debt is debt minus cash; when cash exceeds debt the company has net cash. Leverage magnifies returns to shareholders in good years and losses in bad ones.

FormulaNet debt = total debt − cash and short-term investments

ExampleApple: $84 billion of debt against $147 billion of cash and investments, a net cash position of about $63 billion.

At the tablePlaying with borrowed chips: wins and losses both get bigger, and the lender is paid before you.

See also: Debt-to-Equity, Net Debt / EBITDA, Credit rating

Cyclical, defensive and secular

Also: Cyclical · Defensive · Economic cycle · Secular · Secular trend

A cyclical business rises and falls with the economy (carmakers, chips, steel, airlines); a defensive one sells what people buy regardless (food, utilities, medicines). Secular describes a long-term trend that runs through the cycles.

See also: Beta, Bear market and correction, Margin expansion and operating leverage

Organic growth and constant currency

Also: Organic · Constant currency · Currency-neutral · Comparable sales · Same-store sales · Comps

Organic growth leaves out revenue added by acquisitions or lost by selling businesses. Constant currency also removes exchange-rate effects. Comparable (same-store) sales do the same for retailers, counting only stores open for a year or more.

See also: Revenue Growth (YoY), YoY and QoQ

Impairment and goodwill

Also: Write-down · Goodwill · Impairment charge · Write-off · Intangibles

Goodwill is the premium paid in an acquisition above the value of the assets bought. An impairment, or write-down, cuts an asset's book value when it is worth less than recorded; it lowers reported profit without costing cash at the time.

See also: Notable One-Off Items, Price / Book, GAAP and adjusted (non-GAAP)

Hyperscaler

Also: Hyperscalers · Cloud giants

One of the few companies that run data centers at vast scale, for cloud services or their own apps, and spend tens of billions of dollars a year on them: Amazon, Microsoft and Google, often with Meta and Oracle counted in.

See also: Capex, Segment and revenue mix

REIT

Also: Real estate investment trust

A company that owns or finances income-producing property. To keep REIT status, which spares it corporate income tax on the profit it pays out, it must pay at least 90% of its taxable income as dividends. That keeps REIT dividend yields high.

On the tear sheetDepreciation depresses a REIT's earnings, so its sheet points to FFO and AFFO rather than P/E; the Quality tag leaves REITs out.

Large-cap, mid-cap, mega-cap

Rough size bands by market cap, with no official lines. A common convention puts large-cap at $10 billion or more and mid-cap at about $2 billion to $10 billion. Mega-cap means the very largest.

On the tear sheetThe cards' Giant tag uses $200 billion as its line.

See also: EPS (TTM), TTM

Dividend and No dividend (tags)

Also: No dividend · Dividend tag

Dividend means the company pays one; the tooltip gives the yield as dollars a year per $100 invested. No dividend means shareholders gain only if the price rises. The Dividend tag is left off when the stock already has Income.

See also: Dividend, Dividend Yield, Income (tag)

Head-office tag

The country of the company's head office, e.g. US-based or Ireland-based, taken from the report's HQ line. It is about home base only; many companies earn much of their money abroad.

See also: ADR, Index badges: S&P 500, NDX, DOW, GLOBAL

On the tear sheetThe report viewer shows the same note above the page, with the release date and the date of the figures below.

ETFs, crypto and bonds

Terms from the ETF, crypto and bond reports: what a fund costs and holds, how bonds are priced, and how crypto networks work.

ETF

Also: Exchange-traded fund · Fund · ETP · Exchange-traded product

A fund whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day, like a company's shares. Each share is a slice of the fund's whole portfolio, so one purchase can hold hundreds of stocks, thousands of bonds or a commodity such as gold.

On the tear sheetETF reports follow the stock-report layout, with a Key Fund Metrics table in section 04 and a cost and peer comparison in section 05.

See also: Index fund, Expense ratio, NAV, Holdings

Index fund

Also: Passive fund · Index tracking · Full replication · Tracking difference · Rebalance · Benchmark index · Tracking vs gold

A fund that copies an index instead of picking stocks, usually by holding its members in the index's proportions (full replication). Tracking difference is how far the fund's return falls short of the index's, mostly because of fees.

ExampleVOO, five years to August 2026: 12.75% a year against the S&P 500's 12.79%, a gap of 0.04 points.

At the tablePlaying every seat at the table at once: you get the table's average result, minus the rake.

See also: ETF, S&P 500, Expense ratio

Expense ratio

Also: Fees · Management fee · Total expense ratio · Sponsor fee

A fund's yearly running costs as a percentage of its assets, taken out of the fund's value rather than billed. The management fee is the main part of it.

FormulaAnnual fund operating expenses ÷ average net assets × 100

ExampleVOO's 0.03% costs $3 a year per $10,000 invested.

At the tableThe rake: a small cut taken from every pot, win or lose.

See also: MER, Index fund, NAV

MER

Also: Management expense ratio · Trading expense ratio · TER

The Canadian measure of a fund's yearly cost: management fee plus operating expenses and taxes, as a percentage of assets. Trading costs are reported separately as the trading expense ratio (TER).

ExampleXEQT: MER 0.19%, of which the management fee is 0.17%.

See also: Expense ratio

Net assets (AUM)

Also: Assets under management · AUM · Fund size · Total net assets

The total value of everything a fund owns minus what it owes: the size of the fund.

On the tear sheetVOO reports $1.0 trillion for its ETF share class and $1.8 trillion for the whole fund, which also has mutual-fund share classes.

See also: NAV, ETF

NAV

Also: Net asset value · NAV per share · NAV per unit · Premium · Discount · Premium / discount

Net asset value per share: the fund's assets minus liabilities, divided by its shares, set once a day. An ETF's market price can sit slightly above NAV (a premium) or below it (a discount); large dealers trade to close the gap.

Formula(Fund assets − liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding

ExampleVOO, Sep 23, 2026: market price $707.60, NAV $707.38, a 0.03% premium.

See also: ETF, Net assets (AUM), Bid-ask spread

Holdings

Also: Top 10 holdings · Fund of funds · Look-through · Concentration · Weight

What a fund owns, and each position's weight as a share of the fund. A fund of funds holds other ETFs; looking through to their contents gives the underlying securities.

ExampleXEQT holds 5 ETFs that together own 8,301 securities.

See also: Index fund, ETF

Distribution yield

Also: Trailing 12-month yield · 12-month trailing yield · Trailing yield · Distributions

A fund's cash payouts as a percentage of its price. Issuers often annualize the latest payment; the trailing 12-month yield adds up the past year's payments instead. The two differ when payments vary.

FormulaTrailing: distributions over the last 12 months ÷ price × 100

ExampleVOO: $7.35 of dividends over a year ÷ $707.60 ≈ 1.04%.

See also: 30-day SEC yield, Dividend Yield

30-day SEC yield

Also: SEC yield · Standardized yield

A standardized yield the SEC requires when a US bond or stock fund quotes a yield: income earned over the last 30 days, minus fund expenses, expressed as a yearly rate. It lets funds be compared on equal terms.

See also: Distribution yield, Yield to maturity

Sharpe ratio

Also: Risk-adjusted return · Sharpe

The return earned above a risk-free rate, what short-term government bills pay, for each unit of volatility taken. Higher means more reward per unit of swing.

Formula(Annualized return − risk-free rate) ÷ annualized volatility

ExampleVOO, five years: 9.50% a year above the Treasury-bill rate ÷ 15.76% volatility of those excess returns ≈ 0.60.

On the tear sheetFund and crypto reports compute it from up to 60 monthly returns (ARTI since launch), with the 3-month US Treasury yield (or Canadian T-bill) as the risk-free rate.

At the tableWin rate per unit of variance: how much edge you get for each unit of swing you sit through.

See also: Volatility, Beta, Treasury bill

Bid-ask spread

Also: Spread · Bid · Ask

The gap between the highest price a buyer offers (bid) and the lowest a seller accepts (ask). It is a trading cost paid on every purchase and sale, on top of the expense ratio.

See also: NAV, Expense ratio

Currency-hedged

Also: Hedged · CAD-hedged · Currency hedging · Currency exposure

A fund that uses contracts to cancel most of the effect of exchange-rate moves on its foreign holdings, so its return tracks the assets in the investor's own currency. An unhedged fund passes currency gains and losses straight through.

On the tear sheetARTI's header reads 'CAD, hedged'. VOO notes that US-dollar moves pass straight through for investors who spend another currency.

See also: ETF, Organic growth and constant currency

Treasury bill

Also: T-bill · Bill · Discount rate · Investment rate · UST3M

A US government loan of a year or less with no coupon: it sells below face value and repays face value at maturity, and the difference is the interest. The investment rate states that gain as a yield comparable with notes and bonds.

ExampleSep 21, 2026 auction: $989.85 paid for $1,000 due in 91 days, a 4.113% investment rate.

See also: Treasury note and Treasury bond, Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, Treasury auction

Treasury note and Treasury bond

Also: T-note · T-bond · Treasuries · UST10Y · UST30Y · Government bond

Loans to the US government that pay fixed interest every six months and repay face value at maturity: notes run 2 to 10 years, bonds 20 or 30 years. The interest is taxed federally but not by states.

See also: Coupon, Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, Treasury auction, TIPS and real yield

Government of Canada bond

Also: GoC bond · Canada bond · GOC10Y · Real Return Bond · RRB

A loan to the Canadian federal government that pays fixed interest every six months. The Bank of Canada publishes benchmark yields. Real Return Bonds (RRBs) are Canada's inflation-linked bonds, like US TIPS.

See also: Treasury note and Treasury bond, TIPS and real yield, Policy rate (fed funds rate)

Coupon

Also: Coupon rate · Bond interest

The fixed yearly interest a bond pays, as a percentage of its face value, usually in two payments a year. It is set when the bond is first sold and doesn't change.

ExampleA 4.625% coupon on $1,000 pays $23.125 every six months.

See also: Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, Yield to maturity

Yield (bonds) and the yield curve

Also: Bond yield · Par yield · Yield curve · Rates · Nominal yield

The yearly return a bond pays at today's price. Price and yield move in opposite directions: when yields rise, existing bonds lose value. The yield curve plots one day's yields from short to long maturities.

On the tear sheetThe headline on a bond report is a yield, not a price: for US reports, Treasury's daily par yield (the coupon a new security needs to sell at face value); for Canada, the Bank of Canada benchmark yield.

See also: Yield to maturity, Duration and convexity, Coupon, 2s10s spread

2s10s spread

Also: Yield spread · Inverted yield curve · Curve steepness

The 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year. It is usually positive, since lenders want more to tie money up longer. A negative spread, an inverted curve, has often come before US recessions.

ExampleSep 23, 2026: 5.11% − 4.85% = 0.26 pp.

See also: Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, Policy rate (fed funds rate)

Yield to maturity

Also: YTM

The yearly return from buying a bond at today's price and holding it to maturity, counting every coupon and the repayment of face value, with coupons reinvested at the same rate. For a bond fund, the average across its holdings.

See also: Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, Coupon, 30-day SEC yield

Duration and convexity

Also: Modified duration · Macaulay duration · Convexity · Interest-rate risk · Average duration

Modified duration is roughly the percent a bond's price moves, the opposite way, for a 1 percentage point change in yield. Convexity refines it: prices rise a little more when yields fall than they drop when yields rise.

Formula% price change ≈ −duration × Δyield + ½ × convexity × Δyield²

Example10-year note, duration 7.77: yields up 1 pp, price about −7.4%; yields down 1 pp, about +8.1%.

See also: Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, Yield to maturity

TIPS and real yield

Also: Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities · Real yield · Inflation-linked bond · Index ratio · TIPS10Y · CPI-U

TIPS are Treasury securities whose principal rises and falls with consumer prices (the CPI-U index), so the coupon is paid on an inflation-adjusted amount. Their yield is a real yield: the return above inflation.

ExampleOn Sep 23, 2026 the 10-year TIPS real yield was 2.76%.

See also: Breakeven inflation, Treasury note and Treasury bond, Government of Canada bond

Breakeven inflation

Also: Breakeven · Inflation expectations · Breakeven rate

An ordinary Treasury yield minus the TIPS real yield of the same maturity: the average yearly inflation at which the two pay the same. Markets read it as expected inflation.

FormulaNominal yield − real (TIPS) yield

ExampleSep 23, 2026, ten years: 5.11% − 2.76% = 2.35%.

At the tableThe market's line on inflation: TIPS come out ahead if inflation runs over it, ordinary Treasuries if it runs under.

See also: TIPS and real yield, Yield (bonds) and the yield curve

Policy rate (fed funds rate)

Also: Federal funds rate · Fed funds · Bank of Canada rate · Overnight rate · Fed · Federal Reserve · Central bank

The short-term interest rate a central bank sets: the federal funds target range in the US (the Federal Reserve) and the overnight rate in Canada (the Bank of Canada). It anchors short-term yields and borrowing costs across the economy.

See also: Treasury bill, Yield (bonds) and the yield curve, 2s10s spread

Credit rating

Also: Bond rating · AAA · Aaa · Aa1 · Investment grade · Junk · High yield bonds

A rating agency's grade of a borrower's ability to repay, from AAA (Aaa at Moody's) downward. BBB− (Baa3 at Moody's) and above is investment grade; below that is high yield, or junk.

ExampleMoody's has rated the US Aa1, one notch below the top, since May 2025.

See also: Leverage and net debt, Treasury note and Treasury bond, Net Debt / EBITDA

Treasury auction

Also: Auction · High yield · Bid-to-cover · Reopening · On-the-run · CUSIP · ISIN

Treasury sells new debt at auction: every winning bidder pays one price, set by the highest accepted yield. Bid-to-cover is total bids ÷ amount sold. On-the-run means the latest issue of a maturity; its CUSIP is its ID code.

ExampleAug 12, 2026 10-year auction: high yield 4.683%, bid-to-cover 2.53.

See also: Treasury note and Treasury bond, Treasury bill, Coupon

Cryptocurrency

Also: Crypto · Crypto asset · Coin · Token · Native token · Digital asset

Digital money recorded on a blockchain, with no bank or government issuing it. A network's native token, such as BTC or ETH, pays its transaction fees and, on most networks, rewards the participants who keep the record.

On the tear sheetCrypto trades around the clock every day, so the reports quote a UTC daily close and cross-check it against two exchanges.

See also: Blockchain, Circulating supply, max supply and crypto market cap, Stablecoin, Spot crypto ETF

Blockchain

Also: Ledger · Block · Node · Distributed ledger

A shared record of transactions kept in copies by many independent computers (nodes). New transactions are grouped into blocks, each linked to the one before, so past entries can't be changed without redoing everything after them.

See also: Proof of work and halving, Proof of stake and staking, Cryptocurrency

Proof of work and halving

Also: Mining · Miner · Hash rate · Halving · Block subsidy · PoW

Bitcoin's way of adding blocks: miners race to solve a computing puzzle, and the winner earns new coins (the block subsidy) plus fees. Hash rate measures the total computing power. Every 210,000 blocks, about four years, the subsidy halves.

ExampleThe April 2024 halving cut the subsidy from 6.25 to 3.125 BTC per block.

See also: Proof of stake and staking, Blockchain, Circulating supply, max supply and crypto market cap

Proof of stake and staking

Also: Staking · Validator · Staked · PoS · Staking ETF

A way of adding blocks in which validators lock up (stake) the network's coins as a security deposit instead of mining. They earn new coins and fees, and can lose part of the stake for breaking the rules.

ExampleOn Sep 22, 2026, about 35% of all ETH was staked.

See also: Proof of work and halving, Blockchain, Cryptocurrency

Circulating supply, max supply and crypto market cap

Also: Circulating supply · Max supply · Total supply · Annual issuance · Burn · Network fees · Crypto market cap

Circulating supply is the coins issued so far; max supply is the protocol's hard limit, if any: 21 million for bitcoin, none for ETH. Annual issuance is new coins created in a year. Crypto market cap is price × circulating supply.

FormulaMarket cap = price × circulating supply

ExampleBitcoin, Sep 22, 2026: $86,172.28 × 20,088,131 BTC ≈ $1.731 trillion.

See also: Cryptocurrency, Proof of work and halving, Market cap

Stablecoin

Also: Dollar-pegged token · Pegged token

A crypto token built to hold a steady value, usually one US dollar, typically backed by cash and short-term Treasury bills held by its issuer. It is used to move dollars around crypto networks.

See also: Cryptocurrency, Treasury bill

Spot crypto ETF

Also: Spot bitcoin ETF · Crypto ETF · Bitcoin ETF

An exchange-traded fund that holds the cryptocurrency itself, kept by a custodian, so investors can own its price moves through an ordinary brokerage account. Spot means the actual coins, not futures (contracts to buy or sell later at a set price).

On the tear sheetEach crypto report lists the US and Canadian funds that hold the coin.

See also: ETF, Cryptocurrency, Expense ratio
