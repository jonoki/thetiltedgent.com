01 Company Overview

Agilent Technologies builds and services the instruments that analytical laboratories run on — liquid and gas chromatography, mass spectrometry, spectroscopy, genomics and pathology reagents — plus the consumables, software and field service that keep those instruments producing data. Its customers are pharmaceutical and biotech developers, contract research and manufacturing organisations, hospital and reference labs, universities and government agencies, chemical and semiconductor producers, and food and environmental testing labs. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $6.95B, up 6.7% from $6.51B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through the July 2026 quarter is $7.37B. At the October 30, 2026 close its market value was $49.30B (281.93M shares, per the cover of its Q3 FY2026 10-Q, × $174.87).

Fiscal calendar matters here. Agilent's fiscal year ends October 31, so "FY2026" runs November 2025 through October 2026 and the company has already reported three of its four quarters. Every growth figure on this page is fiscal unless stated otherwise.

The central debate is whether the recovery now visible in the numbers is a cyclical bounce off a destocking trough (the low point after customers ran down their stockpiles) or a structural step-up in earnings power. Agilent spent FY2023–FY2024 in a genuine revenue recession — two consecutive down years as pharma customers cut capital budgets and China demand collapsed. FY2025 turned positive, and fiscal Q3 2026 (quarter ended July 31, 2026) delivered 8.1% reported and 7.3% core growth with 320 basis points of non-GAAP operating margin expansion (3.2 percentage points; a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point). Management credits the margin gain to its Ignite cost and efficiency programme, and says strategic price increases added about 2 points to revenue growth in each of the first three quarters of the fiscal year. The bear reads the same data as a normal instrument-cycle upswing — and since September 10 the shares have risen another 22% with no new results, so the price now asks more of the next print: about 34× trailing GAAP earnings. Sections 04 and 07 take that apart.

The recurring-revenue business, and the single most important thing to understand about Agilent. CrossLab sells consumables (columns, vials, supplies), service contracts, compliance and qualification services, and multi-vendor lab enterprise services — increasingly on instruments Agilent did not build. It is now the largest of the three segments at 41.9% of revenue and carries a 34.3% operating margin, roughly eleven points above the instrument segment. That is the annuity: every chromatograph Agilent ships seeds a decade of column, service and software revenue at materially higher margin. It also explains why the FY2023–24 downturn was survivable — instrument orders can be deferred, but a lab that has already bought the box still has to run it. The trade-off is the ceiling: 5–6% core growth is what an installed-base annuity does. It will not inflect upward the way the pharma instrument line can.

Structural note: in Q3 FY2026 book-to-bill (orders divided by revenue) was above 1.0, the tenth consecutive quarter in which instrument orders matched or outgrew revenue — the leading indicator to watch for both segments.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Installed-base annuity Regulatory qualification lock-in Method-validation switching costs Global field-service density Multi-vendor service beachhead Consumables razor-and-blade 60+ years of HP-lineage IP

The moat is not the instrument — it is the validated method running on it. When a pharmaceutical company files a drug with a regulator, the analytical method used to prove purity and potency is filed with it, tied to a specific instrument platform, column chemistry and software configuration. Changing vendors means revalidating the method and, potentially, filing a change with the FDA or EMA. The cost of that is measured in months and regulatory risk, not in the price difference between two chromatographs. This is why CrossLab's 34.3% operating margin is defensible in a way that hardware margins are not, and why the installed base kept generating cash straight through the FY2023–24 instrument recession. The multi-vendor services push extends the same logic outward: once Agilent's engineers are maintaining a customer's entire lab — including competitors' boxes — Agilent sees the replacement cycle before anyone else does.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Customer capex is discretionary and synchronised. A chromatograph is a capital purchase. When pharma companies tighten budgets, instrument orders are among the first things deferred — and because Agilent, Thermo and Waters sell to the same customer base, the downturns arrive simultaneously across the whole sector. FY2023 (−0.2%) and FY2024 (−4.7%) were exactly that: two consecutive years of declining revenue at a company with a supposedly defensive end market. The annuity cushioned it; it did not prevent it.
02
China concentration and policy risk. China is a large enough revenue base that its swings move the consolidated number, and it is simultaneously exposed to domestic stimulus policy, indigenous-substitution procurement rules favouring local instrument makers, and US export controls. The +9% Q3 print beat a flat guide — which cuts both ways, since the guide itself tells you management does not have confident visibility there.
03
Academic and government funding is a political variable. Academia & government revenue fell 3% in Q3 FY2026 (it grew low single digits outside China) — the weakest line and the one most exposed to appropriations decisions in Washington and Brussels rather than to any business cycle. A material NIH or equivalent funding cut would hit this line directly and quickly, with no annuity offset on new-instrument placements.
04
Price increases added ~2 points of revenue growth for three straight quarters. CEO Padraig McDonnell said strategic pricing added about 200 basis points (2 percentage points) to revenue growth in each of Q1, Q2 and Q3 FY2026, twice the previous year's pace. Price is a real lever in a business whose customers find switching costly — but it is also finite and it eventually meets customer procurement resistance. If the price contribution fades and volume does not replace it, growth slows even with nothing else changed.

Notable One-Off Items

Three distortions matter when reading these numbers. First, the tariff benefit. Agilent's Q3 FY2026 non-GAAP EPS of $1.62 explicitly includes $0.06 of tariff benefit (refunds), and the raised FY2026 guidance of $6.18–$6.21 includes it as well. Strip it out and Q3 non-GAAP EPS is $1.56 — still up roughly 14% YoY, but the headline 18% overstates operating progress. Management separately quoted operating margin of 27.2% excluding tariff refunds, up 2.1 points, against the 28.3% headline up 3.2 points. Use the ex-tariff figures for trend analysis.

Second, the GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is wide and persistent. Q3 GAAP EPS was $1.28 against $1.62 non-GAAP — a 27% difference, and GAAP operating margin of 23.6% against 28.3% non-GAAP. The bridge is amortisation of acquired intangibles and restructuring; it is legitimate accounting but it is not free, and the trailing P/E of 34.49 on this page is calculated on GAAP EPS, not on the non-GAAP figure the company leads with. On the guided non-GAAP FY2026 EPS of about $6.20 the stock trades at 28.2×, not 34×.

Third, the segment restatement. Prior-period segment data under the old LSAG/DGG/ACG structure is not comparable to the current Life Sciences & Diagnostics / CrossLab / Applied Markets reporting. Several third-party data feeds still carry the old segment names with reconstructed figures — those should not be trusted for year-over-year segment comparison, and none are used on this page.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen so persistently that it is described as "overbought" — not that it must fall, only that the recent run has been unusually uninterrupted. Below 30 is "oversold," the mirror image. This is a 6-month RSI, so it reads the medium-term swing rather than day-to-day noise.

The five-year headline is still unflattering, though no longer negative. From the October 2021 close of $157.49 to the October 30, 2026 close of $174.87, the price is up 11.0% — before a dividend yielding well under 1%. Over the same dates the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) rose 64.6% and the Nasdaq-100 ETF (QQQ) 78.8% (price only, Yahoo closes). At the last edition the five-year figure was −9.1%; the whole difference is the autumn rally. Within the last twelve months alone the stock ran from a $108.35 low in May to a $182.99 high in late October (intraday), 69% apart. The 34× trailing multiple is not being paid for five years of compounding; it is being paid for the last three quarters and the next one.

The shape is a long, deep U. Agilent peaked at $159.65 in December 2021 on zero-rate life-sciences enthusiasm, fell through 2022 with other richly valued healthcare stocks as interest rates rose, bounced to $154.98 in November 2022, then ground down through all of 2023 to a cycle low of $103.37 in October 2023 — a 35% drawdown from the December 2021 high — as pharma capex froze and China went quiet. The 2024 recovery to $148.48 by September proved false; the stock gave it all back and made a second low of $107.60 in April 2025 in the tariff-driven selloff. From there the recovery has been genuine but jagged: $153.50 in November 2025, back down to $113.98 by March 2026, then a run to $153.56 in August 2026 on the Q3 beat. After a dip to $143.12 on September 10, September added 12.2% to $172.31 and October another 1.5% to $174.87 — the two highest month-end closes in the five years, above the December 2021 peak of $159.65.

Relative Performance

Over the twelve months to October 30, 2026 Agilent returned +22.0% on price, against +11.2% for the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) and +10.3% for the Nasdaq-100 ETF (QQQ). Over five years it returned +11.0%, against +64.6% and +78.8% (price only, Yahoo closes, same dates). Agilent has beaten both over one year and still trails both over five by a wide margin; the last twelve months took the stock past its 2021 high, but only just.

Against its own sector the picture is more defensible. The whole life-sciences-tools group — Thermo Fisher, Danaher, Waters, Revvity, Bruker — went through the same 2023–2025 slump as customers ran down their stockpiles, and their share prices fell together. The five-year underperformance is a sector story, not a company-specific failure — and so was the autumn rally. From the September 10 close to October 30 (Yahoo closes, our calculation) Agilent rose 22.2%, Thermo Fisher 15.6%, Danaher 15.8%, Waters 15.2%, Mettler-Toledo 30.8% and Revvity 29.8%, while SPY slipped 0.3% and the Health Care Select Sector SPDR fund (XLV) gained 11.6%. Agilent sat in the middle of its group, not at the top. What is company-specific is that Agilent was printing 7.3% core growth and 3+ percentage points of margin expansion at the same time, which is why so many brokers raised targets the morning after the August print.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The instrument recession is over and the recovery is broad: all three segments grew in Q3 FY2026, instrument orders have matched or outgrown revenue for ten straight quarters, China returned to +9% against a flat guide, and pharma reaccelerated to +12% with GLP-1 revenue up over 70%. Price increases have added about 2 points to revenue growth in each quarter this fiscal year, which a business whose customers find switching costly can sustain, and operating margin rose 2.1 points even excluding tariff refunds. CrossLab, now the largest segment at 41.9% of revenue and a 34.3% operating margin, converts every instrument placement into a decade of high-margin annuity. Analysts kept raising targets through October with no new results to prompt it, and the whole lab-tools group is moving up together, which suggests customers' spending is recovering across the industry rather than at one company. ROIC of 16.9% and debt-to-equity of 0.56 leave room for acquisitions.

Bear Case

The price ran 22% in seven weeks with no new results, so the September–October gain is a bet on the Q4 print and the FY2027 guide, not a reaction to them. Five years of ownership has still produced only an 11.0% price return, against 64.6% for SPY, and the stock has rallied to the mid-$150s twice before (Nov 2022, Nov 2025) only to give it back. The FY2026 guide itself embeds deceleration — Q4 core growth of 5.2–6.2% against 7.3% delivered — with the Advanced Therapeutics Division, home of the contract-manufacturing business, expected to see "flattish growth" in Q4. The price increases that added 2 points of revenue growth in each of three quarters cannot go on forever. The 34× trailing GAAP P/E and 3.0 PEG are full prices for 6–8% growth, and the GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is 27%. The price has caught the analysts: the average target is only 4.1% above it, the low target of $155 is below it, monthly RSI is at a five-year high of 80, and UBS kept its Hold even while raising its target to $180.

Top 3 Risks

01
Guided Q4 deceleration becomes a Q4 miss. Management has already guided core growth down from 7.3% to 5.2–6.2% for the quarter ending October 31, 2026, with the Advanced Therapeutics Division (contract manufacturing) expected to be flat against a tough comparison. After a 22% run into the print and with the average analyst target barely above the price, there is little cushion — a modest shortfall against a fully-believed guide would repeat the October 2024 pattern, when the stock lost 12.2% in a single month off a high.
02
China reverses. The +9% China print was the largest positive variance in Q3 against a roughly-flat guide, and it rests on double-digit growth in pharma and food (CFO, Q3 call). Chinese indigenous-substitution procurement policy explicitly favours domestic instrument manufacturers, and US export controls can restrict what Agilent may ship. A swing from +9% back to flat removes a meaningful slice of the group core-growth rate with no offset available.
03
Pricing runs out before volume takes over. Roughly two points of revenue growth per quarter has come from price, three quarters running, in a business where customers face high switching costs and therefore absorb increases — until procurement departments escalate. If the FY2027 guide still leans on price rather than volume, investors will treat the growth as temporary rather than lasting, and a 34× multiple has a long way to fall when that happens.
Industry Commentary
Pharma Reshoring — A Demand Pool That Did Not Exist Two Years Ago
EMERGING TAILWIND

Tariff policy and drug-security politics are pushing pharmaceutical manufacturing back toward the United States and Europe. For an analytical instrument maker this is close to ideal: every new drug-manufacturing facility requires a fully equipped quality-control laboratory, and that laboratory must be qualified before the plant can ship product. Agilent disclosed in Q3 FY2026 that it has received initial reshoring orders from five of the world's top ten pharmaceutical companies, ahead of its own expectations. The relevant detail is the timing: CEO Padraig McDonnell said the orders came "ahead of our expectations", and management sizes the opportunity at about $1 billion through 2030, of which it expects to win at least a third, with revenue building from fiscal 2027. The offsetting caution is that these are first orders and the CEO expects the revenue to be "not linear"; a lab build-out cycle can stretch or stall with construction timelines. Separately, tariffs cut the other way on Agilent's own cost base — the $0.06 per share of tariff benefit in Q3 was refunds, which are by nature non-recurring.

Public Research Funding — The Weakest Line, and the One No Company Controls
ONGOING HEADWIND

Academia and government revenue fell 3% in Q3 FY2026 (it grew low single digits outside China) — the weakest of Agilent's six end markets, and its smallest. This is the line driven entirely by appropriations rather than by any business cycle: NIH and equivalent European research budgets, university capital programmes, national laboratory funding. It is also the market where Agilent has the least commercial leverage, because a grant-funded lab cannot buy what its budget does not cover, whatever the price. Management called the quarter modestly below its expectations. The structural point: the more Agilent's growth concentrates in pharma and advanced materials, the more the academic line becomes ballast rather than engine — and a sharp funding cut would land immediately on new-instrument placements with no service-annuity offset for a year or more.

The AI Capex Boom Reaches the Analytical Lab — Bull and Bear Framing
EMERGING TAILWIND / VALUATION CONSTRAINT

Agilent's chemicals and advanced materials line grew 7% in Q3 FY2026, led by advanced materials, which grew in the high teens in China, where management tied it to the AI infrastructure build-out. Semiconductor fabrication, advanced packaging materials, thermal management compounds and battery chemistry for grid-scale energy storage all require the elemental and molecular characterisation that Agilent's GC, ICP-MS and spectroscopy instruments perform. The bull framing: this is a genuinely new, non-pharma growth vector inside a segment that was the group's worst performer two years ago, and it diversifies the company away from a single end-market cycle. Combined with GLP-1 (+70%) and reshoring, Agilent now has three independent structural demand pools layered on top of a recovering base cycle — which is what the wave of target raises after the August print was pricing. The bear framing: Applied Markets is only 18.4% of revenue, and its 7% growth added about 1.3 points to the group's (our calculation from the release). And the same AI capex cycle that lifts materials testing is absorbing the institutional capital that would otherwise flow to a 6%-growth healthcare instrument maker — which is a large part of why five years of solid execution produced only an 11.0% share-price gain while the S&P 500 ETF rose 64.6%. The AI boom is a modest revenue tailwind and a significant valuation headwind at the same time. Over five years the second effect has been the larger one; the September–October rally across the lab-tools group was the first stretch in a while where investors moved money the other way.
