01 Company Overview

Agilent Technologies builds and services the instruments that analytical laboratories run on — liquid and gas chromatography, mass spectrometry, spectroscopy, genomics and pathology reagents — plus the consumables, software and field service that keep those instruments producing data. Its customers are pharmaceutical and biotech developers, contract research and manufacturing organisations, hospital and reference labs, universities and government agencies, chemical and semiconductor producers, and food and environmental testing labs. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $6.95B, up 6.7% from $6.51B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through the July 2026 quarter is $7.37B. At a $40.35B market capitalisation the company ranks roughly #619 globally.

Fiscal calendar matters here. Agilent's fiscal year ends October 31, so "FY2026" runs November 2025 through October 2026 and the company has already reported three of its four quarters. Every growth figure on this page is fiscal unless stated otherwise.

The central debate is whether the recovery now visible in the numbers is a cyclical bounce off a destocking trough or a structural step-up in earnings power. Agilent spent FY2023–FY2024 in a genuine revenue recession — two consecutive down years as pharma customers cut capital budgets and China demand collapsed. FY2025 turned positive, and fiscal Q3 2026 (quarter ended July 31, 2026) delivered 8.1% reported and 7.3% core growth with 320 basis points of non-GAAP operating margin expansion. Management insists that margin is structural — roughly 200bp of it came from strategic pricing for the third straight quarter, not volume. The bear reads the same data as a normal instrument-cycle upswing being paid for at 28× trailing earnings. Sections 04 and 07 take that apart.

The recurring-revenue business, and the single most important thing to understand about Agilent. CrossLab sells consumables (columns, vials, supplies), service contracts, compliance and qualification services, and multi-vendor lab enterprise services — increasingly on instruments Agilent did not build. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Installed-base annuity Regulatory qualification lock-in Method-validation switching costs Global field-service density Multi-vendor service beachhead Consumables razor-and-blade 60+ years of HP-lineage IP

The moat is not the instrument — it is the validated method running on it. When a pharmaceutical company files a drug with a regulator, the analytical method used to prove purity and potency is filed with it, tied to a specific instrument platform, column chemistry and software configuration. Changing vendors means revalidating the method and, potentially, filing a change with the FDA or EMA. The cost of that is measured in months and regulatory risk, not in the price difference between two chromatographs. This is why CrossLab's 34.3% operating margin is defensible in a way that hardware margins are not, and why the installed base kept generating cash straight through the FY2023–24 instrument recession. The multi-vendor services push extends the same logic outward: once Agilent's engineers are maintaining a customer's entire lab — including competitors' boxes — Agilent sees the replacement cycle before anyone else does.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Customer capex is discretionary and synchronised. A chromatograph is a capital purchase. When pharma companies tighten budgets, instrument orders are among the first things deferred — and because Agilent, Thermo and Waters sell to the same customer base, the downturns arrive simultaneously across the whole sector. FY2023 (−0.2%) and FY2024 (−4.7%) were exactly that: two consecutive years of declining revenue at a company with a supposedly defensive end market. The annuity cushioned it; it did not prevent it.
02
China concentration and policy risk. China is a large enough revenue base that its swings move the consolidated number, and it is simultaneously exposed to domestic stimulus policy, indigenous-substitution procurement rules favouring local instrument makers, and US export controls. The +9% Q3 print beat a flat guide — which cuts both ways, since the guide itself tells you management does not have confident visibility there.
03
Academic and government funding is a political variable. Academia & government grew mid-single digits in Q3 FY2026 — respectable, but it is the slowest line and the one most exposed to appropriations decisions in Washington and Brussels rather than to any business cycle. A material NIH or equivalent funding cut would hit this line directly and quickly, with no annuity offset on new-instrument placements.
04
Pricing contributed ~200bp of margin for three straight quarters. Management is explicit that strategic pricing, not volume leverage, drove roughly two full points of margin expansion in each of the last three quarters. Price is a real lever in a lock-in business — but it is also finite and it eventually meets customer procurement resistance. If pricing contribution fades and volume does not replace it, the margin story stalls even with revenue intact.

Notable One-Off Items

Three distortions matter when reading these numbers. First, the tariff benefit. Agilent's Q3 FY2026 non-GAAP EPS of $1.62 explicitly includes $0.06 of tariff benefit (refunds), and the raised FY2026 guidance of $6.18–$6.21 includes it as well. Strip it out and Q3 non-GAAP EPS is $1.56 — still up roughly 14% YoY, but the headline 18% overstates operating progress. Management separately quoted operating margin of 27.2% excluding tariff refunds, up 210bp, against the 28.3% headline up 320bp. Use the ex-tariff figures for trend analysis.

Second, the GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is wide and persistent. Q3 GAAP EPS was $1.28 against $1.62 non-GAAP — a 27% difference, and GAAP operating margin of 23.6% against 28.3% non-GAAP. The bridge is amortisation of acquired intangibles and restructuring; it is legitimate accounting but it is not free, and the TTM P/E of 28.22 on this page is calculated on GAAP EPS, not on the non-GAAP figure the company leads with. On the guided non-GAAP FY2026 EPS of about $6.20 the stock trades near 23×, not 28×.

Third, the segment restatement. Prior-period segment data under the old LSAG/DGG/ACG structure is not comparable to the current Life Sciences & Diagnostics / CrossLab / Applied Markets reporting. Several third-party data feeds still carry the old segment names with reconstructed figures — those should not be trusted for year-over-year segment comparison, and none are used on this page.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen so persistently that it is described as "overbought" — not that it must fall, only that the recent run has been unusually uninterrupted. Below 30 is "oversold," the mirror image. This is a 6-month RSI, so it reads the medium-term swing rather than day-to-day noise.

The five-year headline is uncomfortable: Agilent shareholders are down. From the October 2021 close of $157.49 to the September 10, 2026 close of $143.12, the price is −9.1% — before a dividend yielding well under 1%. Over a period in which the broad US market rose substantially, this stock went nowhere and took a 51% peak-to-trough round trip within the last twelve months alone to do it. That is the context every bullish datapoint on this page has to be read against. The 28× trailing multiple is not being paid for five years of compounding; it is being paid for the last three quarters.

The shape is a long, deep U. Agilent peaked at $159.65 in December 2021 on zero-rate life-sciences enthusiasm, fell through 2022 with every other long-duration healthcare name, bounced to $154.98 in November 2022, then ground down through all of 2023 to a cycle low of $103.37 in October 2023 — a 35% drawdown from the December 2021 high — as pharma capex froze and China went quiet. The 2024 recovery to $148.48 by September proved false; the stock gave it all back and made a second low of $107.60 in April 2025 in the tariff-driven selloff. From there the recovery has been genuine but jagged: $153.50 in November 2025, back down to $113.98 by March 2026, then a five-month run to $153.56 in August 2026 on the Q3 beat. The September close of $143.12 is a 6.8% pullback from that August high.

What the moving averages say. The 3-month average ($145.02) sits above the 10-month ($132.42) and the price ($143.12) is above the 10-month but has just crossed below the 3-month. That is a still-intact intermediate uptrend showing its first hesitation since April. The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 70.7 in August 2026 — the third-highest reading in five years and squarely at the overbought threshold — and has cooled to 57.9. For comparison, RSI bottomed at 18.0 in April 2022 and 22.2 in October 2023, both of which marked genuine buying opportunities in hindsight. Today's reading is neither a warning nor an invitation: it is the middle of the range after a run.

Relative Performance

Over the last twelve months Agilent returned +17.8% on price. Over five years it returned −9.1%. Precise S&P 500 and NASDAQ-100 total returns for the matching windows were not verified from any page fetched for this report and are therefore not quoted — but the qualitative statement is safe and matters: the S&P 500, Agilent's benchmark index as an NYSE listing, rose substantially over both windows, and the NASDAQ-100 rose more still. Agilent has underperformed both indices over five years by a wide margin, and the last twelve months represent recovery toward an old high rather than new ground.

Against its own sector the picture is more defensible. The entire life-sciences-tools complex — Thermo Fisher, Danaher, Waters, Revvity, Bruker — went through the same 2023–2025 destocking recession, and Agilent's annuity-heavy mix made its earnings decline shallower than several peers even as the share price tracked them down. The five-year underperformance is a sector story, not a company-specific failure. What is company-specific is that Agilent is now the one in that group printing accelerating core growth and 300+ basis points of margin expansion at the same time, which is why nine brokers moved targets on a single morning in August.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The instrument recession is over and the recovery is broad: all three segments grew in Q3 FY2026, book-to-bill has exceeded 1.0 for ten straight quarters, China returned to +9% against a flat guide, and pharma reaccelerated to +12% with GLP-1 revenue up over 70%. Crucially, the margin expansion is not just volume — roughly 200bp per quarter has come from strategic pricing, which a method-validation lock-in business can actually sustain. CrossLab, now the largest segment at 41.9% of revenue and a 34.3% operating margin, converts every instrument placement into a decade of high-margin annuity. On guided FY2026 non-GAAP EPS of ~$6.20 the stock trades near 23×, with ROIC of 16.9%, D/E of 0.56 and 23 analysts averaging a $175 target — the lowest of which still sits 8.5% above spot.

Bear Case

Five years of ownership has produced a −9.1% price return, and the stock has been here twice before (Nov 2022, Nov 2025) only to give it back. The FY2026 guide itself embeds deceleration — Q4 core growth of 5.2–6.2% against 7.3% delivered — with CDMO explicitly turning "flattish." The pricing lever that drove 200bp of margin for three consecutive quarters is finite. The 28× trailing GAAP P/E and 2.04 PEG are full prices for 6–8% growth, and the GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is 27%. Consensus is dangerously tight: 23 analysts, no sells, every target above spot, and nine raises on one morning — sentiment that leaves no cushion for a Q4 miss. And UBS, the newest eye on the name, initiated at Neutral on September 9.

Top 3 Risks

01
Guided Q4 deceleration becomes a Q4 miss. Management has already guided core growth down from 7.3% to 5.2–6.2% for the quarter ending October 31, 2026, with the CDMO line flat on tough comparisons. With every analyst target above spot and no short interest to speak of, there is no positioning cushion — a modest shortfall against a fully-believed guide would repeat the October 2024 pattern, when the stock lost 12.2% in a single month off a similar high.
02
China reverses. The +9% China print was the largest positive variance in Q3 against a roughly-flat guide, and it rests on stimulus-driven laboratory replacement plus food-testing demand. Chinese indigenous-substitution procurement policy explicitly favours domestic instrument manufacturers, and US export controls can restrict what Agilent may ship. A swing from +9% back to flat removes a meaningful slice of the group core-growth rate with no offset available.
03
Pricing runs out before volume takes over. Roughly two points of margin expansion per quarter has come from price, three quarters running, in a business where customers face high switching costs and therefore absorb increases — until procurement departments escalate. If the FY2027 margin bridge still leans on price rather than volume and mix, the market will re-rate the margin story from structural to temporary, and a 28× multiple has a long way to fall when that happens.
Industry Commentary
Pharma Reshoring — A Demand Pool That Did Not Exist Two Years Ago
EMERGING TAILWIND

Tariff policy and drug-security politics are pushing pharmaceutical manufacturing back toward the United States and Europe. For an analytical instrument maker this is close to ideal: every new drug-manufacturing facility requires a fully equipped quality-control laboratory, and that laboratory must be qualified before the plant can ship product. Agilent disclosed in Q3 FY2026 that it has received initial reshoring orders from five of the world's top ten pharmaceutical companies, ahead of its own internal schedule. The relevant detail is the phrase "ahead of schedule" — management had modelled this as an FY2027 story and it is arriving in FY2026. The offsetting caution is that these are first orders, not a quantified pipeline; the company has not put a dollar figure on the opportunity, and a lab build-out cycle can stretch or stall with construction timelines. Separately, tariffs cut the other way on Agilent's own cost base — the $0.06 per share of tariff benefit in Q3 was refunds, which are by nature non-recurring.

Public Research Funding — The Slowest Line, and the One No Company Controls
ONGOING HEADWIND

Academia and government grew mid-single digits in Q3 FY2026 — the slowest of Agilent's five disclosed end markets, and growing at roughly half the rate of pharma. This is the line driven entirely by appropriations rather than by any business cycle: NIH and equivalent European research budgets, university capital programmes, national laboratory funding. It is also the market where Agilent has the least commercial leverage, because a grant-funded lab cannot price-discriminate its way to a purchase it has no budget for. Mid-single-digit growth here is genuinely better than the sector feared eighteen months ago, when the risk case was outright contraction. But the structural point stands: the more Agilent's growth concentrates in pharma and advanced materials, the more the academic line becomes ballast rather than engine — and a sharp funding cut would land immediately on new-instrument placements with no service-annuity offset for a year or more.

The AI Capex Boom Reaches the Analytical Lab — Bull and Bear Framing
EMERGING TAILWIND / VALUATION CONSTRAINT

Agilent's advanced materials line grew in the high teens in Q3 FY2026, explicitly attributed by management to AI infrastructure: semiconductor fabrication, advanced packaging materials, thermal management compounds and battery chemistry for grid-scale energy storage all require the elemental and molecular characterisation that Agilent's GC, ICP-MS and spectroscopy instruments perform. The bull framing: this is a genuinely new, non-pharma growth vector inside a segment that was the group's worst performer two years ago, and it diversifies the company away from a single end-market cycle. Combined with GLP-1 (+70%) and reshoring, Agilent now has three independent structural demand pools layered on top of a recovering base cycle — which is what the nine simultaneous target raises in August were pricing. The bear framing: Applied Markets is only 18.4% of revenue, so even high-teens growth adds roughly a point and a half to the group. And the same AI capex cycle that lifts materials testing is absorbing the institutional capital that would otherwise flow to a 6%-growth healthcare instrument maker — which is a large part of why five years of solid execution has produced a −9.1% share price. The AI boom is a modest revenue tailwind and a significant valuation headwind at the same time, and the second effect has so far been the larger one.
