Section 01

Company Overview

Apple designs, manufactures, and sells premium consumer hardware — iPhone, Mac, iPad, Apple Watch and AirPods — and monetizes the resulting installed base of 2.5 billion-plus active devices through a high-margin Services franchise spanning the App Store, iCloud, Apple Music, advertising, payments and search-default licensing. Fiscal 2025 (ended September 27, 2025) revenue was $416.2B, up +6.4% YoY; the most recent TTM figure through the June 2026 quarter has accelerated sharply to $466.8B (+14.2%). Apple is the world's second-largest company by market value behind NVIDIA, the largest consumer-electronics company on earth, and captured roughly 49% of all global smartphone revenue in Q2 2026 despite shipping fewer units than Samsung.

Notable Executives & Owners

Tim Cook → John Ternus

Tim Cook, CEO since 2011, becomes Executive Chairman on September 1, 2026. John Ternus — at Apple since 2001 and SVP of Hardware Engineering since 2021, the executive behind modern iPhone, Apple silicon Macs, iPad and AirPods hardware — takes over as CEO and joins the board.

Took the CFO seat from Luca Maestri in 2025 after two decades at Apple in financial planning, sales finance and investor relations. Has kept Apple's capital-return machine intact — quarterly dividend now $0.27/share alongside one of the largest buyback programs in corporate history.

Section 03

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat
Brand Power & Pricing Authority Ecosystem Switching Costs Vertical Silicon Integration Distribution Scale (2.5B devices) Two-Sided App Platform Network Effects Supply-Chain Buying Power Privacy/Trust Positioning

Apple's moat is not any single product — it is the compounding cost of leaving. A customer who owns an iPhone, a Watch, AirPods, iCloud storage, an Apple One bundle and a library of App Store purchases faces a switching cost measured in inconvenience, lost data and broken features (iMessage, Handoff, Find My, Health), none of which appear on a price tag. That lock-in gives Apple pricing authority no rival has: blended iPhone ASPs near $946 against roughly $270 for Samsung, and about 49% of all global smartphone revenue on roughly 21% of units. Layered on top is vertical integration — Apple designs its own SoCs (A-series, M-series), modems and increasingly its own AI silicon, capturing margin that PC and Android OEMs hand to merchant chip vendors. Finally, a 2.5-billion-device installed base is the world's most valuable distribution channel, which is precisely why Google pays an estimated $20–26B a year simply to be the default search engine in Safari. That payment is close to pure profit and, notably, is the single most legally exposed line item in the entire model.

Key Vulnerabilities
AI dependency and a ceded platform layer

Apple lost the first round of generative AI. Its answer — a Siri rebuilt on Google's Gemini, unveiled at WWDC26 — solves the product problem but creates a strategic one: Apple now rents the most important new interface layer from its largest search-payment counterparty and a direct hardware competitor. Rollout is already blocked or delayed in the EU and China. If AI assistants become the primary way people access services, Apple's control of the customer relationship weakens exactly where it has historically been strongest.

Regulatory assault on the Services profit pool

Two independent threats converge on the highest-margin revenue Apple has. First, the US search-monopoly case against Google — under appeal as of May 2026 — puts the roughly $20–26B annual default-search payment at risk; losing it would hit operating income almost dollar-for-dollar. Second, the EU's Digital Markets Act: Apple lost its challenge to the App Store rules in July 2026 and is separately appealing a €500M (~$587M) fine, with sideloading and alternative payment rails now mandatory in Europe and similar rules spreading.

Input-cost inflation Apple cannot engineer around

Management called the current memory market "a 100-year flood," with DRAM contract prices up more than 75% YoY and exponential increases still ahead. Apple guided September-quarter gross margin down to 47–48% from 50.1%, and has taken the unusual step of raising iPad and Mac prices. Scale gives Apple better terms than anyone else, but it does not exempt it — and price increases in a soft consumer environment risk trading margin protection for volume.

Concentration: one product, one country, one supplier

iPhone is still roughly half of revenue and the origin of most Services attach. Greater China remains both a major market and — through Foxconn and the broader assembly base — the manufacturing spine, exposing Apple to tariffs, export controls and geopolitical retaliation. At the component level, leading-edge silicon runs almost entirely through TSMC. Diversification into India and Vietnam is real but partial, and the June-quarter supply constraints demonstrated how little slack exists in the system.

Notable One-Off Items

  • Tariff refunds (Q3 FY26, favorable). Roughly 2 percentage points of the 50.1% June-quarter gross margin came from tariff refunds — about $2.2B of gross profit that will not recur at the same magnitude. Guidance assumes only ~1 point of carry-in benefit next quarter. Adjusting for it, "clean" Q3 gross margin was closer to 48%.
  • EU Digital Markets Act fine (unfavorable, contested). A €500M (~$587M) penalty over App Store anti-steering rules is under appeal; Apple separately lost its broader challenge to the DMA App Store regime in July 2026. Immaterial to cash flow, potentially material to the European Services business model.
  • FY2024 European State-aid tax charge (historical). The one-time ~$10.2B charge is why FY2024 net income fell 0.8% on rising revenue, and why FY2025's +22.7% net-income growth and the current +32% TTM figure look artificially strong off that depressed base. Normalize before extrapolating.
  • Memory cost inflation is recurring, not one-off. Worth stating explicitly because it is easy to treat as a transitory shock: DRAM contract prices are up more than 75% YoY and Apple expects increases through at least the September quarter. Model it as a sustained margin headwind, not a blip.

What RSI means: a momentum gauge from 0 to 100 comparing the size of recent gains to recent losses. Above 70 suggests the stock has risen far and fast and may be "overbought"; below 30 suggests the opposite. It is a measure of momentum, not of value — an expensive stock can stay overbought for a long time. Where AAPL sits: monthly RSI peaked above 72 (overbought) in June 2026 at the top of the rally and has cooled to roughly 63 after the earnings selloff — elevated but no longer stretched, and well above the sub-35 readings seen at the December 2022 and April 2025 lows.

That headline hides two completely different regimes: a flat-to-down three years from late 2021 through mid-2025, during which Apple badly lagged the AI trade, followed by a violent 13-month re-rating from roughly $200 to a July 2026 high of $344.57 as the market decided Apple would be an AI beneficiary rather than an AI victim. Section 08

Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Apple is entering its strongest product cycle in a decade — iPhone 18 plus a first foldable, a Gemini-powered Siri that finally makes the ecosystem AI-native, and a Mac line taking share in a shrinking PC market. Demand already exceeds supply across iPhone, Mac and iPad, which is the best problem a hardware company can have. With 2.5B active devices, 1.5B paid subscriptions, ~$137B of free cash flow and net cash, Apple can absorb memory inflation, out-buy every competitor for capacity, and keep shrinking the share count while the Services flywheel compounds at low-double digits.

Bear Case

You are paying 33x forward earnings and a 3.1 PEG for a company that just guided revenue and gross margin below consensus, whose highest-margin segment is decelerating, and whose most profitable single line item — an estimated $20–26B a year from Google — is sitting in front of an appeals court. Memory costs are a structural, not transitory, margin headwind; the AI layer is rented from a competitor; the EU has already broken the App Store model in Europe; and the company is changing CEOs for the first time in 15 years. Very little of that is priced at $303.

Top 3 Risks
  1. Loss of the Google default-search payment. An estimated $20–26B of near-100%-margin annual revenue is directly exposed to the remedy phase of the US search-monopoly case, now on appeal. A cap or ban would cut operating income by a high-single-digit percentage overnight with no offsetting cost lever.
  2. Sustained memory-cost inflation compressing gross margin. DRAM contract prices up 75%+ YoY have already forced guidance down to 47–48% from 50.1% and pushed Apple to raise iPad and Mac prices. If the shortage persists into 2027 as some suppliers warn, either margins or unit volumes give — and Apple has told investors it expects exponential increases to continue near term.
  3. Multiple compression on a decelerating Services line. Apple's 2026 re-rating rests on Services growing low-teens and AI driving an upgrade super-cycle. Services grew 12% and missed; management guided to roughly 9.5% next quarter. If Services settles into single digits, the case for a 33x multiple on a hardware-led business weakens substantially — and consensus price targets were already below the share price a week before earnings.

Industry Commentary

The memory supercycle is squeezing every device maker

Critical Constraint

AI datacenter demand has drained the memory supply chain, and consumer electronics is paying for it. Q4 2025 DRAM contract prices rose more than 75% YoY; memory is 10–15% of a smartphone's bill of materials, and total smartphone BOM costs are running 5–7% higher in 2026. TrendForce cut 2026 smartphone production to −2% (from +0.1%) and notebooks to −2.4% (from +1.7%); Gartner expects the same. Notebook retail prices are up 5–15%. Apple's own CFO commentary called it "a 100-year flood," and teardown estimates put iPhone 18 Pro memory content near $145 versus roughly $39 a generation ago. SK Hynix has warned the shortage may not clear before 2030. Scale insulates Apple relatively — it is squeezing the sub-$400 phone market by an estimated 22% — but it does not exempt it.

Platform regulation is rewriting app-store economics

Ongoing Headwind

The EU's Digital Markets Act has moved from threat to fact. Apple lost its challenge to the App Store rules in July 2026 and is separately appealing a €500M (~$587M) anti-steering fine; sideloading and alternative payment rails are now mandatory in Europe. In the US, the Google search case — under appeal since May 2026 — puts Apple's estimated $20–26B annual default-search payment in play. Both attack the same thing: Apple's ability to tax and gate access to its 2.5-billion-device installed base. App Store revenue is roughly 70% of global app-store consumer spend ($117B of $166B in 2025) on a fraction of the units, so any structural change to commission rates or defaults falls disproportionately on Apple's most profitable dollars.

Premiumization and the AI device cycle — where bulls and bears actually disagree

Emerging Tailwind

The market is polarizing in Apple's favor: it now takes roughly 49% of global smartphone revenue on ~21% of units, with ASPs at $946 versus Samsung's ~$270, while Xiaomi's shipments fell 26% and Samsung's mobile division slipped into a loss. Mac is the only PC line growing (+15.9%) as the industry shrinks. The bull framing: memory inflation and AI hardware requirements are a barrier to entry that kills the low end and consolidates value toward whoever controls silicon and price — which is Apple, and a foldable plus a Gemini-class Siri gives it two fresh reasons to upgrade. The bear framing: premiumization is Apple raising prices into a shrinking unit market, the AI layer is licensed from Alphabet rather than owned, and Services — the part of the business that actually deserves a 33x multiple — is decelerating toward single digits. Both readings fit the same Q3 print, which is why the stock is a 47.8% Strong Buy / 30.4% Hold split.
