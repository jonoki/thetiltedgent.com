01 Company Overview

Airbnb runs a two-sided marketplace for short-term accommodation, and increasingly for experiences and travel services. It owns no property. Hosts list space, guests book it, and Airbnb collects a fee from both sides — which is why the company carries a gross margin of 83.12% on 8,200 employees, numbers that look like software rather than travel.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $12.24B, up 10.26% year over year, on $91.3B of Gross Booking Value (GBV — the total value of bookings made on the platform, before Airbnb's cut) and 533.0M Nights and Seats Booked, up 8%. Revenue for the twelve months to September 2026 has accelerated to $13.93B, +16.7%, and Q3 2026 alone grew 19% to $4.87B, which CEO Brian Chesky called on the call "our fastest revenue growth in more than three years." The company ranks #105 in the S&P 500 and #44 in the NASDAQ-100 by market capitalisation at the 9 November 2026 close.

The single most useful thing to understand about Airbnb is the identity that drives it: revenue ≈ Nights × ADR × take rate. ADR (average daily rate — the average price a guest pays per night) and nights are the two levers of GBV; the take rate (Airbnb's fee revenue expressed as a percentage of GBV) converts GBV into revenue. Across FY2025 that take rate was 13.41% ($12.24B revenue on $91.3B GBV), and for Q4 2026 the company expects it to be only slightly higher than a year earlier. Almost all of Airbnb's growth therefore comes from the two GBV levers. In Q3 2026 volume did most of the work: Nights and Seats Booked grew 16% against ADR up 4%. As Section 02 argues, the two levers are not equal in quality.

These two are not the same quality of growth. Nights growth is real unit demand — more people choosing the platform, more transactions, more hosts drawn in by more guests. ADR growth is a weaker signal: it partly reflects inflation in the underlying accommodation market, partly foreign-exchange translation, and partly mix — more whole homes versus rooms, more North America and EMEA versus lower-priced Asia-Pacific inventory. Airbnb itself splits it by region: in Q3 2026 Latin America ADR was +8% reported but +4% ex-currency, and Asia-Pacific ADR rose just 1%. Of a 21-point GBV gain, volume supplied about sixteen points and price four to five. That is a better split than Q2 2026 (nights +10%, ADR +5%) and much better than 2025, when full-year nights grew only 8% while GBV grew 12% — price was doing a third of the work.

One caution the company's own reporting introduces: the headline volume metric was formerly "Nights and Experiences Booked" and is now "Nights and Seats Booked" — defined in the Q3 letter as nights booked for stays plus seats booked for services and experiences. A seat at a two-hour walking tour and a night in a villa are both one unit. The letter does not split the 16% between the two; it describes "strong double-digit growth in nights stayed." Until the split is published, treat 16% as an upper bound on accommodation-night growth.

The moat is the classic marketplace flywheel, and Airbnb's version is unusually durable because the supply side is fragmented individuals rather than corporations. Over 9 million active listings cannot be replicated by capital alone — a competitor cannot buy 9 million homeowners. Guests go where the inventory is; hosts list where the demand is; reviews accumulate on the platform and are not portable. The brand does real work: Airbnb's traffic skews heavily to direct and unpaid channels, so it relies less on buying search ads than a typical online travel agency. Guests also pay Airbnb's fees before they travel, which shows up as $1.54B of unearned fees on the 30 September 2026 balance sheet. Financially the moat shows up as an operating margin of 22.49% and a ROIC of 17.25% on almost no physical asset base.

Regulatory removal of supply. This is the first-order vulnerability and it is structural, not cyclical. Every city that restricts short-term rentals removes listings permanently — the supply does not come back when travel demand recovers. New York City is the proof case: after Local Law 18 enforcement began on 5 September 2023, registered short-term rental listings fell from over 38,000 in early 2023 to just over 3,000 by mid-2025, with more than 4,300 applications denied outright. Airbnb listings in the city dropped from roughly 22,000 in August 2023 to about 2,300 by early 2024, an ~90% reduction. Barcelona is running the same play more slowly: all 10,101 tourist licences are set to lapse by November 2028. Budapest's 6th district banned short-term rentals outright from 1 January 2026; Greece's prime minister announced in early September 2026 that the freeze on new registrations in central Athens and central Thessaloniki will run through 31 December 2027; Houston began enforcing its registration rule, with a $275 yearly fee, from 2 April 2026. Each of these is small individually; the pattern is not.

Supply growth is the binding constraint on nights growth. Nights cannot grow faster than listings for long — occupancy per listing has a ceiling. Airbnb ended 2025 with "over 9 million active listings", a rounded figure that cannot be compared precisely with prior years, so the market cannot cleanly measure supply growth. What the Q3 2026 letter does say is that active listings grew at a high-single-digit rate while Nights and Seats Booked grew 16%. Part of that gap is seats for experiences and services, which sit inside the same number; the rest has to come from each listing being booked more often, which cannot go on for ever.

Price competition from larger online travel agencies. Booking Holdings generates $28.13B of TTM revenue — about twice Airbnb's $13.93B — and has added alternative-accommodation inventory to a platform that already owns hotel supply. Expedia ($16.23B TTM revenue, +12.9%) is growing, though more slowly than Airbnb's +16.7%. Airbnb's counter-move is to add hotels to its own marketplace, which works but pushes it onto ground where the incumbents have better rate agreements.

Concentration in a single discretionary spending category. Airbnb has one revenue engine. Travel is the most cyclically exposed line in household budgets, and there is no counter-cyclical business to offset it. A demand shock does not just slow nights — it hits ADR and mix simultaneously, so GBV falls faster than volume. Fees that guests prepay for future trips would also shrink, pulling cash flow down faster than profit (see Section 04).

Notable One-Off Items

The single largest distortion in Airbnb's reported history is FY2023 net income of $4,792M on $9,917M of revenue — a 48.32% net margin and $7.24 of diluted EPS. That was not operating performance; operating income that year was only $1,518M. The gap came from a one-off tax valuation-allowance release. Any long-run EPS chart or five-year earnings CAGR that includes FY2023 is broken. The cleaner series is operating income: $542M (FY2021) → $1,893M (FY2022) → $1,518M (FY2023) → $2,553M (FY2024) → $2,544M (FY2025).

Beyond that, FY2025 shows no comparably large non-recurring item. Note that FY2025 net income of $2,511M was down 5.2% from FY2024's $2,648M, yet diluted EPS fell only from $4.11 to $4.04 (−1.7%) — the buyback absorbed roughly two-thirds of the decline. That is a real effect on the per-share numbers and it should be understood as capital allocation, not earnings growth.

Q3 2026 carries a tax effect worth knowing. Diluted EPS rose 43% ($2.21 → $3.16) and net income 38% ($1,374M → $1,890M), but operating income rose 24% ($1,625M → $2,021M). The difference is mostly tax: the provision fell from $418M to $283M, and the 10-Q ties the higher year-earlier charge to a $213M valuation allowance against the Corporate Alternative Minimum Tax (a US minimum tax on large companies' book profits) recognised in the prior year. On our arithmetic the Q3 2026 tax rate was about 13% of pre-tax income ($283M ÷ $2,173M).

The Free Cash Flow Profile — Why It Is Not What It Looks Like

Airbnb's reported free cash flow is large relative to its net income, and most write-ups treat that as a quality signal. Most of the gap is stock-based pay, and understanding it changes how you value the company.

Start with what the gap is not. A guest pays Airbnb at booking and the host is paid around check-in, so Airbnb holds a large balance of other people's money: funds payable and amounts payable to customers, $6.96B on 31 December 2025 and $7.42B on 30 September 2026, after a seasonal peak of $12.22B on 30 June 2026. That money is matched by an equal asset on the balance sheet ("amounts held on behalf of customers"), and Airbnb's cash flow statement books its changes under financing activities, not operating cash flow. It does not inflate free cash flow, and it sits outside the $12.59B of cash and short-term investments.

What does lift operating cash flow above profit, per the 10-Q for the nine months to 30 September 2026: stock-based compensation of $1,359M (an expense paid in shares, so no cash leaves), $312M of deferred taxes, and swings in unearned fees — Airbnb's own fees, collected before the stay and recognised as revenue at check-in. Unearned fees fell from $1,743M at end-2025 to $1,536M at 30 September 2026, after peaking at $2,831M in June, which is why quarterly free cash flow is a poor profitability read: Q2 2026 FCF was $1.3B (35% margin), Q3 2026 $1.43B (29%), Q4 2025 $521M (19%). Same business; the fees for summer trips arrive in spring.

So FY2025 FCF of $4,646M was 1.85x net income of $2,511M, and TTM FCF of $4.90B is 1.53x TTM net income of $3.21B. Two consequences. First, a price-to-FCF multiple (22.8x trailing, or 20.8x on enterprise value of $101.73B) flatters the stock, because stock-based pay is a real cost: Airbnb spends buyback money (repurchases of $2.85B in the first nine months of 2026) partly to soak up the shares it issues to staff. Second, the prepaid fees grow only while bookings grow; in a flat year their contribution goes to roughly zero and in a falling year it turns negative, so FCF would drop faster than earnings in a travel downturn.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. On a monthly series each reading covers half a year of trading, so the signals are slow and few.

Five years, almost nowhere — then two good quarters. ABNB closed November 2021 at $172.54 and was $190.29 at the 9 November 2026 close. That is a +10.3% price return over the chart's five years, with no dividends — roughly 2.0% a year — against +72.5% for the NASDAQ-100 (QQQ) and +65.2% for the S&P 500 (SPY) over the same dates (price returns, Yahoo closes). For most of that period this was a stock that went sideways in a violent range while revenue grew from $8.4B (FY2022) to $13.93B (TTM). The valuation investors were willing to pay fell, until two strong quarters in 2026 pushed the price back up.

The path matters more than the endpoints. The 2022 rate shock took the stock from $171.76 (March 2022) to $85.50 (December 2022), a 50% drawdown on month-end closes, as rising rates cut the value of every company whose profits lie mostly in the future; June 2022 (−26.3%) and May 2022 (−21.1%) were the two worst months on the chart. The 2023 travel recovery drove it back to $152.19 by July 2023. Then came three years of grinding lower: an August 2024 drop from $139.56 to $117.31 (−16%) on a slower growth outlook, and a slow bleed through 2025 to $116.99 in November 2025 — only 37% above the 2022 low, three years later. Then Q2 2026 landed on 6 August and the stock gained 17.4% the next session, closing August at $183.22. It gave back ground to $160.65 in September and $164.39 in October, and Q3 results on 5 November added 11.67% in one session.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What the multiple is actually asking for

Be precise about this. At $190.29 the market cap is $111.80B (Class A and B shares at 15 Oct 2026 × the 9 Nov close) and enterprise value — market cap plus debt minus cash — $101.73B. Against TTM free cash flow of $4.90B that is 22.8x price/FCF and 20.8x EV/FCF; against TTM earnings of $5.34 it is 35.6x; against the $6.28 FY2027 consensus EPS, 30.3x. StockAnalysis puts enterprise value at 32.3 times EBITDA (operating profit before depreciation and amortisation) and 7.3 times sales.

Now run the identity backwards. Revenue ≈ nights × ADR × take rate. The take rate has held near 13.4% a year and the company expects only a slight rise in Q4. ADR grew 4% reported, 5% ex-FX, in Q3, and over time ADR should drift toward accommodation-price inflation of perhaps 2–3% once mix and currency stop helping. That leaves nights as the whole story. To justify roughly 20x EV/FCF when investors want an 8–9% yearly return (the cost of capital), you need mid-to-high single-digit FCF growth for decades, which — with the full-year adjusted EBITDA margin guided at about 36% and limited room to expand — means revenue must compound at around 8–10% indefinitely. Net of a normalised 2–3% ADR, the price implicitly assumes roughly 6–8% nights growth every year, for a very long time. This is our arithmetic, not a company target.

Q3 2026 delivered 16% — well above that bar, though the 16% includes experience and service seats, and Q4 is guided to at least low double digits. FY2025 nights grew 8%. So for now Airbnb is doing more than the price asks, and the debate has moved from "can it grow?" to "how long can volume outrun listings, which grew at a high-single-digit rate in Q3?" The bull case is not that Airbnb is cheap at 36x trailing earnings; it is that 6–8% long-run nights growth from a 9-million-listing marketplace is achievable. The bear case is that it is achievable only if regulation stops, and regulation is not stopping.

Bull Case

An 83.12% gross margin, 22.49% operating margin and 17.25% ROIC on a business that owns no property, with about $10.1B of net cash. Growth is accelerating — revenue +19% in Q3 2026 against +10.26% for FY2025 — and it is volume-led, with Nights and Seats up 16% versus ADR up 5% ex-FX. FY2026 guidance was raised twice in two quarters, now to high-teens growth at about a 36% adjusted EBITDA margin. The diluted share count fell 3.9% in a year, and $2.7B of old authorisation plus a new $12B programme keep shrinking it. Nine million listings is a supply base no competitor can buy.

Bear Case

You are paying 35.6x trailing earnings and 22.8x free cash flow that is 1.53x net income mainly because stock-based pay is left out of it. The stock is up 57% in twelve months, much of it in two results sessions, and the $202.80 average target is only 6.6% above the price. The five-year price return is +10.3% while revenue grew from $8.4B (FY2022) to $13.93B (TTM) — investors spent most of those years paying less for each dollar of profit, for a reason. Regulation removes supply permanently and on a published schedule (Barcelona's 10,101 licences by 2028; NYC already down ~90%). Experiences and Services have no disclosed revenue at all, and the headline volume metric blends seats into nights, so 16% volume growth may overstate accommodation demand.

01
Regulatory supply destruction is permanent and scheduled. New York removed roughly 90% of Airbnb listings after Local Law 18 enforcement began in September 2023 (38,000+ registered listings in early 2023 to just over 3,000 by mid-2025). Barcelona's 10,101 licences all lapse by November 2028. Each city that acts is a market Airbnb cannot re-enter, and the losses compound across the calendar rather than mean-reverting.
02
Free cash flow overstates what shareholders keep. TTM free cash flow of $4.90B is 1.53x net income, mainly because $1,359M of stock-based pay in the first nine months of 2026 is a cost settled in shares, not cash; the buyback then spends cash to offset those shares. Prepaid guest fees add to cash flow while bookings grow and drain it when they shrink, so FCF would fall faster than earnings in a travel downturn, just when investors would also be paying less for each dollar of it.
03
The growth story depends on nights, and the nights metric is blended. "Nights and Experiences Booked" is now "Nights and Seats Booked", with seats for experiences and services counted alongside nights. Accommodation-night growth cannot be isolated from published data, listings grew at only a high-single-digit rate in Q3, and Q4 guidance steps down to at least low double digits. At 36x earnings, a volume print that turns out to be mostly seats is a materially different company from the one the price assumes.
  Industry Commentary
Municipal short-term rental regulation
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is not a footnote risk; it is the defining structural feature of the industry. The mechanism is one-way: a city passes a registration or primary-residence requirement, non-compliant listings are delisted by the platform itself, and the supply never returns because the underlying units are converted back to long-term rentals. New York is the extreme case — enforcement from 5 Sep 2023, registered listings from 38,000+ to just over 3,000 by mid-2025, more than 4,300 applications denied, and Airbnb listings down roughly 90%. The 2026–2028 pipeline is already public: Budapest's 6th district ban (1 Jan 2026), Houston registration enforcement (from 2 April 2026, $275/yr), Greece's announced freeze on new registrations in central Athens and central Thessaloniki through 31 Dec 2027, and Barcelona's phase-out of all 10,101 licences by November 2028. Note that these are disproportionately high-ADR European city-centre markets — the units being removed are worth more per night than the average listing, so the GBV impact exceeds the listing-count impact.
Supply growth as the ceiling on volume growth
ONGOING HEADWIND
Nights booked cannot outrun listings indefinitely — occupancy per listing has a physical ceiling, and pushing occupancy higher eventually degrades the guest experience that the brand rests on. Airbnb ended 2025 with "over 9 million active listings", and that rounding is itself informative: the company is not giving the market a number precise enough to compute supply growth against. FY2025 nights and seats grew 8%, Q2 2026 10% and Q3 2026 16%, against active listings up at a high-single-digit rate in Q3. The Q3 regional picture shows where the volume is coming from: Latin America and Asia-Pacific each over 20%, EMEA mid-teens, North America low double digits (its best in four years). The fastest-growing volume is in the lower-priced regions — Asia-Pacific ADR grew just 1% — so nights growth and revenue growth can drift apart. In Q3 they did not: North America ADR rose 6% and EMEA 7% ex-FX, and revenue grew faster than volume.
The bull/bear frame: a marketplace repriced on two quarters
EMERGING TAILWIND — CONTESTED
Bulls see the August and November 2026 prints as the moment the market recognised what the model is: accelerating volume-led growth (Nights and Seats +16%, ADR +5% ex-FX in Q3), a full-year outlook raised twice to about a 36% EBITDA margin, 17.25% ROIC, no property risk, about $10.1B of net cash, and a buyback that cut the diluted share count 3.9% in a year with $14.7B of authorisation still available. On that view the five years of sideways action were the price coming down from the 2021 post-IPO froth, and the business has now grown into it. Bears observe that the stock closed at $190.29, within 7% of the $202.80 average analyst target, that the 22.8x FCF multiple leaves out $1.36B of nine-month stock-based pay, that the volume metric blends in experience and service seats, and that Experiences and Services still have no published revenue after years of promotion. The reconciliation is arithmetic, not narrative: at these prices the stock needs roughly 6–8% nights growth for a very long time, and every city on the 2026–2028 regulatory calendar makes that number harder to hit.
