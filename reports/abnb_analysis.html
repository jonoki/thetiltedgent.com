01 Company Overview

Airbnb runs a two-sided marketplace for short-term accommodation, and increasingly for experiences and travel services. It owns no property. Hosts list space, guests book it, and Airbnb collects a fee from both sides — which is why the company carries an gross margin of 82.90% on 8,200 employees, numbers that look like software rather than travel.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $12.24B, up 10.26% year over year, on $91.3B of Gross Booking Value (GBV — the total value of bookings made on the platform, before Airbnb's cut) and 533.0M Nights and Seats Booked, up 8%. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through Q2 2026 has accelerated to $13.16B, +13.6%. The company ranks roughly #212 globally by market capitalisation and sits inside both the NASDAQ-100 and the S&P 500.

The single most useful thing to understand about Airbnb is the identity that drives it: revenue ≈ Nights × ADR × take rate. ADR (average daily rate — the average price a guest pays per night) and nights are the two levers of GBV; the take rate (Airbnb's fee revenue expressed as a percentage of GBV) converts GBV into revenue. In Q2 2026 that take rate was roughly 13.2% ($3.6B revenue on $27.2B GBV), essentially unchanged from the prior year. Almost all of Airbnb's growth therefore comes from the two GBV levers — and, as Section 02 argues, they are not equal in quality.

These two are not the same quality of growth. Nights growth is real unit demand — more people choosing the platform, more transactions, more network density, and it compounds because each incremental booking pulls in supply. ADR growth is a weaker signal: it partly reflects inflation in the underlying accommodation market, partly foreign-exchange translation, and partly mix — more whole homes versus rooms, more North America and EMEA versus lower-priced Asia-Pacific inventory. Airbnb itself splits it: Q2 2026 ADR was +5% reported but +4% ex-FX, and in Latin America +9% reported collapses to +2% ex-currency. Strip out currency and price contributes roughly four points of a sixteen-point GBV number; volume contributes about ten. That is a healthy split, and better than 2025's, when full-year nights grew only 8% while GBV grew 12% — i.e. price was doing a third of the work.

One caution the company's own reporting introduces: the headline volume metric was formerly "Nights and Experiences Booked" and is now "Nights and Seats Booked" — experience seats are counted alongside accommodation nights. A seat at a two-hour walking tour and a night in a villa are both one unit. With Experiences supply up nearly 80% year over year, the blended metric is now flattered by a category with a fraction of the economics. Anyone modelling nights growth should treat the 10% as an upper bound on true accommodation-night growth.

NEGATIVE WORKING CAPITAL

The moat is the classic marketplace flywheel, and Airbnb's version is unusually durable because the supply side is fragmented individuals rather than corporations. Over 9 million active listings cannot be replicated by capital alone — a competitor cannot buy 9 million homeowners. Guests go where the inventory is; hosts list where the demand is; reviews accumulate on the platform and are not portable. The brand does real work: Airbnb's traffic skews heavily to direct and unpaid channels, which is the structural reason its gross margin (82.90%) sits far above the economics of a paid-search-dependent online travel agency. And because Airbnb collects from guests up front but pays hosts later, it runs on negative working capital — the marketplace is funded by its own users (see Section 04). Financially the moat shows up as an operating margin of 20.81% and a ROIC of 16.81% on almost no physical asset base.

Regulatory removal of supply. This is the first-order vulnerability and it is structural, not cyclical. Every city that restricts short-term rentals removes listings permanently — the supply does not come back when travel demand recovers. New York City is the proof case: after Local Law 18 enforcement began on 5 September 2023, registered short-term rental listings fell from over 38,000 in early 2023 to just over 3,000 by mid-2025, with more than 4,300 applications denied outright. Airbnb listings in the city dropped from roughly 22,000 in August 2023 to about 2,300 by early 2024, an ~90% reduction, and hotel rates in the city rose about 6% in the following year. Barcelona is running the same play more slowly: all 10,101 tourist licences are set to lapse by November 2028. Budapest's 6th district banned short-term rentals outright from 1 January 2026; Athens has frozen new registrations in three central districts through 2026; Houston began enforcing a $275/year registration requirement on 1 April 2026. Each of these is small individually; the pattern is not.

Supply growth is the binding constraint on nights growth. Nights cannot grow faster than listings for long — occupancy per listing has a ceiling. Airbnb ended 2025 with "over 9 million active listings", a rounded figure that is itself a mild disclosure problem: it cannot be differenced precisely against prior years, so the market cannot cleanly measure supply growth. What is measurable is that FY2025 nights grew 8% and Q2 2026 nights-and-seats grew 10% — and that the seats component (Experiences supply +80%) is now inside that number. If accommodation supply growth is running below high single digits, the 10% headline is borrowing from a lower-value category.

Price competition from better-capitalised OTAs. Booking Holdings generates $28.24B of TTM revenue — more than twice Airbnb's $13.16B — and has aggressively added alternative-accommodation inventory to a platform that already owns hotel supply. Expedia ($15.70B TTM revenue, +12.0%) is growing at a similar clip to Airbnb ex-FX. Airbnb's counter-move is to add hotels to its own marketplace, which works but pushes it onto ground where the incumbents have better rate agreements.

Concentration in a single discretionary spending category. Airbnb has one revenue engine. Travel is the most cyclically exposed line in household budgets, and there is no counter-cyclical business to offset it. A demand shock does not just slow nights — it hits ADR and mix simultaneously, so GBV falls faster than volume, and the working-capital float that flatters free cash flow reverses at exactly the same moment (see Section 04).

Notable One-Off Items

The single largest distortion in Airbnb's reported history is FY2023 net income of $4,792M on $9,917M of revenue — a 48.32% net margin and $7.24 of diluted EPS. That was not operating performance; operating income that year was only $1,518M. The gap came from a one-off tax valuation-allowance release. Any long-run EPS chart or five-year earnings CAGR that includes FY2023 is broken. The cleaner series is operating income: $542M (FY2021) → $1,893M (FY2022) → $1,518M (FY2023) → $2,553M (FY2024) → $2,544M (FY2025).

Beyond that, FY2025 shows no comparably large non-recurring item. Note that FY2025 net income of $2,511M was down 5.2% from FY2024's $2,648M, yet diluted EPS fell only from $4.11 to $4.04 (−1.7%) — the buyback absorbed roughly two-thirds of the decline. That is a real effect on the per-share numbers and it should be understood as capital allocation, not earnings growth.

The Free Cash Flow Profile — Why It Is Not What It Looks Like

Airbnb's reported free cash flow is enormous relative to its net income, and most write-ups treat that as a quality signal. It is mostly a timing artefact, and understanding it changes how you value the company.

The mechanism: a guest pays Airbnb in full at the moment of booking. The host is not paid until roughly check-in. Between those two events — often months, since summer trips are booked in spring — Airbnb holds the guest's money. That balance appears on the balance sheet as funds payable and amounts payable to customers (cash Airbnb is holding that legally belongs to hosts and guests). It is a liability, not equity, and Airbnb earns interest on it while it sits there.

The numbers make the point better than the theory. That balance stood at $6.959B on 31 December 2025 and $12.224B on 30 June 2026 — a $5.27B build in six months, driven entirely by the spring booking wave for summer travel. Unearned revenue rose from $1,743M to $2,831M over the same period. Total current liabilities went from $13.65B to $18.09B. None of that is profit; all of it flows through operating cash flow.

Which is why FY2025 FCF of $4,646M was 1.85x net income of $2,511M, and TTM FCF of $4.86B is 1.81x TTM net income of $2.69B. And why quarterly FCF is almost useless as a profitability read: Q2 2026 FCF was $1.3B, a 35% margin. Q4 2025 FCF was $521M, a 19% margin. Same company, same margins, same business — the difference is that Q2 is when money comes in for trips that have not happened yet, and Q4 is when it goes out.

Three consequences. First, valuing Airbnb on a price-to-FCF multiple (22.2x trailing, or 20.3x on enterprise value of $98.45B) systematically understates the multiple, because a chunk of that FCF is borrowed from hosts and must eventually be paid. Second, the float grows only while bookings grow — in a flat year the working-capital contribution goes to roughly zero, and in a declining year it goes negative, so FCF falls much faster than earnings. Third, a large part of the $12.07B cash balance is not Airbnb's to spend; the company's genuinely discretionary cash is materially smaller than the headline suggests, which matters when assessing how much buyback capacity actually exists.

Five years, almost nowhere — then eight weeks of everything. ABNB closed September 2021 at $167.75 and August 2026 at $183.22. For most of that period this was a stock that went sideways in a violent range while the underlying business roughly doubled its revenue — the entire move was multiple compression, then a single sharp re-rating in August 2026.

The path matters more than the endpoints. 08 Summary & Key Risks

What the multiple is actually asking for

Be precise about this. At $183.22 the market cap is $108.02B and enterprise value $98.45B. Against TTM free cash flow of $4.86B that is 22.2x price/FCF and 20.3x EV/FCF; against TTM earnings of $4.42 it is 41.45x; against the $5.28 FY2026 consensus EPS, 34.7x. EV/EBITDA is 35.66x and EV/Sales 7.48x.

Now run the identity backwards. Revenue ≈ nights × ADR × take rate. The take rate is flat at ~13.2–13.4% and management has given no indication of raising it. ADR grew 5% reported but 4% ex-FX, and structurally ADR should converge toward accommodation-price inflation of perhaps 2–3% once mix and currency stop helping. That leaves nights as the whole story. To justify roughly 20x EV/FCF at an 8–9% cost of capital, you need mid-to-high single-digit FCF growth in perpetuity, which — with margins already at 35% adjusted EBITDA and limited room to expand — means revenue must compound at around 8–10% indefinitely. Net of a normalised 2–3% ADR, the multiple is implicitly underwriting roughly 6–8% nights growth every year, forever.

Q2 2026 delivered 10% — but that 10% includes Experiences seats, and Q3 is guided only to "low double-digit". FY2025 accommodation nights grew 8%. So the market is asking for something close to, but a little below, what Airbnb is currently doing — with no allowance for the regulatory attrition described above, and while paying 41x trailing earnings. The bull case is not that Airbnb is cheap; it is that 6–8% perpetual nights growth from a 9-million-listing marketplace is achievable. The bear case is that it is achievable only if regulation stops, and regulation is not stopping.

Bull Case

An 82.90% gross margin, 20.81% operating margin and 16.81% ROIC on a business that owns no property, funded by $12.22B of other people's money. Growth is accelerating — revenue +17% in Q2 2026 against +10.26% for FY2025 — and it is volume-led, with nights up 10% versus ADR up 4% ex-FX. FY2026 guidance was raised to at least mid-teens growth at a 35.5%+ adjusted EBITDA margin. A 7.5% share-count reduction since FY2023 with $3.4B of authorisation still to run compounds every per-share figure. Nine million listings is a supply base no competitor can buy.

Bear Case

You are paying 41.45x trailing earnings and 22.2x an FCF figure that is 1.81x net income mainly because of a working-capital float that reverses the moment bookings stop growing. The stock trades above the $176.76 average analyst target after a 40% twelve-month move that came almost entirely from one quarter. Five-year total return is +9.2% while revenue nearly doubled — the market has spent five years lowering this multiple for a reason. Regulation removes supply permanently and on a published schedule (Barcelona's 10,101 licences by 2028; NYC already down ~90%). 01
Regulatory supply destruction is permanent and scheduled. New York removed roughly 90% of Airbnb listings after Local Law 18 enforcement began in September 2023 (38,000+ registered listings in early 2023 to just over 3,000 by mid-2025). Barcelona's 10,101 licences all lapse by November 2028. Each city that acts is a market Airbnb cannot re-enter, and the losses compound across the calendar rather than mean-reverting.
02
The free cash flow that supports the valuation is partly float, and float reverses. Funds payable to hosts went from $6.959B to $12.224B in six months — a $5.27B swing that flows through operating cash flow without being profit. In a flat booking year that contribution is zero; in a declining year it is negative. FCF would fall far faster than earnings in a travel downturn, at precisely the moment the multiple is also compressing.
03
The growth story depends on nights, and the nights metric has been diluted. "Nights and Experiences Booked" is now "Nights and Seats Booked", with Experiences supply up ~80% year over year feeding into the same headline. Accommodation-night growth cannot be isolated from published data, and Q3 guidance already steps down to "low double-digit". At 41x earnings, a nights print that turns out to be 6% accommodation and 4% seats is a materially different company from the one the multiple assumes.
  Industry Commentary
Municipal short-term rental regulation
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is not a footnote risk; it is the defining structural feature of the industry. The mechanism is one-way: a city passes a registration or primary-residence requirement, non-compliant listings are delisted by the platform itself, and the supply never returns because the underlying units are converted back to long-term rentals. New York is the extreme case — enforcement from 5 Sep 2023, registered listings from 38,000+ to just over 3,000 by mid-2025, more than 4,300 applications denied, Airbnb listings down roughly 90%, and city hotel rates up about 6% in the following year (which is the political argument for the law, and also the evidence that Airbnb was suppressing hotel pricing). The 2026–2028 pipeline is already public: Budapest's 6th district ban (1 Jan 2026), Houston registration enforcement (1 Apr 2026, $275/yr), Athens' registration freeze in three central districts through 2026, and Barcelona's phase-out of all 10,101 licences by November 2028. Note that these are disproportionately high-ADR European city-centre markets — the units being removed are worth more per night than the average listing, so the GBV impact exceeds the listing-count impact.
Supply growth as the ceiling on volume growth
ONGOING HEADWIND
Nights booked cannot outrun listings indefinitely — occupancy per listing has a physical ceiling, and pushing occupancy higher eventually degrades the guest experience that the brand rests on. Airbnb ended 2025 with "over 9 million active listings", and that rounding is itself informative: the company is not giving the market a number precise enough to compute supply growth against. What can be computed is that FY2025 nights and seats grew 8% and Q2 2026 grew 10%, with Q3 guided to low double-digit. The regional picture shows where incremental supply is actually coming from: India +60%, Brazil origin nights +30%+, Latin America ~20%, Asia-Pacific high-teens — versus high-single-digit growth in North America and EMEA. That is the mix problem in one line: the fastest-growing volume is in the lowest-ADR regions (Asia-Pacific ADR grew just 1%), so nights growth and revenue growth will increasingly diverge.
The bull/bear frame: a marketplace re-rating on one quarter
EMERGING TAILWIND — CONTESTED
Bulls see August 2026 as the moment the market finally recognised what the model is: accelerating volume-led growth (nights +10%, ADR +4% ex-FX), a raised full-year outlook at 35.5%+ EBITDA margin, 16.81% ROIC, no property risk, a self-funding negative-working-capital structure, and a buyback retiring ~3.9% of shares a year. On that view the five years of sideways action were the multiple normalising from a 2021 IPO bubble, and the business has now grown into and past it. Bears observe that the stock closed at $183.22 against an average analyst target of $176.76 — it has run past the street — that the 22.2x FCF multiple flatters itself with $12.22B of host float, that the volume metric now blends in Experiences seats, and that Experiences and Services still have no published revenue after years of promotion. The reconciliation is arithmetic, not narrative: at these prices the multiple requires roughly 6–8% nights growth in perpetuity, and every city on the 2026–2028 regulatory calendar makes that number harder to hit.
