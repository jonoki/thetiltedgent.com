01 Company Overview

Arch Capital Group Ltd. is a Bermuda-domiciled specialty underwriter that writes insurance, reinsurance (insurance sold to other insurance companies, so that they can pass along part of the risk they have taken on) and mortgage insurance (cover that pays a mortgage lender when a borrower defaults and the foreclosure sale does not repay the loan) across the US, Canada, Bermuda, the UK, Europe and Australia. It is one of a small group of Bermuda platforms that operates all three of those businesses at scale, and it is run explicitly as a cycle-management machine: management shrinks premium when prices are inadequate and expands when they are not.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $19.93B, up 14.27% year over year, with net income of $4.36B and diluted EPS of $11.60. On a TTM basis through June 2026, revenue is $19.23B (+0.40%) and EPS is $12.83 — revenue growth has flattened as the company deliberately pulls back from softening property lines. CompaniesMarketCap ranks Arch the 702nd most valuable listed company in the world.

Specialty lines means non-standard, hard-to-price risks — professional liability, excess casualty, construction, marine, programs, travel, entertainment — written outside the commoditised standard market. The combined ratio is the single number that tells you whether underwriting made money: losses plus expenses divided by premiums earned, so below 100% is an underwriting profit and above 100% is a loss. Arch's Insurance segment ran 98.5% in Q2 2026, up 5.1 points from 93.4% a year earlier, and underwriting income collapsed 79% to $27M. Two things did the damage: catastrophe losses added 7.6 points to the loss ratio versus 2.9 points a year earlier, and the expense ratio rose to 35.5% from 33.6% partly on transitional costs from the 2024 acquisition of the US MidCorp and Entertainment insurance businesses. Stripping out catastrophes and prior-year reserve movement, the underlying combined ratio was 91.6% — still a genuine underwriting profit. Net premium fell 5.1%, but management said the underlying decline was only 1.8% excluding deliberate non-renewals.

Read this one twice. Mortgage insurance is 6.7% of Arch's net premium and one third of its underwriting profit, because it runs a combined ratio of 22.8% — meaning roughly 77 cents of every premium dollar drops through as underwriting income. Nothing else in property and casualty insurance looks like that. The mechanism: Arch collects a small monthly premium on high-loan-to-value mortgages and pays claims only when a borrower defaults and the home sells for less than the loan. In an environment of rising or stable house prices and low unemployment, borrowers who fall behind sell into equity rather than default, so incurred losses are minimal and old reserves are released. That is exactly what happened here: the delinquency rate was flat at 2.1%, and excluding prior-year reserve development the combined ratio was 39.8%, not 22.8% — reserve releases supplied roughly 17 points of the reported result. That gap is the tell. Reported mortgage profitability today is partly an accounting harvest of conservatism booked in earlier years, and it is finite.

What a housing downturn would do: this segment is levered to US employment and home-price direction, not to hurricanes. If unemployment rises and house prices stall, three things happen roughly in sequence — new delinquencies rise off the 2.1% base, the reserve-release tailwind reverses into strengthening, and the combined ratio moves from the twenties toward and potentially through the ex-development 39.8% level and beyond. A segment producing $220M a quarter with almost no volatility is worth a high multiple; a segment whose loss ratio is a levered call on the US labour market is not. Growth in the quarter came from a new Australian client and from Arch retaining more risk by buying less quota-share reinsurance — the latter increases earnings and increases downside exposure.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
CYCLE DISCIPLINE THREE UNCORRELATED SEGMENTS MORTGAGE PROFIT POOL BERMUDA TAX & CAPITAL BASE RESERVE CONSERVATISM UNDERWRITER TALENT DEPTH $23.2B EQUITY SCALE
Arch's moat is behavioural and structural rather than technological. Structurally, three segments whose loss drivers are close to uncorrelated — specialty casualty (social inflation and legal environment), reinsurance (catastrophes and cedent behaviour), mortgage (employment and house prices) — let the group keep earning while any one of them is repriced. In Q2 2026 that is visible in the raw numbers: Insurance underwriting income fell 79% and the group still produced $657M of segment underwriting profit and a consolidated combined ratio of 83.5% (82.5% excluding catastrophes and prior-year development). Behaviourally, the moat is the willingness to shrink. Net premium written fell 6.9% group-wide and 10.4% in reinsurance in the quarter because Arch declined property business it thought was under-priced, while gross reinsurance premium was flat — the company is still quoting, just retaining less. That combination, plus 19.94% trailing ROE and a 15.3% annualised operating ROE, is what a compounding underwriter is supposed to look like.

1. The soft cycle is arriving, and it arrives first where Arch earns most. Property rates were down mid-teens percent at the mid-2026 renewals and commercial rates have now fallen for eight consecutive quarters. Reinsurance is 62% of underwriting income, and its underlying ex-catastrophe combined ratio already deteriorated 270bp to 79.9%. Discipline protects the balance sheet, not the earnings line: shrinking premium in a soft market mechanically shrinks profit.

2. Mortgage earnings quality. Roughly 17 points of the 22.8% reported mortgage combined ratio came from releasing prior-year reserves. That is real cash, but it is not a run-rate. Any reader treating $220M a quarter as durable is capitalising a reserve harvest at an underwriting multiple.

3. Catastrophe volatility and accumulation. Current accident-year catastrophe losses were $201M pre-tax in Q2 2026, up from $154M, driven by the Iran conflict and US severe convective storms. War and geopolitical loss is a category that reinsurers historically model poorly, and Arch is buying more retrocession specifically to cut concentration — which costs margin.

4. Integration and expense drag in Insurance. The expense ratio rose to 35.5% from 33.6% on transitional costs from the 2024 MidCorp and Entertainment acquisition. A 98.5% combined ratio in the primary book leaves almost no cushion if casualty severity trends worsen.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It says nothing about value — only about the recent balance of buying and selling pressure.

The five-year trajectory. Over the 60 months plotted, ACGL went from a month-end close of $38.18 in September 2021 to $98.84 — a total return of +158.9%, a compound annual rate of +21.0% before dividends. The calendar-year steps inside that run, measured December close to December close on the same series, were +41.2% in 2022, +18.3% in 2023, +24.3% in 2024 and +3.9% in 2025, with +3.0% year-to-date in 2026. Three years of high-teens-to-low-forties compounding, then a stop. That shape is the whole argument of this report in one line: the hard property-catastrophe market after 2022 did the heavy lifting, and the market stopped paying for it about twenty months ago.

The inflection points. The first twelve months of the window were flat — $38.18 in September 2021 to $45.54 in September 2022, +19.3% and mostly range-bound between $44 and $48 through 2022. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Arch is doing precisely what a disciplined underwriter should do at this point in the cycle: gross reinsurance premium flat, net premium down 10.4%, more retrocession bought, $1.95B of stock repurchased in the first half at roughly 1.5× book. Consolidated combined ratio of 83.5% and a 15.3% annualised operating ROE in a quarter that included $201M of catastrophe and war losses and a 98.5% primary insurance ratio shows how much the three-segment structure absorbs. At 1.45× book with book value compounding ~2.8% a quarter and no dividend to fund, buybacks below intrinsic value do the compounding for you.

Bear Case

The earnings are better than they look and worse than they read. Roughly 17 points of the mortgage segment's 22.8% combined ratio came from prior-year reserve releases, and mortgage supplies a third of underwriting profit; reinsurance supplies another 62% and its underlying ex-catastrophe combined ratio just deteriorated 270bp to 79.9% with property rates down mid-teens. Group net premium written is falling 6.9%, revenue growth has gone to +0.4%, and consensus 2026 EPS of $9.37 is well below the $12.83 trailing figure — the forward P/E of 10.55 versus a trailing 7.70 is the market already pricing the down-cycle. KBW cut its target to $99 — essentially the market price of $98.84, i.e. no upside at all from the most bearish house on the name.

01
Soft-market earnings compression. Property rates fell mid-teens at mid-2026 renewals and commercial rates have declined eight consecutive quarters. Reinsurance is 62% of underwriting income; if January 2027 renewals bring another double-digit decline, the segment's ~79.9% underlying combined ratio deteriorates further and Arch shrinks premium into it by design — protecting the balance sheet but compressing earnings.
02
Mortgage reserve-release exhaustion, or a housing/employment downturn. The mortgage segment's reported profitability depends heavily on releasing reserves booked in earlier, more conservative years, and its 2.1% delinquency rate reflects a benign credit environment. Rising unemployment or flat-to-falling house prices would reverse releases into strengthening and move the combined ratio sharply higher — and Arch has increased its exposure by retaining more risk through lower quota-share cessions.
03
Catastrophe and geopolitical loss accumulation. Q2 2026 catastrophe losses of $201M (up 31% year over year) included losses from the Iran conflict alongside US severe convective storms. War and political-violence exposure is poorly modelled across the industry, and a major Atlantic hurricane in the Aug–Oct peak window could overwhelm a single quarter's underwriting income.
  Industry Commentary
Is the property-catastrophe market still hard? Not any more.
ONGOING HEADWIND
The evidence from Arch's own disclosures says the hard market that began after 2022 has turned. Property rates were down mid-teens percent at the mid-year 2026 renewals; management describes the "early stages" of softening; global commercial insurance rates have now fallen for eight consecutive quarters. The tell is not the headline reinsurance combined ratio of 77.5%, which improved a point — it is the underlying accident-year ratio excluding catastrophes and prior-year development, which deteriorated 270 basis points to 79.9% on mix shift and lower property pricing. Arch's response is textbook: gross premium flat (+0.2%), net premium down 10.4%, more retrocession purchased. That answers the central question of this report — current premium behaviour reflects discipline, not chasing. The cost of that discipline is that earnings fall with the cycle; the benefit is that reserves do not blow up three years later. Casualty is the offset: rate increases are still positive though decelerating, and CEO Nicolas Papadopoulo said "We are much more optimistic on the casualty side," arguing that casualty underwriters must earn an underwriting profit independent of investment yields.
US housing and employment — the hidden third of Arch's earnings
EMERGING TAILWIND — WITH A TAIL
Mortgage insurance produced $220M of underwriting income on $272M of net premium written in Q2 2026 — 33.5% of group underwriting profit from 6.7% of premium. The driver is credit, not weather: the delinquency rate held flat at 2.1%, and with home equity intact, distressed borrowers sell rather than default. Growth came from a new Australian client and from Arch ceding less risk to its own reinsurers. The tail is that the same leverage runs in reverse. The reported 22.8% combined ratio becomes 39.8% once prior-year reserve releases are excluded, and in a genuine housing and employment downturn new delinquencies would rise off the 2.1% base and the release tailwind would flip to reserve strengthening — a double hit to the segment that currently supplies a third of the group's underwriting profit while attracting almost none of the attention. This is the single most under-analysed exposure in the Arch story.
Bull versus bear, framed honestly
THE DECISION
The bull owns a business earning a 15.3% operating ROE at 1.45× book, run by a management team demonstrably willing to shrink — net premium down 6.9% group-wide — and returning 94% of net income through buybacks at prices the CFO calls "very attractive." Book value per share rose 2.8% in the quarter, roughly 11.7% annualised, and for a compounding underwriter that number, not EPS, is the scoreboard. The bear owns three segments all facing their own peak: primary insurance already at a 98.5% combined ratio with no cushion, reinsurance underlying deteriorating into a softening property market, and mortgage carrying a reserve-release tailwind that cannot repeat indefinitely. Consensus 2026 EPS of $9.37 against $12.83 trailing quantifies the expected fade. The honest resolution: this is a high-quality cyclical bought at a fair-to-modest price near a cycle peak, where the defence is not the multiple but the discipline — and Arch's Q2 2026 behaviour is genuine evidence that the discipline is real.
