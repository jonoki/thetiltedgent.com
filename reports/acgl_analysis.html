01 Company Overview

Arch Capital Group Ltd. is a Bermuda-domiciled specialty underwriter that writes insurance, reinsurance (insurance sold to other insurance companies, so that they can pass along part of the risk they have taken on) and mortgage insurance (cover that pays a mortgage lender when a borrower defaults and the foreclosure sale does not repay the loan) across the US, Canada, Bermuda, the UK, Europe and Australia. It is one of a small group of Bermuda platforms that operates all three of those businesses at scale, and it is run explicitly as a cycle-management machine: management shrinks premium when prices are inadequate and expands when they are not.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $19.93B, up 14.27% year over year, with net income of $4.36B and diluted EPS of $11.60. Over the trailing twelve months to September 2026, revenue is $18.78B (−3.9%, StockAnalysis) and diluted EPS is $12.23, the sum of the four quarters' reported figures — revenue is now shrinking as the company deliberately pulls back from property lines where prices are falling. In the third quarter, reported on October 27, 2026, after-tax operating EPS (profit excluding investment gains and losses, currency moves and deal costs) was $2.67 against a Nasdaq consensus of $2.15, and the company bought back $1.4B of its own stock.

Specialty lines means non-standard, hard-to-price risks — professional liability, excess casualty, construction, marine, programs, travel, entertainment — written outside the commoditised standard market. The combined ratio is the single number that tells you whether underwriting made money: losses plus expenses divided by premiums earned, so below 100% is an underwriting profit and above 100% is a loss. Arch's Insurance segment ran 94.4% in Q3 2026, up 1.0 point from 93.4% a year earlier but well below the 98.5% of Q2, and underwriting income was $108M against $129M. Catastrophe losses, including losses tied to the Iran conflict, added 5.7 points against 2.2 points a year earlier. Stripping out catastrophes and prior-year reserve movement, the underlying combined ratio improved to 89.2% from 91.3%: the CFO said most of the loss-ratio improvement came from extra premium earned on marine war-risk cover, and the expense ratio fell to 33.6% from 34.4%, mainly on Bermuda's new refundable tax credits (a Bermuda government tax incentive). Net premium rose 2.7%, mainly in international operations (CFO); excluding non-renewals of business that came with the August 2024 purchase of Allianz's US middle-market and entertainment insurance business, the release puts the growth at 5.8%.

Read this one twice. Mortgage insurance is 6.8% of Arch's net premium and more than a quarter of its underwriting profit, because it runs a combined ratio of 25.5% — meaning roughly 74 cents of every premium dollar drops through as underwriting income. Arch's other two segments ran 94.4% and 78.3%. The mechanism: Arch collects a small monthly premium on high-loan-to-value mortgages and pays claims only when a borrower defaults and the home sells for less than the loan. While house prices hold up and unemployment stays low, borrowers who fall behind sell into equity rather than default, so incurred losses are minimal and old reserves are released. That is what happened again: the CEO said "positive homeowner credit conditions continue to support a low delinquency rate of 2.2% at September 30th", and excluding prior-year reserve releases the combined ratio was 41.6%, not 25.5% — releases supplied 16.1 points of the reported result, which the release attributes to better-than-expected cure rates (late borrowers catching up). That gap is the tell. Reported mortgage profitability is partly a harvest of caution booked in earlier years, and it is finite: the ex-release ratio was 32.7% a year ago and 39.8% in Q2.

What a housing downturn would do: this segment is tied to US employment and home-price direction, not to hurricanes. If unemployment rises and house prices stall, three things happen roughly in sequence — new delinquencies rise off the 2.2% base, the reserve-release tailwind reverses into strengthening, and the combined ratio moves from the twenties toward and potentially through the 41.6% ex-release level. Underwriting income already fell 22.7% to $201M year over year as earned premium dropped 10.3%; the CEO said new loan volume is held back by mortgage rates above 7%, while those same rates keep existing borrowers from refinancing away.
Market context: delinquency rate 2.2% at Sep 30, 2026; persistency (policies still in force a year later) around 80% (Q3'26 call)

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
CYCLE DISCIPLINE THREE UNCORRELATED SEGMENTS MORTGAGE PROFIT POOL BERMUDA TAX & CAPITAL BASE RESERVE CONSERVATISM UNDERWRITER TALENT DEPTH $22.2B COMMON EQUITY
Arch's moat is behavioural and structural rather than technological. Structurally, three segments whose loss drivers are close to uncorrelated — specialty casualty (court awards and the legal environment), reinsurance (catastrophes and the behaviour of the insurers it covers), mortgage (employment and house prices) — let the group keep earning while any one of them is repriced. Q3 2026 shows it: reinsurance underwriting income fell 18.5% and mortgage 22.7%, and the group still produced $702M of underwriting profit, a consolidated combined ratio of 82.5% (81.2% excluding catastrophes and prior-year reserve releases) and a 16.0% annualised operating ROE. Behaviourally, the moat is the willingness to shrink. Reinsurance net premium fell 9.3% because Arch cut participations where returns no longer met its objectives; the CEO's line on the call was "Our objective is not to become an index of the overall market." When growth is scarce, the capital goes back to shareholders instead: $1.4B of buybacks in the quarter. That, plus 18.74% trailing ROE (StockAnalysis), is what a disciplined underwriter is supposed to look like.

1. The soft cycle is here, and it hits first where Arch earns most. In a soft market insurance prices fall because there is more capital chasing the same risks. Prices are falling in property and short-tail lines, and the CEO expects "continued pressure" on property rates into the January renewals. Reinsurance is 56% of underwriting income, and its underlying combined ratio was worse than a year earlier in both of the last two quarters (by 2.7 percentage points in Q2 and 1.6 in Q3, to 78.4%). Discipline protects the balance sheet, not the earnings line: shrinking premium in a soft market shrinks profit.

2. Mortgage earnings quality. 16.1 points of the 25.5% reported mortgage combined ratio came from releasing prior-year reserves. That is real money, but it is not a run-rate, and the release notes a modestly higher level of new delinquencies. Any reader treating $200M a quarter as durable is valuing a reserve harvest as if it were steady underwriting profit.

3. Catastrophe volatility and accumulation. Current accident-year catastrophe losses were $234M pre-tax in Q3 2026, including losses from the Iran conflict, in a quarter the CEO said had light catastrophe activity. Arch's own estimate of its loss from a single 1-in-250-year natural catastrophe in its peak zone, after reinsurance, rose slightly to $1.9B at October 1, 8.6% of tangible equity (CFO, Q3 call). War and political-violence losses are hard to model, and Arch is writing more marine war-risk cover.

4. Book value stalled. Book value per share slipped 0.2% in Q3 to $67.90: the release says rising interest rates cut the value of the bond portfolio (total investment return −0.42%), and buying back stock at about 1.5 times book value also lowers book value per remaining share. For an insurer valued on book, a flat quarter matters more than a strong EPS beat.

Notable One-Off Items. Four items distort the Q3 2026 result and should be set aside before extrapolating. (1) Reserve releases. $179M of favourable prior-year development took 4.5 points off the group combined ratio, and within Mortgage the effect was extreme — a 25.5% reported combined ratio against 41.6% excluding development. (2) Catastrophe and war losses. $234M pre-tax of current accident-year catastrophe loss, including the Iran conflict; at the same time the conflict brought extra marine war-risk premium that lowered the Insurance loss ratio. (3) Bermuda tax credits. The new refundable tax credits lowered the Insurance and Reinsurance expense ratios and cut corporate expenses to $5M from $28M. The CFO stressed they are "not a one-time event", so they distort the comparison with Q3 2025, which had none, rather than the run-rate. (4) Fund income. The gap between $3.00 of GAAP diluted EPS and $2.67 of after-tax operating EPS is mostly $0.66 a share of income from funds accounted for by the equity method, partly offset by $0.29 of realised investment losses — which is why the 8.50 trailing P/E should not be read as a valuation conclusion.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It says nothing about value — only about the recent balance of buying and selling pressure.

The five-year trajectory. Over the 61 points plotted, ACGL went from a month-end close of $41.82 in October 2021 to $103.94 on October 29, 2026 — a price return of +148.5%, a compound annual rate of +20.0% before dividends. Over the same dates SPY, the S&P 500 fund, returned +67.5% and QQQ, the Nasdaq-100 fund, +85.3% (price only). The calendar-year steps inside that run, measured December close to December close on the same series, were +41.2% in 2022, +18.3% in 2023, +24.3% in 2024 and +3.9% in 2025, with +8.4% year-to-date in 2026. Three years of strong gains, then a stall: the hard property-catastrophe market after 2022 (a stretch when prices for catastrophe cover rose sharply) did the heavy lifting, and the market stopped paying for it at the end of 2024.

The inflection points. The first eleven months of the window were flat — $41.82 in October 2021 to $45.54 in September 2022, +8.9%, with every month-end between $40.38 and $48.42. The break came in October 2022: +26.3% in a single month to $57.50, the largest monthly move in the five years, and the point at which the market repriced Bermuda underwriters for the hard market. From there the stock ran almost without interruption to $86.68 at end-October 2023, then gave back 14.3% over two months to close 2023 at $74.27. 2024 was the second leg: a climb through $92.44 in March and $102.63 in May to the highest month-end close of the whole series, $113.09 in August 2024 (+18.1% in that month alone). That was the top. October 2024 fell 11.9% to $98.56 — the largest monthly decline in the series — and the stock closed 2024 at $92.35.

The plateau, and the break above it. From January 2025 through September 2026 every month-end close sat between $86.06 (July 2025) and $100.53 (July 2026). 2026 itself was a round trip — up to $100.15 in February, down 10.8% to $89.34 in May, back to $100.53 in July, then $98.07 in August and $92.36 in September. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Arch is doing what a disciplined underwriter should do at this point in the cycle: reinsurance net premium down 9.3% as it walks away from business that no longer pays, primary insurance back to a 94.4% combined ratio with its underlying ratio improving to 89.2%, and $1.4B of stock bought back in a single quarter — about 109% of nine-month net income. Operating EPS of $2.67 beat the Nasdaq consensus of $2.15 by 24%, the consolidated combined ratio was 82.5% and annualised operating ROE 16.0%. Net investment income keeps rising ($451M, up from $408M a year earlier), and the CFO said new bonds yield about 0.65–0.70 percentage points more than the portfolio's existing ones; he also called the Bermuda tax credits that lowered costs sustainable. With diluted shares down to 341.4M in Q3 from 376.1M a year earlier (Q3 release), every dollar of profit is spread over fewer shares.

Bear Case

The earnings are better than they look and worse than they read. 16.1 points of the mortgage segment's 25.5% combined ratio came from prior-year reserve releases, and the group's $179M of releases took 4.5 points off the total; reinsurance, 56% of underwriting profit, saw its underlying combined ratio worsen again, to 78.4%, with property prices still falling. Revenue is down 3.9% over the trailing year and book value per share slipped to $67.90. Consensus operating EPS of $10.22 for 2026 and $9.89 for 2027 (StockAnalysis, adjusted basis) sit below the $10.71 Arch earned on that basis over the last four quarters. At 1.53 times book after a 21% one-year gain, the price no longer reflects much of the coming downturn in pricing, and Goldman Sachs keeps a Sell with a $95 target.

01
Soft-market earnings squeeze. Property-catastrophe reinsurance prices were down around 16% globally after the July 1, 2026 renewals (Guy Carpenter) and the CEO expects "continued pressure" on property rates into January 2027. Reinsurance is 56% of underwriting income; if the January renewals bring another double-digit decline, the segment's underlying combined ratio (78.4% in Q3) worsens further and Arch shrinks premium into it by design — protecting the balance sheet but cutting earnings.
02
Mortgage reserve releases run out, or a housing/employment downturn. The mortgage segment's reported profit leans heavily on releasing reserves booked in earlier, more cautious years, and the delinquency rate was 2.2% at September 30, with the release noting a modestly higher level of new delinquencies. Rising unemployment or flat-to-falling house prices would turn releases into reserve additions and move the combined ratio sharply higher from the 41.6% it ran before releases in Q3.
03
Catastrophe and war loss accumulation. Q3 2026 catastrophe losses of $234M included losses from the Iran conflict, while Arch wrote more marine war-risk cover. Its own estimate of the after-reinsurance loss from a single 1-in-250-year peak-zone natural catastrophe was $1.9B at October 1, 8.6% of tangible equity — roughly two quarters of operating income in one event.
  Industry Commentary
Is the property-catastrophe market still hard? Not any more.
ONGOING HEADWIND
The evidence says the hard market that began after 2022 (a stretch of rising prices for catastrophe cover) has turned. Property-catastrophe reinsurance prices were down around 16% globally after the July 1, 2026 renewals (Guy Carpenter, a reinsurance broker); on the Q3 call the CEO pointed to "abundant reinsurance capacity, particularly in property and short-tail lines" and expected continued pressure on property rates after a light catastrophe year. The tell is the underlying reinsurance combined ratio excluding catastrophes and prior-year releases, which was 1.6 percentage points worse than a year earlier, at 78.4%, in Q3, after a 2.7-point deterioration in Q2. Arch's response is textbook: gross reinsurance premium down 8.0%, net down 9.3%, more risk passed to outside investors' capital. That answers the central question of this report — current premium behaviour reflects discipline, not chasing: "We will not chase growth for the sake of growth," the CEO said. The cost of that discipline is that earnings fall with the cycle; the benefit is that reserves do not blow up three years later. Casualty is the partial offset: the CEO said rate increases have moderated in some casualty lines but that industry-wide reserve problems from recent years argue for casualty pricing to stay at or above the rate at which claim costs are rising.
US housing and employment — the hidden quarter of Arch's earnings
STEADY — WITH A TAIL
Mortgage insurance produced $201M of underwriting income on $263M of net premium written in Q3 2026 — 28.6% of group underwriting profit from 6.8% of premium. The driver is credit, not weather: the delinquency rate was 2.2%, and with home equity intact, distressed borrowers sell or catch up rather than default. Growth is hard to find: the CEO said new mortgage volume is held back by mortgage rates above 7%, though the same rates keep existing policies on the books (persistency around 80%). The tail is that the same leverage runs in reverse. The reported 25.5% combined ratio becomes 41.6% once prior-year reserve releases are excluded, up from 32.7% a year earlier, and in a genuine housing and employment downturn new delinquencies would rise off the 2.2% base and the release tailwind would flip to reserve strengthening — a double hit to the segment that supplies more than a quarter of the group's underwriting profit while attracting little of the attention.
Bull versus bear, framed honestly
THE DECISION
The bull owns a business earning a 16.0% operating ROE at 1.53× book, run by a management team demonstrably willing to shrink and returning more than all of its net income through buybacks — the CFO said they would likely "return back even more capital" while growth is scarce. The bear owns three segments all past their best pricing: reinsurance underlying results deteriorating into a softening property market, mortgage carrying a reserve-release tailwind that cannot repeat indefinitely, and primary insurance helped this quarter by extra war-risk premium. Book value per share went nowhere in Q3, and for a compounding underwriter that number, not EPS, is the scoreboard. Consensus operating EPS of $10.22 for 2026 and $9.89 for 2027, against $10.71 on the same basis over the last four quarters, quantifies the expected fade. The honest resolution: this is a high-quality cyclical at a fair price near a cycle peak, where the defence is not the multiple but the discipline — and two quarters of falling reinsurance premium and buybacks above earnings are genuine evidence that the discipline is real. The stock's 21% gain over the past year means the market has started to pay for it.
