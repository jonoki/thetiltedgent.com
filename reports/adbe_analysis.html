01 Company Overview

Adobe Inc. is the dominant vendor of professional creative software — Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere Pro, InDesign and Acrobat — sold almost entirely as subscriptions, plus a large enterprise marketing-technology stack (Adobe Experience Cloud) used to build, personalize and measure digital customer experiences. Fiscal 2025 (ended Nov 28, 2025) revenue was $23.77B, up 10.5% YoY, with an 89.3% gross margin and $9.85B of free cash flow; trailing-twelve-month revenue is now $25.97B, and the latest quarter grew 13%. At the August 17 snapshot Adobe ranked roughly #229 globally and #162 in the US by market capitalization — a striking demotion for a company that was worth $271.6B at the end of 2023 and is worth $99.2B at the September 21 close.

That collapse, not the operating results, is the story. Adobe has compounded revenue at roughly 10–13% a year through the de-rating and on September 10 raised FY2026 guidance for the second quarter running, to $26.576–$26.626B with non-GAAP EPS of $24.45–$24.50. Yet the stock trades at only 13.9x trailing and ~9.3x forward earnings — roughly a third of the application-software group — because the market is still pricing a meaningful probability that generative AI structurally impairs seat-based creative software. This page treats that as the single question that matters, and gives both answers equal weight.

What changed since the September 1 edition: the man in charge, and not much else. The quarter Adobe reported on September 10 was a familiar kind of Adobe quarter — revenue up 13% to $6.76B, a sliver ahead of consensus, a sliver of a guidance raise, AI-first ARR up from above $500M to above $650M, and a new milestone of more than one billion monthly active users. The information event came a week earlier. On September 3 the board named Anil Chakravarthy — the former Informatica CEO who has run Adobe's enterprise customer-experience business and worldwide field operations — as CEO from December 1, with Narayen staying on as executive chair. The next day David Wadhwani, who had run the creative and productivity franchise for nearly five years and had been considered a potential CEO candidate himself, announced his resignation. As 24/7 Wall St. put it, “Chakravarthy's record sits in enterprise data and marketing software, further from the creative tools where the AI threat is sharpest.” ADBE fell 6.7% on September 4 and was 13.0% below the previous edition by the eve of the print. The quarter itself moved the stock 1.4%. So the August rally is gone, the fundamentals edged up, and the multiple is back near 14x trailing earnings.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Adobe's moat is workflow, not technology. A studio's asset library, colour pipelines, plug-in ecosystem, client hand-off conventions and — critically — the staff who have spent a decade learning keyboard shortcuts are all denominated in Adobe file formats. .PSD, .AI, .INDD and .PDF function as de-facto industry standards, so switching is a coordination problem across an entire supply chain rather than an individual purchase decision. That is why the company has sustained an 89.3% gross margin, 61.9% ROE and 56.3% ROIC while raising prices for a decade. Firefly adds a second, narrower moat: because Adobe trained on licensed and Adobe Stock content, it can offer enterprise customers commercial indemnification that generic image generators cannot — a real procurement advantage in regulated and brand-sensitive industries. $22.16B of remaining performance obligations (67% current, as of Q3) is contracted revenue that does not evaporate on a news cycle.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Generative AI attacks the unit of value. Adobe sells seats to people who are paid for craft execution. If a marketing team can generate twenty campaign variants from a prompt instead of hiring three designers, the seat count that Adobe's revenue is denominated in shrinks even if Adobe wins the tooling war. This is the mechanism behind a ~9.3x forward multiple on a business whose revenue grew 13% last quarter — the market is not disputing this year's numbers, it is disputing the durability of the seat. The August rally lifted that multiple to about 11x on sector sentiment; the September succession news and a Q3 that added no new evidence about the seat took it back below 10x.

2. The freemium pivot defers monetization with unproven payback. Management explicitly deferred planned Creative Cloud price increases and routed demand into free tiers to expand the funnel. The consequence: FY26 organic ending-ARR growth guidance fell to roughly 8.3% from about 10.2% once you strip out ~$480M of acquired Semrush ARR. Management declined to quantify payback economics on the freemium creative users when pressed in June. That is a two-point growth cut wearing a headline raise, and it is why the June 11 beat-and-raise was met with a 6.8% selloff. Q3 kept the 10.2% ending-ARR target unchanged while the freemium creative base passed 100 million; we did not find a conversion or payback figure in the Q3 materials reviewed for this edition.

3. Earnings growth is increasingly financial, not operational. In Q2 FY2026 GAAP net income grew 1.2% while GAAP EPS grew 7.9% — the gap is a 6.3% reduction in share count. Non-GAAP operating margin compressed from 47.4% in Q1 to 44.5% in Q2 and is guided to ~44.0% in Q3, as sales and marketing grew 16% (faster than revenue) to fund freemium acquisition. Operating cash flow actually declined 1.2% YoY to $2.17B despite 13% revenue growth. Q3 repaired the cash point — a record $2.52B of operating cash flow, +14.8% — but not the margin point: non-GAAP operating margin was 44% against 46.3% a year earlier, GAAP operating margin 34.8% against 36.3%, and another ~9.5 million shares were retired. Buybacks are a legitimate use of capital, but they are not a substitute for operating leverage.

4. A succession that answered the wrong question for the market. The CEO search ended on September 3 with an insider: Anil Chakravarthy, who runs the enterprise customer-experience business and worldwide field operations, takes over on December 1 with Narayen as executive chair. The next day David Wadhwani — the executive who ran the creative franchise, the part of Adobe most exposed to generative AI — announced his resignation, and Jefferies analysts wrote, as reported by Investing.com, that “With Wadhwani's departure, we believe more departures and org changes are likely.” The CFO seat is still held on an interim basis by Steve Day, more than three months after Dan Durn left for Marvell. The incoming CEO inherits the freemium pivot, the FY27 plan and an open CFO search at once. Continuity is the bull reading; the bear reading is that the creative business just lost its leader at the moment it most needs one.

Notable One-Off Items

Goodwill impairment (Q2 FY2026): a $70M write-down in publishing and advertising, worth $0.17 per share of the GAAP result. Excluded from non-GAAP EPS of $5.96.

Recurring litigation accruals: $62M in Q1 FY2026 and $30M in Q2 FY2026. Individually small, but a pattern of "one-offs" that appear every quarter is better read as an ongoing cost of doing business than as a non-recurring item.

Semrush acquisition (closed in Q2 FY2026): contributed roughly $40M of Q2 subscription revenue and ~$480M of ARR. This matters more than its size: the ~$480M is what allowed headline FY26 ending-ARR growth guidance to look unchanged at ~10.2% while organic guidance was cut to ~8.3%. Adobe also acquired Topaz Labs (AI image and video enhancement) in 2026.

The Figma termination fee (historical): Adobe announced a $20B acquisition of Figma in September 2022 — the stock fell sharply on the price — and terminated it in December 2023 under UK/EU regulatory pressure, paying a $1B breakup fee. Figma subsequently IPO'd in July 2025 and carried a $13.3B market cap on $1.28B of revenue as of the Aug 17 snapshot. The Figma market cap was not re-verified for this update.

Buybacks (ongoing, and material enough to distort EPS): Adobe repurchased ~8.5 million shares (~$2.1B) in Q2 FY2026 and ~9.5 million more in Q3, under a $25B authorization added in April 2026; $24.55B remained authorized at the end of Q3 — about 25% of the $99.18B market cap at the September 21 close. StockAnalysis puts the buyback yield at 6.17% on 397.5M shares outstanding. Any EPS growth analysis that ignores this overstates operating momentum.

In plain English: RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It says nothing about whether the business is good.

The five-year trajectory is one of the great de-ratings in large-cap software. Adobe closed at an all-time high of $688.37 on November 19, 2021, when the market capitalized 20%+ growth and 45% margins at more than 50x trailing earnings. It closed September 21, 2026 at $249.52 — a decline of about 64% from the peak on a price basis (Adobe pays no dividend, so price return is total return). The NASDAQ-100 comparison was not re-checked for this edition. The gap is not an earnings gap: revenue grew from $15.8B in FY2021 to $25.97B TTM (+64%) and GAAP EPS grew from $10.02 to $17.91 (+79%). Essentially 100% of the decline is multiple compression — from more than 50x to 13.9x trailing earnings. The August rally and the September reversal netted out to almost nothing: the stock ended this edition's window 1.8% below its August 17 close.

The compression came in three distinct waves, each with a different cause. 2022 was a rates-driven de-rating shared by all long-duration software, made worse by the September announcement of a $20B cash-and-stock bid for Figma at roughly 50x ARR, which the market read as a capitulation on organic competitiveness. 2023 was a violent AI-optimism recovery (+77% for the year) as Firefly launched and investors assumed Adobe would be an AI winner. 2024 through today is the AI-disruption repricing: two down years of −25.5% (2024) and −21.3% (2025) on year-end closes, and −28.7% year-to-date from the $349.99 close of 2025, during which Adobe never missed a quarter. From September 2024 the stock closed below its 10-month moving average every month until August 2026, when the rally lifted it above for one month; September's succession selloff took it back below, and the price back to where that rally began.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Adobe generates $10.59B of free cash flow against a $99.18B market cap — a 10.7% FCF yield on a business with an 89% gross margin, 56% ROIC, $22.16B of contracted backlog and a quarter that just grew revenue 13%. At ~9.3x forward earnings the market is pricing terminal decline, yet Adobe has now raised FY26 guidance twice — to $26.576–$26.626B of revenue and $24.45–$24.50 of non-GAAP EPS — and Q3 produced a record $2.52B of operating cash flow. The AI evidence keeps moving the right way: AI-first ARR went from above $500M to above $650M in one quarter, Firefly app and credit-pack ARR grew 40% quarter over quarter, Acrobat AI Assistant MAUs doubled, and the company passed one billion monthly active users with 100M+ freemium creative users in the funnel. Adobe's licensed training data gives it enterprise indemnification that generic generators cannot match. With $24.55B of buyback authorization — about a quarter of the market cap — retiring shares at under 10x earnings, per-share value compounds at a high-single-digit rate even if growth never re-accelerates. September repriced leadership, not the business: the 12.8% drop since the last edition came from the CEO announcement and a senior departure, while every operating number that arrived afterwards was at or above expectations.

Bear Case

The multiple is low because the earnings base is at risk, not because the market is inefficient. Adobe monetizes seats occupied by people paid for craft execution — exactly the labour generative AI displaces most directly — so unit volume can fall even if Adobe wins on product. Management has already blinked: it deferred Creative Cloud price increases and cut organic FY26 ending-ARR growth guidance to roughly 8.3% from ~10.2%, concealing the cut behind ~$480M of acquired Semrush ARR — and Q3 left the 10.2% headline target unchanged rather than raising it. Quality keeps slipping underneath the headline: non-GAAP operating margin was 44% in Q3 against 46.3% a year earlier, GAAP operating margin fell to 34.8% from 36.3%, and EPS is helped by retiring ~6% of the share count a year. Then the succession: the board chose the executive who runs the enterprise marketing business, the leader of the creative franchise — the part of Adobe most exposed to AI — resigned the next day, and the CFO seat has been interim since June. The guidance raise was a rounding error ($0.03–0.08B on revenue), five analysts still rate the stock Sell, Citi cut its target by $51 after the print, and short interest is 4.37%. A cheap stock with a new CEO, a missing CFO and a departing creative chief is not a de-risked stock — the leadership that designed the freemium bet will not be the leadership that has to prove it pays.

Top 3 Risks
01
Seat compression from generative AI. Adobe's revenue is a function of professional headcount in creative and marketing roles. If generative tools let a team of three do the work of ten, Adobe's addressable seat base contracts structurally — and no amount of product superiority fixes a shrinking denominator. This risk is unfalsifiable in the short run, which is exactly why the multiple stays compressed.
02
The freemium bet fails to convert. Adobe has traded near-term ARR growth for 90M creative freemium users (now 100M+) and 900M+ Acrobat/Express MAUs, and management would not quantify payback economics when pressed. If conversion rates disappoint, the company will have spent two points of growth and two to three points of operating margin to acquire users who never pay — and from December 1 the strategy belongs to a new CEO who did not design it and may reverse it mid-flight.
03
Earnings quality erodes as buybacks do the work. In Q2 GAAP net income grew 1.2% while EPS grew 7.9%; Q3's record cash flow (+14.8%) helped, but margins kept falling and the share count kept shrinking by roughly 6% a year. That is sustainable at $10.6B of FCF — but it means reported EPS growth is not evidence that the operating business is compounding, and it will not cushion a genuine revenue deceleration.
  Industry Commentary
Generative AI: Simultaneously the Threat and the Product
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Adobe is the clearest test case in software of whether an incumbent can sell the technology that commoditizes its own product. The evidence so far is genuinely mixed. On the positive side, AI-first ARR passed $650M at Q3 FY2026, up more than 150% year over year and from above $500M one quarter earlier; Firefly app and credit-pack ARR grew 40% quarter over quarter (the Q2 all-in Firefly figure was near ~$300M); and Acrobat AI Assistant MAUs doubled quarter on quarter. On the negative side, $650M is still only about 2.4% of $27.50B total ARR — nowhere near enough to offset a seat-base contraction, and Adobe is now distributing its agents through third-party surfaces including a ChatGPT plugin covering all 70 of its tools, which trades direct customer ownership for reach. The market's verdict is the ~9.3x forward multiple: it is not waiting for evidence, it has already assumed the answer. That asymmetry is the whole trade — and the round trip of August and September, when the multiple went from 9.8x to 11x on Nvidia's results and back below 10x on a CEO announcement, while Adobe's own numbers came in slightly ahead, shows how much of the price is a claim about narrative and leadership rather than about the quarter.
Seat-Based SaaS Pricing Under Structural Pressure
ONGOING HEADWIND
The entire application-software complex is being forced from per-seat toward consumption and outcome-based pricing, and Adobe is further into that transition than most. Management explicitly tied AI revenue expansion to "credit consumption and agent-based experiences" rather than seat upgrades — a rational adaptation, but one that converts predictable recurring revenue into variable usage revenue that investors capitalize at a lower multiple. The deferral of Creative Cloud price increases is the tell: for a decade Adobe's model was "raise price annually, retain 90%+ of seats." Choosing funnel expansion over price in 2026 is the first crack in that formula, and it cost roughly two points of organic ARR growth guidance. Whether Adobe can rebuild pricing power on a consumption basis is unresolved, and the answer will not be visible in the numbers for several quarters.
The Valuation-vs-Disruption Standoff — What Each Side Needs
BULL vs BEAR FRAMING
The bull needs one thing: evidence that ARR growth stabilizes in the high single digits or better without price increases doing the work. Reinstated Creative Cloud pricing without elevated churn, AI ARR compounding toward $1B+, and a permanent CFO alongside the incoming CEO would each independently support re-rating from ~9.3x toward the low-to-mid teens — which on the current FY27 consensus EPS of $27.67 implies a roughly $360–$415 stock (our arithmetic at 13–15x). The bear needs one thing too: a single quarter where revenue or ARR growth decelerates below guidance. A genuine miss would validate a permanently lower earnings base, and the $195 street-low target becomes the new anchor. September changed the people, not the argument. ADBE is back below the $276.40 consensus target, above Goldman's $200, essentially at Citi's and Evercore's $250 and below Phillip's $261, D.A. Davidson's $290 and Bernstein's $373, and at a 10.7% free-cash-flow yield — more margin of safety than at the September 1 edition. Against that, the two questions that decide the outcome — whether the seat base holds and whether freemium converts — now pass to a CEO who has not yet taken office, with an interim CFO and a vacancy at the top of the creative business. The Q3 print answered neither question; it confirmed that the machine keeps running while they are asked. The sell-side spread ($195 to $377, close to a 2x range) and the 28% Buy / 59% Hold / 13% Sell distribution still reflect genuine disagreement rather than herding. This remains a name where position sizing matters more than conviction.
