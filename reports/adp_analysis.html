01 Company Overview

Automatic Data Processing is the world's largest payroll and human capital management (HCM — the software and services that handle payroll, benefits, time and talent for an employer's workforce) provider, running pay for tens of millions of workers through platforms including RUN Powered by ADP (small business), ADP Workforce Now (mid-market), ADP Vantage and the newer ADP Lyric HCM (enterprise), plus a large PEO (professional employer organization — an arrangement where ADP becomes the co-employer of record for a client's staff, taking on payroll, benefits procurement and workers' compensation administration) business under the ADP TotalSource brand.

Fiscal 2026 closed on June 30, 2026 and results were reported July 29, 2026: revenue of $21.95 billion, up 6.7% YoY (6% organic constant currency), with GAAP diluted EPS of $10.94 (+10%) and adjusted diluted EPS of $11.12 (+11%). ADP is the #107 largest US public company and #47 on the NASDAQ by market value, and it has raised its dividend for 51 consecutive years — one of the longest streaks in the S&P 500. It is also the only large-cap whose own operating data doubles as a widely watched macro indicator: the ADP National Employment Report.

Revenue Mix
Payroll, tax filing, time & attendance, benefits administration, talent and HR outsourcing sold across every client size band — from single-employee shops on RUN, through Workforce Now in the mid-market, to Lyric HCM and Vantage at enterprise scale, plus a multinational business spanning 140+ countries. FY2026 revenue rose 7% reported / 5% organic constant currency, with segment margin up 60 bps to 36.7% (Q4 margin 34.4%, up 90 bps). The three operating tells investors watch: new business bookings +6% to $2.2 billion for the year, client revenue retention flat at 92.1% — near record levels — and US pays per control +1%. Pays per control is ADP's same-store employment metric: the change in the number of employees on payrolls at ADP clients that have been on the books for at least a year. It strips out client wins and losses, so it is effectively a read on the US labour market itself, and at +1% it is signalling a decidedly cool one.

Critically, this segment also books interest on funds held for clients. ADP holds payroll and tax money in transit between employers and employees/tax authorities and invests it in a laddered, high-grade fixed-income portfolio. In FY2026 average client funds balances were $40.4 billion (+7%) at an average yield of 3.4%, producing $1.355 billion of interest revenue, up 14% — roughly 6% of total company revenue and a far larger share of profit, since the associated cost is near zero. FY2027 guidance calls for $1.540–$1.560 billion (+~14%) on 3–4% balance growth and a yield of approximately 3.7%. Because the portfolio is laddered out several years, lower-coupon bonds bought in the 2020–2021 zero-rate era are still rolling into higher-yielding paper, which is why the yield is guided up even as front-end rates ease. That mechanic also runs in reverse: a sustained fall in reinvestment rates would compress this line with a multi-year lag, and it is the single most rate-sensitive item in ADP's P&L.
Industry Size: Payroll software applications ≈ $8.4B (2024) → $11.1B by 2029, ~5.7% CAGR; broader HCM applications ≈ $58.7B (2024) → $81.1B by 2029, ~11.7% CAGR (Apps Run the World). ADP's own revenue base is far larger than the licensed-software market because it also sells outsourced service delivery and earns float income.

Revenue Mix
Under a PEO arrangement ADP becomes the co-employer of record for a client's workforce: it runs payroll, files employment taxes, sponsors and procures the health and retirement benefit plans, and carries workers' compensation administration. The pitch to a 40-person business is Fortune-500 benefit pricing and compliance cover it could never buy alone. FY2026 revenue grew 7% to $7.13 billion on average paid worksite employees up 2% to roughly 762,000 (Q4 average ~775,000).

The margin optics need care. A large slice of PEO revenue is zero-margin pass-through — the health insurance premiums and statutory costs ADP collects and remits on the client's behalf. When pass-throughs grow faster than the service fee, reported segment margin falls even if underlying economics are unchanged, which is exactly what happened in FY2026: segment margin fell 110 bps to 13.1% (Q4 −100 bps to 12.2%) on faster pass-through growth and genuinely higher workers' compensation expense. The second item is a real cost headwind, not an optical one. FY2027 guidance of 5–7% PEO revenue growth is the widest band in the outlook, reflecting how sensitive worksite-employee counts are to small-business hiring and to benefit-plan pricing.

ADP's moat is boring and durable. Payroll is a system of record that touches every employee, every tax authority and every benefits carrier a company deals with; ripping it out mid-year risks misfiled returns, penalties and unpaid staff. That fear shows up as 92.1% client revenue retention — meaning ADP keeps roughly $0.92 of every revenue dollar it had a year ago before any new sales. Layer on compliance depth across US federal, 50 states, thousands of local jurisdictions and 140+ countries, and the barrier is regulatory as much as technical: a startup can build elegant payroll UX in a year; it cannot build two decades of tax-agency filing relationships and audit history in a year. The float portfolio compounds this — $40.4 billion of client money in transit throws off $1.36 billion a year of near-zero-cost interest income that a subscale competitor simply cannot replicate, because float scales with pays processed. Finally, the dataset. ADP's anonymized weekly payroll records cover more than 26 million US private-sector employees, and the ADP National Employment Report — produced with the Stanford Digital Economy Lab and released monthly ahead of the government's payroll print — turns that exhaust into a national macro indicator carried by every financial wire. It is the rarest kind of marketing asset: a free, credibility-building distribution channel that competitors have no way to buy.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Growth is capped by the labour market. Pays per control grew just 1% in FY2026 and is guided to 0–1% in FY2027. ADP's own National Employment Report showed private payrolls rising only 44,000 in July 2026 — the smallest gain in six months. Same-store employment is the volume line under a large share of Employer Services revenue; when it goes to zero, growth has to come entirely from pricing, bookings and cross-sell, and a negative print would turn it into an outright drag.

2. Float income is a rate bet dressed as an operating line. $1.36 billion of FY2026 interest revenue carries almost no associated cost, so it drops disproportionately to profit. It is guided up ~14% in FY2027 as legacy low-coupon bonds roll off, but that tailwind is mechanical and finite. If reinvestment rates fall materially, the portfolio yield stops climbing and eventually reverses — and because the market has been capitalising this income at ADP's full ~25× multiple, the de-rating risk is larger than the earnings risk.

3. AI and the seat-based pricing question. HCM software has historically been priced per employee per month. If agentic AI genuinely compresses headcount in administrative and support functions, the billable unit shrinks. The market has clearly taken that seriously: over the past year Workday is −29%, Paycom −28%, Paylocity −22% and Paychex −13%, while the NASDAQ-100 returned roughly +25%. ADP's −8% is the best of a badly de-rated peer group, not an escape.

4. Well-funded private challengers at the entry point. Rippling (~$1B ARR, $16.8B valuation), Deel (~$1B ARR, $17.3B valuation) and Gusto ($1B of actual trailing revenue, cash-flow positive, $9.3B valuation) are all attacking the small-and-mid-market from the modern-UX, all-in-one direction — precisely where RUN and Workforce Now sit. None is yet material to a $21.9B revenue base, but three simultaneous billion-dollar entrants in one decade is an unusual amount of new competitive energy in a business that saw almost none for thirty years.

Notable One-Off Items

FY2026 carried a modest but identifiable set of non-recurring items, which together explain essentially all of the $0.18 gap between GAAP diluted EPS of $10.94 and adjusted diluted EPS of $11.12:

• Business alignment program — $91 million pre-tax charge. Severance and strategic project costs, the largest single adjustment of the year. This is a restructuring line, and investors should watch whether it recurs in FY2027 — a "one-off" that appears three years running is an operating expense.
• Legal settlement — $18 million net pre-tax charge.
• Workforce optimization reversals — $5 million pre-tax gain (a partial write-back of a prior-period charge).
• ADP Ventures investment gains — $8 million pre-tax gain on the corporate venture portfolio; inherently lumpy and not part of recurring operating earnings.

Two things are notable by their absence. There were no material realised losses on the client funds portfolio disclosed for FY2026 — unrealised marks on the available-for-sale portfolio flow through other comprehensive income rather than earnings, so a rate move can move book equity without touching EPS. And there were no significant divestitures; ADP's portfolio has been stable since the 2014 CDK Global spinoff. That spinoff is worth remembering for a different reason: it is why ADP is rated AA− today rather than AAA. The company held a top-tier AAA rating until 2014, when the separation of the auto-dealer services business triggered a downgrade. Any description of ADP as "AAA-rated" is a decade out of date.

In plain English: RSI compares the size of recent up-months to the size of recent down-months on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 means it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It measures momentum, not value.

Five-year trajectory. ADP closed September 2021 at $199.92 and sits at $277.22 today — a +38.7% price gain, or roughly +54% total return including reinvested dividends (about 9.0% annualised). That is a perfectly respectable outcome in isolation and a poor one in context: over the same window the S&P 500 (SPY) returned roughly +93% and the NASDAQ-100 (QQQ) roughly +107% on a total-return basis. ADP delivered a decade-typical compounding result during a period when the index did something extraordinary.

The shape matters more than the endpoints. The stock did essentially nothing for three years — the November 2022 monthly close of $264.14 was not durably exceeded until mid-2024 — then ran hard from mid-2024 into a $325.53 monthly-close peak in May 2025, then fell 37.6% to a $203.18 close in March 2026 (with a $188.16 intraday 52-week low). The 52-week range of $188.16–$310.08 spans 65% of the low price — an extraordinary amount of realised volatility for a stock with a 0.82 beta and a 51-year dividend record, and a reminder that "defensive" describes the business, not the share price.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A 60% ROIC business with 92.1% revenue retention, a 51-year dividend growth streak and an AA− balance sheet is trading at 25.3× trailing earnings against a NASDAQ-100 at 32.8× — a discount to the index that has almost no precedent for ADP. FY2027 guidance of 9–11% adjusted EPS growth is underpinned by a highly visible ~14% step-up in client funds interest revenue to $1.54–1.56 billion, 70–90 bps of guided margin expansion, and AI productivity that management is already converting into reported margin (140 bps in Q4 alone). Add a 2.45% dividend yield and a 1.3% buyback yield and the shareholder return is ~3.8% before a dollar of growth. If pays per control merely holds at +1% and the multiple normalises toward its history, the arithmetic works without heroics.

Bear Case

Strip out the float and the operating business is growing 5% organically with same-store employment at +1% and guided to 0–1%. Nearly a fifth of ADP's incremental FY2027 profit growth is a bond-portfolio reinvestment tailwind that the market is capitalising at 25×, and it reverses if rates fall further. Two-thirds of the sell side sits at Hold with an average target 3% above spot; the entire Street raised price targets after FY2026 results and virtually nobody raised a rating. Meanwhile three private challengers have each reached ~$1 billion of revenue attacking the SMB entry point, and forward P/E of 22.6 versus a peer median of 17.5 means ADP is still the expensive way to own an industry the market is actively de-rating.

Float income reverses. $1.355 billion of FY2026 interest on funds held for clients is near-costless profit guided to $1.54–1.56 billion in FY2027 on a portfolio yield rising to ~3.7%. That climb depends on maturing low-coupon bonds being reinvested at higher rates. A sustained decline in reinvestment yields flips this from a ~14% growth contributor to a multi-year drag, and because it is being valued at the full corporate multiple, the share-price damage would exceed the earnings damage.
03
Agentic AI compresses the billable unit. HCM pricing is fundamentally per-employee-per-month. If AI reduces headcount in the administrative and support roles that HR software bills for — or if AI-native entrants undercut on price because their cost to serve collapses — the pricing model erodes from both ends. The market has already voted: the four listed HR-software peers averaged roughly −23% over the past year. ADP's counter-argument is that its proprietary data on 26+ million employees is exactly the asset AI needs, and 12 million ADP Assist conversations in FY2026 suggest adoption is real. Which force wins is genuinely unresolved, and it is the dominant swing factor in the multiple.
  Industry Commentary
A Cooling US Labour Market Reads Straight Through to Revenue
ONGOING HEADWIND
Most companies have to infer macro conditions. ADP measures them. Its anonymized weekly payroll data covers more than 26 million US private-sector employees, and the National Employment Report it publishes with the Stanford Digital Economy Lab showed private payrolls rising only 44,000 in July 2026 — the smallest gain in six months, with job-stayer pay growth of 4.4% and job-changer pay growth of 7.0%. The internal equivalent, pays per control, grew just 1% in FY2026 and is guided to 0–1% in FY2027. That metric strips out client wins and losses, so it isolates whether ADP's existing clients are hiring — and right now they are barely adding. It also hits PEO from the same direction: average worksite employees grew only 2% to ~762,000. Same-store employment is the one growth input ADP cannot manufacture with product, pricing or sales execution. The next NER prints Sep 2, 2026.
Float Income: A Mechanical Tailwind With a Rate-Path Expiry
EMERGING TAILWIND
ADP holds payroll and tax dollars in transit — $40.4 billion of average client funds balances in FY2026, up 7% — and invests them in a laddered high-grade bond portfolio. That produced $1.355 billion of interest revenue, up 14%, at an average yield of 3.4%, on essentially zero associated cost. FY2027 is guided to $1.540–$1.560 billion on 3–4% balance growth and a yield of ~3.7%. The counterintuitive part: the yield is guided up even as the front end eases, because bonds bought in the 2020–21 zero-rate era are still maturing and being reinvested at far higher coupons. The extended-portfolio strategy contributed a cited net impact of roughly $1.32 billion. But the mechanism is arithmetic, not skill, and it has a finite life. Once the legacy low-coupon paper has fully rolled, portfolio yield converges on prevailing reinvestment rates — and a sustained fall in those rates turns this line from ADP's most reliable earnings tailwind into a multi-year headwind. Balances themselves also shrink if employment and wages soften, which correlates the two risks in this section.
AI in HR Software: Does It Kill the Seat or Feed the Moat?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the question the whole sector's valuation now hinges on, and the market has already priced an answer. Over the past year Workday −29.4%, Paycom −27.9%, Paylocity −22.3%, Paychex −12.8%, versus a NASDAQ-100 at +24.7%. HCM software is sold per employee per month; if agentic AI genuinely shrinks administrative and support headcount, the billable unit shrinks with it — and AI-native entrants with radically lower cost-to-serve can price beneath incumbents (Gusto says AI now writes half its new code and handles an equal share of support cases).

Bull framing: ADP is the data layer, not the seat. Payroll records for 26+ million US workers, 140+ countries of tax logic and 76 years of compliance history are precisely the proprietary substrate that general-purpose models cannot synthesise. Adoption is measurable rather than aspirational — 12 million ADP Assist conversations in FY2026, Lyric HCM live clients up 94%, and "The Zone" embedding AI into sales and service across US teams in FY2027 — and it is already showing up as margin: +140 bps adjusted EBIT in Q4, +80 bps for the year. Maria Black's read is that "AI is not eliminating jobs at scale; it's reshaping how work gets done," which, if correct, means the seat count survives and ADP keeps the productivity gain.

Bear framing: incumbents always sound like this early. If AI's cost savings are competed away in price rather than retained as margin, ADP ends up with the same seats, lower revenue per seat and a permanently lower multiple. And note that ADP's own margin expansion is currently being helped by AI-driven internal productivity — a benefit available to every competitor, and to entrants with no legacy service organisation to shrink. The honest position is that this is unresolved, that it explains most of the 37.6% drawdown from the May 2025 peak, and that no quarter of results in the next year will settle it.
