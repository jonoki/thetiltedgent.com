⚑ READ THIS FIRST — THE FISCAL YEAR TRAP
Autodesk's fiscal year ends on January 31, so its fiscal-year labels run roughly a year ahead of the calendar. Fiscal 2026 ended January 31, 2026. Fiscal 2027 — the year the company is guiding to right now — ends January 31, 2027, meaning it is overwhelmingly a calendar 2026 year. The most recent reported quarter, "Q2 FY2027," ended July 31, 2026. This is the single easiest thing to get wrong about Autodesk: a headline that says "fiscal 2027 guidance" is describing the year you are living in, not next year. Every fiscal label on this page is stated with its calendar end date attached.
01 Company Overview

Autodesk makes the design software that architects, civil engineers, construction firms and manufacturers actually do their work in — AutoCAD for drafting, Revit for building information modelling, Fusion for integrated mechanical design and manufacturing, and Autodesk Construction Cloud for jobsite and project delivery. In fiscal 2026 (the year ended January 31, 2026) the company reported $7.21B of revenue, up 17.5% from $6.13B in fiscal 2025 — its fastest growth in at least six years. Trailing-twelve-month revenue now stands at $7.79B (+17.9%) with a gross margin of 92.5%. At a $54.03B market capitalisation Autodesk ranks roughly the 464th most valuable listed company in the world.

The business is now essentially all subscription. That matters because a subscription company's reported revenue is a lagging, smoothed version of what it actually sold — and Autodesk has spent the last three years changing how it sells and bills, which distorts almost every year-over-year comparison a casual reader will reach for. Section 04 and the Industry Commentary below unpack those mechanics in detail, because they are where most write-ups on this company go wrong.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
DWG/RVT FILE FORMAT CONTROL TRAINED-WORKFORCE LOCK-IN DECADES-DEEP DRAWING ARCHIVES EDUCATION PIPELINE PROJECT-LEVEL NETWORK EFFECTS 92.5% GROSS MARGIN 57.6% ROIC

It is easy to assert that Autodesk has a moat and much more useful to say precisely what it is made of, because the mechanism here is unusually concrete. First, the people. An architect or civil engineer does not "use a CAD tool" the way an accountant uses a spreadsheet; they spend five to fifteen years building muscle memory in one specific environment — its keyboard shortcuts, its family/component libraries, its annotation conventions, its quirks. Universities and technical colleges teach AutoCAD and Revit, so every graduating cohort arrives pre-trained on Autodesk. A firm that switches platforms is not buying different software, it is temporarily converting its entire billable staff into trainees. Second, the files. Drawings and models are exchanged between the architect, the structural engineer, the MEP consultant, the general contractor and the sub-trades, and they are exchanged in DWG and RVT. A firm that unilaterally leaves has to keep round-tripping into those formats anyway to work with everybody else, which means it pays the switching cost without escaping the format. Third, the archive. A practice of any age holds decades of drawings it is professionally and often legally obliged to be able to reopen — as-builts, permit sets, litigation records. Migrating that archive to a rival platform is a conversion project with genuine liability attached, and conversion is never perfectly lossless. Put together, the switching cost is not a single fee but three simultaneous ones — retraining, interoperability and archive migration — each of which is individually survivable and which together almost never clear a CFO's hurdle rate. That is why a forty-year-old product line can still post 14% growth, and it is what the 57.6% ROIC and 92.5% gross margin are really measuring.

The honest limit of that argument: the moat protects the seat, not the price and not the adjacency. It says nothing about whether Autodesk can keep raising prices without provoking revolt, and nothing about whether it wins construction management or manufacturing, where the customer has no legacy Autodesk archive to be trapped by.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Price-increase fatigue and customer resentment. Autodesk's growth is disproportionately price and mix rather than seat expansion, and management has explicitly been reducing multi-year discounts to improve realisation. Repeatedly raising the cost of a tool customers cannot easily leave is the textbook way to convert a moat into a grievance, and it is precisely the condition under which a cheaper credible alternative — BricsCAD, or an AI-native entrant — gets evaluated seriously for the first time in twenty years.

2. Seat piracy in emerging markets. Unlicensed Autodesk software has been endemic for decades in parts of Asia, Eastern Europe, Latin America and the Middle East, where a legitimate seat can cost a meaningful fraction of a small firm's annual revenue. This has two effects that pull in opposite directions: it suppresses realised revenue in exactly the geographies with the most construction growth, and it simultaneously entrenches the standard, since pirating firms still train their staff on Autodesk and still exchange DWG files. The cloud and subscription shift is the structural remedy — a login is harder to crack than an installer — and management repeatedly cites emerging-market growth as a driver, but the leakage is real and unquantified in public disclosure.

3. Manufacturing is a share-taking fight, not a defended position. In AECO Autodesk defends; in mechanical CAD it attacks Dassault, Siemens and PTC, each of which enjoys exactly the same trained-workforce and file-format lock-in around CATIA, NX and Creo that Autodesk enjoys around Revit. Fusion wins in small and mid-market shops and in CAM; it has not displaced the incumbents in automotive or aerospace, and there is no obvious reason it will.

4. Governance and disclosure history. In April 2024 Autodesk delayed its Form 10-K to conduct an internal investigation into free cash flow and non-GAAP operating margin practices connected to how multi-year enterprise contracts were billed. The internal review concluded without a restatement and the company changed CFOs; shareholder class actions followed and Starboard Value publicly weighed litigation over the timing of the disclosure. The final disposition of any SEC inquiry and of the related shareholder litigation could not be verified from the sources fetched for this page and is therefore not asserted either way. The episode matters less for its dollar exposure than because it concerned the exact metrics — billings, free cash flow, non-GAAP margin — that this business is valued on.

Notable One-Off Items and Reporting Distortions

This is the part of Autodesk that most write-ups fumble, so take it slowly. Three separate mechanical effects are simultaneously distorting the year-over-year comparisons.

1. The transaction model change. Autodesk historically sold through resellers who bought from Autodesk and billed the end customer themselves. Under the new model, Autodesk bills the end customer directly and the reseller acts as an agent of record, earning a commission rather than a distribution margin. Two things follow. First, the amount invoiced is now the full end-customer price rather than the discounted reseller price, which mechanically inflates reported revenue and billings without a single extra seat being sold. Second, the timing of cash collection shifts, because Autodesk is now collecting from thousands of end customers rather than from a handful of distributors. Management quantified the revenue effect precisely: the new model was a roughly 2 percentage point tailwind to Q2 fiscal 2027 revenue growth and is expected to be about 1.5 percentage points for the full fiscal year, while the effect on billings growth in Q2 was "not significant." So the honest read of the headline 16% revenue growth is roughly 14% underlying, and the gap between 16% revenue growth and 10% billings growth is largely this artefact rather than a demand deterioration.

2. Billings, RPO and deferred revenue. For a subscription business these lead reported revenue and are where you look for the truth. Billings were $1,854M, +10%. Deferred revenue was $4,258M, +11%. Total RPO was $7,433M — but grew only 2%, and unbilled deferred revenue actually fell 8% to $3,175M. That 2% RPO print is the number the bears will quote, and taken alone it looks like the business has stopped booking. It has not, which brings us to the third effect.

3. The multi-year-to-annual billing shift. Autodesk has been deliberately reducing the discounts it offers for multi-year prepaid contracts and winding down multi-year Maintenance-to-Subscription renewals. When a customer signs three years up front, all three years land in RPO on day one; when the same customer signs an annual contract, only one year does. So a policy that improves price realisation and revenue quality mechanically depresses total RPO and unbilled deferred revenue growth for as long as the transition runs. The diagnostic is current RPO — the portion due within twelve months — which grew 12% to $5,245M. Near-term contracted business is compounding at double digits while the long tail shrinks by design. Read the 2% and the 12% together or you will misread the company entirely. The same mechanism makes free cash flow look lumpy year to year — collecting one year of cash instead of three is not weakness, it is a different collection schedule — and it is worth remembering that these are the very metrics the April 2024 internal accounting review concerned.

4. MaintainX dilution. The acquisition closed August 3, 2026 and management stated it was not profitable at acquisition and is a drag on fiscal 2027 operating margin, while adding ~$60M of second-half revenue. Fiscal 2027 margin is therefore being reported net of a deliberate acquisition drag. Separately, stock-based compensation is guided to ~9% of revenue in fiscal 2027, down from ~11% in fiscal 2026 — a real reduction in the gap between GAAP and non-GAAP.

This page uses $7.79B, which is internally consistent with the $7.72 EPS and 33.50 P/E used throughout.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 19.89x forward earnings and a 1.19 PEG for a business growing revenue 16–18% with a 92.5% gross margin, 57.6% ROIC and a 41% non-GAAP operating margin, protected by about as literal a switching cost as exists in software. Management has committed to 41% non-GAAP operating margin by fiscal 2029 while absorbing the MaintainX drag, is taking stock comp from ~11% to ~9% of revenue, and is buying back stock ($901M in the first half). Construction is growing over 20% in a trade with structurally low software penetration. If current RPO growth of 12% is the true signal and the 2% total RPO is the accounting artefact it appears to be, the consensus $315 target is not heroic.

Bear Case

Roughly 1.5–2 points of the headline growth is a billing-model artefact, not demand. Total RPO grew 2% and unbilled deferred revenue fell 8% — if that is not purely the multi-year-discount wind-down, it is the leading indicator turning first. The stock has produced a −9.3% five-year return and is down 17.85% over twelve months; the market has repeatedly declined to pay up for this business, and a low multiple that stays low is a value trap, not an opportunity. The 2024 accounting review concerned exactly the metrics the bull case now depends on, its final regulatory disposition is unverified, and a well-resourced activist concluded the cost structure was 10 points too fat and could not get a board seat to fix it.

01
The RPO signal is real, not an artefact. Management attributes 2% total RPO growth and the 8% decline in unbilled deferred revenue entirely to deliberately reducing multi-year discounts. That explanation is plausible and internally consistent with 12% current RPO growth — but it is also exactly what a company would say if demand for long-duration commitments were genuinely softening. This resolves over the next two or three quarters and is the single most important thing to watch.
02
Governance and disclosure tail risk. The April 2024 internal investigation into free cash flow and non-GAAP margin practices ended without a restatement, but the final status of any SEC inquiry and of the resulting shareholder litigation could not be verified here. For a company valued on non-GAAP margin and free cash flow, unresolved questions about how those figures are constructed carry a valuation discount that is difficult to shake off.
03
Growth is priced, not free — and the moat protects seats, not pricing power forever. Autodesk's growth leans heavily on price realisation and reduced discounting rather than seat expansion, in a customer base that cannot easily leave. That works until it provokes a serious evaluation of alternatives, and AI-native design tooling gives a credible entrant a story to tell for the first time in two decades. Add persistent seat piracy in the highest-growth emerging markets, and the ceiling on realised revenue is lower than the installed base implies.
  Industry Commentary
The subscription-metric fog
ONGOING HEADWIND
Autodesk is running three overlapping transitions at once — reseller-billed to direct billing with the partner as agent of record, multi-year prepay to annual billing, and reduced discounting — and each one moves a different reported metric in a different direction. The result is a quarter in which revenue grew 16%, billings grew 10%, current RPO grew 12%, total RPO grew 2% and unbilled deferred revenue fell 8%. Every one of those numbers is true and they tell four different stories. Until the transitions anniversary, no single headline metric is a reliable read on demand, and the market has been discounting the stock for that opacity: 19.89x forward earnings for 16% growth is what a valuation looks like when investors cannot cleanly verify the trajectory.
Construction's late digitisation
EMERGING TAILWIND
Construction is among the least software-penetrated large industries in the world, and it is the one adjacency where Autodesk's design incumbency is a genuine wedge rather than a legacy. Management reported construction growing over 20% year over year and explicitly attributed it to low technology penetration plus the integration of design-and-make workflows — the general contractor already receives the Revit model, so selling it the coordination, takeoff and cost tools on top is a shorter conversation than a cold start. The MaintainX acquisition extends the same logic past handover into operations. AECO is already 50.3% of revenue and growing 17%; if construction and operations compound faster than the core, the mix shifts toward the fastest-growing and least-penetrated part of the portfolio.
AI: moat amplifier or moat solvent?
BULL VS BEAR FRAMING
The bull framing: Autodesk's advantage is not model quality but proprietary data — decades of real building models, real toolpaths, real project outcomes — and the workflow the output has to land in. CEO Andrew Anagnost put it directly: "the future of AI for the built world won't belong to the company with the best single model." On that view AI raises the value of a seat, and Fusion users being among the most engaged with the AI assistant is evidence it converts to growth. The bear framing: the moat is made of trained humans and proprietary file formats, and both are precisely what AI erodes. If a model can generate a compliant, coordinated model from a natural-language brief and export to any format, the fifteen years of muscle memory and the DWG archive stop being barriers. The resolution is empirical and near-term: watch whether Autodesk sells AI as a priced tier that lifts revenue per seat, or bundles it defensively to protect seats it fears losing. Autodesk University on September 15–17, 2026 is the next place to look.
