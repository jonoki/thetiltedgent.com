01 Company Overview

Adyen is the Amsterdam company that large merchants use to take payments online, in stores and inside apps through one system it built itself. It is also a licensed bank, supervised by De Nederlandsche Bank, which lets it plug straight into the card networks instead of renting that access from another bank. Its customers include Google, Microsoft, Spotify and Uber, and in the first half of 2026 it added OpenAI, Aritzia, Xiaomi and the UK government's GOV.UK Pay. FY2025 net revenue was €2,364.2M, up 18% reported and 21% in constant currency, with EBITDA of €1,245.7M (a 53% margin) and net income of €1,062.5M. The first half of 2026 brought €1,302.9M of net revenue (+19%, +21% in constant currency), €803.8B of payments processed (+24%) and diluted EPS of €17.18, up 12.9% from €15.22.

The business kept growing; the share price did not. The October 2, 2026 close of €859.40 is 37.5% below the €1,375.00 year-end 2025 close and 64.4% below the September 2021 month-end where this report's chart starts. The turn came on February 12, 2026. At its November 2025 Investor Day Adyen had said: "We expect annual net revenue to grow between the low- and mid-twenties in 2026." The February letter guided to 20 to 22% in constant currency, and the shares fell 21.9% that session, their worst day of the past year. The CFO then announced his exit (May 27), the stock hit its 52-week closing low of €785.20 on June 11, and the H1 results on August 13 sent it up 16.4% in a day. By October 2 that gain had gone: €859.40 is below the €910.00 close of the day before the results. At about 24 times trailing earnings, the market is asking one question: can Adyen keep growing near 20% a year as its largest customers move to lower volume-tier prices?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

One global platform, built in-house Own banking licences Payment data across channels Share of wallet compounds over a decade Blue-chip enterprise base Net cash, A- credit rating

Adyen's advantage is architectural. It runs one codebase and holds its own licences, so a merchant can switch on a new country or a store network without a new integration. The H1 2026 letter names three layers: a single global platform, global banking licences, and "Dynamic Identification", the data layer that recognises a shopper across online and in-store payments. The test of a moat is whether customers deepen over time, and Adyen's numbers say they do: Google, Microsoft, Spotify and Uber are still expanding usage after a decade, and share of wallet typically doubles from under 20% to over 40%. The 2026 strategy is to widen the moat beyond the payment itself. In the letter's words, "we are no longer just a payments company", with loyalty (Talon.One), usage-based billing (Orb), agent-driven shopping (Adyen Agentic) and treasury (Intelligent Money Movement) added around the transaction.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Scale lowers the price. Take rate fell to 16.2 bps from 16.8 bps a year earlier. The more a big merchant processes, the lower its per-transaction fee, so Adyen's success with its largest customers turns into slower revenue than volume. Volume +24% against net revenue +19% in H1 2026 is the size of that gap today.
02
Big customers move the numbers. In FY2025 one large-volume customer pulled group volume growth from 21% to 8%. Growth is broadening (300 merchants now drive about 60% of it, down from over 70%), but a handful of global enterprises still decide a large part of each year's result.
03
Short visibility, high bar. In February the company wrote that its "visibility into customer plans, and therefore into our own growth outlook, is most meaningful over a 6 to 12 month horizon." The shares fell 21.9% on the day the 20–22% guide replaced the November 2025 "low- and mid-twenties" aim. A company valued on steady 20% growth that can see only a year ahead is exposed to every guidance update.
04
Margin and capex move the wrong way in 2026. The two acquisitions cost about one point of EBITDA margin this year, and 2026 capex is guided to about 7% of net revenue (5% in FY2025 and in H1 2026) as Adyen pulls data-centre spending forward from 2027. Both are guided as temporary; the 2028 aim of EBITDA margin above 55% is unchanged. Meanwhile, finance is led by an interim CFO until February 2027.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items shape the 2026 figures. First, the acquisitions: Talon.One (preliminary consideration €750M, of which €69.8M paid in Adyen shares) and Orb (US$335M, US$33.2M in shares) closed on July 1, 2026. H1 opex carries €6.0M of transaction costs, and together the deals are expected to add about one point to 2026 net revenue growth and take about one point off the EBITDA margin. Second, capex: Adyen is "proactively pulling investment from 2027 into H2 2026" to secure data-centre capacity and pricing, lifting 2026 capex to about 7% of net revenue from a usual 5%; free cash flow in H2 will carry that. Third, interest income: H1 finance income of €143.2M, mostly from balances at central and commercial banks, equals 19.9% of pre-tax income (€720.0M). A lower-rate world trims net income without touching the payments business; the FY2025 letter noted that net income growth "was hindered by the interest rate environment." Adyen reports no adjusted EPS, so the P/E above uses IFRS diluted earnings.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At €859.40 you pay 24.2 times trailing and 22.3 times this year's consensus earnings for a company growing net revenue 21% in constant currency, with a TTM EBITDA margin of 52%, no borrowings, €4.6B of its own cash and an A- credit rating. The customer data supports durability: share of wallet compounds from under 20% to over 40% over a decade, growth is spreading beyond the top 300 merchants, and Platforms (+37%) and Unified Commerce (+25%) are growing faster than the mature Digital pillar. The 2026 margin and capex dents are self-inflicted and guided as temporary, and 9 of 11 firms in recent rating headlines rate it Buy or Outperform with an average target 61% above the price. If Q3 confirms the 21–23% guide, the gap between the numbers and the price is hard to justify.

Bear Case

The bear case says the decline in the growth rate is structural. Each upgrade in scale with the largest merchants comes at a lower take rate (16.2 bps from 16.8 bps), so volume growth of 24% delivers 19% revenue growth, and the next billion euros of volume is priced lower again. The company says it can see only six to twelve months ahead, it guided below its own November 2025 growth aim three months later, and finance is led by an interim CFO until February 2027. About a fifth of pre-tax profit is interest on cash, which falls with rates. Two acquisitions show that Adyen is now buying growth beyond payments rather than only building it. A multiple of 24 times trailing earnings is not cheap for a company whose growth may settle in the mid-teens.

Top 3 Risks

01
Growth falls short of the 20% story. The investment case rests on about 20% annual net revenue growth for years after 2026. February 2026 showed what a two-point gap between aim and guidance does to the price (−21.9% in a day). The October 28 Q3 update and the February 2027 guide are the next two tests.
02
Take-rate erosion at the top of the customer base. Volume tiering is the cost of winning more of each large merchant's payments. If big customers keep shifting volume to Adyen faster than smaller, higher-priced merchants grow, net revenue keeps lagging volume, as it did in H1 2026 (19% against 24%).

Execution through a leadership and integration year. An interim CFO until February 2027, a new Executive Board structure, two acquisitions to integrate and capex pulled forward from 2027. Each is manageable; together they add risk to the guidance in the year the market trusts it least.
Industry Commentary
Payments stocks in 2026: the sector, not just Adyen
SECTOR DE-RATING

Over the year to October 2, 2026, Worldline fell 67.5%, Fiserv 64.7%, Adyen 41.0%, PayPal 23.4%, Nexi 18.9% and Block 3.2%, while the AEX rose 16.5%. Every listed payment peer in this report's set fell; each has its own story, and together they show a sector being priced down. Adyen's own figures show where scale costs it: in H1 2026 processed volume rose 24% but net revenue 19%, because the largest merchants pay less per transaction as they grow. The H2 2025 letter also expected "market volume growth to remain broadly in line with 2025 levels, reflecting continued macroeconomic uncertainty."

Beyond the transaction: loyalty, billing, agents
STRATEGY SHIFT

Adyen's answer to a maturing payments market is to sell more around each payment. In 2026 it bought Talon.One (loyalty and promotions) and Orb (usage-based billing for software and AI companies), launched Adyen Agentic so merchants can accept payments started by AI shopping agents, and launched Intelligent Money Movement for treasury and payouts. Management's own framing in the H1 release: "we built the complete financial operating system for modern commerce to position us for sustained, durable growth." The deals add about one point of revenue growth in 2026. Whether they add margin later, or turn Adyen into a broader software company with a lower margin, will take several halves to show.

The valuation gap: price vs. the Street
WIDE DISAGREEMENT

The average analyst target of €1,382.43 is 61% above the October 2 close, and the lowest target (€931) is still above it. On our numbers Adyen trades at 24.2 times trailing earnings, about 16.8 times EBITDA after cash and a 4.3% free-cash-flow yield, with 2028 margin ambitions above 55%. The bull framing: a 20%-growth, net-cash, high-margin platform has been de-rated with a sector whose weaker members are shrinking. The bear framing: the sell-side has been slow to mark down targets set when 20%+ growth looked certain, and the price is telling them something. The next two data points, Q3 net revenue on October 28 and the 2027 guide in February, will show which side was right.
