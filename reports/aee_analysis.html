01 Company Overview

Ameren is the electric and gas utility for St. Louis and a large slice of the middle of the country: 2.4 million electric customers and 900,000 natural gas customers across 64,000 square miles of central and eastern Missouri and the southern four-fifths of Illinois. It was born on December 31, 1997 when Union Electric (St. Louis) merged with CIPSCO (Springfield, Illinois), then bolted on CILCORP in 2003 and Illinois Power in 2004; the three Illinois companies were folded into a single Ameren Illinois in October 2010. FY2025 revenue was $8.47B and GAAP EPS $5.35 (adjusted $5.03).

A regulated utility is a simple machine to describe and a hard one to handicap. The company spends money on poles, wires, pipes and power plants; regulators add that spending to the rate base; and customers pay rates set to give shareholders an allowed return on equity on the equity-financed part of it. Earnings growth is therefore rate base growth × allowed ROE × how much of that allowed return actually gets earned. On November 5, 2026 Ameren guided to ~13.3% compound annual rate base growth from 2026 through 2031 on a $38.1B five-year capital plan for 2027–2031, and EPS growth of 8–10% a year from 2027 to 2031 off a $5.80 base (the midpoint of 2027 guidance of $5.70–$5.90); the guide issued in February 2026 was 6–8% a year. The gap between those two numbers — 13.3% versus 8–10% — is the whole story: management attributes it mainly to equity dilution, meaning new shares issued to pay for the build (about $5B of equity from 2027 to 2031, of which about $1.7B is already sold forward, per a MarketBeat summary of the Q3 call), and it is only realised if Missouri and Illinois regulators keep the allowed returns where they are. The new ingredient since 2026 began is data centres: 2.8 GW of signed electric service agreements in Missouri, including Google and Amazon projects.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Legal monopoly territory Regulated return on rate base Formula-rate transmission Callaway nuclear baseload Data-centre ESAs signed 12 years of dividend growth

The moat is the franchise: nobody else is allowed to string wires down a street in Ameren's territory. The price of that monopoly is that someone else sets your return. The moat protects volume and existence; it does not protect the rate, and the rate is set in three different rooms — the MoPSC in Jefferson City, the ICC in Springfield, and FERC in Washington. The spread of allowed returns across Ameren's businesses — 8.715% to 9.60% at the state level, with Missouri asking for 10.25% — is the table's current reading of how friendly each room is.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Illinois is a tough table. The ICC allowed 8.715% on electric distribution against 9.27% requested, and 9.60% on gas against 10.70% requested — and cut the gas ask by 43%. Ameren has appealed the ICC's gas rate order (January 2026, per the Q2 release). Every half a percentage point of allowed ROE on a growing rate base compounds; a regulator trimming asks by a third to a half is the single biggest drag on the earnings algorithm.
02
Affordability politics in Missouri. The $343M rate request lands on a customer base where the Energy and Policy Institute reported 90,025 disconnections between March and November 2024 and 166,000 customers behind on bills. A rate case decided in that climate is a rate case where the requested 10.25% ROE is a starting bid, not a forecast.
03
Data-centre concentration and execution. A plan that assumes 2.9 GW of new load by 2031 depends on a small number of very large customers building on schedule; management has said construction delays at customer sites could push back sales and earnings (Q3 call summary). If a hyperscaler slows or cancels, the generation built for it (the 2.1 GW West Alton plant) still has to be paid for — by someone.
04
The balance sheet is doing the heavy lifting. Debt/equity of 1.57, interest coverage (operating profit over interest cost) of 2.94x, negative free cash flow of $2.00B and about $5B of new equity to come. Utilities are built to run like this, but it makes the equity a rates-sensitive instrument: when bond yields rise, both the cost of the build-out and the multiple investors will pay move against the holder.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 21.2x 2026 consensus earnings ($5.42) for a utility that has just raised 2026 guidance to $5.35–$5.55, guided 8–10% a year EPS growth for 2027–2031, and laid out about 13.3% compound rate base growth to 2031 on $38.1B of investment, an identified pipeline above $90B through 2036, 2.8 GW of signed data-centre agreements with Google and Amazon among the counterparties, and a 2.61% dividend yield growing for 12 straight years. If Missouri grants anything near the current 9.53% and the data-centre load converts, the algorithm compounds at roughly 10.6–12.6% a year including the dividend (our estimate: 8–10% EPS growth plus a 2.61% yield).

Bear Case

The algorithm has three moving parts and the holder controls none of them. Illinois has already set returns of 8.715% and 9.60% and cut asks by a third to nearly half; Missouri is being asked for $343M on a customer base where disconnections have been rising. The build is funded by about $5B of new equity and debt already at 1.57x equity, with free cash flow at −$2.00B, so every rise in interest rates hits the cost of capital and the multiple at once. The shares are now 3.0% below their 52-week high and the lowest analyst target ($104) is below the price. And the growth premium rests on a handful of hyperscaler contracts — concentration that looks like a moat until one customer blinks.

Industry Commentary
Data centres turned utilities into growth stocks
EMERGING TAILWIND

For two decades US electricity demand was roughly flat and utilities grew by replacing old assets. Hyperscale data centres changed the arithmetic: Ameren's 2.8 GW of signed agreements and a plan that assumes 2.9 GW of new load by 2031 would have been unthinkable five years ago. More load spreads fixed grid costs across more kilowatt-hours, which can help affordability — management estimates about $21M of base-rate savings for existing Missouri customers over two years, which it ties to the new large-load customers. The catch is that utilities must build generation before the load shows up.

Affordability is the binding constraint
ONGOING HEADWIND

The build-out has to be paid for through customer bills, and regulators are elected or appointed by people who pay those bills. The ICC's decisions — cutting Ameren's grid spending, trimming the gas request by 43%, ordering a 3% energy-burden cap for low-income customers — show a regulator weighing affordability as heavily as reliability. The more the capital plan grows, the more each rate case becomes a negotiation over who pays.

Utilities are rate-sensitive assets
MACRO SENSITIVITY

AEE's September 2022 drop of 13% in a month is the reminder: regulated utilities trade partly as bonds. Allowed ROEs are reset only in rate cases, often a year or more after market rates move, while the share price reprices instantly. A heavily funded build cycle — negative free cash flow and ongoing equity issuance — makes that sensitivity stronger, not weaker.
