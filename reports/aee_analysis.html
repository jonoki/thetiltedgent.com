01 Company Overview

Ameren is the electric and gas utility for St. Louis and a large slice of the middle of the country: 2.4 million electric customers and 900,000 natural gas customers across 64,000 square miles of central and eastern Missouri and the southern four-fifths of Illinois. It was born on December 31, 1997 when Union Electric (St. Louis) merged with CIPSCO (Springfield, Illinois), then bolted on CILCORP in 2003 and Illinois Power in 2004; the three Illinois companies were folded into a single Ameren Illinois in October 2010. FY2025 revenue was $8.47B and GAAP EPS $5.35 (adjusted $5.03).

A regulated utility is a simple machine to describe and a hard one to handicap. The company spends money on poles, wires, pipes and power plants; regulators add that spending to the rate base; and customers pay rates set to give shareholders an allowed return on equity on the equity-financed part of it. Earnings growth is therefore rate base growth × allowed ROE × how much of that allowed return actually gets earned. Ameren has guided to ~10.6% compound annual rate base growth from 2025 through 2030 on a $31.8B five-year capital plan, and EPS growth of 6–8% a year off a 2026 base of $5.35. The gap between those two numbers — 10.6% versus 6–8% — is the whole story: it is paid for by issuing new shares (about $4B of equity needs over 2026–2030) and it is only realised if Missouri and Illinois regulators keep the allowed returns where they are. The new ingredient in 2026 is data centres: 2.8 GW of signed electric service agreements in Missouri, including Google and Amazon projects.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Legal monopoly territory Regulated return on rate base Formula-rate transmission Callaway nuclear baseload Data-centre ESAs signed 12 years of dividend growth

The moat is the franchise: nobody else is allowed to string wires down a street in Ameren's territory. The price of that monopoly is that someone else sets your return. The moat protects volume and existence; it does not protect the rate, and the rate is set in three different rooms — the MoPSC in Jefferson City, the ICC in Springfield, and FERC in Washington. The spread of allowed returns across Ameren's businesses — 8.715% to 9.60% at the state level, with Missouri asking for 10.25% — is the table's current reading of how friendly each room is.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Illinois is a tough table. The ICC allowed 8.715% on electric distribution against 9.27% requested, and 9.60% on gas against 10.70% requested — and cut the gas ask by 43%. Ameren has appealed the ICC's gas rate order (January 2026, per the Q2 release). Every 50 bps of allowed ROE on a growing rate base compounds; a regulator trimming asks by a third to a half is the single biggest drag on the earnings algorithm.
02
Affordability politics in Missouri. The $343M rate request lands on a customer base where the Energy and Policy Institute reported 90,025 disconnections between March and November 2024 and 166,000 customers behind on bills. A rate case decided in that climate is a rate case where the requested 10.25% ROE is a starting bid, not a forecast.
03
Data-centre concentration and execution. A 60% rise in sales by 2029 depends on a small number of very large customers building on schedule. If a hyperscaler slows or cancels, the generation built for it (the 2.1 GW West Alton plant) still has to be paid for — by someone.
04
The balance sheet is doing the heavy lifting. Debt/equity of 1.58, interest coverage of 2.89x, a current ratio of 0.53, negative free cash flow of $1.45B and ~$4B of new equity to come. Utilities are built to run like this, but it makes the equity a rates-sensitive instrument: when bond yields rise, both the cost of the build-out and the multiple investors will pay move against the holder.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 18.9x 2026 consensus earnings for a utility with ~10.6% compound rate base growth through 2030, a $71B+ investment pipeline through 2035, 2.8 GW of signed data-centre agreements with Google and Amazon among the counterparties, a 2.95% dividend yield growing for 12 straight years, and guidance of 6–8% EPS growth that management says it is tracking near the top end of. The stock is 13.9% below its 52-week high with no change to guidance, and every one of the 16 analysts' targets — the lowest is $108 — sits above the close. If Missouri grants anything near the current 9.53% and the data-centre load converts, the algorithm compounds at roughly 9–11% a year including the dividend (our estimate: 6–8% EPS growth plus a 2.95% yield).

Bear Case

The algorithm has three moving parts and the holder controls none of them. Illinois has already set returns of 8.715% and 9.60% and cut asks by a third to nearly half; Missouri is being asked for $343M on a customer base where disconnections have been rising. The build is funded by ~$4B of new equity and debt already at 1.58x equity, with free cash flow at −$1.45B, so every rise in interest rates hits the cost of capital and the multiple at once. And the growth premium now rests on a handful of hyperscaler contracts — concentration that looks like a moat until one customer blinks.

Top 3 Risks

01
Allowed-ROE outcomes. The Missouri order due by May 2027 on a $16.7B rate base, and continuing ICC scrutiny in Illinois where allowed returns are already the lowest in the group. Allowed ROE × rate base is the earnings line; there is no other lever of that size.
02
Interest rates and dilution. ~$4B of equity through 2030, D/E 1.58, interest coverage 2.89x. Higher rates raise the cost of the build and lower the multiple on a 2.95% yield at the same time.
03
Large-load execution. The 60% sales-growth target to 2029 and the 2.1 GW West Alton plant rest on a few large customers building on time. Delays leave the capital committed and the revenue late.
Industry Commentary
Data centres turned utilities into growth stocks
EMERGING TAILWIND

For two decades US electricity demand was roughly flat and utilities grew by replacing old assets. Hyperscale data centres changed the arithmetic: Ameren's 2.8 GW of signed agreements and a management target of ~60% sales growth by 2029 would have been unthinkable five years ago. More load spreads fixed grid costs across more kilowatt-hours, which can help affordability — management estimates about $21M of base-rate savings for existing Missouri customers over two years, which it ties to the new large-load customers. The catch is that utilities must build generation before the load shows up.

Affordability is the binding constraint
ONGOING HEADWIND

The build-out has to be paid for through customer bills, and regulators are elected or appointed by people who pay those bills. The ICC's decisions — cutting Ameren's grid spending, trimming the gas request by 43%, ordering a 3% energy-burden cap for low-income customers — show a regulator weighing affordability as heavily as reliability. The more the capital plan grows, the more each rate case becomes a negotiation over who pays.

Utilities are rate-sensitive assets
MACRO SENSITIVITY

AEE's September 2022 drop of 13% in a month is the reminder: regulated utilities trade partly as bonds. Allowed ROEs are reset only in rate cases, often a year or more after market rates move, while the share price reprices instantly. A heavily funded build cycle — negative free cash flow and ongoing equity issuance — makes that sensitivity stronger, not weaker.
