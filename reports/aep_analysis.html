01 Company Overview

American Electric Power is one of the largest regulated electric utilities in the United States, operating the country's largest electricity transmission network and serving roughly 5.6 million customers across 11 states — with its economically important footprint concentrated in Ohio, Texas, Indiana, Oklahoma, West Virginia and Virginia. The company generated $21.88B of revenue in FY2025, up 10.9% YoY, with GAAP net income of $3.58B and diluted EPS of $6.66. On a TTM basis through June 2026 revenue reached $22.79B (+10.3%). AEP ranks roughly #378 globally and #260 among US-listed companies by market capitalisation, and is a NASDAQ-100 constituent — Invesco's QQQ trust appears among its top ten institutional holders.

What distinguishes AEP from the typical regulated utility right now is scale of committed future demand. Management has signed 69 GW of contracted incremental load through 2030 — a figure larger than the entire peak demand of most US states — concentrated in the ERCOT (Texas), PJM (Ohio/Appalachia) and SPP (Oklahoma) markets. Against that, AEP is running a $78B capital plan for 2026–2030 with line of sight to more than $10B of additional projects, targeting ~11% annual rate base growth and 7–9% annual operating earnings growth.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Largest US transmission network FERC formula rates Legal service monopolies 11-state / 5.6M customer scale Ohio · Texas · Indiana corridors 69 GW contracted load ~13 GW secured gas turbines $5B DOE loan financing

A regulated utility's moat is granted by statute, not won in a market — AEP holds exclusive franchises to serve its territories, and no competitor can build a parallel distribution system. What makes AEP's moat unusually valuable in 2026 is where those franchises sit and what the company already owns. Owning the largest transmission network in the country matters because transmission is the binding physical constraint on connecting new load: a hyperscaler can build a data centre anywhere, but it can only energise it where high-voltage capacity exists or can be built, and AEP's Ohio, Texas and Indiana corridors are precisely where that capacity is being requested. That position converts into earnings through FERC formula rates, which allow AEP to earn its authorised return on transmission capital with an annual true-up rather than waiting years for a state rate case — structurally the highest-quality earnings stream in the utility sector. A second, harder-to-copy advantage is supply-chain pre-emption: AEP has secured roughly 13 GW of gas turbine capacity through 2031 with options on up to 10 GW more through 2035, in a market where turbine delivery slots are sold out for years. Rivals that have not already reserved iron cannot serve incremental load on a hyperscaler's timeline regardless of what their regulators approve.

Key Vulnerabilities

Regulatory disallowance risk is live, not theoretical. H1 2026 operating earnings required adding back a $31M Pirkey Plant disallowance and a $22M Unified Tracker Mechanism disallowance, alongside a −$35M West Virginia PSC order impact. When a commission decides that spending was imprudent, the capital is simply not recoverable — and AEP is about to deploy $78B. Company-wide trailing earned ROE of 9.2% against authorised ROEs in the 9.25–9.75% range shows the group is already under-earning its allowed return.

Balance-sheet and rate sensitivity. AEP carries $53.53B of total debt against $603M of cash, a debt-to-equity of 1.61, current ratio of 0.50 and interest coverage of only 2.51×. Free cash flow is −$5.89B TTM because $13.58B of capex vastly exceeds $7.69B of operating cash flow. Credit metrics sit at the bottom of the target band (S&P FFO/debt 14.6%, Moody's 14.0% vs a 14–15% target). This is a business that must continuously access debt and equity markets; a sustained rise in long rates raises both its cost of capital and the discount rate applied to its bond-proxy dividend.

Large-load concentration and counterparty risk. Roughly 66% of the 69 GW pipeline is in Texas, and ~90% of the 45 GW ERCOT submission is data centres. If AI capex decelerates, several of these projects will not be built. The mitigations are real — 100% Letters of Agreement, near-$2B of customer cash/collateral in ERCOT, 80–90% minimum demand charges — but they convert a demand risk into a legal-enforcement risk against counterparties whose own economics may have changed. Texas "Batch Zero" results do not arrive until April 2027, so the pipeline stays unconfirmed for another eight months.

Political exposure on who pays for the grid. Large-load tariffs are approved in only 5 of 8 states where filed. The entire investment case rests on data centres bearing the cost of capacity they reserve — AEP cites up to $16B of expected cost offsets for residential customers. If a commission or legislature decides those terms are too generous to the utility or insufficiently protective of households, the tariff structure can be reopened, and the earnings profile of the $78B plan changes materially.

The three variables that actually move a regulated utility's multiple are rate base growth (AEP: ~11% CAGR to 2030), dividend yield (3.02%) and relative P/E versus the sector (21.77× vs a 20.78× utilities-sector weighted average). Notable One-Off Items

AEP's headline GAAP numbers are unusually distorted this year, and the distortions are almost entirely in the prior-year base:

• $480M FERC NOLC benefit (2025, non-recurring). A federal net-operating-loss-carryforward benefit booked in 2025 is the primary reason Q2 GAAP EPS fell from $2.29 to $1.31 and H1 GAAP earnings fell from $2,026M to $1,587M. Q2 GAAP earnings at AEP Transmission Holdco dropped from $578M to $225M on this alone, while operating earnings were essentially flat ($224M → $225M).

• Transmission minority-interest sale (completed June 2025). AEP sold a minority stake in its transmission business to fund the capital plan; the reconciliation shows adjusted average equity reduced by $1.79B. This permanently transfers a share of TransCo earnings to the buyer and is a real, ongoing dilution of the segment — not a one-time accounting item.

• Pirkey Plant disallowance: +$31M added back in H1 2026 (partial disallowance of net book value).

• Unified Tracker Mechanism disallowance: +$22M added back in Q2 2026.

• West Virginia PSC order impact: −$35M in H1 2026 (subsequently the authorised ROE was raised to 9.75% from 9.25% on reconsideration).

• Wholesale customer contract agreements: +$23M and mark-to-market commodity hedging of −$8M (Q2) / +$18M (H1).

Net effect: the TTM EPS of $5.77 understates underlying run-rate earnings power. Management's own 2026 operating EPS guidance of $6.25–$6.55 — raised from $6.15–$6.45 — is the cleaner number, and H1 operating EPS of $3.01 (vs $2.98) is tracking to it despite Q2's $1.36 missing a ~$1.52 consensus.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

AEP owns the largest transmission network in the country and sits directly on the Ohio, Texas and Indiana corridors where data-centre load is being requested — and it has already converted that position into 69 GW of contracted load through 2030, backed by Letters of Agreement, ~$2B of ERCOT customer collateral and tariffs carrying 80–90% minimum demand charges over 12–20 year terms. The resulting $78B capital plan drives ~11% rate base growth to $134B by 2030, with transmission rate base alone going $32B → $55B under high-quality FERC formula rates, and management guides to 7–9% annual operating EPS growth with a >9% CAGR. Crucially the plan is already funded: $3B of equity forwards executed in May 2026 covers anticipated marketed growth equity, supplemented by $5B of DOE loans and a June 2025 transmission minority-interest sale. At 21.77× trailing and 18.97× forward earnings with a 3.02% yield, an investor is paying roughly a sector multiple for materially above-sector growth.

Bear Case

Contracted load is not delivered load. Two-thirds of the 69 GW sits in Texas and does not clear the ERCOT Batch Zero process until April 2027; ~90% of it is data centres, so an AI capex slowdown would test collateral and termination provisions rather than produce revenue. Meanwhile the balance sheet is stretched — $53.53B of debt, 2.51× interest coverage, a 0.50 current ratio and −$5.89B free cash flow — with credit metrics (S&P FFO/debt 14.6%) at the floor of the 14–15% target band, leaving no room for cost overruns on a $78B build. Execution is already imperfect: Q2 operating EPS of $1.36 missed a ~$1.52 consensus, H1 required adding back $53M of regulatory disallowances, and company-wide earned ROE of 9.2% is below authorised. Large-load tariffs are approved in only five of eight states, and 5.77% short interest plus ten Holds out of 23 analysts say a meaningful cohort thinks the story is priced.

Top 3 Risks
01
Large-load conversion risk. The 69 GW pipeline is contractual, not physical. The ERCOT 45 GW does not clear Batch Zero until April 2027, and the annual ramp (7 GW in 2026 rising to 20 GW in 2029) assumes counterparties build on schedule. A hyperscaler capex pause would leave AEP enforcing minimum-demand and termination clauses against distressed counterparties instead of collecting on energised load — a materially worse outcome than the tariff structure implies.
02
Financing and interest-rate risk on a negative-free-cash-flow build. AEP is spending $13.58B of capex against $7.69B of operating cash flow and must fund the gap externally at every point in the cycle. With FFO/debt at 14.0–14.6% versus a 14–15% target and debt-to-capitalisation around 60–63%, a downgrade or a sustained rise in long rates raises the cost of the plan and simultaneously compresses the multiple on a stock with a 0.51 beta whose valuation behaves like duration.
03
Regulatory disallowance and the "who pays" backlash. Commissions in eight states control whether $78B of spending earns a return. AEP already absorbed a $31M Pirkey disallowance, a $22M tracker disallowance and a $35M West Virginia order impact in H1 2026 alone. If residential bills rise faster than the promised $16B of cost offsets materialise, the political case for reopening large-load tariffs — approved in only five of eight states — becomes very strong, very quickly.
  Industry Commentary
Data-centre load growth — and the fight over who pays for the grid
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The defining regulatory question of this cycle is whether hyperscalers pay for the capacity they reserve, or whether the cost lands on households. AEP is at the sharp end: 69 GW of contracted load through 2030 — 45 GW in ERCOT (~90% data centres), 18 GW in PJM, 6 GW in SPP — ramping 7 GW (2026), 10 GW (2027), 17 GW (2028), 20 GW (2029), 15 GW (2030). Its answer is a standardised large-load tariff: minimum demand charges of 80–90% (Ohio 85%, Kentucky 90%, Virginia/Indiana/Oklahoma 80%), contract terms of 12–20 years, termination fees applying after year five, and collateral posting. In Texas the commitments are backed by nearly $2B of customer cash or collateral and are 100% compliant with SB 6, the state's large-load legislation. Management quantifies the ratepayer protection as up to $16B of expected cost offsets for residential customers, supported by fully executed take-or-pay electric service agreements. The evidence it is working: H1 2026 total commercial & industrial sales grew 13% and C&I revenues grew 15%. The evidence it is not settled: the tariff is approved in only five of eight states, with the remainder targeted by year-end 2026.

The transmission capex supercycle
EMERGING TAILWIND

Transmission is where a utility earns its best regulated returns, because FERC formula rates true up annually and remove most of the regulatory lag that plagues state retail rate cases. AEP is allocating $33B — 42% of the $78B plan — to transmission (versus 31% generation, 22% distribution, 5% other), taking transmission rate base from $32B in 2025 to $55B in 2030, a 70% increase at ~11% CAGR, inside a group rate base going $85B → $134B. Management states transmission will supply more than 50% of 2026 operating earnings. Beyond the base plan sit $10B+ of identified opportunities: a $4.2B Piketon (Ohio) transmission project with power flowing in 2029, a $4B Wyoming fuel cell project in service in 2028, and $2B+ of new generation. Roughly $5B of DOE loans plus ~$400M of grants are attached to transmission specifically, worth an estimated $1.4B of customer benefit over the life of the facilities — federal policy directly subsidising AEP's highest-return segment.

Interest rates, leverage and how the bull/bear case resolves
ONGOING HEADWIND

Utilities are long-duration assets financed with long-duration debt, so rates hit them twice — through the cost of the capital plan and through the discount rate applied to the dividend. AEP's five-year chart is a direct illustration: a round trip to nowhere from 2021 to the September 2023 trough while long rates rose, then a doubling as they fell and the load story emerged. Today the company carries $53.53B of debt against $603M of cash, interest coverage of 2.51×, debt-to-capitalisation of ~60–63%, and FFO/debt of 14.6% (S&P) / 14.0% (Moody's) at the low end of its own 14–15% target.

How this resolves. The bull path: ERCOT Batch Zero clears in April 2027, the remaining three state tariffs are approved, load converts to billed revenue, rate base compounds at 11%, operating EPS compounds at >9%, and a stock at 18.97× forward earnings re-rates toward the $144.60 street target with a 3.02% yield paid while waiting — the funding stack is already largely in place, so this does not require heavy incremental dilution. The bear path: AI capex slows or interconnection slips, contracted gigawatts stall in queue, commissions balk at consumer bill increases and reopen tariffs, cost overruns on a $78B plan push FFO/debt below 14%, and a levered, negative-free-cash-flow utility de-rates toward Barclays' $129 floor. The decisive variable is not the size of the pipeline — it is the rate at which contracted gigawatts become energised, billed megawatt-hours, and the first hard evidence on that arrives in 2027.
