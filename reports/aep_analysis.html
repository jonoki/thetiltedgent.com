01 Company Overview

American Electric Power is one of the largest regulated electric utilities in the United States, operating the country's largest electricity transmission network and serving roughly 5.6 million customers across 11 states — with its economically important footprint concentrated in Ohio, Texas, Indiana, Oklahoma, West Virginia and Virginia. The company generated $21.88B of revenue in FY2025, up 10.9% YoY, with GAAP net income of $3.58B and diluted EPS of $6.66. On a TTM basis through September 2026 revenue reached $23.61B (+11.1%). AEP is a member of both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100; its market-cap rank in each is shown in the header.

What distinguishes AEP from the typical regulated utility right now is scale of committed future demand. Management has signed 84 GW of contracted incremental load through 2031, after adding 15 GW in the third quarter of 2026, concentrated in the ERCOT (Texas), PJM (Ohio/Appalachia) and SPP (Oklahoma) markets. On 28 October 2026 AEP lifted its five-year capital plan to $100B for 2027–2031 (from $78B for 2026–2030), targeting growth in its rate base (the assets regulators let it earn a return on) of 13% or more a year, and raised its target for annual operating earnings-per-share growth to 9–11% from 7–9%.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Largest US transmission network FERC formula rates Legal service monopolies 11-state / 5.6M customer scale Ohio · Texas · Indiana corridors 84 GW contracted load by 2031 ~13 GW secured gas turbines $5B DOE loan financing

A regulated utility's moat is granted by statute, not won in a market — AEP holds exclusive franchises to serve its territories, and no competitor can build a parallel distribution system. What makes AEP's moat unusually valuable in 2026 is where those franchises sit and what the company already owns. Owning the largest transmission network in the country matters because transmission is the binding physical constraint on connecting new load: a large cloud-computing company can build a data centre anywhere, but it can only energise it where high-voltage capacity exists or can be built, and AEP's Ohio, Texas and Indiana corridors are precisely where that capacity is being requested. That position converts into earnings through FERC formula rates, which allow AEP to earn its authorised return on transmission capital with an annual true-up rather than waiting years for a state rate case — structurally the highest-quality earnings stream in the utility sector. A second, harder-to-copy advantage is supply-chain pre-emption: AEP had secured 13 GW of turbine capacity through 2031 by the Q2 2026 update, in a market where turbine delivery slots are scarce. Rivals that have not already reserved turbines will struggle to serve new data-centre load on the customer's timeline, whatever their regulators approve.

Key Vulnerabilities

Regulatory disallowance risk is live, not theoretical. H1 2026 operating earnings required adding back a $31M Pirkey Plant disallowance and a $22M Unified Tracker Mechanism disallowance, alongside a −$35M West Virginia PSC order impact. When a commission decides that spending was imprudent, the capital is simply not recoverable — and AEP is about to deploy $100B. Five of AEP's eight regulated companies earned less than the 9.7% blended authorised ROE over the twelve months to 30 September 2026 (Appalachian Power 8.5%, SWEPCO 8.9%, Kentucky Power 7.4%), and the group as a whole earned 9.5% (Q3 2026 earnings slides), so it is already under-earning its allowed return.

Balance-sheet and rate sensitivity. At 30 September 2026 AEP carried $54.42B of debt against $418M of cash, a debt-to-equity of 1.66, current ratio of 0.51 and interest coverage of only 2.54× (nine-month operating income ÷ interest expense, 10-Q). Free cash flow is −$3.98B over the last twelve months: $11.56B of construction spending against $7.58B of operating cash flow, before another $4.19B spent buying generation plants. Credit metrics sit near the bottom of the target band (S&P FFO/debt 14.7%, Moody's 14.8% at 30 September 2026 vs a 14–15% target). This is a business that must continuously access debt and equity markets; a sustained rise in long rates raises both its cost of capital and the rate investors use to value a dividend they treat much like bond interest.

Large-load concentration and counterparty risk. At the Q3 update 46 GW of the 84 GW was in Texas, all of it under Letters of Agreement (a commitment short of a final service contract), and 92% of the Texas commitments came from data centres, including crypto miners (Q3 2026 slides). If spending on AI data centres slows, several of these projects will not be built. The mitigations are real — nearly $2B of customer cash or collateral behind the Texas submission (Q2 2026 slides), 80–90% minimum demand charges — but they convert a demand risk into a legal-enforcement risk against counterparties whose own economics may have changed. On ERCOT's timetable shown at the Q2 update, Texas "Batch Zero" results are due in April 2027, so much of the pipeline stays unconfirmed for about six more months.

Political exposure on who pays for the grid. By the Q3 update data-centre tariffs with protections for existing customers had been secured in six states, up from five of the eight where AEP had filed at the Q2 update. The entire investment case rests on data centres bearing the cost of capacity they reserve — AEP cites up to $21B of fixed-cost offsets for residential customers (up from $16B at Q2). If a commission or legislature decides those terms are too generous to the utility or insufficiently protective of households, the tariff structure can be reopened, and the earnings profile of the $100B plan changes materially.

Notable One-Off Items

AEP's headline GAAP numbers are unusually distorted this year, and the distortions are almost entirely in the prior-year base:

• $480M FERC NOLC benefit (2025, non-recurring). A federal net-operating-loss-carryforward benefit booked in 2025 is the primary reason nine-month GAAP EPS fell from $5.61 to $5.10 while operating EPS rose from $4.78 to $5.24 (release basis). Nine-month GAAP earnings at AEP Transmission Holdco dropped from $1,013M to $653M, while operating earnings were essentially flat ($659M → $653M).

• Transmission minority-interest sale (completed 5 June 2025). AEP sold a 19.9% stake in its Ohio and Indiana Michigan transmission companies to KKR and PSP Investments for $2.82B to help fund the capital plan (AEP release, June 2025); AEP's return-on-equity figures exclude $1.79B of paid-in capital tied to the deal (Q3 2026 slides). This permanently transfers a share of TransCo earnings to the buyers and is a real, ongoing dilution of the segment — not a one-time accounting item.

• Pirkey Plant disallowance: +$31M added back in 2026 (partial disallowance of net book value).

• Unified Tracker Mechanism disallowance: +$22M added back in Q2 2026.

• West Virginia PSC order impact: −$35M in H1 2026 (separately, a February 2026 reconsideration order raised West Virginia's authorised ROE to 9.75% from 9.25%).

• Wholesale customer contract agreements: +$45M in the nine months ($22M in Q3) and mark-to-market commodity hedging of +$14M (Q3) / +$32M (nine months).

Net effect: the TTM EPS of $6.12 still carries one-off items. Management's 2026 operating EPS guidance of $6.25–$6.55 — raised in July from $6.15–$6.45, and now expected at or above the top end — is the cleaner number: nine-month operating EPS is $5.24 (vs $4.78), helped by Q3's $2.23 against a $2.04 Nasdaq consensus.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

AEP owns the largest transmission network in the country and sits directly on the Ohio, Texas and Indiana corridors where data-centre load is being requested — and it has converted that position into 84 GW of contracted load through 2031, backed by Letters of Agreement, nearly $2B of Texas customer cash or collateral and tariffs carrying 80–90% minimum demand charges over 12–20 year terms. The new $100B capital plan drives 13%+ annual rate base growth to $162B by 2031, with transmission rate base approaching $70B under FERC formula rates, and management now targets 9–11% annual operating EPS growth from 2027 to 2031, after a Q3 beat. The funding plan is laid out: about 55% from operating cash flow, $4.7B from shares already sold forward, $5.7B of gradual at-the-market share sales, $2.5B of further marketed equity and $5B of DOE loans. At 21.13× trailing and 18.77× 2027 consensus earnings with a 3.00% yield, an investor pays a modest premium to Duke (18.90× trailing) for materially faster growth.

Bear Case

Contracted load is not delivered load. At the Q3 update 46 GW of the 84 GW sat in Texas awaiting the ERCOT Batch Zero process (results due April 2027), and 92% of the Texas commitments were data centres, so a slowdown in AI spending would test collateral and termination provisions rather than produce revenue. Meanwhile the balance sheet is stretched — $54.42B of debt, 2.54× interest coverage, a 0.51 current ratio and −$3.98B free cash flow — with credit metrics (S&P FFO/debt 14.7%) near the floor of the 14–15% target band while the five-year plan grows to $100B from $78B. Execution has been uneven: Q2 operating EPS missed consensus before Q3 beat, 2026 results required adding back $53M of regulatory disallowances, and most operating companies earn below the 9.7% blended authorised ROE. Large-load tariffs had been secured in six states by the Q3 update, against eight where AEP had filed at Q2, and 4.97% short interest plus eight Holds out of 24 analysts say a meaningful minority thinks the story is priced.

Top 3 Risks
01
Large-load conversion risk. The 84 GW pipeline is contractual, not physical. The 45 GW submitted in Texas is not due to clear Batch Zero until April 2027 (ERCOT's timetable at the Q2 update), and the ramp shown at the Q3 update (17 GW added in 2027, 20 GW in 2028, 22 GW in 2029) assumes counterparties build on schedule. A pause in data-centre building by the big cloud companies would leave AEP enforcing minimum-demand and termination clauses against distressed counterparties instead of collecting on energised load — a materially worse outcome than the tariff structure implies.
02
Financing and interest-rate risk on a negative-free-cash-flow build. Over the last twelve months AEP spent $11.56B on construction (plus $4.19B buying generation plants) against $7.58B of operating cash flow, and it must fund the gap externally at every point in the cycle. With FFO/debt at 14.7–14.8% versus a 14–15% target and debt at about 62% of debt plus equity, a downgrade or a sustained rise in long rates raises the cost of the plan and at the same time lowers the price investors will pay for a stock that trades much like a long-dated bond.
03
Regulatory disallowance and the "who pays" backlash. Commissions in eight states control whether $100B of spending earns a return. AEP already absorbed a $31M Pirkey disallowance, a $22M tracker disallowance and a $35M West Virginia order impact in H1 2026 alone. If residential bills rise faster than the promised $21B of cost offsets materialise, the political case for reopening large-load tariffs — secured in six states by the Q3 update — becomes very strong, very quickly.
  Industry Commentary
Data-centre load growth — and the fight over who pays for the grid
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The defining regulatory question of this cycle is whether the big cloud companies building data centres pay for the capacity they reserve, or whether the cost lands on households. AEP is at the sharp end: 84 GW of contracted load through 2031 after 15 GW of new commitments in Q3 2026. At the Q3 update the 84 GW split 46 GW in ERCOT (92% data centres), 31 GW in PJM and 7 GW in SPP, arriving 17 GW (2027), 20 GW (2028), 22 GW (2029), 17 GW (2030) and 8 GW (2031) (Q3 2026 slides). Its answer is a standardised large-load tariff: minimum demand charges of 80–90% (Ohio 85%, Kentucky 90%, Virginia/Indiana/Oklahoma 80%), contract terms of 12–20 years, termination fees applying after year five, and collateral posting. In Texas the commitments are backed by nearly $2B of customer cash or collateral (Q2 2026 slides) and are 100% compliant with SB 6, the state's large-load legislation. Management quantifies the ratepayer protection as up to $21B of fixed-cost offsets for residential customers (up from $16B), from fully executed take-or-pay electric service agreements. The evidence it is working: H1 2026 total commercial & industrial sales grew 13% and C&I revenues grew 15%. The evidence it is not settled: tariffs had been secured in six states by the Q3 update, against eight where AEP had filed at Q2, with the remainder targeted by year-end 2026.

The transmission building boom
EMERGING TAILWIND

Transmission is where a utility earns its best regulated returns, because FERC formula rates true up annually and remove most of the regulatory lag that plagues state retail rate cases. AEP's new 2027–2031 plan puts about $37B of the $100B into transmission, about $36B into generation and about $22B into distribution (Q3 2026 release), with transmission rate base approaching $70B by 2031 inside a group rate base heading to $162B. Management expects transmission to supply more than 50% of 2027 operating earnings (Q3 2026 slides). Two of the projects AEP listed at the Q2 update among $10B+ of opportunities outside the old $78B plan now sit inside the new one: the $4.2B Piketon (Ohio) transmission project, with power flowing in 2029, and a ~$4B Wyoming fuel cell project backed by Google, due in service by the end of 2028. AEP has also secured $5B of DOE loans for transmission projects and $625M of DOE grants, which it estimates will save $1.6B of interest (Q2 and Q3 2026 slides) — federal policy directly lowering the cost of AEP's highest-return segment.

Interest rates, leverage and how the bull/bear case resolves
ONGOING HEADWIND

Utilities are long-lived assets financed with long-term debt, so interest rates hit them twice — through the cost of the capital plan and through the discount rate applied to the dividend. AEP's five-year chart is a direct illustration: a round trip to nowhere from 2021 to the September 2023 trough while long rates rose, then a near-doubling as they fell and the load story emerged. At 30 September 2026 the company carried $54.42B of debt against $418M of cash, nine-month interest coverage of 2.54×, debt at about 62% of debt plus equity, and FFO/debt of 14.7% (S&P) / 14.8% (Moody's) near the low end of its own 14–15% target.

How this resolves. The bull path: ERCOT Batch Zero clears in April 2027, the remaining state tariffs are approved, load converts to billed revenue, rate base compounds at 13%+, operating EPS grows 9–11% a year, and a stock at 18.77× 2027 consensus earnings climbs toward the $141.80 average target with a 3.00% yield paid while waiting — the funding is largely arranged, with about $2.5B of new marketed equity planned, so dilution stays modest. The bear path: AI spending slows or grid connections slip, contracted gigawatts stall in queue, commissions balk at consumer bill increases and reopen tariffs, cost overruns on a $100B plan push FFO/debt below 14%, and investors pay less for a heavily borrowed utility that spends more cash than it brings in, toward Barclays' $120 target. The decisive variable is not the size of the pipeline — it is the rate at which contracted gigawatts become energised, billed megawatt-hours, and the first hard evidence on that arrives in 2027.
