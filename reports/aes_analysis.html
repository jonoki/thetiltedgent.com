01 Company Overview

AES is a global power company: it builds and runs solar, wind, battery-storage, gas and coal plants in the US and Latin America, sells long-term PPAs to corporate buyers, and owns two regulated US utilities, AES Indiana and AES Ohio (the latter serving about 527,000 customers in western Ohio). Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $13.05B; the company ended Q2 2026 with a 10.9 GW PPA backlog, 3.8 GW of it under construction. In February 2026 CEO Andrés Gluski said AES had "signed agreements for nearly 12 GW of energy with data center customers, 9 GW of these are PPAs directly with hyperscalers."

None of that sets the share price any more. On March 2, 2026 AES agreed to be taken private by a consortium led by Global Infrastructure Partners (part of BlackRock) and EQT, with CalPERS and the Qatar Investment Authority as co-underwriters, for $15.00 a share in cash — about $10.7B of equity and ~$33.4B of enterprise value including assumed debt. The awkward part: the stock had closed at $17.28 on February 27 after reports that a deal was imminent. The price agreed was a 40.3% premium to the 30-day VWAP prior to July 8, 2025 — the last full trading day before the first media report of a possible sale — but a discount to where the market had just been. The shares fell 17.8% to $14.21 on the announcement. Chairman Jay Morse's explanation was unusually blunt for a merger release: "AES has a significant need for capital to support growth beyond 2027," and "in the absence of a transaction with the Consortium, the Company would likely require a plan that includes reduction or elimination of the dividend and/or substantial new equity issuances." In plain terms: a growth plan this capital-hungry, funded from a balance sheet carrying $32.9B of debt, was cheaper to finance privately than publicly. Stockholders approved the deal on June 26, 2026 with 97.92% of votes cast in favour. Since March the stock has not been an investment in a power company; it has been a merger-arbitrage bet on regulators and a calendar.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Contracted PPA backlog (10.9 GW) Hyperscaler relationships Regulated utility franchises Interconnection & land positions Development & storage know-how Tax-credit monetisation scale

The real moat is the queue position. In a grid where new data-center load is waiting years for interconnection, a developer that already holds land, interconnection rights and signed 20-year contracts owns something scarce — which is exactly what an infrastructure fund wants to buy and hold for decades. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: every GW of backlog has to be financed before it earns anything, and AES's own board concluded that the public market would only fund it at the cost of the dividend or heavy dilution.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage. Total debt of $32.94B against a $10.58B equity value; debt-to-equity 2.57, interest coverage 1.70x, current ratio 0.75 (StockAnalysis). This is why the equity was the small, volatile slice of a large capital structure — and why the deal EV (~$33.4B) is roughly three times the equity cheque.
02
Negative free cash flow. Trailing operating cash flow $5.03B, capex $6.75B, free cash flow −$1.72B. The growth plan consumes cash; the dividend is paid out of a business that is a net borrower.
03
Regulatory friction at the utilities. The IURC set AES Indiana's allowed ROE at 9.50% — below the 9.75% in the settlement — and then agreed on September 2 to reconsider the order. Commissioner Ziegner was reported as saying the acquisition "is beyond our jurisdiction," so the rehearing is a rate risk to the business, not obviously a veto over the deal.
04
Headline earnings are noisy. Q2 2026 GAAP EPS was $0.60 versus adjusted EPS of $0.44; the GAAP figure was lifted by favourable energy derivatives and a gain on Fluence shares. The 5.7x trailing P/E is built on that noise.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — and for a stock pinned under a cash offer it measures almost nothing.

From the September 2021 close of $22.83 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $14.83, AES lost 35.0% of its price. On dividend-adjusted closes (Yahoo Finance: $18.66 → $14.83) the total return is about −20.5%. The chart has three acts. A 2022 rally to $28.92 in November 2022, when renewable developers were priced as growth stocks. A two-and-a-half-year slide to $10.00 in April 2025, as higher rates raised the cost of funding a capital-hungry build-out. And then the sale: +25.0% in July 2025, the month of the first media report of a possible deal, a run to $17.28 in February 2026, and the drop to $14.09 in March when the price came in at $15.

Since then the line is flat by construction: six month-end closes from March to August between $14.09 and $14.74, and a 10-month moving average of $14.84. The 6-period monthly RSI printed 78.4 in February 2026 — the highest reading in the series, on the deal-anticipation run — and sits at 55.3 now. The lowest reading was 21.9 in October 2023. Neither end of that range tells you anything about the next move: the only things that can move this stock are the regulators and the calendar.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case (the arbitrage)

Buy at $14.83, collect $15.00 at closing plus any dividends declared on the way. Our illustrative math, assuming $0.176 per quarter continues: close by December 31, 2026 with one more dividend → ~2.33% (~8.4% annualised); by March 31, 2027 with two → ~3.52% (~6.7% annualised); at the June 1, 2027 outside date with three → ~4.71% (~6.8% annualised). The deal is 100% equity-financed with no financing contingency, stockholders approved it overwhelmingly, and HSR, CFIUS and the Ohio regulator have signed off.

Bear Case (the break)

The upside is capped at a few percent; the downside is not. If the deal fails, the stock trades back to a stand-alone value for a company with $32.9B of debt, negative free cash flow and a board on record that it would otherwise likely need to cut or eliminate the dividend and/or issue substantial equity. The last pre-rumour reference point is the 30-day VWAP prior to July 8, 2025, to which the $15 offer was a 40.3% premium — implying roughly $10.69 (our calculation); June 2025 closed at $10.52. Picking up coins in front of that steamroller is only worth it if you are confident about FERC, New York and the foreign approvals.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deal break or delay. Remaining approvals named in the 8-K (FERC, NYPSC, foreign) have not been confirmed as granted in the sources fetched. Delay is the likelier failure mode than a break: it lowers the annualised return without changing the endpoint.
02
Asymmetric payoff. $0.17 of gross spread against a fallback value that is unknowable but plausibly well below $15 — the company's own board described the stand-alone path as one requiring dividend cuts or dilution.
03
Dividend not guaranteed. Dividends are "expected to continue in the ordinary course until the closing, subject to approval by AES' Board." A skipped dividend removes most of the arbitrage return in the longer-closing scenarios.
Industry Commentary
Data-center load and the capital it demands
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

The demand story is real: AES has nearly 12 GW of agreements with data-center customers, 9 GW of them PPAs directly with hyperscalers, and its Texas agreements with Google put new generation next to the load. But contracted demand still has to be built, and building it before it earns is expensive. AES's trailing capex of $6.75B against $5.03B of operating cash flow is the whole problem in two numbers. Infrastructure funds with long-dated capital and no quarterly dividend expectation can carry that gap more cheaply than a listed utility with a 4.7% yield to defend.

Regulators and private owners of utilities
ONGOING SCRUTINY

Both AES utilities come under private ownership through this deal, and both states have attached strings. Ohio's approval bars AES Ohio from putting merger costs in customer rates and freezes rates, staffing and operations "because of the merger". Indiana's commission cut AES Indiana's allowed ROE to 9.50% in June and reopened the case in September, including the record on the acquisition and a Google data-center project. Private owners can afford to be patient with regulators, but they cannot ignore them. The allowed return is what the infrastructure buyers are really paying for.
