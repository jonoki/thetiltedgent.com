01 Company Overview

AES is a global power company: it builds and runs solar, wind, battery-storage, gas and coal plants in the US and Latin America, sells long-term PPAs to corporate buyers, and owns two regulated US utilities, AES Indiana and AES Ohio (the latter serving about 527,000 customers in western Ohio). Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $13.58B; the company ended Q2 2026 with a 10.9 GW PPA backlog, 3.8 GW of it under construction. In February 2026 CEO Andrés Gluski said AES had signed agreements for nearly 12 GW of energy with data-center customers, 9 GW of them PPAs directly with hyperscalers.

The third quarter of 2026 (10-Q filed November 3, 2026): revenue was $3,872M against $3,351M a year earlier (+15.5%), and profit per share under US accounting rules (GAAP) was $0.76 against $0.89. Over nine months revenue was $10,474M against $9,132M and GAAP profit per share $2.04 against $0.81. The Associated Press’s automated earnings snapshot, built on Zacks data, reports net income of $553M and adjusted profit per share (which leaves out one-off items) of $0.87. The 10-Q reached the SEC after the close on November 3; at the next session the shares slipped 0.13%, from $14.92 to $14.90.

None of that sets the share price any more. On March 2, 2026 AES agreed to be taken private by a consortium led by Global Infrastructure Partners (part of BlackRock) and EQT, with CalPERS and the Qatar Investment Authority as co-underwriters, for $15.00 a share in cash — about $10.7B of equity and ~$33.4B of enterprise value including assumed debt. The awkward part: the stock had closed at $17.28 on February 27 after reports that a deal was imminent. The price agreed was a 40.3% premium to the 30-day volume-weighted average price (VWAP, the average price paid per share) prior to July 8, 2025 — the last full trading day before the first media report of a possible sale — but a discount to where the market had just been. The shares fell 17.8% to $14.21 on the announcement. Chairman Jay Morse's explanation was unusually blunt for a merger release: "AES has a significant need for capital to support growth beyond 2027," and "in the absence of a transaction with the Consortium, the Company would likely require a plan that includes reduction or elimination of the dividend and/or substantial new equity issuances." In plain terms: a growth plan this capital-hungry, funded from a balance sheet now carrying $33.3B of debt, was cheaper to finance privately than publicly. Stockholders approved the deal on June 26, 2026 with 97.92% of votes cast in favour. Since March the stock has not been an investment in a power company; it has been a merger-arbitrage bet on regulators and a calendar.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Contracted PPA backlog (10.9 GW at Q2) Hyperscaler relationships Regulated utility franchises Interconnection & land positions Development & storage know-how Tax-credit monetisation scale

The real moat is the queue position. In a grid where new data-center load is waiting years for interconnection, a developer that already holds land, interconnection rights and signed 20-year contracts owns something scarce — which is exactly what an infrastructure fund wants to buy and hold for decades. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: every GW of backlog has to be financed before it earns anything, and AES's own board concluded that the public market would only fund it at the cost of the dividend or heavy dilution.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage. Debt of $33.26B at September 30 (10-Q) against a $10.61B equity value; debt-to-equity 2.43, interest coverage 1.85x, current ratio 0.86 (StockAnalysis). This is why the shares were the small, volatile part of a company funded mostly by debt — and why the deal's enterprise value (~$33.4B) is roughly three times what is being paid for the shares.
02
Negative free cash flow. Trailing operating cash flow $4.94B, capex $6.45B, free cash flow −$1.52B (StockAnalysis, rounded; the 10-Q shows $3,447M and $4,916M for the first nine months of 2026). The growth plan consumes cash; the dividend is paid out of a business that is a net borrower.
03
Regulatory friction at the utilities. The IURC set AES Indiana's allowed ROE at 9.50% — below the 9.75% in the settlement — and then agreed on September 2 to reconsider the order. Commissioner Joby Jerrells said the commission has no power to approve or deny the merger itself, though the merger could affect operations it does regulate (WFYI), so the rehearing is a rate risk to the business, not obviously a veto over the deal.
04
Headline earnings are noisy. Q3 2026 GAAP EPS was $0.76 against adjusted EPS of $0.87 (AP, from Zacks data); Q2’s GAAP $0.60 against adjusted $0.44 had been lifted by favourable energy derivatives and a gain on Fluence shares. The 6.0x trailing P/E is built on that noise.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — and for a stock pinned under a cash offer it measures almost nothing.

From the December 2021 close of $24.30 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $14.87, AES lost 38.8% of its price. On dividend-adjusted closes (Yahoo Finance: $19.74 → $14.87) the total return is about −24.7%. The chart has three acts. A 2022 rally to $28.92 in November 2022, when renewable developers were priced as growth stocks. A two-and-a-half-year slide to $10.00 in April 2025, as higher rates raised the cost of funding a capital-hungry build-out. And then the sale: +25.0% in July 2025, the month of the first media report of a possible deal, a run to $17.28 in February 2026, and the drop to $14.09 in March when the price came in at $15.

The 6-period monthly RSI printed 78.4 in February 2026 — the highest reading in the series, on the deal-anticipation run — and sits at 55.6 now. The lowest reading was 21.8 in October 2023. Neither end of that range tells you anything about the next move: the only things that can move this stock are the regulators and the calendar.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case (the arbitrage)

Buy at $14.87, collect $15.00 at closing plus any dividends declared on the way. Our illustrative math from the November 6 close, assuming $0.176 per quarter continues on AES's usual calendar (the shares went ex-dividend on October 30; the next dates would fall in late January and around May 1) and simple (not compounded) annualising: close by December 31, 2026 with no further dividend → ~0.87% (~5.8% annualised); by March 31, 2027 with one → ~2.06% (~5.2% annualised); at the June 1, 2027 outside date with two → ~3.24% (~5.7% annualised). The buyers have equity financing commitments and the deal is not conditional on financing, stockholders approved it overwhelmingly, and HSR, CFIUS, the Ohio regulator, the European Commission and New York have signed off.

Bear Case (the break)

The upside is capped at a few percent; the downside is not. If the deal fails, the stock trades back to a stand-alone value for a company with $33.3B of debt, negative free cash flow and a board on record that it would otherwise likely need to cut or eliminate the dividend and/or issue substantial equity. The last pre-rumour reference point is the 30-day VWAP prior to July 8, 2025, to which the $15 offer was a 40.3% premium — implying roughly $10.69 (our calculation); June 2025 closed at $10.52. Picking up coins in front of that steamroller is only worth it if you are confident about FERC and the other approvals still open.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deal break or delay. The March 2026 8-K made FERC, New York PSC and certain foreign approvals conditions of closing; the New York and EU steps have since cleared, and the October 28 8-K says closing remains subject to “certain additional regulatory approvals and other customary closing conditions.” Delay carries its own cost: it pushes the payoff further out without changing the $15.00 endpoint.
02
Asymmetric payoff. $0.13 of gross spread against a fallback value that is unknowable but plausibly well below $15 — the company's own board described the stand-alone path as one requiring dividend cuts or dilution.
03
Dividend not guaranteed. Dividends are "expected to continue in the ordinary course until the closing, subject to approval by AES' Board of Directors." A skipped dividend removes most of the arbitrage return in the longer-closing scenarios.
Industry Commentary
Data-center load and the capital it demands
LONG-TERM DEMAND

The demand story is real: AES has nearly 12 GW of agreements with data-center customers, 9 GW of them PPAs directly with hyperscalers, and its Texas agreements with Google put new generation next to the load. But contracted demand still has to be built, and building it before it earns is expensive. AES's trailing capex of $6.45B against $4.94B of operating cash flow is the whole problem in two numbers. Infrastructure funds with long-dated capital and no quarterly dividend expectation can carry that gap more cheaply than a listed utility with a 4.7% yield to defend.

Regulators and private owners of utilities
ONGOING SCRUTINY

Both AES utilities come under private ownership through this deal, and both states have attached strings. Ohio's approval bars AES Ohio from putting merger costs in customer rates or changing rates, staffing and operations because of the merger. Indiana's commission cut AES Indiana's allowed ROE to 9.50% in June and reopened the case in September, including the record on the acquisition and a Google data-center project. Private owners can afford to be patient with regulators, but they cannot ignore them. The allowed return is what the infrastructure buyers are really paying for.
