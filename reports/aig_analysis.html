01 Company Overview

American International Group underwrites commercial, institutional and personal property-casualty insurance globally — property, liability, workers' compensation and cyber, sold largely through brokers in North America and international markets. The restructuring story is now finished, not in progress. AIG separated its life and retirement business into Corebridge Financial and, on May 7, 2026, sold its remaining Corebridge interest for approximately $710 million in proceeds. What is left is a property-casualty (P&C) underwriter organised into exactly three reportable premium segments — North America Commercial, International Commercial and Global Personal — plus a corporate "Other Operations" line that ran an adjusted pre-tax loss of $142 million in Q2 2026. This is a materially smaller company than the pre-2020 conglomerate: revenue was $51.8B in FY2021 and $26.6B in FY2025 (−1.54% year over year), with trailing-twelve-month revenue of $26.74B, down 2.4%.

The company ranks #657 globally by market capitalisation at roughly $40.2B — third-largest in StockAnalysis's "Insurance — Diversified" industry group behind Berkshire Hathaway and Sun Life, and roughly one-third the size of pure P&C peer Chubb ($130.9B). Two threads dominate the current investment case and both are addressed in detail below: whether the underwriting improvement is structural or flattered by favourable reserve development and mild catastrophes, and how much of the improvement in per-share figures is simply a shrinking share count rather than a growing business.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
GLOBAL LICENSING FOOTPRINT BROKER DISTRIBUTION SCALE REINSURANCE PURCHASING POWER LONG-TAIL CLAIMS DATA INSURANCE FLOAT CAPITAL & RATINGS STRENGTH COMPLEX-RISK UNDERWRITING

AIG's durable advantages are structural rather than technological. A licensed presence across a large number of jurisdictions is expensive and slow to replicate, and it is what lets AIG write multinational commercial programmes that a domestic carrier cannot. Scale in the broker channel matters because commercial insurance is intermediated: AIG competes for a place on Aon's, Marsh's and WTW's panels, not for retail attention. Scale also buys reinsurance more cheaply and lets management reshape the risk profile by ceding rather than by shrinking — management's central claim is that reinsurance, exited lines and tighter per-risk limits have produced a structurally better book. Finally, an insurer holds float — premium collected now against claims paid later — which it invests for its own account. AIG's float produced $1,127M of net investment income in Q2 2026 ($908M on the adjusted pre-tax income basis management reports) and $1,839M in the first half. That income arrives whether or not underwriting is good, and it is the reason a combined ratio slightly under 100% can still support a double-digit return on equity.

What the moat does not obviously include is pricing power. AIG's ROE on a trailing GAAP basis is 7.22%, well below the 11.1% core operating ROE management reported for Q2 2026, and the shares change hands at 0.99× book value ($76.93 against book value per share of $77.39 at June 30, 2026). A market that believed the underwriting franchise was structurally superior would not price it at book.

Key Vulnerabilities
A.
Reserve-release dependence in the largest US book. North America Commercial posted a Q2 2026 calendar combined ratio of 84.0% against an accident-year ex-catastrophe ratio of 86.7% — the headline flattered the underlying by 2.7 points. Across the first half, $272M of $325M group favourable prior-year development sat in this one segment. Favourable development is finite by construction: once the redundancy in old accident years is released, it cannot be released again.
B.
Catastrophe concentration in Global Personal. $335M of the group's $592M first-half catastrophe load fell in a segment that is only 25.5% of net premiums written. Q2 2026 catastrophe charges of $210M cost 3.4 loss-ratio points. A single severe Atlantic season or a repeat of a large wildfire event would move the group combined ratio by several points, and the peak of the season falls immediately after this snapshot.
C.
Shrinking absolute earnings base. Revenue has fallen every year since FY2021 — $51.8B, $29.7B, $27.8B, $27.0B, $26.6B — and TTM revenue is down another 2.4%. Q2 2026 net income attributable to AIG was $948M against $1,144M a year earlier, a 17% decline; per-share it fell only 10%. The buyback is doing real work, but a company cannot repurchase its way to growth indefinitely.
D.
Leadership transition at the moment of proof. Eric Andersen, a broker executive rather than a career underwriter, took the CEO seat after June 1, 2026, with Peter Zaffino — the architect of the turnaround — moving to Executive Chair. The new management team inherits a book whose durability has not yet been tested through a hard loss year, and the market reaction in January 2026 was unkind: the stock fell from $85.55 at end-December to $74.88 at end-January.

Notable One-Off Items

Three distortions matter when reading AIG's history, and all three are large.

1. The FY2022 revenue cliff. Revenue fell from $51,808M in FY2021 to $29,721M in FY2022, −42.63%. This coincides with the separation of the life and retirement business into Corebridge Financial and its deconsolidation from AIG's accounts. Comparisons that span 2021 and 2022 are comparing two different companies.

2. The FY2024 GAAP loss. AIG reported a net loss of $1,426M (−$2.17 per diluted share) in FY2024 despite operating income of $4,228M. The gap is a non-operating item connected to the divestiture period; the specific driver was not identified in the documents retrieved for this build, and should be confirmed in the FY2024 10-K before any conclusion is drawn from it. FY2025 net income returned to $3,096M ($5.43 per share).

3. The final Corebridge exit. On May 7, 2026 AIG sold its remaining Corebridge interest for approximately $710M. This closes the restructuring; from here, comparisons should be clean.

Prior-year reserve development is not a one-off in the accounting sense — it recurs every quarter — but at $325M in the first half of 2026, roughly 22% of underwriting income, it is a recurring item of non-recurring character. It should be mentally stripped out of any run-rate.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The restructuring is finished — the final Corebridge stake went in May 2026 — and what remains is a focused P&C underwriter growing premium 9% with a Q2 combined ratio of 89.0% and an accident-year ex-cat ratio of 88.1%, both improving. Float threw off $1,839M of net investment income in the first half. Management has retired 59 million shares — 10% of the count — with $4.7B of repurchases over five quarters, and the dividend rose 11% to $0.50 quarterly with a 36.76% payout ratio. All of that is available at 0.99× book value and 9.17× forward earnings, with an average analyst target of $88.50 (+15.0%) and not one published target below the current price.

Bear Case

The improvement is thinner than the headline. The accident-year ex-cat ratio — the number that removes both catastrophes and reserve releases — improved only 30 basis points year over year in Q2 2026, and was flat in Q3 2025. Meanwhile $325M of favourable prior-year development, ~22% of first-half underwriting income, flattered results, and $272M of it came from one segment whose calendar combined ratio (84.0%) beat its underlying ratio (86.7%). Absolute earnings are falling: Q2 net income was $948M against $1,144M, −17%, while per-share fell only 10%. Revenue has declined four straight years. Trailing ROE is 7.22%. At 0.99× book the market is not paying for a franchise — it is paying for net assets, and it may be right.

Top 3 Risks
01
Reserve development normalises or reverses. Favourable development of $325M in 1H26 and $205M in Q3 2025 alone is doing heavy lifting. A hard catastrophe year. The recent run has been benign by historical standards — $210M in Q2 2026 (3.4 loss-ratio points), $180M in Q1 2026 (3.0 points) against $525M and 9.1 points in Q1 2025. Global Personal carries $335M of the $592M first-half load on a quarter of the premium base. A single severe season would erase the entire 30bps of underlying improvement several times over.
03
Buyback arithmetic runs out of road. Per-share figures have been rising while absolute figures fall — the share count is down 11.07% year over year. That is genuine value creation while the stock trades below book, but the effect shrinks as the base shrinks, and it stops helping altogether if the shares re-rate above book. Underneath it, consolidated revenue has fallen every year since FY2021 and trailing ROIC is 7.66%.
  Industry Commentary
Commercial pricing after the hard market
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
AIG is growing net premiums written 9% while consolidated revenue falls 2.4% — growth is coming from exposure and retention, not from the headline rate environment that carried the sector through 2023–24. Q1 2026's 36% constant-dollar growth in North America Commercial against Q2's 9% shows how uneven that is. The unavoidable tension: an underwriter that has deliberately tightened limits and ceded more to reinsurers has fewer levers to grow when pricing softens, and management's structural-improvement claim is precisely a claim about writing less of the wrong business.
Catastrophe load and reinsurance economics
ONGOING HEADWIND
AIG's catastrophe load has been light: 3.4 loss-ratio points in Q2 2026, 3.0 in Q1 2026, versus 9.1 points in Q1 2025. Management attributes the reduction to reinsurance structure and tighter limits rather than luck. The problem is that reinsurance is bought at a price, and a book protected by heavy cession keeps less of the upside in a quiet year. Global Personal's $335M share of the $592M first-half load shows where the retained exposure still sits. Nothing in the data retrieved for this build separates "better structure" from "kinder weather" — only a genuinely severe season will.
Higher-for-longer rates and the value of float
EMERGING TAILWIND
Net investment income of $1,127M in Q2 2026 and $1,839M in the first half — with Q3 2025 adjusted NII "over $1.0B", up 15% year over year — is the quiet engine. Float income scales with yields, not with underwriting skill, and it arrives every quarter. Bull framing: at 0.99× book with a 2.60% dividend, a 36.76% payout and an 11.07% annual reduction in share count, an investor is buying a levered claim on rising float income and does not need underwriting heroics. Bear framing: if the earnings are coming from the bond portfolio rather than the underwriting book, then AIG is a bond fund with a 7.22% ROE and a catastrophe tail — and book value, not earnings, is the right ceiling on what to pay.
