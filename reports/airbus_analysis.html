01 Company Overview

Airbus builds passenger jets, helicopters, military aircraft and satellites. It was formed in 2000 as EADS from the merger of France's Aerospatiale Matra, Germany's DASA and Spain's CASA, and the three governments still hold stakes through their holding companies. Its main rival in large jets is Boeing. In FY2025 Airbus delivered 793 commercial aircraft and booked revenue of €73.42B, up 6%, with EBIT Adjusted of €7.13B and reported EPS of €6.61. Roughly a fifth of the business is defence: €14.24B of FY2025 revenue, 19.4% of the total.

The story here is not demand; it is the factory. At June 30, 2026 Airbus had 9,222 commercial aircraft on order and not yet delivered — 11.6 years of its 2025 output (our calculation). Airlines want more planes than Airbus can build, and the binding constraint is the supply chain: engines above all. The FY2025 release put it in writing — "Pratt & Whitney's failure to commit to the number of engines ordered by Airbus is negatively impacting this year's guidance and the ramp-up trajectory." So 2026 is a ramp year: management targets around 870 deliveries, EBIT Adjusted of around €7.5B and free cash flow before customer financing of around €4.5B. First-half results on July 29, 2026 left all three targets unchanged: revenue rose 12% to €33.18B, EBIT Adjusted 24% to €2.73B and reported EPS 47% to €2.84, and the second quarter beat the analyst consensus Airbus itself compiles. On July 21, 2026 it went further with a mid-term target of €12–13B of EBIT Adjusted in 2029 and a €5B share buyback over three years. At €189.30 the market pays 25.2x trailing earnings for that path. The question for the stock is execution: can Airbus turn a full order book into delivered aircraft, and cash, on schedule?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

9,222-aircraft backlog Duopoly in large jets Certification & safety record A321 single-aisle franchise Airline fleet commonality European defence anchor Net cash €8.4B

A new jet programme costs billions and takes years to certify, and an airline that flies one family of aircraft trains its pilots and stocks its spare parts around it. That is why the large-jet market has had so few sellers for so long. Airbus's backlog is the proof: at June 30, 2026 it held 9,222 aircraft, and in H1 2026 Airbus booked 886 gross orders while delivering 351. The weakness is that a moat around demand does nothing for supply. Airbus can't sell planes it can't build, and building them depends on engines, cabin parts and aerostructures from a supply chain it only partly controls.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Engines set the pace. The FY2025 release blamed Pratt & Whitney's failure to commit to the engines Airbus ordered for both the 2026 guidance and a slower A320 ramp: rate 70–75 by the end of 2027 rather than earlier. On the H1 call Faury said Airbus had no finished aircraft waiting for engines but no spare engines either, and that the dispute with Pratt & Whitney is still being negotiated. One supplier's shortfall can cap the whole company's output.
02
The euro–dollar rate. Revenue is mostly in dollars and much of the cost base is in euros; Airbus says about 60% of its dollar revenue is naturally hedged by dollar purchasing (H1 2026 presentation). The hedge book ($74.3B at $1.22) smooths this for years, but the 2029 mid-term target itself assumes €1 = $1.22. The ECB reference rate on October 2, 2026 was $1.1225 per euro.
03
Cash arrives late. Inventory rose €6.94B in six months to €48.62B, and H1 free cash flow before customer financing was −€1.17B. Meeting the ~€4.5B full-year guide implies about €5.7B of cash in the second half (our calculation). If deliveries slip into 2027, so does the cash.
04
Integration and tariffs. The former Spirit AeroSystems work packages that Airbus took over cost €188M of adjustments in FY2025 and another €123M in H1 2026. The 2026 guidance includes the impact of currently applicable tariffs, and assumes no further disruption to trade, air traffic or the supply chain.

Notable One-Off Items

Airbus's "Adjustments" bridge reported EBIT to EBIT Adjusted, and they move around. In FY2025 they were −€1,046M: −€624M of dollar working-capital mismatch and balance-sheet revaluation, −€188M for the Spirit AeroSystems work packages, −€105M for the Defence and Space workforce plan, −€73M on the A400M and −€56M of other items. In H1 2026 they netted to just +€18M, as a +€124M dollar effect and a +€60M provision release offset −€123M of Spirit integration costs and −€43M of other items. The bigger gap between reported and adjusted EPS sits below the operating line: adjusted net income also excludes the other financial result, €181M in H1 2026, which mainly reflects revaluations of equity investments and Airbus Ventures funds and does not recur on a schedule. Adjusted income before tax was €2,732M against €2,931M reported (H1 2026 presentation). That is why H1 reported EPS (€2.84) sits above adjusted EPS (€2.61), and why the trailing P/E reads lower than the forward one.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Demand is not the question. Airbus has 9,222 jets on order, 11.6 years of 2025 output, and in H1 2026 booked 886 gross orders while delivering 351. Defence and Space has turned from −€566M of EBIT Adjusted in 2024 to +€798M in 2025, and its order intake nearly doubled in H1 2026. The second quarter beat the company-compiled consensus by 11% on EBIT Adjusted and by 34% on free cash flow before customer financing. Management has put numbers on the next leg: €12–13B of EBIT Adjusted in 2029 against €7.13B in 2025, cash conversion around 1, and a €5B buyback on top of a 1.7% dividend. The balance sheet carries €8.4B of net cash. If the A320 reaches rate 75 and the A350 reaches 12, earnings growth does the work: on the FY2027 consensus the stock is already at 21.7x, and 18 of 24 analysts rate it a Buy with an average target 22% above the price.

Bear Case

A backlog is a promise of work, not of profit, and Airbus's profit depends on suppliers it doesn't control. The 2026 guidance already absorbed a Pratt & Whitney engine shortfall, the A320 ramp was pushed out to the end of 2027, and 395 of this year's ~870 deliveries — 45% — still had to be made in the last four months as of August 31; even the bottom of the ±20 band management allows, 850, needs 375. The cash flow is back-loaded too: −€1.17B in H1 against ~€4.5B for the year. At 25.2x trailing and 26.0x forward earnings the stock leaves little room for a slipped quarter. The 2029 target assumes €1 = $1.22, against $1.12 on October 2. The stock is 14.5% below its January high, and a beat-and-hold in July was met with a 2.8% fall: the market wants delivered aircraft, not promises.

Top 3 Risks

01
Supply chain and engines. Airbus's own release blames Pratt & Whitney for a slower A320 ramp and a lower 2026 guide. Any further shortfall — engines, cabins, or the former Spirit AeroSystems work packages still being integrated — caps deliveries, and deliveries are revenue.
02
A back-loaded year. 475 deliveries through August against a ~870 target leaves 395 for September–December, roughly 99 a month after 59 a month so far. Missing it would hit EBIT and the ~€4.5B cash guide together, at a 25x multiple.
03
Currency and trade. Revenue is mostly in dollars and much of the cost base is in euros, with about 60% of dollar revenue matched by dollar purchasing. The $74.3B hedge book at $1.22 protects the next few years, but the 2029 target is built on the same $1.22, and the euro traded at $1.1225 on October 2. The 2026 guidance assumes current tariffs hold and no new trade disruption.
Industry Commentary
A sold-out industry that can't build fast enough
BINDING CONSTRAINT

The commercial aircraft business in 2026 is a queue. Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025, still below the 863 of 2019, the best year in its 2016–2025 delivery tables, and is targeting about 870 this year. Its backlog grew in H1 2026 even as deliveries rose 15%. On the H1 call Faury said passenger traffic had dipped slightly on the Middle East conflict and higher oil prices, and that Airbus had seen no order cancellations or deferral requests as a result. The bottleneck is upstream: engines, cabins and aerostructures. Airbus has taken over former Spirit AeroSystems work packages and is paying integration costs (€123M of Adjustments in H1 2026). For investors, that turns the usual cyclical question on its head. The risk is not that airlines stop ordering; it is that suppliers can't keep pace with the orders already placed.

European defence spending reaches the order book
EMERGING TAILWIND

Defence and Space order intake reached €9.3B in H1 2026, against €5.1B a year earlier, after a record €17.7B in 2025. Group defence revenue was €14.24B in 2025, 19.4% of the total. On the H1 call management cited the C295 (six orders year to date), an A400M initiative among seven NATO nations, and a Romanian helicopter contract under the European SAFE (Security Action for Europe) initiative. Space work grew in September with Eutelsat's authorisation to proceed on 229 more OneWeb satellites and the start of IRIS², the EU's sovereign satellite network. The European listed defence peers have cooled over the past year — Thales −18.4%, Leonardo −14.9%, BAE −6.7% — but Airbus's defence segment is the one part of the group where margins are rising fastest: H1 EBIT Adjusted rose 84%.

The dollar is a slow-moving risk
STRUCTURAL

Aircraft are priced in dollars worldwide; Airbus's factories are mostly in Europe. To make that workable, Airbus hedges years ahead: $74.3B of cover at June 30, 2026 at an average $1.22 per euro, with $9.5B added in H1 at the same blended rate. The hedges explain why a weaker dollar shows up only gradually, through a "less favourable hedge rate" in the commercial results and a smaller euro value on the €619B order book. They also explain why the 2029 target is set at $1.22. With the euro at $1.1225 on October 2, 2026, the spot rate is kinder to Airbus than the rate in its targets. The risk is a move the other way that lasts long enough to reach the unhedged years.
