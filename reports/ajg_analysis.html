01 Company Overview

Arthur J. Gallagher is an insurance broker, not an insurer. That distinction is the single most important thing to understand before reading another line of this page. Gallagher does not write policies, does not hold reserves against claims, and does not carry underwriting risk — so there is no combined ratio here (the usual insurer scorecard of claims plus costs against premiums), and none of the catastrophe math that drives an insurer's earnings applies. It sells advice and placement: it sits between a client that needs coverage and a carrier that will write it, and it earns a commission or a fee on the premium that changes hands. It also runs a large third-party claims administration business, Gallagher Bassett, under the Risk Management segment. FY2025 revenue before reimbursements was about $13.8B, up 21% from about $11.4B; over the twelve months to September 2026 it is about $16.2B (FY2025, less $10,192M for the first nine months of 2025, plus $12,601M for the first nine months of 2026), and in Q3 alone revenue before reimbursements rose 18.2% while organic growth was 5%. The gap is acquisitions.

The central debate on this page is what an owner is actually buying at 42.8× trailing earnings. Two things are happening at once and they point in opposite directions. On one side, Gallagher closed the $13.45B acquisition of AssuredPartners in August 2025 — the largest deal in its history, nearly a fifth of its own market value today — funded with about $14B raised through a stock offering and senior notes in Q4 2024 and Q1 2025, and it carries $13.49B of total debt. On the other side, the underlying business is slowing for a reason that has little to do with execution: property-casualty insurance pricing has turned, with property renewal premiums down 6% in Q3 2026 (down 10% in Q2). A broker's revenue is a slice of premium. When premium falls, the slice falls with it. At a September investor update management trimmed its 2026 Brokerage organic growth outlook to about 5% from about 5.5%, and on Oct 29 it held that figure. The question is whether the buy-and-integrate machine can outrun the pricing cycle, and what happens to the share price if it can't.

How a broker actually gets paid, plainly. A client — a trucking fleet, a hospital system, a mid-market manufacturer — needs insurance. Gallagher assesses the risk, goes to the carriers, negotiates terms, and places the policy. For that it takes a commission, typically a percentage of the premium, or a negotiated fee. It never touches the claim payment. The consequence is that Gallagher's revenue is a derivative of premium levels, not of loss experience. When the property-casualty market is “hard” — capacity scarce, rates rising — the same client, the same policy, the same work generates more revenue every year. When the market turns “soft,” the reverse happens automatically. In Q3 2026 property renewal premiums fell 6% (a 10% fall in Q2), casualty rose 4% and professional lines 2%; excluding property, average renewal premiums rose 3% (Q3 call, Earnings Whispers transcript). Brokerage organic growth was 5%: base commissions and fees grew about 4% and contingent revenue (bonus commissions from carriers) was flat (Q3 release). Outlook: management cut its 2026 Brokerage organic growth outlook to about 5% from about 5.5% at a September investor update, dropping a fourth-quarter pickup that had been implied at roughly 7% in July (Cantor Fitzgerald note reported Sep 24; Earnings Whispers). On the Q3 call it kept about 5% for both Q4 and the full year. From Q4 the organic figure also includes AssuredPartners, which the CFO put at a 20–25 basis point drag. The CFO said benefits came in a bit below the September forecast, mostly because of a contingent commission that was missed and the timing of several consulting projects, which the CEO said clients pushed out while focused on rising medical and prescription costs. The Q4 outlook has growth in the two international regions slowing to about 3% from 9% and 6% in Q3. An analyst on the call put AssuredPartners' organic growth at about 3.5%, down from about 4% a quarter earlier; the CFO called that a small difference from business mix, with no systemic shift. AssuredPartners contributed $285M of adjusted EBITDAC in the quarter. Margin: Brokerage adjusted EBITDAC margin (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and changes in acquisition earnouts, as a share of revenue) rose to 35.3% from 33.3%; on management’s breakdown, underlying expansion was 60 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) and AssuredPartners added about 310, of which about 90 were synergies, the savings and other gains from combining the businesses (call transcript).

Announced December 2024 and closed August 18, 2025, this is the deal that matters. Orlando-based AssuredPartners was the No. 5 independent property-casualty agency in the United States by Insurance Journal's 2024 ranking and 14th globally by AM Best, carrying roughly $2.9 billion of adjusted revenue for the twelve months to September 30, 2024 and about 10,900 employees. Gallagher paid $13.45B in cash, or about $12.45B net of a deferred tax asset — nearly a fifth of its $71.94B market value today, bought in a single transaction. Financing: per the Q3 2026 release, Gallagher raised about $14B through a stock offering and senior notes in Q4 2024 and Q1 2025. The effect shows in the numbers: 256.42M shares outstanding and total debt of $13.49B against $1.21B of cash, for net debt of $12.28B (stockanalysis.com). Set against Q3 adjusted EBITDAC of $1,253M, annualised to about $5.0B, that is net debt of roughly 2.5× (our calculation, $12.28B ÷ $5.01B; not a company-disclosed ratio). The dilution showed in the earnings line: FY2025 diluted EPS fell to $5.74 from $6.50 in FY2024 even though net income rose from $1,463M to $1,494M — more shares, same profit, lower per-share result. Trailing GAAP EPS has since recovered to $6.55, just above the FY2024 level. Integration is the test: the target is ~$160M of annualized synergies by end-2026 and ~$325M by early 2028, which the CFO said is mostly in the expense line and unchanged on the Q3 call; synergies added about 90 basis points to the Brokerage margin in Q3, so they are starting to show.

The trade in one line: Gallagher swapped a clean balance sheet and a bigger share count for a business growing about 3.5% organically, at a profit margin that lifts the segment's average. Whether that was a good trade depends on whether the $325M of synergies lands.

Gallagher Bassett is a TPA (third-party administrator): when a large employer or a self-insured entity has a workers' compensation or liability claim, Bassett investigates, manages and settles it on that client's behalf for a fee. This is the part of Gallagher that is not premium-linked. Its revenue scales with claim volume, headcount covered and contract wins — not with the property-casualty price cycle — which makes it a genuine, if small, counterweight to the softening property market. It is the better-performing segment on both axes: 10% organic growth in Q3 (12% in Q2) against Brokerage's 5%, and an adjusted EBITDAC margin of 22.8%, up from 21.9% a year earlier. Reported revenue rose from $402M to $457M, +13.7%. Management expects about 9% organic growth for the full year and a Q4 and full-year margin “north of 22%” (Q3 call, Earnings Whispers transcript). At 11.6% of revenue it cannot rescue the consolidated growth rate, but it does mean the “broker exposed to soft pricing” story is not the whole company.

A broker holds client premium in trust between collecting it and remitting it to the carrier — fiduciary assets — and earns interest on that money. It is real, recurring, and entirely a function of short-term rates: a rate-cutting cycle takes a bite out of it with no operational cause. Separately, Gallagher raised the AssuredPartners financing well ahead of the August 2025 close and parked the proceeds. The Q3 2026 release puts the interest earned on those proceeds at about $363M in the first nine months of 2025 (about $1.05 per share after tax), and $76M in Q3 2025, both booked in the Brokerage segment. That is a one-time artefact of the deal, not an ongoing earnings stream, and it makes 2026's year-over-year comparisons look worse than the underlying business. The money was spent at the August 18, 2025 closing, so from Q4 2026 the year-earlier quarter no longer carries it. On tax: management points to roughly $3.4B of future tax savings from tax credits and deductible intangible amortisation; the CFO told analysts to model cash taxes at about 10% of EBITDA — a cash-flow advantage that GAAP earnings understate.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
NO UNDERWRITING RISK CLIENT RETENTION / STICKY RENEWALS SERIAL ACQUIRER, DEAL PRICES FALLING FAMILY-FIRM SELLER APPEAL CARRIER PANEL ACCESS GALLAGHER BASSETT DATA SCALE CAPITAL-LIGHT, HIGH FCF CONVERSION

The core advantage is structural and boring, which is usually the good kind. Insurance placement is an annually renewing, relationship-priced service with very high retention: the incumbent broker knows the client's risk profile, holds the claims history, and is the party the client calls at two in the morning. Switching brokers is disruptive and rarely worth it for a few basis points of commission. Layered on top is the acquisition machine — Gallagher has bought hundreds of independent agencies over decades. In Q3 2026 it closed five acquisitions with about $102M of estimated annualized revenue, and 19 in the first nine months with about $209M (Q3 release), against about 25 in the same nine months of 2025. The pipeline is 30-plus signed or in-preparation term sheets representing roughly $300M of annualized revenue, down from about $500M a quarter earlier (Earnings Whispers), and the CEO said on the call that deal prices are down and M&A is starting to slow; the CFO put acquisition capacity at more than $12B over two years. A buyer that acquires cheaply while trading at a much higher multiple on its own earnings creates value arithmetically — and Gallagher's pitch to family-owned sellers, that they are joining a 99-year-old family firm rather than a private-equity owner looking to resell, is a real and hard-to-copy sourcing edge. Add the capital-light model — $2.89B of free cash flow on about $16.2B of revenue, with no reserves to fund — and you get the machine.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The commission model cuts both ways. Revenue is a percentage of premium, so a softening property-casualty market is a direct, mechanical headwind regardless of how well the business is run. Property renewal premiums fell 6% in Q3 2026 (10% in Q2), and Brokerage organic growth of 5% is the visible result; management cut its 2026 Brokerage outlook to about 5% in September and still expects about 5% in Q4, against roughly 7% implied in July. Casualty is still firming (+4%), but if the soft market broadens beyond property the organic line compresses further with no offsetting lever.
02
Integration and leverage concentration in one deal. AssuredPartners is not one of Gallagher's usual small purchases; it is nearly a fifth of the company's market value. It sits behind $13.49B of total debt and a larger share count, and it grows organically at about 3.5% — below Gallagher's combined 5%. The synergy targets ($160M by end-2026, $325M by early 2028) are the justification for the price paid, and mostly come from cutting costs rather than selling more.
03
The acquisition engine is slowing. The acquisition pipeline fell to about $300M of annual revenue from $500M in one quarter, and the CEO said M&A is starting to slow. Acquisitions supply most of reported growth (13 of 18 points in Q3); fewer of them lowers the growth rate even if prices fall.
04
Valuation carries no margin for error. At 42.8× trailing earnings against a peer median of 21.1×, AJG is priced at roughly double its own industry. Part of that gap is a genuine artefact — GAAP earnings are depressed by amortisation of acquired intangibles, and the forward multiple of 21.4× on FY2026 consensus adjusted EPS tells a gentler story. But a price that depends on the market accepting the adjusted number can fall quickly if that number disappoints; the stock was a cent short of the Q3 consensus.

Notable one-off items. This is an unusually distorted set of financials and the distortions all run the same direction. In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the December 2021 close of $169.67 to $280.58 on November 9, 2026, AJG returned +65.4% in price terms — a compound annual rate of about 10.9% over 4.9 years, before dividends. The shape matters more than the total. The stock climbed for three and a half years, from $169.67 to a monthly-close peak of $347.44 in May 2025 — a gain of 105% — riding years of rising property-casualty premiums. Then it broke. From that May 2025 peak to the May 2026 close of $201.11, AJG fell 42.1%, an extraordinary drawdown for a 0.46-beta stock that did not miss a payment, lose a segment or restate anything. Over that stretch the property price cycle turned, taking organic growth down with it, and the company had just borrowed and issued shares to buy a business worth nearly a fifth of its own market value. Since May 2026 the stock has recovered +39.5% in six months, with a dip to $228.11 at the September 30 close and a jump to $276.07 by October 30. It is +12.2% over 52 weeks but still 19.2% below its May 2025 peak.

Relative performance. AJG's beta of 0.46 means the stock moves less than half as much as the market on average, and yet it delivered a 42% peak-to-trough drawdown in a period the broad market did not experience one of that scale. Low beta is a statement about average co-movement, not about risks specific to one company — and the AssuredPartners financing plus the pricing cycle were both specific to Gallagher. Against its own industry the market's verdict is clear in the valuations: AJG trades at 42.8× trailing earnings versus 18.3× for Aon, 19.9× for WTW, 22.4× for Brown & Brown and 24.5× for Marsh & McLennan (MRSH). Even allowing for the amortisation distortion, investors are still paying up for Gallagher relative to every listed peer.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A capital-light, no-underwriting-risk business with 0.46 beta, $2.89B of free cash flow and a 99-year-old sourcing advantage is buying agencies at falling prices while its own earnings are valued far higher — value creation by arithmetic, with 30-plus term sheets and more than $12B of capacity behind it. The GAAP numbers understate the business badly: a forward P/E of 21.4× on FY2026 adjusted EPS (19.0× on FY2027) against 42.8× trailing, and cash taxes of about 10% of EBITDA, helped by $3.4B of future tax savings. AssuredPartners is delivering synergies (about 90 basis points of Brokerage margin in Q3), Gallagher Bassett is growing 10% organically with a higher margin, property price declines eased to 6% from 10%, and management's early view of 2027 is similar growth with steady margin gains. Buy the steady grower that fell hard while the pricing cycle explains why.

Bear Case

You are paying 42.8× trailing earnings — double the peer median — for a business whose organic growth is 5% and was guided down in September, whose largest-ever acquisition grows slower than it does (about 3.5%), and whose revenue is mechanically tied to a property-casualty price cycle that is still falling. The balance sheet that used to be a strength now carries $13.49B of debt ($12.28B net), return on invested capital is about 7%, close to the cost of capital, and shareholders were diluted to fund it. The reported profitability rests on adjustments: $406M GAAP versus $782M adjusted in a single quarter. The acquisition pipeline fell 40% in a quarter, from about $500M to about $300M, and the stock missed the Q3 EPS consensus by a cent. Interest on client funds falls with rates. The equity story now depends on synergy delivery and on organic growth stabilising near 5%.

Top 3 Risks

01
The soft market broadens beyond property. Property renewal premiums are still −6%; casualty (+4%) and professional lines (+2%) are what keep average renewal pricing positive. If casualty follows property down, Brokerage organic growth goes below the ~5% guide, with no operational fix available, because the revenue is a percentage of a shrinking number.
02
AssuredPartners synergies underdeliver. The $160M-by-2026 and $325M-by-2028 targets are the justification for $13.45B, the extra shares and $13.49B of debt. A shortfall would not just miss earnings; it would call into question the buy-cheap-and-improve logic that underwrites the whole acquisition strategy, at a moment when return on invested capital is only about 7%.
03
The share price falls if adjusted earnings disappoint. At double the peer P/E and a $253-to-$388 spread in analyst targets, the market is paying for a quality premium that has already survived a 42% drawdown once. Two firms downgraded in the week after the print. That is what a demanding price looks like: the good news has to be close to perfect.
Industry Commentary
Critical Constraint
The property-casualty market has turned soft
After several years of rising property-casualty premiums, property renewal premiums fell 6% in Q3 2026 (10% in Q2). Casualty is still firming (+4%) and professional lines are up 2%, so excluding property, renewals are +3%. For a broker this is not a demand problem — clients still need coverage — it is a price problem: the same policy generates less commission. Management said premium changes do not translate one-for-one into revenue, since the amount of exposure, policy limits and coverage also matter (Q3 call). The direct evidence of the drag is in the number that matters: Brokerage organic growth of 5%, against Risk Management's 10% in a segment that is fee-based and not premium-linked.
Emerging Tailwind
Agency prices are falling in the buyer's favour
On the Q3 call the CEO said multiples are down and acquisition activity is starting to slow; Gallagher's own five Q3 acquisitions brought in about $102M of annualised revenue. The pipeline is 30-plus term sheets worth roughly $300M of annualised revenue against more than $12B of capacity over two years. For a serial acquirer, cheaper agencies partly offset weaker premium growth — but fewer deals also means less acquired growth, and the CEO said a tax-law change could bring sellers back.
Ongoing Headwind — and the bull/bear frame
Rate-sensitive client funds, and a stock priced for perfection
Brokers earn interest on premiums held in trust between collection and remittance — high-margin income that shrinks as short rates fall, with no operational cause and no management lever. The deal-financing interest ($76M in Q3 2025, about $363M in the first nine months of 2025) made 2026 comparisons look worse than the business, and drops out from Q4 2026. The bull frame: a defensive, 0.46-beta business is being handed cheaper agencies to buy while its share price has already fallen and partly recovered; a forward P/E of 21.4× on FY2026 adjusted EPS looks reasonable for steady margin gains. The bear frame: organic growth is decelerating for structural reasons, return on invested capital is about 7%, debt is $13.49B, GAAP earnings are about half the adjusted number, and at 42.8× trailing the market has priced in the synergies before they arrive. Both frames use the same facts. The tiebreaker is Q4 2026, when the comparison base is clean and management's ~5% Brokerage guide gets its test.
