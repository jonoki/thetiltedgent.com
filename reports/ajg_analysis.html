01 Company Overview

Arthur J. Gallagher is an insurance broker, not an insurer. That distinction is the single most important thing to understand before reading another line of this page. Gallagher does not write policies, does not hold reserves against claims, and does not carry underwriting risk — so there is no combined ratio here, and none of the catastrophe math that drives an insurer's earnings applies. It sells advice and placement: it sits between a client that needs coverage and a carrier that will write it, and it earns a commission or a fee on the premium that changes hands. It also runs a large third-party claims administration business, Gallagher Bassett, under the Risk Management segment. FY2025 revenue was $13.01B, up 19.0% from $10.93B; the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $15.11B, up 30.8% — and that acceleration is almost entirely acquired, not organic.

The central debate on this page is what an owner is actually buying at 43.6× trailing earnings. Two things are happening at once and they point in opposite directions. On one side, Gallagher closed the $13.45B acquisition of AssuredPartners on August 18, 2025 — the largest deal in its history, roughly a fifth of its own market value — funded with equity and debt that raised the share count 6.6% year over year and left $14.11B of total debt on the balance sheet. On the other side, the underlying business is decelerating for a reason that has nothing to do with execution: property-casualty pricing has turned, with property renewal premiums down 10% in Q2 2026. A broker's revenue is a slice of premium. When premium falls, the slice falls with it. The question is whether the roll-up machine can out-run the pricing cycle, and what happens to the multiple if it can't.

How a broker actually gets paid, plainly. A client — a trucking fleet, a hospital system, a mid-market manufacturer — needs insurance. Gallagher assesses the risk, goes to the carriers, negotiates terms, and places the policy. For that it takes a commission, typically a percentage of the premium, or a negotiated fee. It never touches the claim payment. The consequence is that Gallagher's revenue is a derivative of premium levels, not of loss experience. When the property-casualty market is "hard" — capacity scarce, rates rising — the same client, the same policy, the same work generates more revenue every year with no extra effort. When the market turns "soft," the reverse happens automatically. That is why organic growth of 5% in Q2 2026 is not a sign of a broken business: property renewal premiums fell 10% in the quarter while casualty firmed 3% and professional lines were up 1%. Excluding property, average renewal premiums rose 3%. Management calls it a "pricing reset after several years of a very strong hard market" and notes clients are redeploying the savings into higher limits — which softens, but does not eliminate, the revenue drag. Within Brokerage, Americas Retail P&C grew 5% organically and Gallagher Re — the reinsurance broking arm — grew 9%, the fastest sub-unit, even as property catastrophe reinsurance pricing moved lower on abundant capacity. The AssuredPartners business contributed $701M of Q2 revenue at a 32% adjusted EBITDAC margin — a higher margin than the segment average — while growing only ~4% organically. Margin profile: this is a people business with high incremental margins; underlying margins expanded 50 basis points in the quarter.

Announced December 2024 and closed August 18, 2025, this is the deal that matters. Orlando-based AssuredPartners was the No. 5 independent property-casualty agency in the United States by Insurance Journal's 2024 ranking and 14th globally by AM Best, carrying roughly $2.9 billion of adjusted revenue for the twelve months to September 30 before the deal. Gallagher paid $13.45B in cash, or about $12.45B net of a deferred tax asset — against its own market capitalisation of $67.34B today, that is roughly a fifth of the company bought in a single transaction. Financing: Gallagher's own closing release and the trade coverage fetched both describe it only as "previously disclosed equity and debt financing" without breaking out the split, so the exact size of the equity offering and the debt issued could not be verified from the sources fetched and is not stated here. What is verifiable from the numbers is the effect: shares outstanding are 256.34M, up 6.59% year over year — stockanalysis.com reports a buyback yield of −6.59%, meaning net issuance, not repurchase — and total debt stands at $14.11B against $1.39B of cash, for net debt of $12.72B. Set against an annualised Q2 adjusted EBITDAC run-rate of roughly $4.8B, that implies leverage in the region of 2.6× (author's computation from the two verified figures, not a company-disclosed ratio). The dilution is visible in the earnings line: FY2025 diluted EPS fell to $5.74 from $6.50 in FY2024 even though net income rose from $1,463M to $1,494M. More shares, same profit, lower per-share result. On the other side of the ledger, integration is running ahead of plan: run-rate synergies were raised to ~$160M by end-2026 and ~$325M by early 2028, and the acquired business runs at a 32% adjusted EBITDAC margin, above the segment average.

The trade in one line: Gallagher swapped a clean balance sheet and 6.6% of its equity for $2.9B of revenue growing ~4% organically, at a margin above its own. Whether that was a good trade depends entirely on whether the $325M of synergies actually lands.

Gallagher Bassett is a TPA (third-party administrator): when a large employer or a self-insured entity has a workers' compensation or liability claim, Bassett investigates, manages and settles it on that client's behalf for a fee. This is the part of Gallagher that is not premium-linked. Its revenue scales with claim volume, headcount covered and contract wins — not with the P&C rate cycle — which makes it a genuine, if small, counterweight to the softening property market. It is currently the better-performing segment on both axes: 12% organic growth against Brokerage's 5%, and margin expansion of 140 basis points to 22.3%, against Brokerage's 50 bp of underlying expansion. Reported revenue rose from $392M to $453M, +15.6%. Full-year 2026 guidance sits at 9% organic for this segment against 5.5% for Brokerage, so management expects the gap to persist but narrow. At 11.5% of revenue it cannot rescue the consolidated growth rate, but it does mean the "broker exposed to soft pricing" story is not the whole company.

A broker holds client premium in trust between collecting it and remitting it to the carrier — fiduciary assets — and earns interest on that float. It is real, recurring, and entirely a function of short-term rates: a rate-cutting cycle takes a bite out of it with no operational cause. Right now that line is tangled with something else. Gallagher raised the AssuredPartners financing well ahead of the August 2025 close and parked the proceeds, earning $144M of interest income in Q2 2025 alone, worth $0.42 per share. That is a one-time artefact of a deal, not an ongoing earnings stream, and it makes every year-over-year comparison through 2026 flatter than the underlying business. CFO Doug Howell said explicitly that this "comparability noise" resolves by Q4 2026, once the financing is fully deployed. On tax: management points to roughly $3.4B of future tax savings from tax credits and deductible intangible amortisation, holding the cash tax rate near 10% of EBITA — a genuine cash-flow advantage that GAAP earnings understate. On clean-energy tax-credit investments specifically: no mention was found in any source fetched for 2026. This item may have run off, but that could not be confirmed either way, so nothing is asserted.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
NO UNDERWRITING RISK CLIENT RETENTION / STICKY RENEWALS SERIAL ACQUIRER AT 9–11× EBITDAC FAMILY-FIRM SELLER APPEAL CARRIER PANEL ACCESS GALLAGHER BASSETT DATA SCALE CAPITAL-LIGHT, HIGH FCF CONVERSION

The core advantage is structural and boring, which is usually the good kind. Insurance placement is an annually renewing, relationship-priced service with very high retention: the incumbent broker knows the client's risk profile, holds the claims history, and is the party the client calls at two in the morning. Switching brokers is disruptive and rarely worth it for a few basis points of commission. Layered on top is the acquisition machine — Gallagher has bought hundreds of independent agencies over decades, and in Q2 2026 alone completed seven tuck-ins adding $63M of annualised revenue at a weighted-average 11.3× EBITDAC, with a pipeline of 30-plus signed or in-preparation term sheets representing roughly $500M of annualised revenue. Crucially, valuations have reset in Gallagher's favour: management said deals at 15–17× EBITDAC are "largely a thing of the past," with recent valuations closer to 9×. A buyer that acquires at 9–11× EBITDAC while trading at a substantially higher multiple on its own earnings creates value arithmetically — and Gallagher's pitch to family-owned sellers, that they are joining a 99-year-old family firm rather than a private-equity flip, is a real and hard-to-copy sourcing edge. Add the capital-light model — $2.29B of free cash flow on $15.11B of revenue, with no reserves to fund — and you get the machine.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The commission model cuts both ways. Revenue is a percentage of premium, so a softening property-casualty market is a direct, mechanical headwind regardless of how well the business is run. Property renewal premiums fell 10% in Q2 2026 and reinsurance catastrophe pricing moved lower again at mid-year on abundant capacity. Brokerage organic growth of 5% is the visible result. If the soft market deepens or broadens beyond property into casualty — which is currently still firming at +3% — the organic line compresses further with no offsetting lever.
02
Integration and leverage concentration in one deal. AssuredPartners is not a tuck-in; it is a fifth of the company. It carries $14.11B of total debt behind it, it diluted shareholders by 6.6%, and it grows organically at ~4% — below Gallagher's own 6%. The synergy targets ($160M by end-2026, $325M by early 2028) are the justification for the price paid. They are also, conveniently, unverifiable from outside until they either show up in margin or don't.
03
Rate sensitivity in fiduciary and interest income. Interest on premiums held in trust is high-margin income that arrives without effort — and leaves the same way. Falling short rates reduce it directly. The $144M of Q2 2025 interest income on parked acquisition financing makes the trajectory harder to read for another two quarters, which is itself a disclosure risk: investors dislike lines they cannot model.
04
Valuation carries no margin for error. At 43.6× trailing earnings against a peer median near 21.5× and the S&P 500 at 26.1×, AJG is priced at roughly double its own industry. Part of that gap is a genuine artefact — GAAP earnings are depressed by amortisation of acquired intangibles, and the forward multiple of 18.6× tells a very different story. But a multiple that depends on the market accepting the adjusted number is a multiple that can compress quickly if the adjusted number disappoints.

Notable one-off items. This is an unusually distorted set of financials and the distortions all run the same direction. First, acquisition accounting: Q2 2026 GAAP net earnings of $324M versus adjusted net earnings of $734M ($2.84 per diluted share) — a gap of more than two to one, driven by amortisation of AssuredPartners intangibles, integration costs and changes in estimated earnout payables. Trailing GAAP P/E, ROE and ROIC are all mechanically depressed by this and should not be read as operating deterioration. Second, the financing interest income: $144M in Q2 2025 alone ($0.42 per share) earned on acquisition proceeds held before the August 2025 close — a prior-year benefit that makes 2026 year-over-year comparisons look worse than the business is. Management says this resolves by Q4 2026. Third, share issuance: shares outstanding rose 6.59% year over year, which is why FY2025 EPS fell to $5.74 from $6.50 despite net income rising. Fourth, seasonality: Q1 is structurally Gallagher's strongest quarter (Q1 2026 revenue $4,630M, net income $822M) and Q4 the weakest (Q4 2025 net income $151M) — comparing sequential quarters here is meaningless. In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $167.67 to $262.69 on September 4, 2026, AJG returned +56.7% in price terms — a compound annual rate of about 9.4% before dividends, which added roughly a further point a year. That is a respectable but unspectacular five years, and the shape matters more than the total. The stock climbed almost uninterrupted for three and a half years, from $167.67 to a monthly-close peak of $347.44 in May 2025 — a gain of 107% — riding the hardest property-casualty market in two decades. Then it broke. From that May 2025 peak to the May 2026 close of $201.11, AJG fell 42.1%, an extraordinary drawdown for a 0.51-beta name that did not miss a payment, lose a segment or restate anything. Two things did it: the property rate cycle turned, taking organic growth down with it, and the market re-underwrote a company that had just levered up and diluted 6.6% to buy a fifth of itself. Since May 2026 the stock has recovered +30.6% in four months. It remains −13.5% over 52 weeks and −24.4% below its May 2025 peak.

Relative performance. A precise total-return comparison against the S&P 500 and the NASDAQ-100 over this exact window could not be verified from the sources fetched and is therefore not stated as a number. What can be said from the data on this page: AJG's beta of 0.51 against the SPY proxy's 1.01 means the stock is structurally half as volatile as the index, and yet it delivered a 42% peak-to-trough drawdown in a period the broad market did not experience one of that scale. Low beta is a statement about average co-movement, not about idiosyncratic risk — and the AssuredPartners financing plus the pricing cycle were both idiosyncratic. Against its own industry, the market's verdict is unambiguous in the multiples: AJG trades at 43.6× trailing earnings versus 17.8× for Aon, 20.6× for WTW, 22.5× for Brown & Brown and 22.7× for Marsh & McLennan (MRSH). Even allowing generously for the amortisation distortion, the market is still paying up for Gallagher relative to every listed peer — after a 13.5% year.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A capital-light, no-underwriting-risk business with 0.51 beta, $2.29B of free cash flow and a 99-year-old sourcing advantage is buying agencies at ~9–11× EBITDAC while its own earnings are capitalised far higher — value creation by arithmetic, with 30-plus term sheets and ~$10B of capacity queued behind it. The GAAP numbers understate the business badly: forward P/E of 18.6× against trailing 43.6×, consensus FY2026 EPS of $13.18 (+23%), and a ~10% cash tax rate on $3.4B of tax attributes. AssuredPartners is integrating ahead of plan with synergies raised to $325M by early 2028 at a 32% margin, Gallagher Bassett is compounding at 12% organic with 140 bp of margin expansion, and the comparability noise clears in Q4 2026. Buy the de-rated compounder while the pricing cycle is the reason it is cheap.

Bear Case

You are paying 43.6× trailing earnings — double the peer median — for a business whose organic growth is 6% and falling, whose largest-ever acquisition grows slower than it does (~4%), and whose revenue is mechanically tied to a property-casualty rate cycle that has just turned down 10%. The balance sheet that used to be a strength now carries $14.11B of debt and $12.72B net, ROIC has fallen to 6.6% — arguably at or below cost of capital — and shareholders were diluted 6.6% to fund it. The reported profitability rests on adjustments: $324M GAAP versus $734M adjusted in a single quarter. Fiduciary and interest income falls with rates. The entire equity story now depends on synergy delivery and an AI margin programme that is, so far, a slide.

Top 3 Risks

01
The soft market broadens beyond property. Property renewal premiums are already −10%; casualty (+3%) and professional lines (+1%) are the only things holding average renewal pricing positive. If casualty follows property down, Brokerage organic growth goes below 5% and the 5.5% full-year guide breaks — with no operational fix available, because the revenue is a percentage of a shrinking number.
02
AssuredPartners synergies underdeliver. The $160M-by-2026 and $325M-by-2028 targets are the justification for $13.45B, 6.6% dilution and $14.11B of debt. A shortfall would not just miss earnings; it would call into question the multiple-arbitrage logic that underwrites the whole roll-up strategy, at a moment when ROIC is already only 6.6%.
03
Multiple compression on any adjusted-earnings disappointment. At double the peer P/E and a $250-to-$388 spread in analyst targets, the market is paying for a quality premium that has already survived a 42% drawdown once. The stock fell on a two-cent EPS beat in July because revenue missed by $50M. That is what a demanding multiple looks like: the good news has to be perfect.
Industry Commentary
Critical Constraint
The property-casualty market has turned soft
After several years of the hardest P&C market in two decades, property renewal premiums fell 10% in Q2 2026 and property catastrophe reinsurance pricing "moved lower again" at the mid-year renewals on abundant capacity. Casualty is still firming (+3%) and professional lines are flat-to-up (+1%), so excluding property, renewals are +3%. For a broker this is not a demand problem — clients still need coverage — it is a price problem: the same policy generates less commission. Management frames it as clients redeploying savings into higher limits, which recaptures some of the lost revenue. The direct evidence of the drag is in the number that matters: Brokerage organic growth of 5%, against Risk Management's 12% in a segment that is fee-based and not premium-linked.
Emerging Tailwind
Acquisition multiples have reset in the buyer's favour
The same cheap-capital era that inflated agency valuations has ended. Management stated on the Q2 call that deals at 15 to 17× EBITDA are "largely a thing of the past," with current valuations closer to 9×; Gallagher's own seven Q2 tuck-ins were done at a weighted-average 11.3× EBITDAC for $63M of annualised revenue. The pipeline is 30-plus term sheets worth roughly $500M of annualised revenue against approximately $10B of deployment capacity over two years. For a serial acquirer, a soft market for agencies is worth more than a hard market for premiums — this is the single most underappreciated offset to the pricing headwind.
Ongoing Headwind — and the bull/bear frame
Rate-sensitive float, and a stock priced for perfection
Brokers earn interest on premiums held in trust between collection and remittance — high-margin income that shrinks as short rates fall, with no operational cause and no management lever. That line is currently unreadable because of the $144M earned in Q2 2025 on parked AssuredPartners financing (worth $0.42 per share), which the CFO says washes out by Q4 2026. The bull frame: a defensive, 0.51-beta compounder is being handed a soft agency market to buy into while its own de-rating has already happened — the forward multiple of 18.6× is not demanding for 23% expected EPS growth. The bear frame: organic growth is decelerating for structural reasons, ROIC has fallen to 6.6%, leverage is at a record, GAAP earnings are less than half the adjusted number, and at 43.6× trailing the market has priced in the synergies before they arrive. Both frames use the same facts. The tiebreaker is Q4 2026, when the noise clears and the combined company reports cleanly for the first time.
