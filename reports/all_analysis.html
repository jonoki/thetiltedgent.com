01 Company Overview

The Allstate Corporation is the largest publicly traded personal lines property-casualty insurer in the United States — meaning it sells insurance to households, principally private passenger auto and homeowners, rather than to businesses. That distinction matters more than it sounds: a personal lines book is millions of small, highly regulated, rate-filed policies whose prices can only be changed with state insurance-department approval, which is why Allstate could not simply re-price its way out of the 2022–23 inflation shock. The company also runs a large Protection Services franchise (extended vehicle and consumer-product warranties, roadside, identity protection) that adds policy count without adding catastrophe risk. FY2025 revenue was $67.69B, up 5.58% year over year, with net income of $10.17B; on a TTM basis revenue is $70.14B (+5.9%) and net income $13.19B. Allstate ranks #393 in the world by market capitalisation per companiesmarketcap.com.

The engine of the company and the source of both its 2022–23 humiliation and its 2025–26 rehabilitation. Severity (the average cost of a claim once it happens) exploded in 2022 as used-car values, replacement parts and body-shop labour repriced, while frequency (how often claims happen per insured car per year) normalised off pandemic lows. Rate filings lag by construction, so the combined ratio blew through 100 and Allstate posted group net losses of −$1.39B in FY2022 and −$0.32B in FY2023. Management's answer was to take rate hard and let policies go: the book shrank on purpose. The live question is whether the lost policies come back. Q2 2026 says they are — auto policies in force are up 2.8% and new business applications reached 2.347 million in the quarter versus 1.478 million in Q2 2023. But note the tell: premium is growing only +1.2% while policy count grows +2.8%. That gap is average premium per policy falling — Allstate is now competing on price to win units back, which is exactly what you would expect after a rate cycle over-shoots. The direct channel has gone from 28% to 34% of new auto business.

A completely different risk shape from auto, and it deserves separate treatment. Homeowners profitability is not driven by severity drift but by the catastrophe load — wildfire, severe convective storm (hail and straight-line wind), hurricane. Those are lumpy, correlated, and increasingly expensive to lay off, because reinsurance repriced sharply after 2023 and attachment points moved up, leaving primary carriers like Allstate retaining more of the first dollar of a mid-sized event. Allstate's response was rate plus underwriting restriction plus rebuilding the reinsurance tower. It has worked: homeowners produced $226 million of underwriting income in Q2 2026, having been a loss-maker, and new business applications rose 46.8% to 411,000 from 280,000 in Q2 2023 — the fastest growth anywhere in the company. Watch the mismatch in the other direction from auto: homeowners premium is up 11.4% against only 2.9% policy growth, so this line is still earning in rate, not buying units. That is the healthier of the two mixes, but it is also the one that a single bad wildfire season can erase.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE CLAIMS NETWORK 90+ YEAR BRAND MULTI-CHANNEL DISTRIBUTION STATE RATE-FILING DEPTH TELEMATICS & PRICING DATA 215.9M POLICY BASE CAPITAL & REINSURANCE ACCESS

Personal lines insurance is not a business with a wide moat so much as one with high, boring barriers. Allstate's real advantages are structural rather than proprietary: a claims-handling and repair-network footprint that fixes cars more cheaply per claim than a subscale carrier can, ninety-plus years of brand recall that lowers customer acquisition cost, an actuarial and regulatory apparatus that can file and defend rate in fifty separate states simultaneously, and decades of loss data — increasingly enriched by telematics — that lets it segment risk finely enough to avoid adverse selection. Distribution is genuinely multi-channel: exclusive agents, independent agents (largely via National General), direct-to-consumer and call centres, with the direct channel now taking 34% of new auto business, up from 28%. That mix matters because it lets Allstate flex acquisition spend toward whichever channel is cheapest per policy. What it does not have is pricing power. Auto insurance is a commodity bought on price by a consumer who can re-shop in ninety seconds, and the two most feared competitors — Progressive and GEICO — have historically run structurally lower expense ratios. Allstate's moat is competent scale, not immunity.

Expense ratio and the cost of buying growth. The expense ratio is where a personal lines carrier's growth ambition becomes visible, because advertising runs straight through it. Allstate has spent $1.068 billion on advertising in 2026 year-to-date, after $2.1 billion in full-year 2025 versus just $638 million in 2023. That is more than a tripling of marketing spend in three years, and it is the single clearest quantification of the "win the policies back" strategy. It is also why the underlying combined ratio has stopped improving: at 79.4 in Q2 2026 it was essentially flat, down only 0.1 points, even as the recorded ratio improved 4.5 points. Loss cost improvement is being handed straight back to the advertising line.

Catastrophe and reinsurance dependence. Homeowners earnings rest on a reinsurance programme that has repriced materially since 2023 and on a wildfire and convective-storm environment that has not been kind. Q2 2026 catastrophe losses were still $1.7 billion even in a year characterised as good, down 13.5%. A single California wildfire season or a bad Gulf hurricane can move a quarter by dollars per share, and the market discounts that by refusing to pay a normal multiple for the earnings.

Regulatory rate lag. The 2022–23 losses were not an underwriting failure so much as a regulatory-timing failure: severity moved faster than departments of insurance would approve rate. That mechanism is unchanged and will fire again in the next inflation shock. Certain states — California in particular — are structurally slow, which is precisely where catastrophe exposure is highest.

Reserve development is a two-way street. Q2 2026's recorded combined ratio benefited from roughly 4.6 points of favourable prior-year re-estimates. Favourable reserve development is genuine cash, but it is also non-repeatable and it flatters the recorded ratio relative to the underlying one. Investors paying for an 86.6 combined ratio should be aware that the underlying figure is 79.4 and the difference between them is catastrophe load net of prior-year releases.

The five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 adjusted close of $113.58 to $260.58 on 28 August 2026, ALL returned +129.4% on price alone — roughly 18% compounded — and that understates total return, since the series is dividend-adjusted and Allstate paid throughout. But the path is the point, not the endpoint. The stock spent two full years going nowhere: it was $113.58 in September 2021 and $105.24 in September 2023, a 7% loss over twenty-four months, while the S&P 500 was recovering from its own 2022 drawdown. Relative performance. Against the S&P 500, ALL was a substantial laggard from late 2021 through mid-2023 — dead money while the index recovered — and a substantial outperformer from October 2023 onward, with the +129.4% five-year price return comfortably ahead of a typical index outcome over the same window. Against its own sector, the picture is more nuanced: the property-casualty group broadly re-rated as rate hardened across the industry, so some of Allstate's move is beta to a good P&C cycle rather than company-specific alpha. What is company-specific is the depth of the hole — ALL fell further in 2022–23 than commercial-lines peers because personal auto is where the severity shock landed, and it has therefore recovered more violently. The low beta of 0.16 is a reminder that this stock's drawdowns are driven by weather and claim costs, not by the equity market.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The margin repair is real and the market is still paying a single-digit forward multiple for it — 9.31× forward, 7.4× FY2026 consensus EPS of $35.25, 2.11× book. Underwriting income rose 56.7% in Q2 2026 with a recorded combined ratio of 86.6, homeowners has turned profitable at $226M, and policies in force are growing again across every line (auto +2.8%, homeowners +2.9%, total 215.9M +3.8%). Meanwhile the company is buying back stock aggressively — $1.0B in Q2 with $2.6B of authorisation and $9.5B (~$37/share) of deployable capital. A business earning a 44.2% adjusted ROE with a 0.16 beta at 9× forward earnings is either mispriced or the market is telling you the earnings are not real.

Bear Case

The earnings are, in part, not real. TTM EPS of $50.09 versus FY2026 consensus of $35.25 is a 30% step down, and the gap is $1.1B of investment gains plus ~4.6 points of favourable prior-year reserve development that cannot repeat. Strip those out and the honest number is the underlying combined ratio of 79.4 — which was flat year on year. Margin improvement has stopped. To grow, Allstate is now spending: $2.1B on advertising in 2025 against $638M in 2023, with auto premium growing only 1.2% against 2.8% policy growth — average premium per policy is falling. That is the classic shape of a carrier giving margin back to buy units. Add one bad wildfire or hurricane season and the 86.6 combined ratio and 2.11× book both look expensive.

  Industry Commentary
Catastrophe economics have permanently reset
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Property catastrophe reinsurance repriced sharply after the 2023 losses and, more importantly, attachment points moved up — reinsurers now sit further from the first dollar of loss, so primary carriers absorb more of the frequency-driven mid-sized events (hail, straight-line wind) that have become the industry's real problem. Allstate still absorbed $1.7 billion of catastrophe losses in Q2 2026 alone, and that was a 13.5% improvement. This is the structural reason personal lines insurers trade at a discount to their reported ROE: the market refuses to capitalise earnings it knows are weather-dependent.

The personal auto growth war has restarted
ONGOING HEADWIND

Every major personal auto carrier fixed its margin in 2023–24 by raising rate and shedding policies. All of them now want those policies back at the same time, and the only lever is price and advertising. Allstate's marketing spend went from $638 million in 2023 to $2.1 billion in 2025, with $1.068 billion already spent in 2026 year-to-date. Shopping activity is elevated because consumers who ate three years of rate increases are re-shopping. The industry outcome of a simultaneous land-grab is predictable: expense ratios rise, average premium falls, and the underlying combined ratio drifts back up from today's 79.4.

Higher-for-longer rates are a genuine tailwind — and the bull/bear fulcrum
EMERGING TAILWIND

An $87.8 billion portfolio reinvesting at higher yields lifted net investment income to $1.0 billion in Q2 2026, up $255 million, with trailing-twelve-month investment income of $3.8 billion versus $2.4 billion in 2022. That is a $1.4 billion annual earnings uplift arriving without any underwriting risk. The bull frames it this way: investment income is durable, growth is returning across every line, and 9.3× forward earnings with a 44.2% adjusted ROE and a 0.16 beta is a mispricing. The bear frames it this way: investment income and reserve releases are exactly the low-quality earnings propping up a peak combined ratio while the underlying ratio has stalled flat and advertising spend triples. Both are looking at the same $260.58 price. The tiebreaker will be Q3's underlying combined ratio read against auto policies in force — margin held and units grown means the bulls were right; units grown and margin given away means KBW's $250 was.
