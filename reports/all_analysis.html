01 Company Overview

The Allstate Corporation is one of the largest publicly traded personal lines property-casualty insurers in the United States, fourth in auto and second in homeowners by 2025 premium — meaning it sells insurance to households, principally private passenger auto and homeowners, rather than to businesses. That distinction matters more than it sounds: a personal lines business is millions of small, highly regulated policies whose prices can only be changed with state insurance-department approval, which is why Allstate could not simply raise prices to escape the 2022–23 inflation shock. The company also runs a large Protection Services franchise (extended vehicle and consumer-product warranties, roadside, identity protection) that adds policy count without adding catastrophe risk. FY2025 revenue was $67.69B, up 5.58% year over year, with net income of $10.17B; on a TTM basis, through the third quarter of 2026, revenue is $70.23B (+5.1%) and net income applicable to common shareholders $11.52B. Allstate ranks #185 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation.

The engine of the company and the source of both its 2022–23 humiliation and its 2025–26 rehabilitation. Severity (the average cost of a claim once it happens) exploded in 2022 as used-car values, replacement parts and body-shop labour repriced, while frequency (how often claims happen per insured car per year) normalised off pandemic lows. Rate filings (the price increases insurers must ask states to approve) take time, so the combined ratio blew through 100 and Allstate posted group net losses of −$1.39B in FY2022 and −$0.32B in FY2023. Management's answer was to raise prices hard and let policies go: the business shrank on purpose. Q3 2026 shows the policies coming back and what it costs to win them: auto policies in force rose 3.4% to 26.2 million and new business grew 6.3%, but earned premium grew only 0.6%, and CFO Chris Lown told the Nov 5 call that average auto premiums fell 2.7% over the last year. The underlying auto combined ratio (excluding catastrophes and prior-year reserve changes) rose to 89.8 from 86.0. Jess Merten, President of Property-Liability, gave an adjusted figure that strips out favourable claims development from earlier in the year: "Adjusting for this, the combined ratio was 91.4, better than our mid-90s target." CEO Tom Wilson called the move deliberate: "When we decided to move that combined ratio up more towards the target, it was to drive growth". The recorded auto combined ratio was 83.2, helped by 9.1 points of favourable prior-year reserve changes, and underwriting income (premiums less claims and expenses) was $1.62B (−6.0%). New issued auto applications reached 9.4 million over the latest 12 months, up 59% from 5.9 million three years earlier, per Merten.

A completely different risk shape from auto, and it deserves separate treatment. Homeowners profitability is driven less by the slow rise in claim costs than by the catastrophe load — wildfire, severe convective storm (hail and straight-line wind), hurricane. Those losses are lumpy, tend to arrive together, and are costly to pass on to reinsurers (insurers' own insurers). Reinsurance prices jumped about 37% worldwide at the January 2023 renewals, per Howden, and attachment points (the loss level at which reinsurance starts to pay) moved up. Prices have since fallen, back to about 2022 levels by Autonomous's July 2026 estimate, and Allstate keeps the first $1 billion of a single nationwide event under its 2026 programme. Q3 2026 was the stress test: homeowners catastrophe losses of $1.33 billion were up 177.7% from $479 million, the recorded combined ratio went to 89.5 from 71.5, and the line still earned $452 million of underwriting income, down from $1.11 billion a year earlier. The underlying combined ratio barely moved, at 60.1 against 59.8. Premium is still running well ahead of units — earned premium up 11.5% against 3.3% policy growth — and new business rose 10.2% in the quarter; Merten put homeowners new business at more than 1.5 million policies over the latest 12 months, up 39% from 1.1 million three years earlier. That is the healthier of the two mixes, but it is also the one that a single bad wildfire season can erase.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE CLAIMS NETWORK 90+ YEAR BRAND MULTI-CHANNEL DISTRIBUTION STATE RATE-FILING DEPTH TELEMATICS & PRICING DATA 217.6M POLICY BASE CAPITAL & REINSURANCE ACCESS

Personal lines insurance is not a business with a wide moat so much as one with high, boring barriers. Allstate's real advantages are structural rather than proprietary: a claims-handling and repair-network footprint that fixes cars more cheaply per claim than a smaller insurer can, ninety-plus years of brand recall that lowers customer acquisition cost, actuarial and regulatory teams that can file and defend price increases in fifty separate states at once, and decades of loss data — increasingly enriched by telematics — that lets it segment risk finely enough to avoid adverse selection (being left with the customers other insurers priced out). Distribution is genuinely multi-channel: exclusive agents, independent agents (largely via National General), direct-to-consumer and call centres, with the direct channel taking 34% of new auto business at the Q2 2026 report, up from 28%. That mix matters because it lets Allstate flex acquisition spend toward whichever channel is cheapest per policy. What it does not have is pricing power. Auto insurance is a commodity bought on price by a consumer who can re-shop in ninety seconds, and the two most feared competitors — Progressive and GEICO — have historically run structurally lower expense ratios. Allstate's moat is competent scale, not immunity.

Expense ratio and the cost of buying growth. The expense ratio is where a personal lines carrier's growth ambition becomes visible, because advertising runs straight through it. On the Q3 call Merten put it plainly: "Advertising spend has increased from $638 million in 2023 to over $2 billion for the latest 12 months." That is more than a tripling in three years, and it is the clearest measure of the "win the policies back" strategy. It is also why margin has started to give ground: the underlying combined ratio for Property-Liability rose to 80.5 in Q3 2026 from 78.7, and for auto to 89.8 from 86.0. Wilson said the move toward the target was made to drive growth, so the question is no longer whether Allstate is paying for units but how far it will go.

Catastrophe and reinsurance dependence. Homeowners earnings rest on a reinsurance programme that cost $1.23 billion in 2025, and on wildfire and severe-storm seasons that have not been kind. Q3 2026 catastrophe losses were $1.65 billion, nearly three times the $558 million of a year earlier, though the nine-month total of $4.61 billion was 2.9% lower than 2025. Lown's summary: "While catastrophe losses were higher this quarter, on a trailing 12-month basis, they were broadly consistent with the prior year." A single California wildfire season or a bad Gulf hurricane can move a quarter by dollars per share, and investors pay a low price relative to earnings that weather can take away.

Regulatory rate lag. The 2022–23 losses were not an underwriting failure so much as a regulatory-timing failure: claim costs moved faster than state insurance departments would approve price increases. That mechanism is unchanged and will fire again in the next inflation shock. Certain states — California in particular — are structurally slow, which is precisely where catastrophe exposure is highest.

Reserve development is a two-way street. Q3 2026's recorded combined ratio benefited from 5.1 points of favourable prior-year reserve changes (9.1 points in auto), up from 2.7 points a year earlier. Favourable reserve development is genuine cash, but it cannot be counted on to repeat and it flatters the recorded ratio. Investors looking at an 86.6 combined ratio should remember that the underlying figure is 80.5, that catastrophes added about 11 points, and that prior-year releases took 5.1 points back out.

Notable One-Off Items. Several distortions matter enough to change the conclusion. First, a Q3 2026 net loss on investments and derivatives of $545 million versus a $252 million gain a year earlier — a $797 million swing, which is why net income applicable to common shareholders was $2.05 billion ($8.03 per diluted share) while adjusted net income was $2.49 billion ($9.76 per diluted share). Second, 5.1 points of favourable prior-year reserve changes in the quarter, 9.1 points in auto; they lifted the recorded result but cannot be extrapolated. Third, catastrophes cost $1.65 billion against $558 million, and Lown tied the 6.5-point rise in the recorded ratio primarily to 7.1 points of higher catastrophe losses. Fourth, the gulf between TTM EPS of $44.09 and FY2027 consensus EPS of $28.44 — a 35% step down — says analysts do not expect these earnings to last, so any valuation anchored on the 5.78 trailing P/E is anchored on noise. Finally, adjusted EPS of $9.76 beat the Nasdaq consensus of $7.03 by 38.8%, the fourth straight quarter of beats above 35%, a reminder that quarterly results in this business are only loosely forecastable.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation one.

The five-year trajectory. From a November 2021 close of $108.72 to $254.81 on 6 November 2026, ALL returned +134.4% on price alone — roughly 18.8% a year — before dividends, against +61.4% for SPY and +66.6% for QQQ on the same dates. But the path is the point, not the endpoint. The stock spent nearly two years going nowhere: it was $108.72 in November 2021 and $111.41 in September 2023, a 2.5% gain over twenty-two months, with the lowest month-end close of $107.81 in August 2023. That flat stretch is exactly the window in which auto claim costs outran approved prices and Allstate booked group net losses of −$1.39B (FY2022) and −$0.32B (FY2023). Everything since is investors paying up for a repaired business: from the August 2023 low of $107.81 the stock has risen roughly 31% a year. The 52-week range of $191.13 – $281.11 and the +28.69% 52-week change put the price in the upper third of its range, 3.6% above its 50-day average ($245.89) and 10.8% above its 200-day average ($230.06), with a 14-day RSI of 53, neither stretched nor washed out.

Against the S&P 500, ALL was a substantial laggard from late 2021 through mid-2023 and a substantial outperformer from October 2023 onward: the +134.4% price return from November 2021 compares with +61.4% for SPY over the same dates. Against its own sector the picture is more nuanced: property-casualty insurers broadly rose as insurance prices went up across the industry, so some of Allstate's move came with the sector rather than from anything Allstate did alone. What is specific to Allstate is the depth of the hole — personal auto is where the severity shock landed, so it fell further and has recovered harder. The low beta of 0.12 is a reminder that this stock's drawdowns are driven by weather and claim costs, not by the equity market.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The margin repair has survived a heavy storm quarter and the market is still paying a single-digit multiple for it — 6.6× FY2026 consensus EPS of $38.46, 9.0× FY2027 consensus of $28.44, 2.12× book. Property-Liability earned $2.02 billion of underwriting income in Q3 even with catastrophe losses nearly triple last year's, and $6.69 billion over nine months (+47.5%). Policies in force are growing in both big lines and overall (auto +3.4%, homeowners +3.3%, total 217.6M +3.9%), and new business is up 125% to 12.4 million items, per Wilson. Meanwhile the company returned $4.4 billion in 12 months, retiring 5.7% of its shares, with about $9 billion of parent-company capital behind it. A business earning a 39.7% adjusted ROE with a 0.12 beta at 9× forward earnings is either mispriced or the market is telling you the earnings are not real.

Bear Case

The earnings are, in part, not real. TTM EPS of $44.09 against FY2027 consensus of $28.44 is a 35% step down, and analysts are not assuming it recovers. The underlying auto combined ratio rose to 89.8 from 86.0, and management says it moved the ratio up toward its mid-90s target on purpose: Merten's adjusted figure is already 91.4. To grow, Allstate is spending: over $2 billion on advertising in the latest 12 months against $638M in 2023, with auto earned premium up only 0.6% against 3.4% policy growth and average auto premiums down 2.7%. Favourable prior-year reserve changes contributed 5.1 points to the recorded ratio and cannot be relied on. Book value per share fell to $120.23 from $123.38 in a single quarter as rates rose. Add one bad wildfire or hurricane season and 2.12× book looks expensive.

01Catastrophe concentration in homeowners. Q3 2026 carried $1.65B of catastrophe losses, of which $1.33B hit homeowners, and the recorded homeowners combined ratio went to 89.5 from 71.5. Wildfire and severe convective storm exposure is geographically concentrated, and Allstate keeps the first $1 billion of a single nationwide event under its 2026 reinsurance programme. A single severe season can erase a year of homeowners underwriting income.
02Growth bought rather than earned. Advertising has more than tripled since 2023, auto earned premium growth (+0.6%) trails policy growth (+3.4%) and average auto premiums fell 2.7% in a year. Management has said it is deliberately moving the auto margin toward its mid-90s target to grow. If that takes the underlying ratio higher than investors expect, the share price falls with it.
03Reserve and regulatory reversal. Favourable prior-year reserve changes of 5.1 points are flattering the recorded combined ratio today; the same mechanism runs in reverse when claim-cost assumptions prove too low. Combined with the lag in state approval of price increases — the exact failure that produced the FY2022 and FY2023 net losses — the next inflation shock would hit earnings before Allstate could respond, and the mechanism has not changed.
  Industry Commentary
Storm losses stay high even as reinsurance gets cheaper
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Reinsurance prices jumped about 37% worldwide at the January 2023 renewals, per Howden, and attachment points moved up, so primary insurers kept more of mid-sized events such as hail and straight-line wind. Prices have been falling since, and Guy Carpenter's global index was down about 16% at the mid-2026 renewals, but cheaper reinsurance does not stop storms. Allstate recorded $1.65 billion of catastrophe losses in Q3 2026 alone, up from $558 million a year earlier, and its 39.7% adjusted return on equity sits beside a trailing P/E of 5.78.

The personal auto growth war has restarted
ONGOING HEADWIND

Allstate fixed its margin in 2023–24 by raising prices and letting policies go, and it now wants those policies back. Its levers are price and advertising. Advertising went from $638 million in 2023 to over $2 billion in the latest 12 months, and average auto premiums fell 2.7% over the last year. When several large insurers chase the same customers, advertising bills rise, average premiums fall and margins slip: Allstate's underlying combined ratio rose in Q3 to 80.5 from 78.7.

Higher rates help investment income and hurt book value
MIXED TAILWIND

Net investment income rose to $1.18 billion in Q3 2026, up $228 million, on an $87.4 billion portfolio: before expenses, $107 million of the rise came from larger balances and higher bond yields and $128 million from private equity valuation gains. Over nine months it reached $3.12 billion against $2.56 billion. The same higher rates cut the other way on the balance sheet: the portfolio's total return was −2.1% in Q3 and unrealized net capital losses were about $2.6 billion before tax. The bull frames it this way: investment income is durable, policy counts are growing in auto and homeowners, and 9.0× FY2027 earnings with a 39.7% adjusted ROE and a 0.12 beta is a mispricing. The bear frames it this way: investment income and reserve releases are the low-quality earnings propping up profit while the underlying ratio climbs and advertising spend triples. Both are looking at the same $254.81 price. The tiebreaker will be the next quarters' underlying auto combined ratio read against auto policies in force — margin held and policies grown means the bulls were right; policies grown and margin given away means the bears were.
