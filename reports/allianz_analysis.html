01 Company Overview

Allianz is Europe's insurance heavyweight: a Munich company that sells car, home, business, life and health cover in almost 70 countries to around 97 million customers, and that also owns two of the world's big money managers, PIMCO and Allianz Global Investors, which together run about €2.2 trillion for outside clients. In 2025 it booked €186.9B of total business volume (premiums plus fund fees), a record €17.4B of operating profit (up 8.4%) and €10.8B of net income for shareholders. The board paid out €17.10 a share for 2025, 11% more than the year before.

The way to think about Allianz is as a house that sits on three tables at once. In property-casualty it is the bookmaker: it takes premiums up front and wins if claims plus costs come in under 100 cents per premium euro — the combined ratio ran at 92.2% in the first nine months of 2026 (93.8% in the third quarter, when natural catastrophes cost 5.1 points). In life and health it holds savings for decades and earns a margin on them. In asset management it charges fees on other people's money. The first nine months of 2026 were the best in the company's history on operating profit (€14.1B, +7.8%), and on November 11 Allianz raised the top of its full-year target range to about €18.6B. The capital buffer, a Solvency II ratio, stood at 219% after buying just over half of PIMCO's outstanding employee shares, which cost about four points of the ratio, per the third-quarter call. The shares closed August at a five-year month-end high of €450.80, then September took 7.7% off, including a two-day 7.2% slide on September 22–23 while the DAX barely moved. They then climbed to €477.20 on November 13, the highest close in the five-year window, and gave back 5.3% by November 20. At €451.80 the stock trades at 14.8× trailing earnings with a 3.8% dividend yield. The question for 2027: can a business already earning a core return on equity near 20% keep growing earnings per share at its 7–9% target while the commercial insurance cycle softens?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: ~97M customers, ~70 countries Three uncorrelated profit engines Capital strength (Solvency II 219%) PIMCO fixed-income franchise German retail distribution €56.4B locked-in life profit (CSM) Underwriting data across lines

The real edge is diversification you can't buy quickly. A bad catastrophe year hits property-casualty; a bond-market rout hits asset management and the life book's balance sheet; a recession hits new life sales. These rarely all go wrong in the same quarter, so Allianz's operating profit has stepped up steadily (€16.0B in 2024, €17.4B in 2025, €14.1B in the first nine months of 2026). A 219% Solvency II ratio means it holds more than twice the capital the regulator requires, which is why it can pay a 4%-plus dividend, buy back €2.5B of stock (completed on September 22) and still sign deals such as HSBC Life Singapore. The weakness of a giant is the same thing seen from the other side: growth has to come from many mature markets at once, and the group is big enough that a single lost unit, such as a stalled acquisition, barely moves the total.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The commercial pricing cycle is turning. Commercial internal growth was 3% in the third quarter of 2026 (1% in the second) and the commercial combined ratio for the quarter worsened to 93.5% from 92.0%. In its interim report Allianz expects property-casualty premium growth across the industry to slow in 2026, and its second-quarter release tied that quarter's 1% commercial growth to Allianz having "continued to carefully manage the market environment". On the November 12 call management said it expects better terms from its reinsurance renewals as that market softens. When prices soften, an insurer either gives up volume or margin.
02
Catastrophes are a fat tail. Natural catastrophes cost 5.1 points of the third quarter's loss ratio, against 0.3 points a year earlier, which pushed property-casualty operating profit down 7.9% in the quarter; the nine-month combined ratio of 92.2% still sits inside the 92–93% range. Allianz's results releases carry a standing warning that catastrophes and capital-market shocks "may severely affect" operating profit; the outlook range of about €16.4B to €18.6B has that spread for a reason.
03
Asset management is market-dependent. PIMCO and AllianzGI earn fees on asset levels; in the third quarter adverse market effects from interest-rate moves more than offset €47B of net inflows and currency gains, and outside assets slipped to €2,152B from €2,161B. A bond or equity sell-off reverses more of that, and the segment produced 20.3% of nine-month operating profit before Corporate and Other.
04
Conduct risk has a price tag. In May 2022 Allianz agreed to pay about $6 billion in the US over the collapse of AllianzGI's Structured Alpha funds in 2020; the shares fell 7.8% on August 2, 2021, the first session after it disclosed a US Justice Department probe. In its half-year report Allianz SE says it is not aware of any threatened or pending proceedings with significant effects on the group's financial position or profitability, but the episode showed what one fund unit's conduct can cost the group.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the 2025–2026 comparisons. First, the sale of Allianz's 26% stakes in its Indian joint ventures with Bajaj (the main 23% tranche closed on January 8, 2026 for about €2.1B, per Allianz) brought a first-quarter 2026 gain of about €1.1B. Second, the second quarter of 2025 contained a divestment gain on the UniCredit joint venture (about €0.3B of net income), and Allianz took offsetting measures costing about €0.6B of net income in the nine months after the Indian sale; adjusted for all of these, nine-month core net income growth is 7% rather than the reported 10.6%, and third-quarter growth is 3% rather than 1.2%. Third, the buy-back shrinks the share count: the weighted average was 377.7M shares in the first nine months of 2026 versus 383.8M a year earlier, so per-share growth outruns profit growth by roughly 1.6 points.

The third quarter against the forecasts

Allianz collects the published forecasts of the analysts who cover it (14 brokers for this quarter), posts the averages two business days before each print and sets the actual results beside them afterwards. Against that consensus the third quarter was a broad beat. Operating profit came in at €4,709M against a mean forecast of €4,297M (+9.6%, shown by Allianz as +10%), with Asset Management (€1,119M vs €947M) doing most of the beating, Property-Casualty ahead (€2,204M vs €2,114M) and Life/Health a little ahead (€1,469M vs €1,446M); the Corporate and Other cost centre, with consolidation items, cost less than forecast (−€84M vs −€208M). Shareholders' net income was €2,784M against €2,506M (+11%) and core net income €2,889M against €2,536M (+14%). The property-casualty combined ratio of 93.8% matched the forecast exactly, though catastrophes cost 5.1 points against 4.7 expected and the run-off result (releases from earlier-year reserves) was higher than forecast. Weaker spots: the Solvency II ratio of 219% was two points under the 221% expected, and Allianz's sheet notes a four-point hit from the PIMCO M-unit purchase alongside higher market volatility and inflation; the contractual service margin of €56.4B was below the €57.6B forecast; value of new business (€1,043M vs €1,092M) was 4% light; and property-casualty internal growth of 4.5% missed the 5.5% expected. Asset Management net inflows of €47B beat the €30B expected.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 20, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of €194.84 to the November 20, 2026 settled close of €451.80, Allianz shares gained 131.9% on price alone. Add back the €68.50 per share of dividends paid from 2022 to 2026 and the total return is +196.0%, against +62.5% for the DAX (which includes dividends) over the same window. The shape is a slow-then-fast climb. Allianz spent 2022 falling to a five-year low of €161.80 (September 2022), a year in which rates rose and the Structured Alpha settlement landed, moved between roughly €200 and €230 through most of 2023, and then climbed almost without pause from early 2024 to a five-year month-end high of €450.80 in August 2026. The November 20 close is €1.00 above that, and the highest close in the window is €477.20 on November 13, the day after the results.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how stretched that run became: it stayed above 70 for most of 2024 and 2025 and peaked at 92.7 in April 2025, the highest reading in the series. September's fall brought it down to 58.1 and October's 5.4% gain lifted it to 65.5; the November 20 point reads 69.2, just under the overbought line. The price is above both its 10-month average (about €412) and its 3-month average (about €436): the long uptrend is intact and the short-term momentum has picked up again. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 14.8× trailing earnings for a company that just posted a record nine months, runs a 92.2% combined ratio, earns about a 19% underlying core return on equity and holds 219% of the capital its regulator demands. Underlying core EPS grew 8% in the nine months, inside the 7–9% plan, third-quarter operating profit beat the analysts' consensus by about 10%, management raised the top of its full-year range to about €18.6B, and the share count is shrinking. On top of that comes a 3.8% dividend that has risen in each of the last five years. PIMCO and AllianzGI took in €132B of net new money in nine months, 40% more than a year earlier, and the segment's profit rose 20.3%. September's 7.7% fall came between results dates; the February 24, 2027 full-year print is the next chance to show the numbers.

Bear Case

The record is already in the price: the stock more than doubled in five years, trades at nearly 2.8× book and carries a consensus Hold with six Sell or Strong Sell ratings out of 17 and an average target only 0.8% above the share price. Property-casualty operating profit fell 7.9% in the third quarter as catastrophes cost 5.1 points, commercial insurance growth is 3% and its combined ratio worsened, and management itself said the full-year combined ratio will likely run slightly above the first nine months. Third-quarter core net income grew only 1.2% (3% underlying), Barclays kept its Underweight rating with a €385 target on November 16, and the shares fell 5.3% from their November 13 high to November 20. The M&A pipeline (HSBC Life Singapore, the PIMCO M-unit buy-out, UOBAM, the Greek minority sale, reported talks over the AA) adds execution and capital risk; the M-unit purchase alone cost about four points of the Solvency II ratio. The two-day 7.2% drop on September 22–23, steeper than Generali's or AXA's over the same sessions, shows how fast a stock near its high can fall.

Top 3 Risks

01
Catastrophe and pricing cycle. A heavy natural-catastrophe year combined with softening commercial prices would hit the segment that produced 49% of nine-month operating profit before Corporate and Other. The combined-ratio range of 92–93% is the line to watch.
02
Capital markets. A sharp fall in bond or equity markets cuts asset-management fees (in the third quarter, interest-rate moves already pushed outside assets down by more than the inflows added) and dents the Solvency II ratio, which in turn limits buy-backs.
03
Deal execution and valuation. Several acquisitions are in flight at once while the stock trades near the top of its five-year range. A misstep, or a large deal priced richly, would meet a market with little patience: six of 17 analysts already rate the shares a Sell or Strong Sell.
Industry Commentary
The commercial insurance cycle is softening
KEY CONSTRAINT

In its half-year report Allianz expects premium growth in property-casualty insurance to slow in 2026 and says the sector remains exposed to natural catastrophes, cyberattacks and political and military crises. Its own numbers show the split: over the first nine months retail grew 7% internally with a 92.5% combined ratio (91.6% a year earlier), while commercial grew 3% with a 91.9% combined ratio (91.3%); in the third quarter alone the commercial combined ratio rose 1.5 points to 93.5%. Allianz chose margin over volume. That protects the combined ratio, but it caps the growth of the business that now earns about half the group's operating profit. The UK was the largest drag on the first half's accident-year loss ratio (0.3 points), in what the company calls an increasingly challenging market. On the November 12 call management said it expects better terms from reinsurers as that market softens.

Higher-for-longer rates: a tailwind with a sting
MIXED

Allianz's interim report notes that inflation turned up again in the first half of 2026 on energy prices, that the European Central Bank raised its policy rate by 25 basis points (0.25 percentage points) in June, and that long-term rates stayed high in both Europe and the US. For an insurer that is mostly good news: reinvested premiums and life savings earn more, and the report itself cites higher long-term rates as a driver of demand for savings products. The sting is twofold. Rising claims inflation feeds straight into motor and property repair costs — the CEO's point that insurance costs are outrunning incomes. And when rates rise, the bonds that asset managers like PIMCO run for clients lose value, which pulls down the fees charged on them.

Capital to spare: buy-backs and bolt-ons
TAILWIND

A 219% Solvency II ratio funds three uses of capital at once. Allianz bought back €2.5B of its own stock in 2026, finishing on September 22. It is paying a dividend that rose 11% to €17.10. And it is buying growth in Asia and in asset management: HSBC Life Singapore (€2.0B with the distribution deal), UOB Asset Management (€376M) and the PIMCO M-units (at least €1.4B), while selling out of its Indian joint ventures (about €2.1B for the main 23% tranche) and, in an agreement signed October 14, 2026, selling a 30% stake in Allianz European Reliance, its Greek business, to National Bank of Greece alongside a long-term exclusive bank-distribution deal. The bull reading: disciplined capital allocation that compounds per-share value. The bear reading: deals pile up at a time when the shares already trade at a premium to AXA and Munich Re.
