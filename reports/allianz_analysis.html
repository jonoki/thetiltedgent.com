01 Company Overview

Allianz is Europe's insurance heavyweight: a Munich company that sells car, home, business, life and health cover in almost 70 countries to around 97 million customers, and that also owns two of the world's big money managers, PIMCO and Allianz Global Investors, which together run about €2.2 trillion for outside clients. In 2025 it booked €186.9B of total business volume (premiums plus fund fees), a record €17.4B of operating profit (up 8.4%) and €10.8B of net income for shareholders. The board paid out €17.10 a share for 2025, 11% more than the year before.

The way to think about Allianz is as a house that sits on three tables at once. In property-casualty it is the bookmaker: it takes premiums up front and wins if claims plus costs come in under 100 cents per premium euro — the combined ratio ran at 91.4% in the first half of 2026. In life and health it holds savings for decades and earns a margin on them. In asset management it charges fees on other people's money. The first half of 2026 was the best half-year in the company's history on operating profit (€9.4B, +8.6%), the capital buffer rose to a Solvency II ratio of 225%, and the shares closed August at a five-year month-end high of €450.80. Then September took 7.7% off, including a two-day 7.2% slide on September 22–23 while the DAX barely moved. At €416.20 the stock trades at 13.6× trailing earnings with a 4.1% dividend yield. The question for 2027: can a business already earning a core return on equity near 20% keep growing earnings per share at its 7–9% target while the commercial insurance cycle softens?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: ~97M customers, ~70 countries Three uncorrelated profit engines Capital strength (Solvency II 225%) PIMCO fixed-income franchise German retail distribution €57.3B locked-in life profit (CSM) Underwriting data across lines

The real edge is diversification you can't buy quickly. A bad catastrophe year hits property-casualty; a bond-market rout hits asset management and the life book's balance sheet; a recession hits new life sales. These rarely all go wrong in the same quarter, so Allianz's operating profit has stepped up steadily (€16.0B in 2024, €17.4B in 2025, €9.4B in the first half of 2026). A 225% Solvency II ratio means it holds well over twice the capital the regulator requires, which is why it can pay a 4%-plus dividend, buy back €2.5B of stock and still sign deals such as HSBC Life Singapore. The weakness of a giant is the same thing seen from the other side: growth has to come from many mature markets at once, and the group is big enough that a single lost unit, such as a stalled acquisition, barely moves the total.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The commercial pricing cycle is turning. Commercial internal growth slowed to 1% in the second quarter of 2026 and the commercial combined ratio for that quarter worsened to 91.8% from 90.3%. In its interim report Allianz expects property-casualty premium growth across the industry to slow in 2026, and on the August 7 call CFO Claire-Marie Coste-Lepoutre said that in AGCS, its large-corporate book, "we have observed a rapid softening in the second quarter, in particular around property, around natural resources and construction". When prices soften, an insurer either gives up volume or margin.
02
Catastrophes are a fat tail. Natural catastrophes cost only 1.5 points of combined ratio in the first half of 2026, a light period by the company's own account. Allianz's results releases carry a standing warning that catastrophes and capital-market shocks "may severely affect" operating profit; the full-year outlook of €17.4B ± €1B has that range for a reason.
03
Asset management is market-dependent. PIMCO and AllianzGI earn fees on asset levels; €68B of the second quarter's AuM growth was market gains. A bond or equity sell-off reverses that, and the segment produced 18.7% of first-half operating profit.
04
Conduct risk has a price tag. In May 2022 Allianz agreed to pay about $6 billion in the US over the collapse of AllianzGI's Structured Alpha funds in 2020; the shares fell 7.8% on August 2, 2021, the first session after it disclosed a US Justice Department probe. In its half-year report Allianz SE says it is not aware of any threatened or pending proceedings with significant effects on the group's financial position or profitability, but the episode showed what one fund unit's conduct can cost the group.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the 2025–2026 comparisons. First, the sale of Allianz's 26% stakes in its Indian joint ventures with Bajaj (closed in two tranches on January 8 and March 12, 2026, for about €2.3B) produced a pre-tax gain of about €1.3B and lifted first-quarter 2026 net income by about €1.1B. Second, the second quarter of 2025 contained a divestment gain on the UniCredit joint venture, which is why second-quarter 2026 core net income fell 12.7% (to €2.60B) even though operating profit rose 10.6%; Allianz puts underlying core net income growth at 10% for the quarter and 9% for the half. Third, the buy-back shrinks the share count: the weighted average was 379.0M shares in the first half of 2026 versus 385.0M a year earlier, so per-share growth outruns profit growth by roughly 1.6 points.

The second quarter against the forecasts

Allianz collects the published forecasts of the analysts who cover it (14 brokers for this quarter), posts the averages two business days before each print and sets the actual results beside them afterwards. Against that consensus the second quarter was an operating beat and a net-income miss. Operating profit came in at €4,874M against a mean forecast of €4,604M (+6%), with Life/Health (€1,544M vs €1,411M) and Asset Management (€933M vs €871M) doing the beating. Property-Casualty was a shade under (€2,459M vs €2,474M), and its 91.9% combined ratio was 0.8 points worse than the 91.1% expected; Allianz's own comment puts that down to smaller releases from prior-year reserves, a choice it calls prudent in light of inflation uncertainty. Below the operating line it went the other way: non-operating items of −€1,040M against −€678M forecast (about half of it offsetting measures tied to the Indian joint-venture sale) left shareholders' core net income at €2,600M, 8% under the €2,817M consensus. The Solvency II ratio (225%) matched the forecast, and third-party net inflows of €39B beat the €35B expected.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of €194.84 to the October 2, 2026 settled close of €416.20, Allianz shares gained 113.6% on price alone. Add back the €68.50 per share of dividends paid from 2022 to 2026 and the total return is +172.7%, against +65.3% for the DAX (which includes dividends) over the same window. The shape is a slow-then-fast climb. Allianz spent 2022 falling to a five-year low of €161.80 (September 2022), a year in which rates rose and the Structured Alpha settlement landed, chopped between roughly €200 and €230 through most of 2023, and then re-rated almost without pause from early 2024 to a five-year month-end high of €450.80 in August 2026.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how stretched that run became: it stayed above 70 for most of 2024 and 2025 and peaked at 92.7 in April 2025, the highest reading in the series. September's fall brought it down to 58.1 — neutral. The price is still above its 10-month average (about €401) but below its 3-month average (about €428): the long uptrend is intact, the short-term momentum has cooled. Note that May closes include the dividend going ex: the 12.1% fall in May 2023, the worst month in the window, includes the €11.40 dividend that went ex on May 5, 2023.

Relative performance

Against its home index Allianz has been a clear winner: +172.7% total return over five years versus +65.3% for the DAX, and +20.2% versus +3.3% over the last year. Against fellow euro-listed insurers the one-year price picture is mixed: Allianz's +14.9% trails Generali (+25.8%) but beats AXA (+3.7%) and Munich Re (−8.0%). On valuation Allianz sits between them: 13.6× trailing earnings against 9.5× for Munich Re, 11.3× for AXA and 14.1× for Generali, with a dividend yield (4.1%) a little under the 4.7% peer average. On these numbers the market pays a premium over AXA and Munich Re, not a growth multiple.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 13.6× trailing earnings for a company that just posted a record half-year, runs a 91.4% combined ratio, earns about a 19% underlying core return on equity and holds 225% of the capital its regulator demands. Underlying core EPS grew 10% in the first half, above the 7–9% plan, second-quarter operating profit beat the analysts' consensus by 6%, and the share count is shrinking. On top of that comes a 4.1% dividend that has risen in each of the last five years. PIMCO and AllianzGI took in a record €84B of net new money in six months, so the fee engine is accelerating just as rates stay high. September's 7.7% fall came between results dates; the November 12 print is the next chance to show the numbers.

Bear Case

The record is already in the price: the stock more than doubled in five years, trades at nearly 2.5× book and carries a consensus Hold with four Sell or Strong Sell ratings out of 17. Commercial insurance growth slowed to 1% in the second quarter and its combined ratio worsened, while the first half had a light catastrophe load (1.5 points). A normal or heavy storm season would push the ratio toward or above the 92–93% range. Headline core net income already fell 12.7% in the second quarter on one-offs, 8% below the analysts' consensus, and Morningstar and Intesa Sanpaolo both turned negative after the print. The M&A pipeline (HSBC Life Singapore, the PIMCO M-unit buy-out, UOBAM, reported talks over the AA) adds execution and capital risk. The two-day 7.2% drop on September 22–23, steeper than Generali's or AXA's over the same sessions, shows how fast a stock near its high can de-rate.

Industry Commentary
The commercial insurance cycle is softening
KEY CONSTRAINT

In its half-year report Allianz expects premium growth in property-casualty insurance to slow in 2026 and says the sector remains exposed to natural catastrophes, cyberattacks and political and military crises. Its own numbers show the split: retail grew 7% internally in the first half with a flat 91.7% combined ratio, while commercial grew 4% — just 1% in the second quarter — and its quarterly combined ratio rose 1.5 points to 91.8%. Allianz chose margin over volume. That protects the combined ratio, but it caps the growth of the business that now earns half the group's operating profit. The UK was the largest drag on the accident-year loss ratio (0.3 points), in what the company calls an increasingly challenging market.

Higher-for-longer rates: a tailwind with a sting
MIXED

Allianz's interim report notes that inflation turned up again in the first half of 2026 on energy prices, that the European Central Bank raised its policy rate by 25 basis points in June, and that long-term rates stayed high in both Europe and the US. For an insurer that is mostly good news: reinvested premiums and life savings earn more, and the report itself cites higher long-term rates as a driver of demand for savings products. The sting is twofold. Rising claims inflation feeds straight into motor and property repair costs — the CEO's point that insurance costs are outrunning incomes. And bond-heavy asset managers like PIMCO live with mark-to-market swings on the assets they manage.

Capital to spare: buy-backs and bolt-ons
TAILWIND

A 225% Solvency II ratio funds three uses of capital at once. Allianz is buying back up to €2.5B of its own stock in 2026 (€1.4B, or 3.8 million shares, by June 30). It is paying a dividend that rose 11% to €17.10. And it is buying growth in Asia and in asset management: HSBC Life Singapore (€2.0B with the distribution deal), UOB Asset Management (€376M) and the PIMCO M-units (at least €1.4B), while selling out of India for about €2.3B. Allianz is recycling capital from minority stakes into businesses it controls. The bull reading: disciplined capital allocation that compounds per-share value. The bear reading: deals pile up at a time when the shares already trade at a premium to AXA and Munich Re.
