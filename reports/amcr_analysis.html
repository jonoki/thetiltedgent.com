01 Company Overview

You have handled Amcor's product today and never seen its name. It makes the film, bottles, jars and tubes that food, drink, medicine and beauty brands put their products in, across Europe, North America, Latin America and Asia Pacific. On April 30, 2025 it closed an all-stock combination with Berry Global, and the numbers jumped: fiscal 2026 (year to June 30, 2026) net sales were $23.51B, up 56.6% from $15.01B, GAAP net income was $1.106B against $511M, and diluted GAAP EPS was $2.38. On management's adjusted measure, EPS was $4.02, up 13%.

The two EPS figures tell the story. Adding more than half again to a packaging company's sales is easy on paper. Proving that the merged company earns more per share is much harder, because the deal paid for Berry in new Amcor shares (StockAnalysis shows the share count up 45.6% year on year). The adjusted figure strips out amortisation and integration costs; GAAP leaves them in. The market is paying 17.7x the GAAP figure and about 10.5x the adjusted one. The whole bet is whether the $650M synergy target (cost savings from the combination, due by fiscal 2028) closes that gap. Management says fiscal 2026 delivered $285M of synergies, ahead of plan. The stock still sits at $42.15, almost exactly where it closed 2025. Two more changes make this year awkward to read: a 1-for-5 reverse split on January 14, 2026, and a move from a June to a December fiscal year-end, with a six-month transition period running to December 31, 2026.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in resin purchasing Customer qualification lock-in Material-science know-how Global plant footprint (~400 sites) Healthcare & food-safety compliance Staples-heavy end markets

Packaging looks like a commodity, and a single roll of film is one. The moat comes from everything around it. A food or drug maker that changes its package has to re-test shelf life, re-validate filling lines and, for medical products, sometimes re-file with regulators. That makes suppliers sticky once they are qualified. Scale matters too: the release puts the combined company at 75,000 employees across roughly 400 locations in 40+ countries, which means it can supply a multinational brand in every region and buy resin in quantities few rivals can match. The weakness is that switching costs protect share, not margin. Consolidated gross margin is 19.95%, so the business depends on passing input-cost moves through to customers, and that pass-through always runs a quarter or two behind.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The balance sheet is doing heavy lifting. Debt/EBITDA is 3.92x (StockAnalysis), debt-to-equity 1.28, and the Altman Z-score is 1.37, low by that model's conventions. None of this is a crisis for a staples-exposed business with steady cash flow. It does mean the equity is geared, so a shortfall in EBITDA hits the share price harder than the same shortfall would at a less indebted peer.
02
The dividend exceeds GAAP earnings. $2.60 a year against $2.38 of GAAP EPS gives a 109.24% payout ratio (StockAnalysis). On adjusted EPS of $4.02 the payout is about 65%, and MarketBeat projects about 60% next year. Which view you take depends on whether you think the adjustments are real. A 6.17% yield is the market asking for that proof.
03
Volumes were barely positive. Q4 comparable volumes were up only about 0.5–1%, with healthcare volumes declining and liquids and beauty flat. Synergies can lift margins for three years. They cannot create demand.
04
Input-cost and geopolitical shocks. Management says the Middle East conflict tied up about $500M of working capital. Resin-based packaging is exposed to oil-linked input prices, and the lag in passing costs through makes quarterly margins noisy.

Three items distort the comparisons. Second, the 1-for-5 reverse split, approved by shareholders on November 6, 2025 and effective January 14, 2026, cut shares outstanding from about 2.3 billion to about 461 million. Every pre-2026 per-share figure in this report (prices, dividends, EPS) has been multiplied by 5 to be comparable. Third, the fiscal year-end is moving from June 30 to December 31. The next reported "year" is a six-month transition period to December 31, 2026, guided at adjusted EPS of $1.80–$1.90. The FY26 GAAP comparison is also flattered by Q4 FY25, which was a GAAP loss (−$0.10 diluted EPS) in the quarter the deal closed.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value, and a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $57.95 (split-adjusted) to the September 21, 2026 close of $42.15, AMCR lost 27.3% of its price. Dividends change the picture a lot. About $12.52 per share (split-adjusted) went out over the period, and Yahoo's dividend-adjusted series puts the total return at roughly −6.6% over five years. For a stock like this, most of the return is the dividend. The shape: a 2022 peak of $65.50 (May), a long slide through 2023 as volumes fell, a 2024 rally to $57.20, and then eighteen months stuck around $40–$50 while the Berry deal was announced, closed and integrated. The chart shows a stock the market has parked while it waits for the synergies to show up in the numbers.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 10.5x adjusted earnings and 10.3x forward for a global consumer-packaging leader that sells mostly into food, pet care and healthcare demand and pays a 6.17% dividend while you wait. The integration is running ahead of plan: $285M of synergies in the first full year against a $650M three-year target, and Q4 adjusted EPS up 23%. Volumes have just turned positive. Leverage is guided down to 3.5–3.6x by December and toward ~3x after that. Five non-core divestitures are already closed. The lowest analyst target on the Street is roughly today's price. If synergies land on schedule, the gap between GAAP and adjusted EPS closes, the payout ratio falls to about 60%, and the market no longer needs a 6% yield to hold the stock.

Bear Case

GAAP EPS of $2.38 doesn't cover a $2.60 dividend. Debt/EBITDA is 3.92x, the Altman Z-score is 1.37, and organic volume growth is about 1%. Consensus FY2027 revenue growth is an estimated 1.2%. Almost all of the EPS growth story is cost synergies, and cost synergies run out: once the $650M is in, the company is a slow-growth converter with a large debt load, exposed to resin prices and single-use-plastics regulation. JPMorgan moved to the sidelines three days after a beat. The stock has done nothing for a year and a half, and its five-year total return is negative even with a 5–6% dividend reinvested. On this view, the high yield reflects the market's view of risk, not a bargain.

Top 3 Risks

01
Synergy shortfall with leverage still high. The investment case assumes $650M of savings by FY28. A miss would hit a company carrying $15.14B of debt, where the dividend already exceeds GAAP earnings.
02
Volume stagnation. Q4 comparable volumes were up only ~0.5–1%, with healthcare declining. If consumer demand weakens again, as in the FY2023–24 downturn, synergies get spent covering lost volume instead of growing earnings.
03
Input costs, geopolitics and regulation. The Middle East conflict has already tied up ~$500M of working capital. Resin prices follow oil, pass-through lags, and rigid single-use plastics are a standing regulatory target. None of these is thesis-breaking on its own, but together they keep quarterly results noisy.
Industry Commentary
Scale consolidation in consumer packaging
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Amcor has grown by merging: Bemis in June 2019, which brought the NYSE listing and S&P 500 membership, and Berry Global in April 2025. The logic is the same both times. Packaging converters compete on purchasing scale and plant utilisation, so the larger company buys resin more cheaply and fills its lines more fully. The cost is balance-sheet risk. Berry added 57% to sales in its first full year and pushed leverage to levels management is now working down. Consolidation is therefore the constraint on the business, not just its strategy: until leverage is near ~3x, capital goes to paying down debt and the dividend rather than growth.

Demand: staples-steady, but barely growing
ONGOING HEADWIND

Packaging volumes follow what people buy at the grocery store and the pharmacy. That demand is steady but grows slowly, but cyclical swings still make it lumpy: FY2024 revenue fell 7.2%. The latest data shows volumes turning modestly positive (about +0.5% to +1% in Q4 FY26), led by food service, pet care and protein. Consensus revenue growth for the next fiscal year is an estimated 1.2%, so the market expects no help from the top line. Any volume beat is upside.

The yield trade — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $42.15 AMCR yields 6.17%, trades at 9.50x EV/EBITDA and has a 0.60 beta. The bull framing: this is a staples-like income stream priced as if the merger were going wrong, when the evidence so far (synergies ahead of plan, EPS up 23% in Q4, volumes turning) says it is going right. Once leverage falls below 3.5x, the yield should compress toward the ~60% payout MarketBeat projects. The bear framing: a GAAP payout ratio above 100%, debt/EBITDA near 4x and an Altman Z of 1.37 describe a stock whose yield is high because the market doubts it can be sustained. The way to tell which is right is not the P/E. It is two numbers reported every quarter: cumulative synergies against the $650M target, and leverage against the 3.5–3.6x December guide.
