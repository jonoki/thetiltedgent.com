01 Company Overview

You have handled Amcor's product today and never seen its name. It makes the film, bottles, jars and tubes that food, drink, medicine and beauty brands put their products in, across Europe, North America, Latin America and Asia Pacific. On April 30, 2025 it closed an all-stock combination with Berry Global, and the numbers jumped. Over the twelve months to September 30, 2026 net sales were $23.89B, GAAP (standard accounting) net income was $1.086B and diluted GAAP EPS was $2.33. On management's adjusted measure, EPS was $4.00. The September quarter, reported November 4, is the first whose year-ago quarter already has all of Berry in it. Net sales rose 7% to $6.13B: raw-material costs passed on to customers added 8 points, divested businesses took away 2, and volumes added 1. Adjusted EPS was $0.95, against $0.97 a year earlier.

What changed with this report is the forecast. Amcor cut its adjusted EPS guidance for the six months to December 31, 2026 to $1.70–$1.80 from $1.80–$1.90, because energy and distribution costs jumped late in September, faster than it could raise prices. Management says prices catch up by the end of December and that 2027 is unaffected. The stock fell 5.48% the next session and 7.5% over two sessions. The two EPS figures still tell the bigger story. Adding more than half again to a packaging company's sales is easy on paper. Proving that the merged company earns more per share is much harder, because the deal paid for Berry in new Amcor shares. The adjusted figure strips out amortisation and integration costs; GAAP leaves them in. The market is paying 19.3x the GAAP figure and about 11.2x the adjusted one. The whole bet is whether the $650M synergy target (savings and extra sales from the combination, most of it due by the end of 2027) closes that gap. Management counts $285M of synergies in fiscal 2026 and $60M more in the September quarter. At $44.87 the stock is 7.6% above where it closed 2025. Two more changes make this year awkward to read: a 1-for-5 reverse split on January 14, 2026, and a move from a June to a December fiscal year-end, with a six-month transition period running to December 31, 2026.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in resin purchasing Customer qualification lock-in Material-science know-how Global plant footprint (~400 sites) Healthcare & food-safety compliance Staples-heavy end markets

Packaging looks like a commodity, and a single roll of film is one. The moat comes from everything around it. A food or drug maker that changes its package has to re-test shelf life, re-validate filling lines and, for medical products, sometimes re-file with regulators. That makes suppliers sticky once they are qualified. Scale matters too: the release puts the combined company at 75,000 employees across roughly 400 locations in 40+ countries, which means it can supply a multinational brand in every region and buy resin in quantities few rivals can match. The weakness is that switching costs protect share, not margin. Consolidated gross margin is 19.78%, so the business depends on passing input-cost moves through to customers, and that pass-through runs behind the cost. The November guidance cut is that lag at work: energy and freight bills rose late in September faster than prices did.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The balance sheet is doing heavy lifting. Net debt was 3.76x EBITDA on management's measure at September 30 and debt/EBITDA is 4.10x on StockAnalysis's wider debt figure. Debt-to-equity is 1.26 and the Altman Z-score, a formula that gauges financial distress, is 1.4, low by that model's conventions. It means a shortfall in EBITDA hits the share price harder than the same shortfall would at a less indebted peer.
02
The dividend exceeds GAAP earnings. $2.60 paid over the last twelve months against $2.33 of GAAP EPS gives a 112% payout ratio, and the board raised the quarterly rate to $0.66 on November 4. On adjusted EPS of $4.00 the new $2.64 annual rate is a payout of about 66%. At $2.64 a share the dividend costs about $1.2B a year, more than the last twelve months' free cash flow. Which view you take depends on whether you think the adjustments are real. A 5.79% yield is the market asking for that proof.
03
Volumes are barely positive. September-quarter organic volumes were up 1%, with Flexibles flat. Synergies can lift margins for three years. They cannot create demand.
04
Input-cost and geopolitical shocks. This is the risk that showed up in the quarter. Higher energy and distribution costs forced the guidance cut, raw-material pass-through added $435M to sales, and management says the Middle East conflict still ties up about $500M of working capital. Resin-based packaging is exposed to oil-linked input prices, and the lag in passing costs through makes quarterly margins noisy.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the comparisons. First, the Berry combination (closed April 30, 2025) inflated every year-over-year growth rate for fiscal 2026 and adds purchase-accounting amortisation that separates GAAP EPS ($2.33) from adjusted EPS ($4.00). The September 2026 quarter is the first with all of Berry in both years. Second, the 1-for-5 reverse split, approved by shareholders on November 6, 2025 and effective January 14, 2026, cut shares outstanding from about 2.3 billion to about 461 million. Every pre-2026 per-share figure in this report (prices, dividends, EPS) has been multiplied by 5 to be comparable. Third, the fiscal year-end is moving from June 30 to December 31. The next reported "year" is a six-month transition period to December 31, 2026, guided at adjusted EPS of $1.70–$1.80, cut on November 4 from $1.80–$1.90. The FY26 GAAP comparison is also flattered by Q4 FY25, which was a GAAP loss (−$0.10 diluted EPS) in the quarter the deal closed.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 11.2x adjusted earnings and 10.9x forward for a global consumer-packaging leader that sells mostly into food, pet care and healthcare demand and pays a 5.79% dividend while you wait. The integration is on plan: $60M of synergies in the September quarter on top of $285M in fiscal 2026, against a $650M three-year target. Volumes rose 1%. Management describes the guidance cut as timing: prices catch up to energy and freight costs by the end of December, 2027 still gets double-digit adjusted EPS growth, and the December quarter should bring about $800M of free cash flow. Leverage is guided to about 3.6x by December and about 3x by the end of 2027. The average analyst target of $49.71 sits about 11% above the price. If synergies land on schedule, the gap between GAAP and adjusted EPS narrows and the market no longer needs a near-6% yield to hold the stock.

Bear Case

GAAP EPS of $2.33 doesn't cover a $2.60 dividend, and twelve-month free cash flow of $772M falls well short of the roughly $1.2B the new dividend rate costs each year. Net debt rose to 3.76x EBITDA in the September quarter, the Altman Z-score is 1.4, and organic volume growth is 1%. StockAnalysis's consensus has revenue growing 1.8% next year. Almost all of the EPS growth story is cost synergies, and cost synergies run out: once the $650M is in, the company is a slow-growth packaging maker with a large debt load, exposed to resin prices and single-use-plastics regulation. The November cut took $0.10 off a six-month forecast less than two months before the period ends. JPMorgan moved to Hold in August and Deutsche Bank followed the day after this print. The stock closed below its May 2025 level of $45.55, and its total return since November 2021 is about zero even with every dividend reinvested. On this view, the high yield reflects the market's view of risk, not a bargain.

Synergy shortfall with leverage still high. The investment case assumes $650M of savings by FY28. A miss would hit a company carrying $14.84B of debt, where the dividend already exceeds GAAP earnings.
02
Volume stagnation. September-quarter organic volumes were up only 1%, with Flexibles flat. If consumer demand weakens again, as in the FY2023–24 downturn, synergies get spent covering lost volume instead of growing earnings.
03
Input costs, geopolitics and regulation. Energy and distribution costs have already forced one guidance cut, and the Middle East conflict still ties up about $500M of working capital. Resin prices follow oil, pass-through lags, and rigid single-use plastics are a standing regulatory target. None of these would sink the company on its own, but together they keep quarterly results noisy.
Industry Commentary
Scale consolidation in consumer packaging
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Amcor has grown by merging: Bemis in June 2019, which brought the NYSE listing and S&P 500 membership, and Berry Global in April 2025. The logic is the same both times. Packaging makers compete on purchasing scale and plant utilisation, so the larger company buys resin more cheaply and fills its lines more fully. The cost is balance-sheet risk. Sales rose 57% in fiscal 2026, Berry's first full year in the numbers, and the deal pushed leverage to levels management is now working down. Consolidation is therefore the constraint on the business, not just its strategy: until leverage is near ~3x, capital goes to paying down debt and the dividend rather than growth.

Demand: staples-steady, but barely growing
ONGOING HEADWIND

Packaging volumes follow what people buy at the grocery store and the pharmacy. That demand is steady but grows slowly, and cyclical swings still make it lumpy: FY2024 revenue fell 7.2%. The latest data shows volumes up 1% in the September quarter, led by pet food and food service. StockAnalysis's consensus has revenue growing 1.8% next year, so analysts expect little help from the top line. Any volume beat is upside.

The high dividend: two ways to read it
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $44.87 AMCR yields 5.79%, trades at about 10.4x EV/EBITDA (StockAnalysis's 10.39x at the November 9 close, restated) and has a 0.54 beta. The bull framing: this is a staples-like income stream priced as if the merger were going wrong, when the evidence so far (synergies on plan, volumes up 1%) says it is going right. Once leverage falls below 3.5x, the market should need less yield to hold the stock. The bear framing: a GAAP payout ratio above 100%, debt/EBITDA near 4x and an Altman Z of 1.4 describe a stock whose yield is high because the market doubts it can be sustained. The way to tell which is right is not the P/E. It is two numbers reported every quarter: cumulative synergies against the $650M target, and leverage against the 3.6x December guide.
