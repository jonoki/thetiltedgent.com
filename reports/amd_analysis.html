01 Company Overview

Advanced Micro Devices designs high-performance CPUs (central processing units), GPUs (graphics processing units), and adaptive SoCs (system-on-chip) for data centers, PCs, gaming consoles, and embedded markets — it is fabless, outsourcing manufacturing primarily to TSMC. AMD posted FY2025 revenue of $34.6B, up 34% YoY, and has accelerated sharply in 2026: Q2 FY26 revenue hit a record $11.5B (+50% YoY) as Data Center revenue more than doubled. It is the #2 merchant supplier of both x86 server CPUs (record ~46% revenue share vs. Intel) and AI accelerators (an estimated 5–7% share vs. NVIDIA's ~80%), and at ~$766B in market value ranks as the world's ~#18 largest public company.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
x86 Duopoly License Chiplet & Packaging Lead TSMC Leading-Edge Access Annual GPU/CPU Cadence Hyperscaler Co-Design Deals Console Semi-Custom Lock-In Xilinx Adaptive Portfolio

AMD's moat rests on being one of only two companies licensed to build x86 processors — an architecture still running the vast majority of servers and PCs — combined with a durable execution lead over Intel: its chiplet-based design approach (pioneered with Zen in 2017) lets AMD mix TSMC's best process nodes per component, yielding better cost, yield, and cadence than Intel's monolithic designs have managed for nearly a decade. That execution converted into a record ~46% server CPU revenue share. In AI, the moat is thinner but compounding: an open-ecosystem counter-position to NVIDIA (ROCm software, UALink-over-Ethernet in Helios vs. proprietary NVLink, 288GB→HBM4 memory-capacity leadership per GPU), plus deep multi-year co-design relationships — OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Oracle have all committed to MI400/Helios deployments, with warrant structures that financially bind the two largest customers to AMD's success. The Xilinx portfolio adds a ~50%-share, high-margin adaptive-computing franchise with decade-long design-win lifecycles in defense and industrial.

Key Vulnerabilities

NVIDIA's CUDA software gravity. NVIDIA holds ~80% of AI accelerator revenue and its CUDA ecosystem remains the default for AI development; independent analyses suggest AMD GPUs achieve materially lower real-world utilization of theoretical peak performance than NVIDIA's, narrowing AMD's 30–50% hardware price advantage to a slimmer effective TCO (total cost of ownership) edge, especially in training.

Custom silicon (ASIC) encroachment. Hyperscaler in-house chips — Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium, Meta MTIA, largely built with Broadcom and Marvell — already represent an estimated 15–25% of accelerator compute and are growing faster than AMD's merchant share. Every ASIC dollar is a dollar carved out of the merchant GPU TAM AMD is chasing; ironically, Meta is simultaneously AMD's 6GW partner and an MTIA builder.

Customer concentration and circular deal structures. The OpenAI (6GW) and Meta (6GW) agreements carry warrants for up to 160M AMD shares each — up to ~20% combined potential dilution on ~1.63B shares out — and the commitments are "up to" milestones, not firm orders. A capex pause at two or three AI labs would hit both revenue expectations and sentiment; GAAP earnings will also absorb warrant-related charges as tranches vest.

Geopolitics and supply dependence. US export controls on MI308 China shipments cost AMD ~$800M in charges in Q2 2025 (~$440M net for the year), and rules can tighten again. As a fabless company, AMD depends on TSMC (largely Taiwan) for leading-edge wafers and CoWoS packaging, and on a tight HBM4 supply chain (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron) shared with NVIDIA.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

AMD is the only merchant silicon vendor with credible #2 positions in both server CPUs (record ~46% revenue share and rising) and AI accelerators, attacking a TAM management sizes at ~$1T by 2030 from just 5–7% share — small share gains move the needle enormously. Twelve-plus gigawatts of contracted "up to" commitments (OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, Oracle, Microsoft) de-risk the MI400/Helios ramp, Q3 guidance of $13B implies accelerating growth, and a 0.73 PEG with a near-debt-free balance sheet means the growth isn't expensively priced on growth-adjusted terms.

▼ Bear Case

At 43.5x forward earnings and 18.8x sales, perfection is priced into a business whose AI GPUs still deliver materially lower real-world utilization than NVIDIA's, whose customers are simultaneously building their own ASICs, and whose two anchor deals carry up to ~320M shares of combined warrant dilution (~20% of the float). The stock's 2.49 beta and history of 55–65% drawdowns mean any AI-capex digestion, HBM4/CoWoS supply slip, or China policy shock gets amplified violently — and Gaming (−31% YoY) shows what a downcycle looks like.

01
Execution risk on the MI400/Helios ramp. H2'26 numbers depend on flawless rack-scale delivery against constrained HBM4 and advanced-packaging supply; the "up to" gigawatt commitments only convert to revenue if deployment milestones are hit on schedule.
02
Competitive compression from both ends. NVIDIA's Rubin generation and CUDA lock-in above, hyperscaler custom ASICs (15–25% of accelerator compute and growing faster than AMD) below — AMD must out-execute both to keep its 5–7% share expanding.
03
Valuation + dilution + geopolitics stacked on a 2.5-beta stock. A 43x forward multiple leaves no cushion for warrant-vesting GAAP charges, renewed China export restrictions, or Taiwan/TSMC supply disruption — any of which could trigger drawdowns of the magnitude AMD holders have absorbed twice in five years.
  Industry Commentary: What's Moving Semis Right Now
HBM4 & Advanced Packaging Supply
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Every credible AI roadmap now bottlenecks on the same two inputs: HBM4 (high-bandwidth memory) from SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron, and TSMC CoWoS-class advanced packaging. AMD's Helios rack packs 31TB of HBM4 across 72 GPUs — memory capacity is literally its headline spec advantage (~50% more than NVIDIA's Rubin NVL72) — which makes AMD proportionally more exposed to HBM allocation than any rival. AMD has been courting Korean HBM suppliers directly, but NVIDIA's volume gives it purchasing priority; a single quarter of tight HBM4 allocation could push gigawatt-milestone revenue (and warrant-vesting optics) to the right.

Custom Silicon vs. Merchant GPUs
ONGOING HEADWIND

The AI accelerator market (~$200B+ in 2026, inference now ~two-thirds of spend) is splitting three ways: NVIDIA (~80% of revenue), custom ASICs co-designed with Broadcom/Marvell (an estimated 15–25% of compute — Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Meta MTIA), and AMD (~5–7%). The ASIC slice is growing faster than AMD's. The nuance: inference workloads reward AMD's memory-capacity and cost-per-token positioning (AMD claims ~30% more tokens per dollar vs. Rubin NVL72, per its own simulations), so the same inference shift funding ASICs also plays to AMD's strongest hand. Whether AMD's share lands nearer 5% or 15% of a $500B–$1T 2028–2030 TAM is essentially the entire bull/bear debate.

Vendor-Financed AI Capex: Tailwind with an Asterisk
EMERGING TAILWIND

2026's defining structure is the equity-linked compute deal: AMD granted OpenAI and Meta warrants of up to 160M shares each, tied to gigawatt deployments and stock-price hurdles — customers literally paid in upside to secure supply. Bull framing: this contractually aligns the largest AI buyers with AMD's roadmap for multiple generations, converting a speculative share-gain story into scheduled deployments (~$100B+ potential revenue), with vesting hurdles that only pay customers if shareholders win too. Bear framing: it's circular financing reminiscent of 1990s vendor financing — revenue recognized from customers being paid in equity, up to ~20% potential dilution, and concentration in a handful of unprofitable-or-capex-straining AI labs whose spending is itself the industry's biggest single point of failure. Both framings are true; position sizing is where they get reconciled.
