01 Company Overview

Advanced Micro Devices designs high-performance CPUs (central processing units), GPUs (graphics processing units), and adaptive SoCs (system-on-chip) for data centers, PCs, gaming consoles, and embedded markets — it is fabless, outsourcing manufacturing primarily to TSMC. AMD posted FY2025 revenue of $34.6B, up 34% YoY, and has accelerated sharply in 2026: Q3 FY26 revenue hit a record $13.4B (+45% YoY) as Data Center revenue nearly doubled to $8.5B, and management guides Q4 to about $17B and 2027 revenue growth above 80%. It is the #2 seller of both x86 server CPUs (34.5% of units in Q2 2026 per Mercury Research, behind Intel, and 46.4% counting only its EPYC chips against Intel's Xeon SP) and AI accelerators (an estimated 7–10% of 2026 sales vs. NVIDIA's ~75%, per Silicon Analysts), and at ~$736B in market value ranks #15 in the S&P 500.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
x86 Duopoly Chiplet & Packaging Lead TSMC Leading-Edge Access Annual GPU/CPU Cadence Hyperscaler Co-Design Deals Console Semi-Custom Lock-In Xilinx Adaptive Portfolio

AMD's moat rests on being one of the two companies that make nearly all x86 processors, the chip design still running most servers and PCs, combined with an execution lead over Intel: its chiplet approach (building a processor from several smaller chips, first used with Zen in 2017) lets AMD mix TSMC's best manufacturing processes per component, with better cost and a steadier product schedule than Intel has managed for much of the past decade. That execution lifted AMD to 34.5% of x86 server units in Q2 2026 (Mercury Research). In AI, the moat is thinner but growing: an open alternative to NVIDIA (ROCm software, open networking standards in Helios instead of NVIDIA's own NVLink, and more memory per GPU: 432GB of HBM4 on the MI455X vs. 288GB on NVIDIA's Rubin), plus multi-year relationships in which customers help shape the chips — OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Oracle all had their first Helios racks running by the Q3 report, with warrants that give OpenAI and Meta a financial stake in AMD's success. The Xilinx business adds high-margin adaptive chips (41% segment operating margin in Q3) sold into long-lived defense and industrial programs.

Key Vulnerabilities

NVIDIA's CUDA software gravity. NVIDIA holds an estimated ~75% of 2026 AI accelerator revenue (Silicon Analysts) and its CUDA software remains the default for AI development, so any edge AMD offers on hardware price or memory has to outweigh the cost of switching software.

Custom silicon (ASIC) encroachment. Hyperscaler in-house chips — Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium, Meta MTIA, largely built with Broadcom and Marvell — are estimated at about 15–19% of 2026 accelerator sales for those two suppliers combined (Silicon Analysts), and Broadcom's AI chip revenue rose 221% in its latest quarter. Every ASIC dollar is a dollar carved out of the GPU market AMD is chasing; ironically, Meta is simultaneously AMD's 6GW partner and an MTIA builder.

Customer concentration and circular deal structures. The OpenAI (6GW) and Meta (6GW) agreements carry warrants for up to 160M AMD shares each — up to ~20% combined potential dilution on ~1.64B shares out — and the commitments are "up to" milestones, not firm orders. A pause in capital spending at two or three AI labs would hit both revenue expectations and sentiment. The accounting has already started: Q3 GAAP earnings absorbed a $1.1B non-cash charge on the warrants, and CFO Jean Hu said those quarterly adjustments are expected to increase. No warrant shares had vested by Sep 26 (10-Q), and AMD says it will guide the share-count effect in 2027.

Geopolitics and supply dependence. US export controls on MI308 China shipments cost AMD ~$800M in charges in Q2 2025 (~$440M net for the year), and rules can tighten again. As a fabless company, AMD depends on TSMC (largely Taiwan) for leading-edge wafers and CoWoS packaging (the advanced process that joins chips to their memory), and on a tight HBM4 supply chain (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron) shared with NVIDIA. Dr. Su told analysts on the Q3 call, "It is true that demand is higher than supply."

A shrinking PC market and cash tied up in inventory. Management is planning for 2027 PC unit shipments to fall by a double-digit percentage as memory costs lift system prices. Inventory rose about $3B in Q3 to $11.5B, which cut free cash flow to $554M from $1.53B a year earlier, and AMD expects to keep investing in inventory and capacity through 2027.

Notable One-Off Items: Three distortions matter when reading AMD's GAAP numbers. (1) Customer warrants: the OpenAI and Meta agreements each include performance warrants for up to 160M shares. 08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

AMD holds the #2 position among chip sellers in both server CPUs (34.5% of x86 units in Q2 2026, and AMD says it gained revenue share in Q3) and AI accelerators, attacking a data-center market management sizes at ~$1T by 2030 from an estimated 7–10% of AI accelerators — small share gains move the needle enormously. Q3 was the first revenue quarter for MI455X and Helios, all five lead customers have racks running, Q4 is guided to about $17B, and management expects 2027 revenue growth above 80% with the supply secured to support it. With a 0.39 PEG and $15.8B of cash and short-term investments against $7.1B of debt, the growth isn't expensively priced if those numbers land.

▼ Bear Case

At 25.5x FY2027 consensus adjusted earnings, 55.5x FY2026's and 16.2x trailing sales, a lot of good news is priced into a business still chasing NVIDIA's CUDA software lead, whose customers are also building their own ASICs, and whose two anchor deals carry up to ~320M shares of combined warrant dilution (~20% of shares outstanding). The stock's 2.54 beta and two falls of more than 60% in five years, plus the 37.7% slide into Nov 5, mean any pause in AI spending, HBM4/CoWoS supply slip, or China policy shock gets amplified — and Gaming (−40% YoY) and a planned double-digit decline in 2027 PC units show what a downcycle looks like. Free cash flow fell to $554M in Q3 as inventory rose by about $3B.

01
Execution risk on the MI400/Helios ramp.   Industry Commentary: What's Moving Semis Right Now
HBM4 & Advanced Packaging Supply
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Every credible AI roadmap now bottlenecks on the same two inputs: HBM4 (high-bandwidth memory) from SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron, and TSMC CoWoS-class advanced packaging. AMD's Helios rack packs 31TB of HBM4 across 72 GPUs — memory capacity is literally its headline spec advantage (~50% more than NVIDIA's Rubin NVL72) — which leaves AMD especially exposed to HBM supply. A single quarter of tight HBM4 allocation could push gigawatt-milestone revenue (and the timing of warrant vesting) later. On the Q3 call Dr. Su said AMD is working with customers to share rising memory and component costs, and that it has secured the wafers, substrates, packaging and memory for much higher growth next year.

Custom Silicon vs. Merchant GPUs
ONGOING HEADWIND

The AI accelerator market is splitting three ways, on Silicon Analysts' 2026 estimates: NVIDIA (~75% of revenue), custom ASICs co-designed with Broadcom and Marvell (about 15–19% — Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Meta MTIA), and AMD (~7–10%). The ASIC slice is growing fast: Broadcom's AI chip revenue rose 221% in its latest quarter. The nuance: running AI models rewards memory capacity and low cost per answer, where Dr. Su says AMD continues to offer "very strong advantages" on tokens per dollar, so the same shift funding ASICs also plays to AMD's strongest hand. Whether AMD's share stays in the single digits or climbs well beyond them is essentially the entire bull/bear debate.

Vendor-Financed AI Spending: Tailwind with an Asterisk
EMERGING TAILWIND

2026's defining structure is the equity-linked compute deal: AMD granted OpenAI and Meta warrants of up to 160M shares each, tied to gigawatt deployments and stock-price hurdles — customers literally paid in upside to secure supply. Bull framing: this contractually aligns the largest AI buyers with AMD's roadmap for multiple generations, converting a speculative share-gain story into scheduled deployments, with vesting hurdles that only pay customers if shareholders win too. Bear framing: it's circular financing reminiscent of 1990s vendor financing — revenue recognized from customers being paid in equity, up to ~20% potential dilution, and concentration in a handful of AI labs that are unprofitable or spending heavily, whose spending is itself the industry's biggest single point of failure. Both framings are true; position sizing is where they get reconciled.
