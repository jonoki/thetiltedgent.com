01 Company Overview

AMETEK is not really a product company. It is a capital allocation machine wrapped around a manufacturing operating system: it buys small, market-leading makers of niche instruments and electromechanical devices — analytical instruments, precision metrology, power monitoring, aerospace sensors, medical device components — applies a standardised playbook of sourcing, facility consolidation and low-cost-region shared services, and reinvests the resulting cash into the next deal. FY2025 revenue was $7.40B, up 6.6% from $6.94B; the trailing twelve months through September 2026 is $8.19B, and Q3 2026 sales were a record $2.22B, up 17%. The company ranks #221 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $52.44B.

The central debate on this page is not whether the operating model works — the margin record says it does — but what you are paying for it, and which engine is actually driving the number. AME trades at 32.3× trailing GAAP earnings and roughly 26.9× the 2026 consensus adjusted figure, a persistent premium to the industrial average. Reported revenue growth of 17% in Q3 2026 looks like a growth company; organic growth was 10%, with roughly seven points of the reported 17% coming from acquisitions, and management's own full-year guide is now mid-teens total on high single-digit organic. Over a longer window, revenue grew 7.5% a year from FY2021 to FY2025 with acquisitions in every one of those years, so the growth from the existing businesses was lower than that. A reader who sees only the reported number will misjudge this business entirely. Every growth figure below is therefore split into organic, acquisition and FX wherever the company discloses the split — and flagged where it does not.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
NICHE MARKET LEADERSHIP SPECIFICATION LOCK-IN REGULATORY-MANDATED DEMAND ACQUISITION PLAYBOOK LOW-COST SHARED SERVICES PRICING POWER OVER INFLATION STRONG CASH GENERATION

The moat is smallness, repeated. Each AMETEK business is a leader in a market too small to attract a large competitor's capital and too technical for a cheap one to enter — a mass spectrometer for a specific chemical process, a sensor qualified into a specific aircraft, a component designed into a specific orthopaedic implant. Once a product is written into a customer's specification or a regulatory filing, replacing it costs the customer requalification time and risk, not just a purchase order. That is why management could say on the Q2 call (Aug 2026) that it "offset inflation and tariffs a little more than price", and on the Q3 call (Nov 3) the CEO said: "Our inflation number was about 3% and pricing was in excess of that." — pricing power here is a function of switching cost and mission-criticality, not brand.

Layered on top is the second, more valuable moat: the acquisition system itself. Many niche manufacturers are family- or private-equity-owned. AMETEK buys them, plugs them into global sourcing and a low-cost-region service footprint, and moves them toward its own high-20s margin. The half-point gap between the company's 26.8% adjusted operating margin and its 27.3% core margin in Q3 2026 is the visible cost of newly acquired assets; EMG's move from 20.0% (FY2024) to a 27.2% Q3 2026 adjusted margin is the visible payoff once they mature. The balance sheet — gross debt/EBITDA of 2.3× at September 30, 2026 (net 2.1×), up from 0.8× and 0.6× at June 30 because Indicor was bought largely with debt, with a $3.5B revolver running to June 2031 — is not a defensive feature. It is the fuel tank, and about $5B of it has just been used.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The model requires deals, and deals require sellers at sane prices. Organic growth alone does not support the price investors pay for each dollar of earnings (see Section 04). If the private-market price of high-quality niche instrument businesses rises — and Danaher, Roper, Fortive and a wall of private equity are hunting the same assets — AMETEK either overpays or stops growing at this rate. There is no third option that preserves the current growth rate.
▸
Integration risk scales with deal size. AMETEK's competence is proven on small add-on deals. Indicor closed on Aug 26, 2026 at a total purchase price of $5.15B (10-Q), roughly 10% of today's $52.44B market cap, and brought a group of businesses into both segments at once — well beyond its usual pace of small deals. The 10–12% synergy target (over three years, CFO on the Q3 call) is management's number, not a realised one, and the purchase accounting is preliminary. A large deal that under-delivers hurts the premium investors pay for management's record as much as the earnings.
▸
Cyclicality hidden by the acquisition line. Look at FY2025's first half: Q1 2025 revenue fell 0.24% YoY and Q2 2025 grew 2.49%. That was the underlying industrial cycle showing through, and it was largely papered over by deals within a couple of quarters. The reported line is smoother than the business.
▸
Tariffs and supply chain. Management says it prices ahead of inflation and tariffs (Q2 and Q3 calls) — "a little more than price" — but that is a running battle, not a settled advantage. A step-change in trade costs, or a customer base that pushes back after several years of price, would compress the core margin expansion the whole story rests on.
▸
The GAAP-to-adjusted wedge is structural, not occasional. FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS was $6.40 against adjusted $7.43 — a 16% gap; Q3 2026 was $1.85 GAAP against $2.21 adjusted, a 19% gap. For a serial acquirer, acquisition-related intangible amortisation is a permanent feature of the income statement, because there is always another deal. Anyone valuing AME on the adjusted number is valuing it before charging for the acquisitions that produce the growth.

Notable One-Off Items

For most companies this section lists exceptions. For AMETEK it describes the business model, which is precisely why it deserves attention.

Acquisition-related costs are perpetual, not one-off. FY2025 carried $37.3M of pretax Faro Technologies acquisition costs. Q2 2026 acquisition-related costs included Indicor bridge financing fees, and the deal then closed in Q3. The gap between GAAP and adjusted EPS was $1.03 in FY2025 ($6.40 vs $7.43, 16%) and $0.36 in Q3 2026 ($1.85 vs $2.21, 19%). A serial acquirer always has a deal in flight, so "excluding acquisition-related costs and intangible amortisation" is not a normalisation — it is a description of a cost the company intends to keep incurring forever.

The Q3 2026 orders number is genuinely exceptional and should not be annualised. Orders of $2.65B, up 35% (17% organic), produced a book-to-bill of about 1.19 and a record backlog of $4.54B, up 27% since the start of the year; the gap between 35% and 17% is orders from acquisitions and currency. Backlog builds of this shape can reflect customers ordering earlier in a lengthening lead-time environment as much as pure demand; the tell will be whether shipments over the next three quarters convert it at the guided rate.

The FY2025 comparison base is soft. Q1 2025 revenue was −0.24% YoY and Q2 2025 +2.49%. Part of the striking 2026 growth — Q1 +11.3%, Q2 +15.0%, Q3 +17% — comes from an easy comparison. Measured over the two years combined, growth is good rather than extraordinary.

Indicor is only partly in the numbers on this page. The deal closed on August 26, so Q3 includes about five weeks of it: a little over $100M of sales. A full quarter of its sales, the interest on the new debt and the amortisation of the acquired intangibles arrive in Q4; the CEO expects about $0.08 a share of 2026 earnings from it and guides Q4 sales up in the low-20% range.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The question, stated properly. AMETEK trades at 32.3× trailing GAAP earnings, 26.9× the 2026 consensus, and a PEG of 1.83. That premium is defensible only if the earnings stream is both durable and reproducible. Durability is not in dispute: 26.6% operating margins, 20% net margins, 11.5% ROIC, 0.97 beta, a 25.3% maximum monthly-close drawdown (in 2022), and $4.54B of backlog at a 1.19 book-to-bill. Reproducibility is the whole argument.

Split the growth and the argument becomes concrete. Reported Q3 2026 revenue growth was 17%. Organic was 10% (CEO, Q3 call) — so about 40% of the headline was acquired. Within that, EIG grew 16% and EMG 20% reported; on the call the CEO put EMG's organic growth at 13%, with acquisitions adding about seven points. Management's FY2026 guide is now mid-teens total on high single-digit organic, so roughly half of this year's growth is bought, with Indicor adding the most in Q4 (sales guided up in the low-20% range). The Q3 release and call give no separate FX figure; the CEO's 10 points organic and seven from acquisitions add up to the full 17%. Now go back one year, to the period the acquisitions were not covering: Q1 2025 revenue −0.24%, Q2 2025 +2.49%. That is the underlying business in a soft stretch for industrial companies.

So: does organic growth justify the price, or does the return depend on continued successful M&A? The honest answer is the second, and it should be said plainly rather than hedged. Over FY2021–FY2025 revenue compounded at 7.5% ($5.55B → $7.40B) with acquisitions in every one of those years; GAAP EPS compounded at 10.8% ($4.25 → $6.40), the difference being margin expansion and a modestly shrinking share count. An investor paying 26.9× forward earnings for a business whose organic growth is guided to high single digits this year is, arithmetically, paying for the acquisition engine — for management's continued ability to source niche businesses at sensible prices and lift their margins by several hundred basis points inside two years. The good news is that this is not a faith-based claim. It is measurable, and AMETEK publishes the measurement: EMG's segment margin went 20.0% (FY2024) → 23.3% (FY2025) → 27.2% (Q3 2026 adjusted). The half-point gap between company-wide adjusted and core margin is the current cost of newly acquired assets, and management put a 6–12 month clock on closing the FARO portion of it on the Q2 call. Watch those two numbers and you are watching the thesis directly.

The fuel tank, partly used. This is where AMETEK differs from most highly valued industrial companies, but the picture changed in Q3. Gross debt/EBITDA is 2.3×; net debt/EBITDA is 2.1× at September 30, 2026, against 0.8× and 0.6× at June 30. Total debt is $6.7B against $2.3B at year-end 2025 — including $4.0B of term loans, $975.7M drawn on the revolving credit facility (upsized to $3.5B, maturing June 2031) and $306.0M of commercial paper (10-Q) — and cash is $666M. Free cash flow runs at $1.93B TTM (data site), is guided at 115–120% of net income for 2026 against capex of about $160M, and the dividend consumes 19.2% of earnings. After the dividend (roughly $312M a year on 229.3M shares), that leaves about $1.6B a year of discretionary cash before any borrowing. The CFO put cash and available credit facilities at $2.7B on the Q3 call. That is a very different starting point from the 0.6× of the previous edition; the CEO's stated priorities are unchanged: acquisitions first, opportunistic buybacks second, the dividend third.

Where the premium could break. Not on demand — the backlog and orders make a 2027 revenue collapse implausible. It breaks if the price of the raw material rises. AMETEK, Danaher, Roper, Fortive and every mid-market private equity fund are competing for the same pool of niche instrument businesses. If purchase prices rise faster than the savings AMETEK can extract, the return on each new deal falls toward the cost of capital, the 11.5% company-wide ROIC drifts down, and the premium has nothing left to stand on — with no operating metric having missed. Indicor, closed on Aug 26, 2026 at a total purchase price of $5.15B and financed with debt, is the first large-scale test of whether the playbook survives being scaled up. A 10–12% cost synergy target achieved on a deal of that size would arguably justify the premium outright. A miss would change how investors price more than one quarter.

Bull Case

A proven operating system pointed at an acquisition environment the CEO called constructive on the Q3 call. Q3 2026 delivered 17% sales growth (10% organic), a 26.8% adjusted operating margin with core margin up 110 basis points, adjusted EPS +17% and a guidance raise to $8.53–$8.57, with orders +35% (+17% organic) producing a record $4.54B backlog at a 1.19 book-to-bill. Aerospace and defence and automation both grew mid-teens organically. Margins prove the playbook works — EMG went from 20.0% to 27.2% in two years — and free cash flow was a record $527.4M in the quarter, 124% of net income. Pricing ran ahead of inflation (CEO: inflation about 3%, pricing in excess of that). Beta 0.97 and a 25.3% worst monthly-close drawdown in five years: market-like volatility by the numbers.

Bear Case

You are paying 32.3× trailing GAAP earnings (26.9× this year's consensus) for a business whose reported growth is roughly 40% bought and whose organic growth is guided to high single digits for the year. In early 2025, with the acquisition line not covering, revenue actually declined (Q1 2025, −0.24%). The GAAP-to-adjusted wedge is 16–19% every period and is permanent by construction, because a serial acquirer always has a deal in flight. ROIC of 11.5% and ROE of 14.7% are good, not the 20%+ a premium multiple usually implies. Total debt nearly tripled to $6.7B and net debt to EBITDA went from 0.6× to 2.1× in one quarter to buy Indicor; the 10–12% synergy figure is a target, not a result, and the purchase accounting is preliminary. The reaction to a clean beat and raise — down 10.3% over the two sessions after the Nov 3 close — shows how little room a premium price leaves; Mizuho and Baird cut targets after the print, and six of 20 analysts rate the stock Hold.

01
Acquisition-price inflation. The model's return depends on buying high-margin niche manufacturers at prices where a 10–12% cost-synergy programme still creates value. Competing against Danaher, Roper, Fortive and private equity for the same assets, purchase prices are the one input AMETEK does not control. If they rise, the return on each new deal falls toward the cost of capital and a 1.83 PEG has no support — without a single quarter missing.

Only about five weeks of Indicor are in Q3, so Q4 is the first full quarter. Backlog quality and the comparison base. Orders +35% (17% organic) and a backlog up 27% since the start of the year are exceptional, and exceptional order books sometimes reflect customers ordering earlier into lengthening lead times rather than pure end demand. Meanwhile 2026 growth looks better against a base in which Q1 2025 revenue fell. If Q4 2026 and 2027 convert the backlog below the guided rate, the market discovers that the organic growth rate underneath was lower than it looked — which is exactly the number that does not support 26.9× forward earnings.
  Industry Commentary
Everyone Is Running the Same Playbook
ONGOING HEADWIND
Danaher pioneered the model — buy niche market leaders, apply a named business system, reinvest in more deals. Roper industrialised it and then migrated it toward software. Fortive was spun out of it. Emerson has been converting itself into a version of it. AMETEK's variant is the most narrowly manufacturing-focused of the group and, on the fetched data, the most consistently margined: 26.59% operating margin and 19.91% net margin on $8.19B of trailing revenue. But five well-capitalised strategics plus the entire mid-market private equity industry are now bidding for the same family-owned instrument businesses. The scarce resource in this sector is no longer operational skill — it is a reasonably priced seller. That competition is structural and it gets worse, not better, as each acquirer's balance sheet recovers.
Electrification, Grid and Semiconductor Capex
EMERGING TAILWIND
The instrumentation sector's demand is downstream of infrastructure spend, and three streams are running at once: grid modernisation (AMETEK's power businesses grew mid-single digits in Q3 2026 with orders up double digits on grid expansion and data-centre demand; on the Q2 call RTDS Technologies had booked a hyperscaler order for data-centre power-infrastructure simulation), semiconductor capacity (Zygo precision metrology designed into leading platforms; semiconductor-related sales up mid-teens and orders up more than 30% in Q3, on about 6% of the business, per MarketBeat's call summary), and aerospace & defence rebuild (mid-teens organic growth in Q3). Management called this, on the Q2 call, "the beginning stages of a multiyear infrastructure build-out." The honest caveat: these are individual businesses inside an $8.2B-revenue company, so the effect is a few points on the organic line, not a transformation.
Tariffs, Pricing, and Who Actually Has Power
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Tariff and input-cost inflation is the sector-wide test of whether "niche market leader" means anything. AMETEK's answer on the Q2 call was that it "offset inflation and tariffs a little more than price", (on the Q3 call, Nov 3, the CEO said "Our inflation number was about 3% and pricing was in excess of that.") — it recovered costs and kept a sliver, which is precisely what a company with specification lock-in and regulatory-mandated demand should be able to do, and what a commodity manufacturer cannot. Management attributes the ability to differentiated IP, high switching costs and mission-critical positioning. Bull framing: a company that out-prices tariffs while expanding core margin 110 basis points has demonstrated pricing power under live fire — that is the strongest possible evidence for the moat, and it justifies a premium to the sector. Bear framing: price-over-cost is a recurring negotiation, not a permanent right. Several consecutive years of price increases build customer resistance, and a step-change in trade costs would compress the core margin expansion that carries the entire valuation argument. The margin, not the demand, is where tariffs would show up.
