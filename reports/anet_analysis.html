01 Company Overview

Arista Networks designs and sells high-performance Ethernet switching, routing, and network software for AI clusters, cloud data centers, campus networks, and service-provider backbones — all running a single, unmodified software image called EOS (Extensible Operating System, a Linux-based network OS with a published state database). It has become the default merchant-silicon alternative to Cisco inside the hyperscale data center, and is now the principal Ethernet supplier to AI back-end fabrics that until recently were dominated by InfiniBand. FY2025 revenue was $9.01B, up 28.6% YoY, and the business has since accelerated: TTM revenue through Q2 2026 is $10.54B, up 32.6% YoY, with Q2 2026 alone at $3.04B (+37.7%) — the company's first $3 billion quarter. At roughly $265B of market capitalization Arista is about the 47th most valuable US-listed company and the 65th most valuable public company globally, remarkable for a firm with just 5,115 employees (roughly $2.06M of revenue per employee).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Single EOS Binary Image CloudVision Lock-In Merchant Silicon Leverage Hyperscaler Design-Ins 20-Year Codebase, No Forks $2.06M Revenue / Employee Debt-Free, $13.3B Net Cash

Arista's moat is architectural rather than legal. Because EOS is a single, unmodified software image running across every platform — from a 48-port campus switch to a 400-terabit AI spine — a hyperscaler qualifies the software once and inherits it everywhere, and Arista avoids the release-branch combinatorics that make Cisco's estate expensive to maintain. That structural choice is why 5,115 employees can support $10.5B of revenue and 43% operating margins while spending far less on R&D in absolute dollars than Cisco or Nvidia. The second layer is the qualification cycle itself: getting a switch into a Microsoft or Meta AI fabric takes 12–24 months of joint engineering, congestion-control tuning, and failure-mode testing; once in, displacing it mid-cluster is operationally reckless. The third layer is merchant silicon — by riding Broadcom's Tomahawk/Jericho roadmap rather than building custom ASICs, Arista converts a fixed cost into a variable one and gets to market on new speed grades (800G, now 1.6T) at least as fast as vertically integrated competitors, while keeping ROIC extraordinarily high. Finally, CloudVision converts hardware sales into a management-plane dependency with $5.10B of deferred revenue behind it. The moat's honest limit: Arista does not own the silicon, so anything Broadcom sells to Arista, Broadcom will also sell to a white-box ODM at a lower price.

Customer Concentration — the dominant single risk

Microsoft and Meta have each individually crossed the 10%-of-revenue disclosure threshold — approximately 20.5% and 14.6% respectively in FY2024 per Arista's 10-K, i.e. roughly a third of the company in two customers. With Cloud & AI Titans now an estimated ~54% of revenue, that concentration has almost certainly deepened in 2026, not eased. These are also the most sophisticated buyers alive: they design their own racks, publish their own switch specs (SONiC/OCP), and negotiate with full knowledge of Arista's bill of materials. A single one of them moving 20% of a build to white-box removes more revenue than Arista's entire Providers sector.

NVIDIA Vertical Integration

Nvidia sells Spectrum-X Ethernet and NVLink/InfiniBand as part of an integrated rack-scale system (GB200/GB300 NVL72 and successors). When the GPU vendor bundles the fabric, ships it pre-validated, and warrants end-to-end performance, the incremental friction of buying a third-party spine rises. Nvidia's networking line is already running at a $25B+ annualized rate and growing faster than Arista's entire business. Arista's counter — open Ethernet, multi-vendor GPU support, and the UEC standard — is credible, but it is a fight against the highest-margin, best-capitalized company in the supply chain.

White-Box / Merchant Silicon Commoditization

Arista's chips come from Broadcom. So do Celestica's, Accton's, and Quanta's. When a hyperscaler runs SONiC (an open-source network OS originally from Microsoft) on an ODM box, it captures most of Arista's hardware value at a fraction of the gross margin. This is why gross margin fell from 64.06% in FY2025 to 62.93% in Q2 2026 despite record volumes — mix and pricing pressure at the titan tier is real and structural. The bull rebuttal is that AI fabrics are hard enough that operators pay for EOS; the bear rebuttal is that this was said about front-end networks too.

Valuation & Cyclicality of the Capex Cycle

At a 66.6× trailing and 45.3× forward P/E, the stock discounts several more years of 30%+ growth. Arista's revenue is 100% derived from other companies' capital budgets, and its two largest customers are simultaneously its largest concentration and the most-watched capex lines in the S&P 500. A single quarter in which Microsoft or Meta signals digestion — as happened in early 2025 and again in Q1 2026 — has historically taken 25–35% off the stock in weeks, regardless of Arista's own reported results.

Notable One-Off Items

Four distortions are worth carrying in your head when you look at the reported numbers:

1. Tariffs. The 2025–2026 US tariff regime on imported electronics and components is a live cost-of-goods headwind. Management has repeatedly framed the ~63% gross-margin guide as absorbing tariff costs; the exact dollar amount has not been separately quantified in Arista's public commentary, so it is embedded in COGS rather than disclosed as a one-off. Treat the FY2025→2026 margin decline as roughly half mix, half tariff/input cost.

2. Inventory and purchase commitments. Inventory sits at $2.54B and Arista carries substantial non-cancellable purchase commitments to lock in Broadcom silicon and optics supply for AI builds. This is a deliberate bet on demand. If a hyperscaler defers, the risk is an inventory write-down and a purchase-commitment charge landing in a single quarter — the same mechanism that produced a large charge in the 2022–2023 supply-chain cycle.

3. Stock-based compensation. The GAAP/non-GAAP EPS gap was $0.07 in Q2 2026 ($0.95 vs $1.02), implying roughly $90M per quarter or ~$360M annualized of SBC. That is ~3.4% of revenue — modest by megacap-tech standards, but it means the headline 45× forward P/E is on a non-GAAP-adjusted consensus and the true GAAP multiple is higher.

4. Interest income is a real earnings line. With $13.34B in cash and short-term investments and zero debt, Arista earns roughly $500M+ of pre-tax interest income annually at current short rates — call it ~$0.32 of annual EPS, or ~10% of earnings. That is genuine, but it is rate-dependent, not operating income, and a meaningful easing cycle would quietly trim EPS growth.

5. Stock split. Arista executed a 4-for-1 stock split effective December 2024. Every price, EPS, and range figure on this page is split-adjusted. Pre-split quotes above $700 that you may encounter in older commentary correspond to roughly $175 on today's basis.

The five-year trajectory. On a split-adjusted basis ANET closed August 2021 at $23.10 and August 12, 2026 at $210.50 — a total return of roughly +811%, or about a 55% compound annual growth rate over five years, with no dividends. For scale, the S&P 500 returned on the order of +80% (roughly 12–13% annualized) over the same window; that index figure is an approximation and was not independently verified in this session. ANET therefore beat its benchmark by something like 7-to-1 cumulatively. But the path was violent. The stock spent all of 2022 dead money (−16% for the year, trading between $23 and $35), then compounded relentlessly from the AI inflection in mid-2023, then delivered three separate drawdowns of 27%+ inside eighteen months. A 1.61 beta undersells the experience: this is a stock that has repeatedly given back a year of gains in six weeks and then made new highs within two quarters.

Relative performance. Against the S&P 500, ANET's roughly +811% five-year total return compares with an index return on the order of +80% — outperformance of roughly 730 percentage points, or about 43 points of annualized alpha. Against its own sector the gap is nearly as wide: Cisco, the closest large-cap comparable, has compounded at a fraction of that pace (7.4% TTM revenue growth versus Arista's 32.6%), and the broader Computer Hardware industry trades at a 31× weighted P/E with a 12.9% aggregate profit margin against Arista's 66.6× and 38.4%. The uncomfortable inference for a buyer today is that essentially all of the relative outperformance came from two sources — a tripling of revenue and a tripling of the multiple — and only one of those can plausibly repeat.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Arista is the pure-play toll booth on AI networking at the exact moment Ethernet won the back-end fabric argument against InfiniBand, and growth is accelerating — from +28.6% in FY2025 to +32.6% TTM to +37.7% in Q2 2026, with FY2026 guided to roughly $12.6B (~+40%). It does this with 63% gross margins, a 38.4% net margin, $5.16B of free cash flow, zero debt, $13.3B of cash, and $5.10B of deferred revenue underwriting the forward guide. The 1.6T speed transition is the next share-gain window, campus and software diversify away from the titans, and at 2.05 PEG the multiple is not actually extended relative to the growth being delivered.

Bear Case

You are paying 66.6× trailing earnings for a hardware company whose revenue is roughly a third concentrated in two customers, both of which design their own switches and both of which can pause a build with one budget meeting. Gross margin is already eroding (64.1% → 62.9%) under titan mix and tariffs, Nvidia is bundling a validated Ethernet fabric into every rack it ships, and white-box ODMs buy the same Broadcom silicon Arista does. With 30 of 30 analysts at Buy or better and short interest at 1.02%, positioning is maximally crowded — there is no marginal bull left to convert, and history says the drawdowns come 30% at a time.

01
Customer concentration. Microsoft (~20.5%) and Meta (~14.6%) of FY2024 revenue — roughly a third of the company — and Cloud & AI Titans has since grown to an estimated ~54% of revenue. There is no diversification argument that survives one of those two cutting orders; the Enterprise and Providers sectors combined are smaller than the top-two exposure.
02
Nvidia's vertical bundle and merchant-silicon commoditization. Nvidia ships Spectrum-X pre-integrated with its rack-scale GPU systems at a $25B+ annualized networking run-rate, while Celestica, Accton, and Quanta buy the identical Broadcom chips Arista uses and sell them at ODM margins with open-source SONiC on top. Arista's differentiation is EOS and CloudVision — real, but software defending a hardware price point is a compressing position, visible already in the 113 basis points of gross-margin decline from FY2025 to Q2 2026.
03
Valuation compression on any capex wobble. At 45.3× forward earnings with consensus modelling +38% EPS growth and 100% of analysts at Buy, the stock is priced for continuation with no margin for error. Arista has now had three separate 27%+ drawdowns since January 2025 — none of them caused by its own results, all of them caused by sentiment on someone else's capital budget. A 1.61 beta plus a 66× multiple means the mathematical downside on a growth disappointment is severe: reverting merely to Cisco-like 34× trailing would imply roughly a 50% price decline at unchanged earnings.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Matters Right Now
Ethernet Won the AI Back-End
EMERGING TAILWIND
Two years ago the AI back-end network — the fabric connecting GPUs to each other during training — was overwhelmingly InfiniBand, a proprietary Nvidia/Mellanox technology. That argument is effectively over: the Ultra Ethernet Consortium standardized the congestion-control and lossless-delivery features that InfiniBand used to monopolize, and hyperscalers preferred a fabric they could source from multiple vendors. Arista is the single largest beneficiary: back-end networking was essentially zero revenue for it in 2023 and is now the primary driver of a 37.7% growth quarter. The estimated AI networking TAM (back-end plus front-end) is roughly $40B by 2028 growing at a ~35% CAGR — larger than Arista's entire current addressable base. The catch is that Nvidia read the same memo and pivoted Spectrum-X to Ethernet, so Arista won the protocol war and now has to win the vendor war inside it.
The Hyperscaler Capex Cycle Is the Whole Model
ONGOING HEADWIND
Arista has no consumer business, no recurring-usage revenue of consequence, and no captive installed base that spends through a downturn. Roughly 54% of revenue is estimated to come from a handful of companies whose combined 2026 AI capital expenditure runs into the hundreds of billions and is set annually by boards that read the same headlines everyone else does. The empirical record is unambiguous: the −33% move in February–March 2025 and the −27% move from November 2025 to March 2026 were both triggered by hyperscaler capex sentiment, not by anything Arista reported — in both windows Arista's own revenue growth was above 27%. Networking is also a late-cycle line item within a data-center build: GPUs are ordered first and switches are installed as clusters come online, which means Arista's revenue lags the capex signal by two to three quarters in both directions. That lag flatters the numbers on the way down and delays the recovery on the way up.
Merchant Silicon Cuts Both Ways — Bull vs. Bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Arista's decision not to build ASICs is why it earns 38.4% net margins on 5,115 employees and why it reached 800G volume early — Broadcom does the silicon R&D, Arista does the software and the systems engineering. The bull framing: that asset-light model produces ~$2.06M of revenue per employee, ~285% reported ROIC (~28–30% on total equity), $5.16B of free cash flow, and the ability to ride every Tomahawk/Jericho generation without a fabrication bet — with EOS and CloudVision as the durable differentiator that a white-box vendor cannot copy. The bear framing: Broadcom sells the same chip to Celestica and Accton, Microsoft open-sourced SONiC precisely to commoditize the software layer, and Nvidia now bundles a validated fabric with the GPUs. In that world Arista's 63% gross margin is a spread that competitors are structurally incentivised to attack, and the 113 basis points already lost between FY2025 and Q2 2026 is the first data point in a long series rather than a mix artifact. Which framing is right is, more than growth or TAM, the actual question in this stock at 66× earnings.
