01 Company Overview

Aon plc is one of the two dominant global insurance and reinsurance brokers, sitting between commercial clients who need to transfer risk and the insurers and capital markets who underwrite it. It earns commissions and fees for placing property & casualty and reinsurance cover, and consulting/advisory fees in health benefits and retirement/wealth — an asset-light, capital-efficient model that carries no underwriting risk on its own balance sheet. The company reports through two segments, Risk Capital and Human Capital, but manages and discloses growth through four solution lines: Commercial Risk, Reinsurance, Health and Wealth.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $17.181B, up 9% year over year from $15.698B, with organic revenue growth of 6% — the metric the market actually trades on, since it strips out acquisitions, disposals and currency. Adjusted operating margin expanded to 32.4% from 31.5%, adjusted diluted EPS rose 9% to $17.07, and free cash flow grew 14% to $3.218B. At a $73.9B market cap Aon is roughly the 344th most valuable listed company in the world and the #2 broker globally behind Marsh & McLennan.

The most recent quarter (Q2 2026, reported July 29, 2026) showed revenue of $4.246B, up 2% reported but +5% organic, with all four solution lines each growing 5% organically. Adjusted operating margin was 28.9%, up 70 bps; adjusted EPS of $3.81 rose 9% and edged consensus by a penny, while revenue of $4.246B fell short of the ~$4.28B consensus. The shares slipped on the print.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Aon's moat is a scale-and-data flywheel that is very hard to rebuild. Global corporates and insurance carriers need a broker that can place a program across dozens of jurisdictions and hundreds of markets simultaneously; only two or three firms can do that, and the placement volume Aon sees gives it a proprietary view of loss experience, pricing and terms that it monetises back to clients as analytics and catastrophe modelling. Retention is structurally high because switching brokers mid-program is operationally disruptive and risks coverage gaps, and because the broker's institutional knowledge of a client's claims history is genuinely difficult to transfer. The economics are exceptional: 48.2% gross margin, 22.3% net margin, 44.7% ROE and 16.1% ROIC on a business that requires essentially no fixed capital and takes no underwriting risk. Aon Business Services — the shared technology, data and operations layer sitting under both segments — is the internal mechanism that converts that scale into margin, and management credits it with the "operating leverage that funds growth investments." Layered on top is the 3x3 Plan, a three-year strategic roadmap to accelerate the Aon United strategy through targeted investment in talent, technology and analytics, coupled with the Accelerating Aon United restructuring program.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Commission revenue is levered to a pricing cycle Aon does not control. Roughly half of revenue sits in Commercial Risk, where a large share of income is a percentage of premium. Marsh's Global Insurance Market Index showed global commercial rates down 6% in Q2 2026, the eighth consecutive quarterly decline, with property down 12% and cyber down 4% (twelfth straight quarterly decline). Management says organic growth tracks nominal GDP more than price, and the record supports that so far — but the arithmetic of a shrinking premium base is a persistent drag, and there is no visible catalyst to turn the cycle while capacity remains abundant.

2. Balance sheet leverage from NFP. The $13.0B NFP deal was funded with $7.0B of cash and assumed liabilities plus $6.0B of Aon equity (19.0 million shares). Total debt peaked at $17.016B at end-2024 and had come down to $15.249B by end-2025, but at $15.87B TTM the debt-to-equity ratio of 1.63 is more than double the ~0.78 peer median. That is a real constraint on the pace of both buybacks and further M&A, and it means Aon's excellent ROE is partly an artefact of a thin equity base rather than pure operating quality.

3. Fiduciary investment income shrinks as rates fall. Brokers hold client premium in fiduciary accounts and keep the interest. That was a windfall in the high-rate years and is now reversing: full-year 2025 fiduciary investment income was $271 million, and Q2 2026 fell 12% year over year to $58 million, with management noting that "higher average balances were more than offset by lower interest rates." This is high-margin, essentially costless revenue, so every dollar lost drops straight out of operating income.

4. Key-person and execution risk around the CFO seat. Edmund Reese left as CFO effective immediately on Aug 17, 2026 — an abrupt departure mid-integration, less than three weeks after the Q2 print. The interim appointment of an internal planning executive with a strong external CV mitigates it, and guidance was reaffirmed, but an unplanned CFO change during a $13B integration and a multi-year restructuring program is exactly the kind of event that widens the range of outcomes on cost and cash-flow targets.

Notable One-Off Items

Aon's GAAP numbers carry meaningful non-recurring distortion, and the gap between the 25.3% FY2025 GAAP operating margin and the 32.4% adjusted operating margin is almost entirely these items:

Accelerating Aon United restructuring. FY2025 program expense was $365 million ($129M of it in Q4 2025). Cumulative savings reached $320 million by Q2 2026, with $25 million of net savings booked in the quarter contributing roughly 60 bps of margin expansion. Management is targeting a further $100 million of savings in 2026 en route to $450 million of total annual savings by 2027. Charges should taper as the program winds down, which is a mechanical GAAP tailwind into 2027.

NFP transaction and integration costs. $77 million in FY2025 (down to $11 million in Q4 2025) — meaningfully lower than the 2024 run-rate, indicating the acquisition-cost drag is largely behind the P&L.

NFP Wealth business disposal. Aon divested NFP's wealth arm; the resulting $267 million tax payment is the single reason Q2 2026 free cash flow fell 34% year over year to $483 million. Strip it out and cash generation was up. Do not extrapolate the Q2 FCF decline.

Currency. FX was a $0.09 per share tailwind in Q4 2025, and Aon guided to roughly +$0.36 per share in Q1 2026 and +$0.39 per share for full-year 2026. Around 2% of expected 2026 EPS is therefore currency, not operations — worth adjusting for when comparing adjusted EPS growth to organic revenue growth.

Reported-versus-organic gap. FY2025 reported revenue growth of 9.5% against 6% organic means roughly a third of headline growth came from acquisitions and currency. In Health Solutions the gap was far wider (15.1% reported vs 5% organic). No material litigation or impairment items were disclosed in the FY2025 or Q2 2026 releases reviewed for this note.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. Between 30 and 70 is ordinary two-way trading.

Five-year trajectory. AON closed August 2021 at $286.86 and August 19, 2026 at $348.43 — a price return of roughly +21.5% over five years, or approximately +27% total return once the ~0.9%/yr dividend is added back (dividend contribution is an estimate, not a verified reinvested figure). That is a compound annual price return of about 4.0% — a genuinely poor five-year outcome for a business that compounded earnings at a healthy clip, and the entire story is multiple compression plus a two-stage de-rating.

The shape is three distinct regimes. From late 2021 through late 2023 the stock chopped sideways in a roughly $270–$345 band while the hard P&C market lifted commission revenue and rising rates fattened fiduciary income — good fundamentals, no multiple expansion. From December 2023 (NFP announcement, $291.02) through February 2025 the stock ran hard to a $409.12 peak as investors bought the middle-market expansion story and margin expansion. Since February 2025 it has ground steadily lower to a $311.65 April 2026 trough — a −24% drawdown from the high — as the pricing cycle turned, fiduciary income began shrinking, and the NFP leverage sat visibly on the balance sheet. The last four months have retraced part of that, with July 2026 closing at $360.55 before the August pullback to $348.43.

Technically the stock is neutral: at $348.43 it is roughly on its 50-day moving average ($349.36) and ~3% above its 200-day ($337.43), mid-way through a $304.59–$382.34 52-week range. Neither the trend nor the momentum picture is making a statement right now.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Aon is compounding 5–6% organic revenue growth and expanding adjusted margins 70–80 bps a year through the worst commercial pricing environment in a decade — eight consecutive quarters of rate declines, property down 12% — which is the single strongest available evidence that the business is driven by exposure growth, new business and analytics rather than premium inflation. It does this at 48% gross margin, 22% net margin and 16.1% ROIC with no underwriting risk and almost no capital intensity, and it trades at 19.2× trailing and 17.3× forward earnings, a discount to both its peer median and the index. NFP added a genuine structural asset — a middle-market platform where commissions are higher and retention stickier — and $350M+ of further tuck-ins year to date extend it. Meanwhile debt is falling, restructuring savings ramp to $450M by 2027, and management is retiring stock at a record $1.1B half-year pace against an 18% payout ratio.

Bear Case

Roughly half of revenue is a percentage of a premium base that is shrinking, and there is no visible end to the soft market while capacity is abundant and insurers are competing on terms as well as price. Fiduciary investment income — high-margin, costless revenue — is already down 12% year over year and falls further with every rate cut. Almost all current margin expansion is restructuring savings (60 of Q2's 70 bps), an engine that switches off in 2027. The balance sheet carries $15.87B of debt at 1.63× equity versus a 0.78 peer median, so the headline 44.7% ROE is substantially leverage rather than quality. The revenue miss in Q2 and the abrupt CFO exit three weeks later are not obviously connected, but both land during a $13B integration. At an average target of $402 the street is already paying for the soft-market thesis being wrong.

01
The soft P&C market outlasts the offsets. Global commercial rates fell 6% in Q2 2026, the eighth straight quarterly decline, with property −12%, financial/professional lines −3% and cyber −4%. Aon's "net market impact" has stayed in its expected zero-to-two-point positive range so far, but if declines steepen or new business slows, commission compression flows through with almost no cost offset. It limits how aggressively Aon can pursue further middle-market M&A or buy back stock, raises refinancing sensitivity, and is the most likely explanation for the persistent valuation discount to Marsh & McLennan. Deleveraging is real but slow.

The Accelerating Aon United program has produced $320M of cumulative savings and targets $450M by 2027; it contributed roughly 60 of the 70 bps of Q2 2026 adjusted margin expansion. Once the program completes, Aon must deliver margin progress from revenue mix, pricing and Aon Business Services productivity alone — an unproven proposition in a soft market, and the point at which the CFO vacancy matters most.
  Industry Commentary
The P&C Soft Market Is Now Structural, Not Cyclical Noise
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Marsh's Global Insurance Market Index put global commercial insurance rates down 6% in Q2 2026 — the eighth consecutive quarterly decline. Property fell 12%, financial and professional lines 3%, and cyber 4% in its twelfth straight quarter of declines. Only US casualty is still rising, at +2%. Regionally the declines are broad and deep: IMEA −16%, Pacific −13%, Latin America −9%, UK −8%, Canada −7%, Europe −6%, Asia −5%, US −2%. Marsh attributes it to "abundant capacity and strong insurer competition," reinforced by strong insurer profitability, surplus capital, cheaper reinsurance and higher investment returns — and notes insurers are now competing on coverage breadth, terms and deductibles as well as price. For a broker, this is a direct hit to the commission base. Aon's counter-argument is that organic growth correlates with client capital investment and nominal GDP rather than with rate, and its numbers back that up so far — 6% organic in both 2024 and 2025, 5% in Q2 2026. But the drag is arithmetic and persistent, and none of the conditions creating it show signs of reversing.
Rate Cuts Are Draining Fiduciary Investment Income
ONGOING HEADWIND
Brokers earn interest on client premium held in fiduciary accounts between collection and remittance. It is pure-margin revenue with essentially no associated cost, which made it a powerful earnings tailwind through the high-rate era — Aon booked $271 million in FY2025. It is now reversing. Q2 2026 fiduciary investment income was $58 million, down 12% year over year, with management stating plainly that "higher average balances were more than offset by lower interest rates." The mechanic is unforgiving in both directions: because there is no cost base, every dollar of lost fiduciary income falls straight out of operating income and works directly against the 70–80 bps of guided margin expansion. Note this compounds with the soft market — falling premiums mean smaller fiduciary balances and lower rates on them. On the other side of the ledger, lower rates reduce interest expense on Aon's $15.87B of debt, which fell $33 million to $179 million in Q2. For a leveraged broker that partial offset is real, but it is not a wash.
Broker Consolidation Into the Middle Market — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND
The defining industry trend is the mega-brokers rolling up the fragmented US middle market, where commission rates are higher, competition is less price-transparent, and retention is stickier than in global large-account broking. Aon bought that position wholesale with NFP for $13.0B in April 2024 — 7,700+ colleagues across the US, Puerto Rico, Canada, the UK and Ireland, spanning P&C brokerage, benefits consulting and retirement advisory — and has since added $350M+ of tuck-ins in H1 2026 alone, extending into MGU/MGA delegated-underwriting capability. Gallagher (+30.8% TTM revenue growth) and Brown & Brown (+34.6%) are executing the same playbook with even more aggressive M&A, which is bidding up target multiples for everyone.

Bull framing: the middle market is genuinely less rate-sensitive, so consolidating into it is the correct structural hedge against the soft cycle; Aon is running NFP through the Aon Business Services platform to accelerate its growth, and the market is not yet paying for that at 17.3× forward earnings. Bear framing: the strategy is capital-hungry at precisely the moment Aon is the most levered of the big four brokers (1.63 D/E vs 0.78 peer median), roll-up accounting makes organic performance harder to audit from outside, and rising acquisition multiples mean today's tuck-ins earn less than NFP did. The resolution comes in the organic growth line over the next four quarters: if middle-market mix genuinely keeps Commercial Risk organic at 5–6% while global rates keep falling, the bull case is proven. If organic slips toward 3–4%, the leverage will look like a mistake made at the top of a cycle.
