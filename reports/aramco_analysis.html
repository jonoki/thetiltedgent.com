01 Company Overview

Saudi Aramco is the national oil company of Saudi Arabia: it finds, pumps, refines and sells the Kingdom's oil and gas, and owns refineries and chemical plants at home and abroad. Its story starts with a May 29, 1933 concession granted by the Saudi government to the company's predecessor; the state listed a sliver of it on the Saudi Exchange on December 11, 2019. In 2025 it produced 12.9 million barrels of oil equivalent a day (mmboed), earned net income of SAR 350.2B ($93.4B) on revenue of SAR 1.67T ($445.7B), and paid shareholders SAR 320.4B in dividends. At the November 8, 2026 close of SAR 26.08 the whole company is valued at about US$1.68 trillion.

2026 dealt Aramco the strangest hand in its history. The US–Iran conflict choked shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel most Gulf oil sails through. CEO Amin Nasser called the second quarter "one of the most challenging ever in the history of Saudi Aramco", with attacks on company facilities and flows through the Strait at a tenth of pre-conflict levels. Output fell hard: total production was 9.46 mmboed in Q2 2026, down 26% from 12.78 a year earlier. Yet the price of every barrel it could still ship jumped 62% to $108.1, the East-West Pipeline carried crude across the country to Red Sea ports, and adjusted net income rose 33% to $33.4B, 7.4% above the $31.1B analysts had expected in a Bloomberg compilation. The bypass, already hit at a pumping station in April, was struck again in September: on September 11 Saudi Arabia stopped the East-West Pipeline as a precaution after drones launched from Iraq hit it in the Riyadh and Medina areas. The stock has moved modestly: SAR 26.08 is up 9.4% on the year-end 2025 close of 23.83. At 14 times trailing earnings with a 5.20% dividend yield, the stock trades less like a beneficiary of $100 oil and more like a state-controlled bond with an oil-price coupon — and the question for a minority holder is whether that coupon, a SAR 1.36 a year base dividend, stays safe.

The third quarter, reported November 3, answered part of that. Adjusted net income was $31.2B (SAR 117.0B), up about 9% on a year earlier and 6.6% below Q2 (our calculation), and above the $30.66B median of 12 analysts that Aramco shared (CNBC, Reuters). Reported net income rose 35% to SAR 135.9B, helped by a SAR 25.9B gain on selling its PRefChem stake. Production recovered to 11.05 mmboed from 9.46 in Q2 but was still 17% below the 13.27 of a year earlier, the realized price eased to $95.2 from $108.1, and free cash flow of SAR 105.8B ($28.2B) covered the SAR 82.1B base dividend with room to spare. The company says supply reliability held despite some disruptions to the East-West Pipeline during the quarter.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

12.0 mmbpd sustainable capacity East-West Pipeline to the Red Sea Low-cost, long-lived reserves Integrated refining (51% of own crude) Gas growth to 2030 Gearing 5.5% Sovereign backing

Aramco's edge is a royal flush few rivals can even sit down with: the Kingdom's fields, a maximum sustainable capacity of 12 million barrels a day, and infrastructure built for exactly the hand 2026 dealt. The East-West Pipeline reached its maximum 7.0 million barrels a day in Q1 2026, per the Q1 release, routing crude across the peninsula to Red Sea terminals and around the blocked Strait. Add refineries that take about half of its crude, international storage, and a balance sheet with gearing of 5.5% against total equity of SAR 1.86T, and Aramco can keep selling when others cannot. Nasser's claim on the August 4 call (ROACE was 22.1% again at September 30) is that 12-month ROACE of 22.1% is "around double the IOCs average", the IOCs being the big listed international oil companies.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One country, one chokepoint. Every barrel comes from Saudi Arabia, and its export network runs through the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea corridor, via the Bab el-Mandeb strait or the Suez Canal, as Nasser described it on August 4. Nasser said on August 4 that Aramco remains concerned that the Hormuz disruption and threats to Bab el-Mandeb shipping "could have a significant long-term impact on the world economy". Company facilities were attacked during Q2 and again in July 2026 (the Q2 interim report called the impact not material at June 30); the Q3 interim report says facilities of the company and its affiliates in the Kingdom were targeted again during and after the quarter, with the impact not expected to be material at September 30. In September the bypass was hit again: on September 11 the Foreign Ministry said drones from Iraq had struck the East-West Pipeline in the Riyadh and Medina areas, causing injuries and material damage, and an Energy Ministry official told the state news agency SPA the pipeline had been stopped as a precaution.
02
The minority holder sits at the state's table. With 81.48% held directly by the Government and 16% transferred to PIF and its companies, public shareholders own about 2.5%. Dividend policy, capital spending and domestic fuel pricing are decided under a controlling shareholder that also collects Aramco's royalties and income taxes. The performance-linked dividend shrank from SAR 0.6680 a share for 2024 to SAR 0.0136 for 2025, and total dividends fell 31%.
03
The dividend ran ahead of the cash — until Q3. In the first nine months of 2026 Aramco paid SAR 246.2B in base dividends against free cash flow of SAR 221.6B — a SAR 24.6B gap, down from SAR 48.3B at the half-year because Q3 free cash flow (SAR 105.8B) beat the quarter's dividend (SAR 82.1B) by SAR 23.7B (our calculation). Cash and equivalents fell from SAR 243.1B at December 31 to SAR 143.5B at September 30, though part of that moved into debt securities, and gearing is 5.5% against 3.8% at the end of 2025. Q3 also brought a favorable working-capital swing; if it does not repeat, and with the SAR 9.9B ($2.6B) HUMAIN cash contribution made after the quarter, the "progressive" base dividend leans on the balance sheet again.
04
Oil price in both directions. Earnings move with crude. In 2025, at a $69.2 average realized price, net income fell 12% to SAR 350.2B. The 2026 windfall depends on the disruption that is also cutting volumes; a reopened Strait could bring back barrels and lower prices at the same time.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things bend the reported numbers. First, the PRefChem gain: Q3 net income includes a SAR 25.9B ($6.9B) gain on selling Aramco's stake in the Malaysian refining and petrochemicals venture to PETRONAS, which lifts reported earnings, EPS and the trailing margins above; adjusted net income (SAR 117.0B in Q3) excludes it and ran below reported (SAR 135.9B). Second, working capital: Q2 free cash flow absorbed a $13.6B build, mostly receivables from the state for domestic fuel sold below world prices, and Q3 free cash flow benefited from favorable working-capital movements. Third, share count: the 2022 and 2023 one-for-ten bonus share issues raised the share count to 242 billion; Yahoo's history and this page's chart are adjusted for them, so older per-share prices look lower than the quotes of the day. A buyback of up to 350 million shares (SAR 11.25B cap) for employee share plans had bought 83.8 million shares by September 30, 2026 — too small to move per-share figures.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 8, 2021 close of SAR 30.99 (bonus-adjusted) to the November 8, 2026 close of SAR 26.08, Aramco lost 15.8% of its price while the S&P 500 (SPY) gained 56.8%. Dividends change the picture: on Yahoo's dividend-adjusted series, which reinvests every payout, the five-year return is about +5.7% — this is a stock whose return has come almost entirely as cash. The shape is a spike and a long slide: a run to SAR 38.09 in May 2022, the highest month-end of the window, a second peak near SAR 35 in September 2023, then nearly two years of decline to a five-year low month-end of SAR 23.80 in August 2025, after the performance-linked dividend was cut. The 2026 oil shock lifted the price only to SAR 27.76 at its April month-end.

The 6-period monthly RSI sank to 11.8 in August 2025, the most oversold reading in the five-year series, in a run of twenty-one straight months below 45 (January 2024 to September 2025). It recovered to 66.7 in April 2026 and now reads 50.5, neutral. The 10-month moving average, about SAR 26.35, sits just above the current price: the 2026 rally has faded back below its trend line.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

No oil company is better built for a supply shock. Aramco kept selling through the worst disruption in its history, realised $108.1 a barrel in Q2, lifted Q2 adjusted net income 33%, held Q3 adjusted net income at $31.2B (up about 9% on a year earlier), and reports gearing of only 5.5% and 22.1% ROACE. Management argues that inventories drained by the crisis will need up to 18 months of extra supply to rebuild even after the Strait reopens — demand Aramco, with 12.0 million barrels a day of sustainable capacity, is positioned to meet. Meanwhile the base dividend, a 5.20% yield, has been raised four years running, analysts' lowest target is above the price, and gas projects due in 2026–2027 add volume sold mainly at home.

Bear Case

You are a 2.5% passenger in a car the state drives. The 2026 windfall rests on a conflict that also cut production 26%, attacks have hit company facilities and, in September, stopped the East-West Pipeline that carries crude around the Strait, and the payout has run ahead of the cash: over nine months base dividends of SAR 246.2B exceeded free cash flow of SAR 221.6B by SAR 24.6B, and gearing is 5.5% against 3.8% at the end of 2025. In 2025 the performance-linked dividend nearly vanished and total dividends fell 31%. The stock has lost 16% on price over five years while the S&P 500 gained 57%; at SAR 26.08 it is 4.5% above its February 2026 month-end of SAR 24.96, and a reopened Strait could bring lower prices as well as more barrels.

Top 3 Risks

01
Geopolitics at the wellhead. The US–Iran conflict cut production to 9.46 mmboed in Q2 (11.05 in Q3, against 13.27 a year earlier) and brought attacks on Aramco facilities. The bypass is exposed too: on September 11 Saudi Arabia stopped the East-West Pipeline as a precaution after drone strikes. Any further escalation — a wider closure, longer outages on the pipeline or at the Red Sea terminals, or Bab el-Mandeb shipping threats — hits volume with no offset.
02
Dividend sustainability. At SAR 1.3572 a year, the base dividend is 73% of TTM EPS and slightly larger than TTM free cash flow (a 5.20% yield against a 5.1% free-cash-flow yield). Q3 free cash flow of SAR 105.8B covered the quarter's SAR 82.1B base dividend; whether that repeats, with the HUMAIN cash contribution paid after the quarter, is the test.
03
Controlling shareholder and thin float. The Government's 81.48% and PIF's 16% leave little room for minority influence on domestic fuel pricing, capital projects such as the HUMAIN stake, or the timing of future share sales by the state.
Industry Commentary
The Hormuz supply shock
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Aramco's own account of 2026, given on its August 4 call: the prolonged US–Iran conflict removed an average of 11 million barrels a day of liquids supply, the world lost over 2.6 billion barrels of oil that had been destined for industry, and strategic and commercial inventory releases of an estimated 9 million barrels a day masked the gap. The IEA's emergency release of 426 million barrels was due to end in August. Nasser's argument is that inventories are now near operational minimums and must be rebuilt, which would keep calls on crude high into 2027. That is the company's view, not a forecast this page endorses: prices could fall fast if the Strait reopens, and Brent futures have already swung between about $71 (January) and $118 (March) at 2026 month-ends on Yahoo's daily closes, and the front-month contract fell from $117.64 on October 30 to $98.32 on November 6 while Aramco's share price moved +0.6% (October 29 close of SAR 25.92 to SAR 26.08) and ExxonMobil's fell 11.4% ($187.25 to $165.87).

National oil companies vs the listed majors
STRUCTURAL

Aramco competes with ExxonMobil, Chevron and Shell for customers and capital, but it is not built like them. Its royalties and income taxes go to its controlling shareholder, and its domestic fuel sales are priced below world levels and topped up by the state with a lag — the mechanism the CFO says accounts for most of Q2's $13.6B working-capital build. The listed majors answer to dispersed shareholders; Aramco answers to one. For a minority investor that is the trade: a world-class asset base and a high dividend, in exchange for no say in how either is run.

Gas as the second engine
EMERGING TAILWIND

The quieter story is gas. Aramco targets about 80% more sales-gas capacity by 2030 versus 2021, with Jafurah — which started production in 2025, per the FY2025 release — aimed at 2.0 billion cubic feet a day of sales gas by 2030, and the Fadhili expansion adding about 1.15 billion. Management ties the gas build-out to rising domestic demand, including from power and petrochemicals, so its customers sit mostly inside the Kingdom rather than across the Strait. If the conflict drags on, a growing domestic gas business is the part of Aramco least exposed to it.
