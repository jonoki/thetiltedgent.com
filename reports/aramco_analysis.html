01 Company Overview

Saudi Aramco is the national oil company of Saudi Arabia: it finds, pumps, refines and sells the Kingdom's oil and gas, and owns refineries and chemical plants at home and abroad. Its story starts with a May 29, 1933 concession granted by the Saudi government to the company's predecessor; the state listed a sliver of it on the Saudi Exchange on December 11, 2019. In 2025 it produced 12.9 million barrels of oil equivalent a day (mmboed), earned net income of SAR 350.2B ($93.4B) on revenue of SAR 1.67T ($445.7B), and paid shareholders SAR 320.4B in dividends. At the October 1, 2026 close of SAR 25.28 the whole company is valued at about US$1.63 trillion.

2026 dealt Aramco the strangest hand in its history. The US–Iran conflict choked shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel most Gulf oil sails through. CEO Amin Nasser called the second quarter "one of the most challenging ever in the history of Saudi Aramco", with attacks on company facilities and flows through the Strait at "a tenth of pre-conflict levels". Output fell hard: total production was 9.46 mmboed in Q2 2026, down 26% from 12.78 a year earlier. Yet the price of every barrel it could still ship jumped 62% to $108.1, the East-West Pipeline carried crude across the country to Red Sea ports, and adjusted net income rose 33% to $33.4B, 7.4% above the $31.1B analysts had expected in a Bloomberg compilation. The bypass, already hit at a pumping station in April, was struck again in September: on September 11 Saudi Arabia stopped the East-West Pipeline as a precaution after drones launched from Iraq hit it in the Riyadh and Medina areas. The stock has barely moved: SAR 25.28 is up 6.1% on the year-end 2025 close of 23.83. At 15 times trailing earnings with a 5.37% dividend yield, the stock trades less like a beneficiary of $100 oil and more like a state-controlled bond with an oil-price coupon — and the question for a minority holder is whether that coupon, a SAR 1.36 a year base dividend, stays safe while cash flow is squeezed.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

12.0 mmbpd sustainable capacity East-West Pipeline to the Red Sea Low-cost, long-lived reserves Integrated refining (52% of own crude) Gas growth to 2030 Gearing 6.2% Sovereign backing

Aramco's edge is a royal flush few rivals can even sit down with: the Kingdom's fields, a maximum sustainable capacity of 12 million barrels a day, and infrastructure built for exactly the hand 2026 dealt. The East-West Pipeline reached its maximum 7.0 million barrels a day in Q1 2026, per the Q1 release, routing crude across the peninsula to Red Sea terminals and around the blocked Strait. Add refineries that take more than half its crude, international storage, and a balance sheet with gearing of 6.2% against equity of SAR 1.80T, and Aramco can keep selling when others cannot. Nasser's claim on the August 4 call is that 12-month ROACE of 22.1% is "around double the IOCs average", the IOCs being the big listed international oil companies.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One country, one chokepoint. Every barrel comes from Saudi Arabia, and its export network runs through the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea corridor, via the Bab el-Mandeb strait or the Suez Canal, as Nasser described it on August 4. Nasser said on August 4 that Aramco remains concerned that the Hormuz disruption and threats to Bab el-Mandeb shipping "could have a significant long-term impact on the world economy". Company facilities were attacked during Q2 and again in July 2026; the interim report called the impact not material at June 30. In September the bypass was hit again: on September 11 the Foreign Ministry said drones from Iraq had struck the East-West Pipeline in the Riyadh and Medina areas, causing injuries and material damage, and an Energy Ministry official told the state news agency SPA the pipeline had been stopped as a precaution.
02
The minority holder sits at the state's table. With 81.48% held directly by the Government and 16% transferred to PIF and its companies, public shareholders own about 2.5%. Dividend policy, capital spending and domestic fuel pricing are decided under a controlling shareholder that also collects Aramco's royalties and income taxes. The performance-linked dividend shrank from SAR 0.6680 a share for 2024 to SAR 0.0136 for 2025, and total dividends fell 31%.
03
The dividend is outrunning the cash. In the first half of 2026 Aramco paid SAR 164.2B in base dividends against free cash flow of SAR 115.9B — a SAR 48.3B gap drawn from cash, which fell from SAR 243.1B to SAR 167.4B over the half. Gearing rose from 3.8% at the end of 2025 to 6.2%; Aramco attributes the second-quarter rise, from 4.8%, mainly to dividend payments and capital spending. Management blames a timing effect; if Q3 does not reverse it, the "progressive" base dividend becomes a debt-funded promise.
04
Oil price in both directions. Earnings move with crude. In 2025, at a $69.2 average realized price, net income fell 12% to SAR 350.2B. The 2026 windfall depends on the disruption that is also cutting volumes; a reopened Strait could bring back barrels and lower prices at the same time.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things bend the reported numbers. First, the working-capital swing: Q2 2026 free cash flow absorbed a $13.6B build, mostly receivables from the state for domestic fuel sold below world prices, which the CFO expects to reverse in Q3. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

No oil company is better built for a supply shock. Aramco kept selling through the worst disruption in its history, realised $108.1 a barrel, lifted Q2 adjusted net income 33%, and still reports gearing of only 6.2% and 22.1% ROACE. Management argues that inventories drained by the crisis will need up to 18 months of extra supply to rebuild even after the Strait reopens — demand Aramco, with 12.0 million barrels a day of sustainable capacity, is positioned to meet. Meanwhile the base dividend, a 5.37% yield, has been raised four years running, analysts' lowest target is above the price, and gas projects due in 2026–2027 add volume sold mainly at home.

Bear Case

You are a 2.5% passenger in a car the state drives. The 2026 windfall rests on a conflict that also cut production 26%, attacks have hit company facilities and, in September, stopped the East-West Pipeline that carries crude around the Strait, and the payout already exceeds the cash: first-half base dividends were SAR 48.3B more than free cash flow and gearing has risen from 3.8% to 6.2%. In 2025 the performance-linked dividend nearly vanished and total dividends fell 31%. The stock has lost 15% on price over five years while the S&P 500 gained 78%; at SAR 25.28 it sits barely above its February 2026 month-end of SAR 24.96, and a reopened Strait could bring lower prices as well as more barrels.

Top 3 Risks

01
Geopolitics at the wellhead. The US–Iran conflict has already cut Q2 production to 9.46 mmboed and brought attacks on Aramco facilities. The bypass is exposed too: on September 11 Saudi Arabia stopped the East-West Pipeline as a precaution after drone strikes. Any further escalation — a wider closure, longer outages on the pipeline or at the Red Sea terminals, or Bab el-Mandeb shipping threats — hits volume with no offset.
02
Dividend sustainability. At SAR 1.3572 a year, the base dividend is 80% of TTM EPS and larger than TTM free cash flow (a 5.37% yield against a 5.0% free-cash-flow yield). Q3 2026 must show the working-capital reversal management promised, or the dividend leans further on the balance sheet.
03
Controlling shareholder and thin float. The Government's 81.48% and PIF's 16% leave little room for minority influence on domestic fuel pricing, capital projects such as the HUMAIN stake, or the timing of future share sales by the state.
Industry Commentary
The Hormuz supply shock
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Aramco's own account of 2026, given on its August 4 call: the prolonged US–Iran conflict removed an average of 11 million barrels a day of liquids supply, the world lost over 2.6 billion barrels of oil that had been destined for industry, and strategic and commercial inventory releases of an estimated 9 million barrels a day masked the gap. The IEA's emergency release of 426 million barrels was due to end in August. Nasser's argument is that inventories are now near operational minimums and must be rebuilt, which would keep calls on crude high into 2027. That is the company's view, not a forecast this page endorses: prices could fall fast if the Strait reopens, and Brent futures have already swung between about $71 (January) and $118 (March) at 2026 month-ends on Yahoo's daily closes.

National oil companies vs the listed majors
STRUCTURAL

Aramco competes with ExxonMobil, Chevron and Shell for customers and capital, but it is not built like them. Its royalties and income taxes go to its controlling shareholder, and its domestic fuel sales are priced below world levels and topped up by the state with a lag — the mechanism the CFO says accounts for most of Q2's $13.6B working-capital build. The listed majors answer to dispersed shareholders; Aramco answers to one. For a minority investor that is the trade: a world-class asset base and a high dividend, in exchange for no say in how either is run.

Gas as the second engine
EMERGING TAILWIND

The quieter story is gas. Aramco targets about 80% more sales-gas capacity by 2030 versus 2021, with Jafurah — which started production in 2025, per the FY2025 release — aimed at 2.0 billion cubic feet a day of sales gas by 2030, and the Fadhili expansion adding about 1.15 billion. Management ties the gas build-out to rising domestic demand, including from power and petrochemicals, so its customers sit mostly inside the Kingdom rather than across the Strait. If the conflict drags on, a growing domestic gas business is the part of Aramco least exposed to it.
