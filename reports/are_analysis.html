01 Company Overview

Alexandria is the landlord to the lab bench. Founded in 1994 by Joel S. Marcus and Jerry M. Sudarsky and public since 1997, it built a REIT dedicated to life-science real estate: laboratory and office campuses clustered next to research universities in what it calls its AAA innovation clusters, Greater Boston, the San Francisco Bay Area, San Diego, Seattle, Maryland, Research Triangle and New York City. It owns 36.5 million rentable square feet of operating properties, 80% of its annual rental revenue comes from its "Megacampus" platform, and 57% comes from investment-grade or publicly traded large-cap tenants (all as of September 30, 2026). GICS files it under Office REITs; economically it is a specialist in a building type (wet labs with heavy ventilation, power and floor loading) that ordinary office landlords cannot easily supply.

For a REIT the earnings line to read is FFO, not EPS, and at Alexandria the two tell completely different stories. GAAP says the company lost $8.41 per share over the last twelve months (our arithmetic from the company's releases), mostly because of write-downs: $2.20B of real-estate impairments in 2025, $222.5M in Q2 2026 and $637.9M in Q3 2026, the last tied to buildings it has classified as held for sale. FFO per share as adjusted, the company's own measure, says it still earns cash: $7.16 over the last four quarters. The problem is the direction. FFO as adjusted was $9.47 in 2024 and $9.01 in 2025, and is guided to $6.38–$6.42 for 2026, roughly 29% below 2025 at the midpoint. The dividend followed: cut 45% in December 2025 from $1.32 to $0.72 a quarter. The stock closed at $222.96 in December 2021 and at $39.91 on October 28, 2026, two sessions after the third-quarter report. The market has priced the landlord as if the biotech slowdown that emptied some of its space is permanent. The question this report works through is whether an 87.1%-occupied portfolio at under 6x trailing FFO is a value, or a portfolio still marking itself down.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Cluster locations near research universities Megacampus scale (80% of ARR) Specialised lab build-out 57% investment-grade / large-cap tenants 7.7-yr average lease term 97% leases with annual escalators 11.6-yr average debt term

The moat is location plus specification. A lab tenant needs to be within reach of the talent, universities and venture money of a handful of clusters, and it needs a building engineered for wet labs. Alexandria spent three decades assembling those buildings in those places, and long leases with fixed escalators lock in the rent. What the moat does not protect against is supply and demand inside the cluster. When tenants have more choice, the landlord pays for it at renewal, and renewal rents 6.9% lower on a cash basis over the first nine months of 2026 are that bill. The moat protects the franchise; it does not protect the price per square foot.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Occupancy has stopped falling, for now. 90.9% → 87.7% → 86.9% → 87.1% over three quarters, with year-end guidance of 86.2–87.8%, and the company expects average occupancy to dip again for a while in 2027 as 1.4M RSF of leases expire and some tenants may wind down (Q3 release). On a 36.5M RSF portfolio, each point of occupancy is roughly 365,000 square feet (our arithmetic) of empty space still carrying taxes and operating costs.
02
Asset values keep being marked down. $2,202.8M of real-estate impairments in 2025, $222.5M in Q2 2026 and $637.9M in Q3 2026 (plus $12.4M of impairments on non-real-estate venture investments in Q1 2026). Impairments are excluded from FFO as adjusted, but each one is the company saying part of its balance sheet is worth less than it was carried at. CFO Marc Binda told the October 27 call: "We could have impairments over the next couple of quarters if these types of assets subsequently meet the accounting requirements for held-for-sale designation …" (Motley Fool transcript); assets are designated held for sale once the company decides to sell them and expects to do so within a year.
03
The funding plan depends on selling. The 2026 capital plan still leans on $1.1–$2.7B of dispositions, cut from $2.1–$3.7B after the August notes issue. If buyers demand lower prices, Alexandria either accepts them, which would support the bear view of its assets, or sells less and borrows more.
04
Tenant funding cycle. 43% of annual rental revenue comes from tenants that are not investment-grade or large-cap public companies (the complement of the reported 57%). Those tenants are more exposed to the life-science funding climate.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items dominate the GAAP numbers. First, impairments: $2,202.8M of real-estate impairment charges in 2025, $222.5M in Q2 2026 and $637.9M in Q3 2026 (on assets held for sale), which is why GAAP shows a loss while FFO stays positive. Second, a $366.4M gain on early extinguishment of debt in Q1 2026, from February tender offers that repurchased $1.33B of debt principal for $952.2M in cash; it turned Q1 GAAP EPS positive ($2.10 per the release; $2.11 on StockAnalysis). Third, the dividend reset on December 3, 2025, which the company said preserves about $410 million of liquidity a year. None of the three changes what a tenant pays in rent, which is why FFO, occupancy and renewal spreads are the numbers to watch.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 5.6x trailing FFO and 6.2x the 2026 guide for the specialist lab landlord, with a 7.22% yield covered more than twice by guided FFO (45% payout). The damage is visible and quantified: $3.06B of real-estate impairments across 2025 and the second and third quarters of 2026, the dividend reset, and $1.33B of notes bought back in a tender. Occupancy rose to 87.1% in Q3, leasing reached 1.24M RSF, cash renewal spreads improved from −15.8% in Q1 to −4.3% in Q2 and −3.3% in Q3, 80% of rent comes from Megacampus assets, 57% from investment-grade or large-cap tenants, and leases run 7.7 years on average with escalators on 97% of them. Completed sales plus deals under deposit or in late-stage talks already exceed the reduced 2026 disposition midpoint. If occupancy stabilises in the high 80s and the sales close at reasonable prices, the market is pricing a trough as a new normal.

Bear Case

Every trend that feeds FFO is still pointed down or barely flat: occupancy 90.9% → 87.1% in nine months, same-property NOI −9.2% for the first nine months, FFO from $9.47 (2024) to $6.40 guided (2026), and a guidance midpoint that implies about $1.40 for the fourth quarter against $1.54 in the third. The company already expects occupancy to dip again in 2027 as 1.4M RSF of leases expire. Cheap on P/FFO is what a shrinking FFO stream looks like, and selling assets to fund construction and debt reduction shrinks it further. The Q3 write-down tied $637.9M of impairments to the assets being sold, and the CFO said more could come over the next couple of quarters if more assets are designated for sale. Four of 16 analysts now rate it Sell or Strong Sell, up from two at the last edition (September 21).

Top 3 Risks

01
Occupancy and rent reset. Occupancy at 87.1% with year-end guidance as low as 86.2%, renewal rents still negative on a cash basis, and a 2027 dip already flagged by the company. If tenant demand in the clusters stays soft, the rent roll resets lower as leases roll.
02
Execution on $1.1–$2.7B of dispositions. The capital plan requires selling assets. Weak prices force a choice between dilutive sales, more leverage (6.9x net debt and preferred to adjusted EBITDA, 3Q26 annualised, or 6.6x pro forma for the Campus Point sale, against a 5.6x–6.2x 4Q26 target) or less construction.
03
Further write-downs. $2.2B of impairments in 2025, $222.5M in Q2 2026 and $637.9M in Q3 2026 show carrying values are still being revised, and the CFO has said more may come. More write-offs would not hit FFO as adjusted directly, but they erode book equity and credibility.
Industry Commentary
A tenant's market in lab space
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Alexandria's own numbers describe the market: occupancy down 3.8 points in nine months (90.9% to 87.1%) despite a small third-quarter rise, same-property NOI down 9.2% over the first nine months of 2026, renewal rents below expiring rents.

Rates and the REIT cost of capital
ONGOING HEADWIND

A REIT that funds development needs its cost of capital below its development yield. At 5.6x trailing FFO, new equity is expensive, which is why the 2026 plan is funded with asset sales, retained cash (a 47% FFO payout in Q3) and, since August, $1.0B of junior subordinated notes rather than share issuance. The balance sheet has room — $4.56B of liquidity and an 11.6-year average debt term — but leverage is targeted at 5.6x–6.2x net debt and preferred to adjusted EBITDA for 4Q26, not lower.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: 5.6x FFO and a 7.22% covered yield for a portfolio concentrated in the main US life-science clusters is a price for permanent decline, and leasing volume, narrowing renewal discounts and a first rise in occupancy suggest the decline is slowing. The bear framing: FFO is guided down about 29% this year, occupancy guidance has been lowered once already and a 2027 dip is flagged, and the business is shrinking itself to fund itself. The way to decide is not the multiple. It is two numbers reported every quarter: occupancy and cash renewal spreads. When occupancy stops falling and cash spreads turn positive, FFO has found its floor.
