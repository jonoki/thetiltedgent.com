01 Company Overview

Ares Management is a global alternative asset manager: it runs private credit (loans made outside the banks), real estate, infrastructure and private equity funds for institutions and, increasingly, individual investors. It ended September 2026 with $690.6 billion of assets under management (AUM), up 16% year over year, of which $416.4 billion was fee-paying (FPAUM), up 13%. About 65% of that pool sits in the Ares Credit Group. In the third quarter GAAP total revenues were $1.43 billion, down 13.5% from $1.66 billion a year earlier, because carried interest (the share of fund profits Ares earns when investments are sold) fell from $464.7 million to $161.7 million; GAAP management fees rose 8.8%. GAAP net income attributable to Ares was $85.3 million, or $0.21 a share, against $1.15 a year earlier. At a $42.3 billion market capitalisation, Ares ranks #263 in the S&P 500. Blackstone ($147.4B), KKR ($84.7B) and Apollo ($68.4B) are larger listed alternative managers; Blue Owl ($16.2B) is smaller.

The central debate in this report is still a valuation debate, though the quarter gave the bears a little more to work with. On the measures management guides to — FRE, FRE margin and after-tax realized income per share — Ares printed solid numbers: FRE $524.2M (+11%), FRE margin 42.5% (from 41.4%), realized income $490.1M (+8%), after-tax realized income per share $1.26 (+6%), and $31.0 billion of gross capital raised. But growth slowed from the second quarter (FRE +20%, realized income +31%, per share +25%), fundraising came in below Q2’s record $36.4 billion, and on the call CFO Jarrod Phillips said 2026 FRE margin growth will be “slightly above the midpoint of our 0 to 150 annual basis point range” (a basis point is 0.01 percentage points). The stock closed October 30 at $128.15, 35.3% below its January 2025 month-end high of $198.22 and down 13.0% over the past 52 weeks (October 30, 2025 close: $147.29). Over the same 52 weeks the share prices of Blackstone and KKR fell 20.1% each, Blue Owl’s 34.9% and Apollo’s 6.2%, so what is being repriced is how much investors will pay for the fee earnings of private-credit managers, not Ares’s ability to grow them. Whether that is a discount or a warning is the question.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

PERPETUAL CAPITAL $234B REPEAT LENDING RELATIONSHIPS SCALE IN FINDING LOANS INSURANCE BALANCE SHEET LONG FUND-INVESTOR RELATIONSHIPS MANY FUND TYPES, SAME CLIENTS FOUNDER-LED PARTNERSHIP

The core advantage is contractual duration. An alternative manager’s moat is not brand or technology — it is how long the capital stays and how hard it is to leave. Ares has $233.9 billion of perpetual capital (+23% year over year), and its Q3 presentation puts 84% of AUM in perpetual or long-dated funds and 93% of management fees in such funds, so fee revenue does not reprice with the market the way a traditional fund manager’s does. Layer on lending relationships that keep generating new loans and a wealth channel that reached $82 billion of AUM (+41% year over year, per Arougheti on the call), and the origination franchise keeps growing without needing to win every competitive auction.

Key vulnerabilities

1. Credit concentration. About 65% of AUM is credit, and much of that is floating-rate lending to leveraged middle-market borrowers (mid-sized companies carrying a lot of debt). Non-accruals were 1.8% in US direct lending and portfolio earnings grew 8% to 9%, so the book is currently clean — but a genuine default cycle would hit fee-paying AUM, carried interest and the insurance book together, and the other segments combined are smaller than credit.

2. The market now pays less for each dollar of earnings. The stock is down 35.3% from its January 2025 month-end high while FRE grew 19% in the first nine months of 2026 ($1,479.7M, per the Q3 presentation) — the drawdown is a lower price for the earnings, not lower earnings. If investors permanently value private-credit fee streams like a traditional asset manager’s, operating performance will not rescue the share price.

3. Retail / wealth-channel flows are noisy. Q3 equity commitments from the wealth channel exceeded $4.2 billion, the second-best quarter on record, and Arougheti said net new redemptions fell about 60% from the prior quarter. But these vehicles let individual investors ask for their money back on a schedule, which is where sentiment shocks transmit fastest.

4. Reliance on performance income and the payout. GAAP net income of $85.3M in Q3 sits against a $1.35 quarterly dividend on roughly 330M shares (about $446M, our calculation), so the dividend is funded out of after-tax realized income ($449.6M in Q3), not GAAP earnings — the payout ratio on GAAP earnings is about 439% ($5.40 ÷ $1.23). Realized net performance income was only $11.8M in Q3 against $138.0M year to date; the full-year range Phillips gave on the call, $300–325M, implies roughly $162–187M in the fourth quarter, so Q4 needs asset sales to happen.

Notable one-off items. Three distortions are worth flagging. RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 it has been sold hard enough that sellers may be. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a stock can stay overbought for years, as ARES did through most of 2023–24.

Five years, two entirely different stocks. From $84.74 at the end of October 2021 to $128.15 on October 30, 2026, ARES returned +51.2% on price alone — about 8.6% a year compounded — before a dividend now annualising at $5.40 per share. That headline conceals a violent round trip: the stock more than tripled from the June 2022 low of $56.86 to the January 2025 high of $198.22 (+248.6% in 31 months), then gave back 35.3% of that peak over the following twenty-one months. Anyone who bought at the January 2025 peak is down more than a third; anyone who bought the June 2022 low is up about 125%. (All month-end closes, price only.)

The monthly RSI tells the story more cleanly than the price line. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operating business is still compounding while the stock trades 35% below its high. FRE grew 11% in Q3 and 19% in the first nine months to $1,479.7M, with margin at 42.5%; after-tax realized income per share grew 6% to $1.26 ($5.04 annualised, ~25.4x at the Oct 30 close); and $31.0B was raised in the quarter with management expecting another record year. Most importantly, $117.1B of AUM is already contracted but not yet paying fees — roughly $826.9M of potential annual management fees, against a current $1.07B quarterly fee base. Add a 4.2% dividend yield, $173.8B of dry powder, direct lending non-accruals at 1.8% and improving, and Ada Infrastructure layering $50–100M of FRE from 2027. The consensus target sits 14.1% above the Oct 30 close and nobody rates it Sell.

Bear Case

About two-thirds of AUM is credit, much of it floating-rate lending to leveraged middle-market borrowers, in the twenty-first month of a sector-wide selloff that has taken 35% off Blue Owl’s share price and 20% off KKR’s in the past year. Growth is slowing: management fees rose 10% in Q3 (15% year to date), credit deployment fell 33%, secondaries FRE fell 32%, and the CFO trimmed 2026 margin expansion to slightly above the midpoint of its range. GAAP earnings are thin ($0.21 in Q3, $1.23 TTM), the dividend is funded from realized income rather than GAAP profit, debt-to-equity is 1.72 and beta is 1.55. The fourth quarter needs roughly $162–187M of performance income to meet the full-year range, and if credit quality turns, fee AUM, carried interest and the insurance book deteriorate together.

A private-credit default cycle. Credit is about 65% of AUM and the portfolio is concentrated in leveraged middle-market borrowers. A permanently lower price for the same earnings. The 35.3% drawdown from the January 2025 high happened while FRE kept growing. 03
A closed exit window persisting into 2027. Private equity AUM fell 2% and Q3 realized net performance income was just $11.8M. Sustained drought in asset sales would compress carried interest, and with GAAP EPS at $1.23 TTM against a $5.40 dividend there is no GAAP cushion if it does.
Industry Commentary
The listed-alternatives selloff
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the dominant fact in the sector right now and it is not an Ares story. Over the 52 weeks to October 30, Blue Owl’s share price fell 34.9%, KKR’s and Blackstone’s 20.1% each, Ares’ 13.0% and Apollo’s 6.2% — five different business mixes, one common direction. Blue Owl trades at 73.8x trailing GAAP earnings and yields 8.9% ($0.92 ÷ $10.33), a combination that usually means investors doubt how long the earnings will last. On October 22 all five rose together (Blackstone +8.4%, Blue Owl +5.9%, Ares +5.7%, KKR +3.6%, Apollo +2.8%), which shows how closely the group trades as one. Until investors again pay more for each dollar of fee earnings, good quarters buy relief rallies, not a higher long-run price.
A slow exit market and the carried-interest gap
ONGOING HEADWIND
Realized net performance income — carried interest actually banked — was $11.8M in Q3 and $138.0M in the first nine months, while the $300–325M full-year range Phillips gave on the call implies roughly $162–187M in Q4. Private equity AUM fell 2% and secondaries FRE fell 32% as last year’s one-off catch-up fees dropped out. A closed exit window delays carried interest, slows the return of capital to investors, and therefore slows their ability to commit to new funds. Ares is better insulated than a buyout-heavy peer — only 3.5% of AUM sits in private equity — but a persistently shut window eventually reaches every manager’s fundraising.
Investor demand and the AI infrastructure build
EMERGING TAILWIND
Two flows run the other way. First, demand for Ares’ funds: Q3 gross fundraising was $31.0B with over half from outside the Credit Group (release), and on the call Arougheti said wealth AUM reached $82B (+41%) and wealth-channel equity commitments topped $4.2B, the second-best quarter on record. Second, digital infrastructure: Arougheti put the capital need at a little over $5 trillion between now and 2030, and Co-President Blair Jacobson said projects typically run $3–5B with bank financing still available. That is AI exposure arriving through a real-assets fee stream rather than through a chip-maker’s share price.
Bull versus bear, framed
THE DECISION
The bull and the bear agree on the operating facts and disagree on one thing: what a dollar of private-credit fee-related earnings is worth. The bull sees $416.4B of fee-paying AUM growing 13%, $117.1B of contracted fees waiting to switch on, a 42.5% FRE margin still edging up, a 4.2% dividend that was raised by more than 20% in the second quarter, and a stock 35% below its high with a consensus target 14.1% higher and no Sell rating — a valuation problem that fixes itself as earnings arrive. The bear sees a book that is two-thirds credit at 1.72x debt-to-equity and a 1.55 beta, GAAP EPS of $1.23 supporting a $5.40 dividend, fee growth slowing to 10%, margin guidance trimmed, and twenty-one months of evidence that this market will not pay up for these earnings however fast they grow. Holding the 10-month moving average at $124.36 is the near-term technical test; Q4 performance income and the February 2027 results are the fundamental ones.
