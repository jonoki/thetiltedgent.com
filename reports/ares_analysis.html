01 Company Overview

Ares Management is a global alternative asset manager that ended the June 2026 quarter with $671.3 billion of assets under management (AUM), up 17% year over year, of which $409.9 billion was fee-paying (FPAUM). Roughly two-thirds of that pool sits in the Ares Credit Group, which makes Ares the most credit-concentrated of the large listed alternatives platforms. Fiscal 2025 GAAP revenue was $5.60 billion, up 44.2% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue has since reached $5.99 billion (+24.0% YoY). At a $43.5 billion market capitalisation, Ares is the world's ~590th most valuable public company and the fourth-largest of the listed US alternative managers, behind Blackstone ($159.8B), KKR ($90.8B) and Apollo ($76.3B).

The central debate in this report is a valuation debate, not an operating one. On the metrics management actually guides to — FRE, FRE margin and after-tax realized income per share — Ares just printed one of its best quarters ever: FRE $491.1M (+20%), FRE margin 42.2% (from 41.2%), realized income $521.5M (+31%), after-tax realized income per share $1.29 (+25%), and a record $36.4 billion of gross capital raised. Yet the stock is 34.1% below its January 2025 high of $198.22 and down 25.6% over the last 52 weeks. Peers de-rated in lockstep — Blackstone's market cap is down 23.1% YoY, KKR's 26.5%, Blue Owl's 40.6% — so what is being repriced is the multiple the market will pay for private-credit fee streams, not Ares' ability to compound them. Whether that de-rating is a discount or a warning is the question.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

PERPETUAL CAPITAL $223B INCUMBENCY LENDING SCALE ORIGINATION INSURANCE BALANCE SHEET LP RELATIONSHIP DEPTH MULTI-STRATEGY CROSS-SELL FOUNDER-LED PARTNERSHIP

The core advantage is contractual duration. An alternative manager's moat is not brand or technology — it is how long the capital stays and how hard it is to leave. Ares has $223.3 billion of perpetual capital growing 34% a year and $409.9 billion of fee-paying AUM sitting inside fund structures with multi-year lock-ups; fee revenue therefore does not reprice with the market the way a traditional manager's does. Layer on the direct-lending incumbency effect — 75% of US direct-lending deployment in Q2 went into companies Ares already finances — and the origination franchise compounds without needing to win competitive auctions. Institutions now represent 75% of AUM and more than 80% of gross equity inflows over the trailing twelve months, which is the opposite of the retail-flow fragility the market has been pricing in.

Key vulnerabilities

1. Credit concentration. Two-thirds of AUM is credit, and the great majority of that is floating-rate lending to leveraged middle-market borrowers. Non-accruals are under 2% and portfolio EBITDA is growing 9%, so the book is currently clean — but a genuine default cycle would hit fee-paying AUM, carried interest and the Aspida/insurance book simultaneously. Ares has less strategy diversification to absorb that than Blackstone or KKR.

2. Multiple compression is the actual risk being realised. The stock is down 34.1% from its January 2025 high while FRE grew 20% — the entire drawdown is de-rating. If the market permanently reprices private-credit fee streams from growth multiples toward traditional-manager multiples, operating performance will not rescue the share price.

3. Retail / wealth-channel flows are noisy. Wealth fundraising grew 15% to $3.9 billion in Q2 and core US individual-investor redemptions fell 35% quarter over quarter to 2.5% of NAV — but the fact that management had to address redemptions at length signals the non-traded BDC and perpetual retail vehicles are where sentiment shocks transmit fastest.

4. Realisation dependence and payout math. GAAP net income of $150.6M in Q2 against a $1.35 quarterly dividend on ~330M shares means the dividend is funded out of realized income, not GAAP earnings — the stated payout ratio screens at 232.9% on a GAAP basis. That is normal for the structure but leaves no GAAP cushion if realisations stall.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 it has been sold hard enough that sellers may be. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a stock can stay overbought for years, as ARES did through most of 2023–24.

Five years, two entirely different stocks. From $84.74 at the end of October 2021 to $130.63 on September 10, 2026, ARES returned +54.2% on price alone — a respectable but unremarkable ~9% compound annual rate, before a dividend that has risen from $1.88 per share in 2021 to $5.40 annualised today. That flat-looking headline conceals a violent round trip: the stock more than doubled from the June 2022 trough of $56.86 to the January 2025 peak of $198.22 (+248.5% in 31 months), then surrendered 34.1% of that peak over the following twenty months. Anyone who bought the 2024 momentum has lost money; anyone who bought the 2022 rate shock has tripled it.

The monthly RSI tells the story more cleanly than the price line. It sat above 80 for most of 2024 and touched 90.8 at the January 2025 top — a reading that essentially never persists. It then collapsed to 22.8 by March 2026, the most oversold the stock has been since the June 2022 rate shock, before recovering to 47.6 in September. The 10-month moving average, which has been falling since peaking at $172.55 in July 2025 and now sits at $129.15, has just been reclaimed from below by spot at $130.63. That is the first tentative sign of trend repair in nearly a year, and it is exactly the kind of level that either holds or fails decisively.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operating business is compounding at the rate management promised while the stock trades 34% below its high. FRE grew 20% to $491.1M with margin expanding to 42.2%, after-tax realized income per share grew 25% to $1.29 ($5.16 annualised, ~25.3x at spot), and fundraising set a record at $36.4B. Most importantly, $114B of AUM is already contracted but not yet paying fees — roughly $828M of future annual management fees, against a current $1.03B quarterly fee base — so a meaningful slice of 2027–28 revenue is already signed. Add a 4.1% dividend yield growing 20% a year, $170B of dry powder into a market where capital is scarce, and Ada Infrastructure layering $50–100M of FRE from 2027. Every analyst revision in the last two months was upward; consensus sits 12.9% above spot and nobody rates it Sell.

Bear Case

Two-thirds of AUM is credit, mostly floating-rate lending to leveraged middle-market borrowers, in the eighteenth month of a sector-wide de-rating that has already taken 40% off Blue Owl and 26% off KKR. If the market has decided private-credit fee streams deserve a traditional-manager multiple rather than a growth multiple, 20% FRE growth will not stop the slide — the last twenty months are the proof. GAAP earnings are thin ($0.49 in Q2, $2.22 TTM), the dividend is funded from realized income rather than GAAP profit, ROIC screens at 4.6%, debt-to-equity is 1.72, and beta is 1.52. Private equity AUM grew just 3%, confirming the exit window is shut, and if credit quality turns — non-accruals are under 2% today — fee AUM, carried interest and the insurance book deteriorate simultaneously with nothing else on the platform big enough to offset it.

Industry Commentary
The listed-alternatives de-rating
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the dominant fact in the sector right now and it is not an Ares story. Over the trailing twelve months Blue Owl's market capitalisation fell 40.6%, KKR's 26.5%, Ares' 25.6% and Blackstone's 23.1% — four different business mixes, one common outcome. Blue Owl now trades at 88x trailing earnings and yields 8.76%, a combination that only occurs when the market doubts an earnings stream's durability. The entire cohort was repriced from a growth-multiple framework toward something closer to a financials multiple, and that happened while fee earnings across the group grew double digits. Until that reverses, good quarters buy relief rallies, not re-ratings.
A frozen M&A and exit market
ONGOING HEADWIND
Management described a sluggish M&A market on the Q2 call, and the segment data confirms it: private equity AUM grew just 3% year over year while every other segment grew 17% or better. A closed exit window delays carried interest, slows the return of capital to limited partners, and therefore slows their ability to commit to new vintages. Ares is structurally better insulated than a buyout-heavy peer — only 3.6% of AUM sits in private equity, and the Secondaries Group (+30% AUM) actively profits from the liquidity squeeze — but a persistently shut window eventually reaches every manager's fundraising cadence.
Institutional under-allocation and the AI infrastructure build
EMERGING TAILWIND
Two structural flows run the other way. First, allocation: institutions now account for 75% of Ares AUM and more than 80% of gross equity inflows over the last twelve months, and management's position is that they remain under-allocated to private credit — Pathfinder III closing at $8.5B against a $6.5B target and a firm-wide pipeline up nearly 20% quarter over quarter support that. Second, digital infrastructure: Ada Infrastructure is executing 7 campuses and 22 data-centre investments at roughly 1 gigawatt of compute, sized by management at $50–100M of FRE from 2027. That is real AI exposure arriving through a real-assets fee stream rather than a semiconductor multiple.
Bull versus bear, framed
THE DECISION
The bull and the bear agree on every operating fact and disagree on exactly one thing: what a dollar of private-credit fee-related earnings is worth. The bull sees $409.9B of fee-paying AUM growing 17%, $114B of already-contracted fees waiting to switch on, a 42.2% FRE margin still expanding, a 4.1% dividend growing 20% a year, and a stock 34% below its high with a consensus target 12.9% higher and not one Sell rating — a multiple problem that fixes itself as earnings arrive. The bear sees a 66%-credit-concentrated balance sheet at 1.72x debt-to-equity and 1.52 beta, GAAP EPS of $2.22 supporting a $5.40 dividend, and twenty months of evidence that this market will not pay up for these earnings no matter how fast they grow. Reclaiming the 10-month moving average at $129.15 is the near-term technical arbiter; the Q3 print in late October is the fundamental one.
