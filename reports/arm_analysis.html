01 Company Overview
Arm does not make chips — or at least, it didn't until this year. It designs the CPU instruction set and processor cores that sit inside roughly every smartphone on earth and, increasingly, inside the data center: Nvidia's Grace, Amazon's Graviton, Microsoft's Cobalt and Google's Axion are all Arm-based. Arm earns money two ways: an up-front licence fee when a chipmaker takes its IP (intellectual property), and a royalty on every chip that ships. In fiscal 2026 (year ended March 31, 2026) revenue was $4.92B, up 22.8% YoY — the third straight year of 20%+ growth — split $2.61B royalty and $2.31B licence. Arm's parent, SoftBank, still owns the overwhelming majority of the shares; the public float is only ~142.6M of 1.07B shares.
The central debate: ARM trades at roughly 257x trailing GAAP earnings and ~102x forward earnings for a business growing revenue in the low-20s. The bulls argue that royalty-per-chip is structurally rising (Armv9 at ~2x the v8 rate, CSS at ~2x v9 again), that the AI data-center CPU shift from x86 to Arm is only 23% done, and that the new in-house "AGI CPU" business could add $15B of revenue by FY2031. The bears point out that all of that is already in the price — twice — after a stock that ran from $105 in January to $452 in June and then fell 34% in July.

The annuity. Every Arm-based chip that ships pays a royalty, and the trend that matters is royalty per chip, not units. Three levers are lifting it: (1) Armv9 — the current architecture generation — carries roughly twice the royalty rate of Armv8 and was ~33% of royalty revenue in Q1 FY27, up from near zero a few years ago; (2) CSS (Compute Subsystems) — pre-integrated, pre-verified CPU clusters that Arm sells as a near-finished design — command roughly 2x a standalone v9 royalty, and later CSS generations reach 3x; (3) mix shift toward data center, where chips are bigger and pricier — data-center royalties more than doubled YoY in Q1 FY27, and 1.5B Neoverse cores have now shipped (500M in the last nine months). Arm says over 70% of the royalties it forecasts through FY2031 are at rates already under contract, and models a 20% royalty CAGR FY26–31. The near-term wrinkle: Q2 FY27 royalty guidance is only "low-teens" growth and full-year "high-teens" (trimmed from ~20%) because smartphones — still the largest royalty pool — are expected to decline double digits. Yes, Arm now makes chips — this was verified from Arm's own Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27 releases. At its "Arm Everywhere" event on March 24, 2026 the company unveiled the Arm AGI CPU, production silicon aimed at the host-CPU slot in agentic-AI racks, claiming >2x performance per rack versus x86 and up to $10B of capex savings per gigawatt. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

350B+ Chips Shipped Since 1990 22M+ Developers ~99% Smartphone CPU Share Contracted Royalty Rates to FY31 97–98% Gross Margin Power-Efficiency Lead vs x86 CSS Raises Switching Costs
Arm's moat is the instruction set: decades of software — every mobile OS, every app store, an increasing share of Linux server workloads — compiled for Arm, plus 22M+ developers who never think about it. That installed base means a chipmaker choosing Arm gets the ecosystem for free, and a chipmaker leaving Arm has to drag its customers' software with it. The economics are near-perfect: 97.5% gross margin, negligible capex, and a royalty that compounds as the world's chip content grows. Arm's share of total chip value rose from 42% to 53% between FY22 and FY26, cloud-compute share from 9% to 23%, networking from 26% to 37%, and automotive/robotics from 31% to 44% (Arm's disclosure). CSS deepens the lock-in by taking on the integration work customers used to do themselves — and charges roughly double for it.

Key Vulnerabilities

Channel conflict from making its own chips: Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Qualcomm and MediaTek are all Arm licensees. The AGI CPU puts Arm on the same shelf as their products. If large customers respond by shifting new designs toward RISC-V or in-house architectures, the royalty annuity that justifies the multiple erodes. Meta as anchor customer is a strength, but concentration in one hyperscaler cuts both ways.

The Qualcomm litigation went against Arm (verified): In December 2024 a Delaware jury found Qualcomm did not breach its licences by using Nuvia-designed Oryon cores; on September 30, 2025 the court entered a full and final judgment for Qualcomm and denied Arm a new trial. Arm said it would appeal. Qualcomm's separate January 2025 suit against Arm (alleging breach and anti-competitive conduct) was expected to go to trial around spring 2026 — its outcome, and the status of Arm's appeal, were not verified from any source fetched for this report. The practical consequence is that Qualcomm keeps paying the lower architecture-licence royalty on its Snapdragon X and forthcoming phone chips, capping one of Arm's largest customers' royalty-per-chip.

RISC-V: The open, royalty-free instruction set is winning microcontrollers and Chinese designs where export politics make Arm licences a risk. Share in premium application processors and data center is still small, but every RISC-V win in China is a permanent hole in future royalty growth.

Smartphone dependency and the free-float squeeze: Handsets remain the largest royalty pool and Arm now expects a double-digit unit decline. Meanwhile, with only ~142.6M shares in public hands and 12.6% of that float sold short, the stock moves violently — beta of 3.91 — in both directions. Any SoftBank secondary or margin-loan event would be a supply shock to a thin float.

Notable One-Off Items

GAAP vs non-GAAP gap: Q1 FY27 GAAP operating income was $91M (7.1% margin) against non-GAAP $531M (41.2%). The difference is overwhelmingly stock-based compensation. The forward P/E and consensus EPS quoted everywhere are non-GAAP; the trailing 257x is GAAP. Know which one you're looking at.

Related-party licensing: ~$193M of Q1 FY27 licence revenue (about 15% of total revenue) came from SoftBank, the controlling shareholder, with a ~$200M/quarter run-rate expected. It is real revenue under real contracts, but it is not arm's-length demand from the open market.

FY24 operating income collapse: FY24 operating income was $76.5M vs $712.5M in FY23 because of IPO-related stock-comp charges — FY24 GAAP EPS of $0.29 is not a clean base year for growth math.

FCF volatility: FY25 free cash flow was $178M versus $998M in FY24 and $979M in FY26 — timing of licence collections, not a business change.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Arm is the toll road for the post-x86 data center: cloud-compute share went from 9% to 23% in four years and data-center royalties are doubling annually. Royalty-per-chip rises mechanically as v9 (2x rate) and CSS (2x again) replace v8, with 70%+ of FY31 royalties already contracted. Layer on the AGI CPU — $2B of demand before a single meaningful dollar of revenue — and management's $25B / >$9 EPS FY2031 framework makes today's $252 look like ~28x FY31 earnings. The thin float means any upside surprise is amplified.

Bear Case

At 257x GAAP and 102x forward earnings, ARM is priced for the FY2031 plan to work perfectly — and the plan requires a company with no manufacturing experience to build a $15B chip business against Nvidia, Intel, AMD and the very hyperscalers whose royalties fund it. Royalty growth is already slowing to high-teens on smartphone weakness; Qualcomm has legally locked in a lower royalty; RISC-V is a permanent ceiling in China. The stock has just shown it can lose 47% in six weeks on no company-specific news, and SoftBank's ~90% stake is an overhang that will eventually be sold.

Valuation with no margin for error — A PEG of ~4 on forward numbers means the stock needs both the growth rate and the multiple to hold. A de-rating from 100x to 60x forward, with earnings intact, is a 40% drawdown — and that has happened twice since 2025.
2
Execution and channel conflict in the AGI CPU business — Only ~$1B of the $2B demand has secured capacity; wafers, substrates and memory are all rationed. Hardware gross margins will dilute the 97% model, and licensees may retaliate by diversifying architectures.

  Industry Commentary
Is Hyperscaler AI Capex Peaking?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The July 2026 sell-off was the market's first serious test of the "AI capex goes up forever" assumption: weak reads from Alphabet and Meta knocked ~20% off the PHLX Semiconductor Index in weeks. For Arm this matters twice over. Data-center royalties — the fastest-growing line, more than doubling YoY — are a direct function of how many Grace, Graviton, Cobalt and Axion CPUs the hyperscalers deploy. And the AGI CPU's $2B demand pipeline is capex from the same budgets. Arm's own TAM math ($330B cloud-AI market in FY26 growing to $1.25T by FY31) assumes the build-out continues at roughly 30% a year. If hyperscaler spending merely flattens, both the royalty growth rate and the AGI CPU ramp slow simultaneously — and a 100x multiple has no cushion for "flat".
Smartphone Units, RISC-V and the Royalty Base
ONGOING HEADWIND
Handsets are still the largest slice of Arm's royalty pool, and management now expects double-digit unit declines this fiscal year — the reason full-year royalty guidance slipped from ~20% to "high-teens" and Q2 to low-teens. Arm is offsetting units with rate (v9 at ~33% of royalties and climbing, CSS at 2–3x), which is why royalty revenue can still grow 22% while the phone market shrinks. The longer-term erosion comes from RISC-V: royalty-free, open, and politically attractive in China, where Arm licences carry export-control risk. It has taken microcontrollers and is creeping into application processors. Every Chinese SoC that launches on RISC-V is a royalty Arm never collects — small today, but the FY2031 20% royalty CAGR assumes Arm keeps winning the designs it has always won.
The x86-to-Arm Migration in the Data Center
EMERGING TAILWIND — BULL & BEAR IMPLICATIONS
Bull case: AI racks need host CPUs that are cheap on power, and Arm's cloud-compute share going from 9% to 23% in four years says the migration is real. Nvidia's Grace/Vera, all four hyperscalers' in-house CPUs, and now IBM's mainframe all run Arm. Arm says spending on Arm-based accelerated servers has surpassed x86 in the last two quarters. If the data-center CPU market doubles to $100B+ by FY31 and Arm's share keeps compounding, royalties alone justify a large business — before the AGI CPU adds a hardware line.

Bear case: Arm's biggest data-center royalty payers are also its most capable potential competitors, and the AGI CPU forces them to decide whether they want to buy from a supplier who now sells against them. Intel and AMD will not concede the host-CPU slot quietly, and every Arm hardware dollar comes with 30–40% margins instead of 97%. The migration can be real and still not be worth 100x earnings.
