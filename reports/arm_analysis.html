01 Company Overview
Arm does not make chips — or at least, it didn't until this year. It designs the CPU instruction set and processor cores that sit inside roughly every smartphone on earth and, increasingly, inside the data center: Nvidia's Grace, Amazon's Graviton, Microsoft's Cobalt and Google's Axion are all Arm-based. Arm earns money two ways: an up-front licence fee when a chipmaker takes its IP (intellectual property), and a royalty on every chip that ships. In fiscal 2026 (year ended March 31, 2026) revenue was $4.92B, up 22.8% YoY — the third straight year of 20%+ growth — split $2.61B royalty and $2.31B licence. In the latest quarter (Q2 of fiscal 2027, ended September 30, 2026) revenue rose 27% to $1.44B, but licences did most of the work: licence revenue jumped 50% while royalties grew only 8%. Arm's parent, SoftBank, still owns the overwhelming majority of the shares; the public float (the shares anyone can actually trade) is only ~144.5M of 1.07B shares.
The central debate: ARM trades at roughly 198x trailing GAAP earnings and ~77x this fiscal year's expected non-GAAP earnings, even after falling 61% from its June high. The bulls argue that royalty-per-chip keeps rising as newer designs replace older ones (Armv9 at ~2x the v8 rate, CSS at ~2x v9 again), that data-center royalties doubled again last quarter, and that the new in-house "AGI CPU" business could add $15B of revenue by FY2031. The bears point to the quarter: royalties, the recurring stream the valuation rests on, grew just 8% and are guided to low single digits next quarter, and the stock that ran from $105 in January to $452 in June has already given back most of that rise.

The recurring half. Every Arm-based chip that ships pays a royalty, and the trend that matters is royalty per chip, not units. Three levers lift it: (1) Armv9, the current architecture generation, carries roughly twice the royalty rate of Armv8 and was ~33% of royalty revenue in Q1 FY27, up from near zero a few years ago; (2) CSS (Compute Subsystems), pre-integrated CPU clusters that Arm sells as a near-finished design, command roughly 2x a standalone v9 royalty, and later generations reach 3x; (3) mix shift toward data center, where chips are bigger and pricier. Data-center royalties doubled YoY again in Q2 FY27 and physical-AI royalties (cars and robots) grew 50%. Arm says about 70% of the royalties it forecasts for fiscal 2027–2031 are at rates already under contract, and models 20% annual royalty growth FY26–31. The quarter showed the limit of that arithmetic: total royalties rose only 8% to $670M, short of the low-teens growth guided in July, because smartphones, still the largest royalty pool, are being squeezed by high memory prices. CFO Jason Child said edge-AI royalties, the group that includes smartphones, were "roughly flat year-on-year" and guided fiscal Q3 royalties to grow "low teens sequentially and low single digits year-on-year." Royalties grew 14.9% in the first half, so the "high teens" full-year rate Arm pointed to in July would now take a sharp fourth-quarter jump; management did not repeat it on the November call.

Yes, Arm now makes chips. At its "Arm Everywhere" event on March 24, 2026 the company unveiled the Arm AGI CPU, production silicon aimed at the host-CPU slot in agentic-AI racks, claiming >2x performance per rack versus x86 and up to $10B of savings on data-center spending per gigawatt. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

350B+ Chips Shipped 30M+ Developers ~99% Smartphone CPU Share Contracted Royalty Rates to FY31 97–98% Gross Margin Power-Efficiency Lead vs x86 CSS Raises Switching Costs
Arm's moat is the instruction set: decades of software — every mobile OS, every app store, an increasing share of Linux server workloads — compiled for Arm, plus more than 30M developers who never think about it. That installed base means a chipmaker choosing Arm gets the ecosystem for free, and a chipmaker leaving Arm has to drag its customers' software with it. The economics are near-perfect: 97.4% gross margin, modest spending on equipment, and a royalty that grows as the world's chip content grows. Arm's share of total chip value rose from 42% to 53% between FY22 and FY26, cloud-compute share from 9% to 23%, networking from 26% to 37%, and automotive/robotics from 31% to 44% (Arm's disclosure). Arm also says its share of servers at the top cloud companies went from 15% in calendar 2024 to more than 50% in calendar 2025. CSS deepens the lock-in by taking on the integration work customers used to do themselves — and charges roughly double for it.

Key Vulnerabilities

Competing with its own customers by making chips: Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Qualcomm and MediaTek are all Arm licensees. The AGI CPU puts Arm on the same shelf as their products. If large customers respond by shifting new designs toward RISC-V or in-house architectures, the steady royalty income that justifies the high share price erodes. Meta as anchor customer is a strength, but relying heavily on one big cloud company cuts both ways.

The Qualcomm fight is not over: In December 2024 a Delaware jury found Qualcomm did not breach its licences by using Nuvia-designed Oryon cores; on September 30, 2025 the court entered a full and final judgment for Qualcomm and denied Arm a new trial, and Arm appealed to the Third Circuit on October 1, 2025. Qualcomm's separate suit went to a jury in October 2026. On October 13 the jury found Arm breached the Qualcomm architecture licence by failing to provide required software patches and development tools, and interfered with Qualcomm's relationship with Meta, but rejected several other allegations and awarded just $2 in damages (Arm says it won four claims and lost two at $1 each). The bigger stake sits with the judge: Maryellen Noreika still has to decide whether Qualcomm can invoke a contract provision that would let it stop paying Arm royalties for five years, potentially worth billions (Computerworld, Oct 15); Arm argues the provision is unenforceable. In its Q2 FY27 6-K, Arm's management says it does not believe any pending legal matter is reasonably possible to have a material adverse effect.

RISC-V: The open, royalty-free instruction set is gaining ground in microcontrollers and Chinese designs where export politics make Arm licences a risk. Share in premium application processors and data center is still small, but every RISC-V win in China is a permanent hole in future royalty growth.

Smartphone dependency and the small public float: Handsets remain the largest royalty pool, and CFO Child says high memory prices are hurting unit sales, particularly in mid-range and cheaper phones. Edge-AI royalties were roughly flat in Q2 FY27. Meanwhile, with only ~144.5M shares in public hands and 12.2% of those sold short at the October 15 settlement, the stock moves violently in both directions: its beta (how much it tends to move for each 1% the market moves) is 3.94. SoftBank has also borrowed against its stake, through a margin loan Bloomberg reported was raised to $25B in September 2026 and secured by 769M Arm shares as of May, so any SoftBank sale or forced selling would put a flood of shares up for sale against a small pool of tradeable shares.

Notable One-Off Items

GAAP vs non-GAAP gap: Q2 FY27 GAAP operating income was $201M (13.9% margin) against non-GAAP $638M (44.2%). The difference is mostly stock-based compensation. GAAP diluted EPS fell to $0.12 from $0.22 even though GAAP operating income rose 23%, because of items below the operating line, while non-GAAP EPS rose 33% to $0.52. The forward P/E and consensus EPS quoted everywhere are non-GAAP; the trailing 198x is GAAP. Nasdaq's calendar shows $0.15 against a $0.22 Nasdaq consensus, on its own basis, which matches neither of Arm's figures. Know which one you're looking at.

Related-party licensing: $208.7M of Q2 FY27 revenue (about 14% of the total) came from SoftBank affiliates under the agreement Arm's 6-K calls a consulting agreement and the CFO describes as technology licensing and design services, and total related-party revenue was $424M (29%). CFO Child expects the SoftBank agreement to keep contributing about $209M a quarter for the rest of the fiscal year. It is real revenue under real contracts, but it is not arm's-length demand from the open market.

FY24 operating income collapse: FY24 operating income was $76.5M vs $712.5M in FY23 because of IPO-related stock-comp charges — FY24 GAAP EPS of $0.29 is not a clean base year for growth math.

FCF volatility: FY25 free cash flow was $178M versus $998M in FY24 and $979M in FY26. Q2 FY27 non-GAAP free cash flow was $110M, down from $411M a year earlier; the trailing twelve months came to $1.10B.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Arm is the toll road for the move away from x86 in the data center: it says its share of servers at the top cloud companies went from 15% in calendar 2024 to more than 50% in calendar 2025, and data-center royalties doubled again last quarter. Royalty-per-chip rises mechanically as v9 (2x rate) and CSS (2x again) replace v8, with about 70% of the royalties it forecasts for fiscal 2027–2031 already at contracted rates. Add the AGI CPU, with purchase orders through calendar 2027 and up to $2B of revenue expected over FY27–28, and management's $25B / >$9 EPS FY2031 framework makes today's $175 look like ~19x FY31 earnings. The small float means any upside surprise is amplified, and the stock is already down 61% from its high.

Bear Case

At 198x GAAP and 77x this fiscal year's expected earnings, ARM is still priced for the FY2031 plan to work, and the plan requires a company with no manufacturing experience to build a $15B chip business against Nvidia, Intel, AMD and the very cloud companies whose royalties fund it. Royalty growth has slowed to 8%, with low single digits guided next, on smartphone weakness. Qualcomm won the Nuvia licence fight and a judge could still let it stop paying royalties for five years. RISC-V is a permanent ceiling in China. The stock has fallen 61% from its June high in under five months, and SoftBank's almost-90% stake, with a $25B loan against it, is a large block of shares that may eventually be sold.

  Industry Commentary
Is AI Spending by the Big Cloud Companies Peaking?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The July 2026 sell-off was the market's first serious test of the assumption that AI spending goes up forever: by July 20 the PHLX Semiconductor Index was almost 20% below its record high. October delivered a second test, with ARM down 29% for the month, including 10% on October 30 after Intel's weak fourth-quarter outlook hit chip stocks. For Arm this matters twice over. Data-center royalties, the fastest-growing line and doubling YoY, are a direct function of how many Grace, Graviton, Cobalt and Axion CPUs the big cloud companies deploy. And AGI CPU revenue depends on the same budgets. Arm's own market math ($330B cloud-AI market in FY26 growing to $1.25T by FY31) assumes the build-out continues at roughly 30% a year. If those companies' spending merely flattens, both royalty growth and the build-up of AGI CPU sales slow at once, and a 77x forward P/E has no cushion for flat spending.
Smartphone Units, RISC-V and the Royalty Base
ONGOING HEADWIND
Handsets are still the largest slice of Arm's royalty pool, and high memory prices are squeezing unit sales, especially in mid-range and cheaper phones. That is the main reason total royalties grew only 8% in Q2 FY27 and are guided to low single digits in Q3, even with data-center royalties doubling. CFO Child cited IDC's forecast of about a 1% decline next year and said edge-AI royalties should pick up again as the phone market normalises. Arm offsets units with rate (v9 at ~33% of royalties in Q1 FY27, CSS at 2–3x), but for now rate has only held edge-AI royalties roughly flat. The longer-term erosion comes from RISC-V: royalty-free, open, and politically attractive in China, where Arm licences carry export-control risk. It is gaining in microcontrollers and creeping into application processors. Every Chinese chip that launches on RISC-V is a royalty Arm never collects: small today, but Arm's plan for 20% annual royalty growth through FY2031 assumes it keeps winning the designs it has always won.
The x86-to-Arm Migration in the Data Center
EMERGING TAILWIND — BULL & BEAR IMPLICATIONS
Bull case: AI racks need host CPUs that are cheap on power, and Arm says its share of servers at the top cloud companies went from 15% in calendar 2024 to more than 50% in calendar 2025, with more than 60% expected by the end of 2026. Nvidia's Grace/Vera and the in-house CPUs at Amazon, Google and Microsoft run Arm, and so will the next-generation IBM mainframe chip announced in August. If the data-center CPU market doubles to $100B+ by FY31 and Arm's share keeps growing, royalties alone justify a large business, before the AGI CPU adds a hardware line.

Bear case: Arm's biggest data-center royalty payers are also its most capable potential competitors, and the AGI CPU forces them to decide whether they want to buy from a supplier who now sells against them. Intel and AMD will not concede the host-CPU slot quietly, and every Arm hardware dollar comes with a gross margin in the high 30s to low 40s at first, per the CFO, instead of 97%. The migration can be real and still not be worth 77x forward earnings.
