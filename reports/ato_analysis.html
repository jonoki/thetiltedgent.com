01 Company Overview

Atmos Energy is the largest pure-play natural gas LDC in the United States: over 3.4 million customers in eight states, about 76,000 miles of distribution and transmission mains, and — the part most people miss — Atmos Pipeline–Texas (APT), a ~5,700-mile intrastate pipeline with five storage facilities that spans the Texas shale basins and connects to the Waha, Katy and Carthage hubs. Roughly 2.1 million of the customers are in Texas, and about 65% of distribution rate base sits there. The company traces to 1906 in the Texas Panhandle; its Energas gas division was spun out of Pioneer Corporation in 1983 and took the Atmos name in October 1988. The 2004 acquisition of TXU Gas added its Texas distribution and pipeline operations.

A regulated utility does not sell gas for profit; it earns an allowed return on the capital it has invested in pipes, called rate base. So the story of Atmos is a capital-spending story. Rate base went from $18.8B at FY2024 to $21.4B at FY2025 (+13.8%) and, by the company's estimate on the November 5 call, to about $25B at FY2026 (+15%). Its plan, rolled forward to fiscal 2031, takes it to about $46B on roughly $29B of capital spending, about 88% of it aimed at safety and reliability — replacing old steel and cast-iron pipe. The allowed returns are 9.8% blended on distribution and 11.45% on APT. Diluted EPS was $8.49 in FY2026 (up 13.8% from $7.46), at the top of the $8.40–$8.50 guidance range. FY2027 guidance is $9.05–$9.25, and the company targets 6–8% annual EPS growth from the midpoint of that range through FY2031; the CFO put fiscal 2031 EPS at $12.35–$12.55 on the November 5 call. FY2026 was the 24th straight year of EPS growth, and the indicated FY2027 dividend of $4.36 (+9.0%) would be the 43rd straight annual increase.

The stock closed at $173.22, 8.8% below its April 2026 month-end of $189.98 but 10.7% above its September 30 close of $156.44, after a year-end quarter that met its guidance rather than raising it. The first two sessions after the results were small declines (−0.8% and −1.0%). Nothing in the reported numbers broke, so the question this report asks is what the market is pricing: the cost of funding a plan that nearly doubles rate base, the durability of Texas regulatory support, or simply the price of a bond-like stock (one held for its steady dividend) in a world of higher rates?

Pipes under the street in Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Kentucky, Tennessee, Kansas, Colorado and Virginia. The earnings engine is simple: spend on replacement pipe, file under an annual mechanism, earn the allowed return. Atmos says more than 90% of annual capital begins to earn within six months of the end of the test year (the period a rate filing covers) and ~99% within twelve, because most jurisdictions have annual filing mechanisms (Texas GRIP and RRM, Louisiana RSC, Mississippi SRF, Kentucky PRP, Tennessee ARM and others). The six-month figure was 95% until a Mississippi rate case moved that state's reviews from forward-looking to historical, a decision Atmos is challenging in court (November 5 call transcript). Weather normalization covers 98% of distribution revenue, so a warm winter does not break the year. Growth is also physical: in fiscal 2026 the company added about 49,000 residential customers (more than 37,000 in Texas), about 3,200 commercial customers and 17 industrial or large-load customers (November 5 call transcript). Q3 FY2026 distribution net income was $89.4M, up 26.8% from $70.5M. Customers pay for all of it — the company estimated in May that gas cost itself is ~38% of the bill in FY2026, falling to ~27% by FY2030, which means the delivery share the utility earns on keeps rising.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchised monopoly territories Annual rate mechanisms (>90% capex earning ≤6 months) Weather normalization on 98% of dist. revenue APT hub connectivity (Waha, Katy, Carthage) Texas "all fuels" policy support 60% equity cap, $3.8B liquidity

The moat is legal, not competitive. Nobody else can lay a second gas main down the same Dallas street, and the regulator in return caps what Atmos can earn. What makes Atmos unusual among utilities is regulatory lag — or rather the lack of it. Most utilities spend first and wait a year or more for a rate case; Atmos says over 90% of its annual capital starts earning within six months. That is why earned returns sit close to allowed ones: GAAP ROE of 9.79% on book equity against a 9.8% blended distribution allowance. The trade-off is concentration: ~65% of distribution rate base and all of APT are in Texas, so the company's economics are, to a first approximation, a bet on Texas regulators continuing to let it earn a fair return on its pipes.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Texas concentration. Two-thirds of distribution rate base and the entire higher-return pipeline sit under one state's regulators and legislature. Texas policy has been supportive — in FY2026 Atmos deferred about $175M of pre-tax costs under Texas Rule 7.7102, which covers infrastructure spending (November 5 call transcript) — but what one set of lawmakers and regulators grants, a later one can revisit. Earnings that depend on a favourable rule carry the risk that the rule changes.
02
The customer bill. Nearly doubling rate base to about $46B means the delivery charge customers pay must rise substantially. Atmos argues gas cost falls as a share of the bill (~38% in FY2026 to ~27% by FY2030, in the May 2026 plan), but affordability is the constraint every regulator watches, and the political tolerance for rising bills is not a number in any filing.
03
Funding and dilution. FY2025 free cash flow was −$1.51B and the twelve months to June 30 were −$2.02B — negative by design, as capex ($4.2B in FY2026) runs far ahead of cash earnings. The company expects about $17B of new long-term financing over the five-year plan, from debt and from selling new shares, and the plan includes about $500M of corporate alternative minimum tax, with payments starting in FY2027; in May it guided average diluted shares up from 161M in FY2025 to 168–171M in FY2026. EPS growth has to outrun the share count, and the cost of that capital is set by markets, not regulators.
04
Non-repeatable pipeline spreads. APT's through-system gains rode Texas hub spreads of $4.66 versus $1.77 a year earlier over the first nine months. Management said spreads returned to historical levels in the fourth quarter, and that the roughly $149M year-over-year jump in Texas cost deferrals will not repeat in FY2027 (November 5 call transcript). That slice of earnings is gone from the base, and some of it is being credited back to customers through Rider REV.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items flattered FY2026 relative to the underlying trend. First, APT through-system gains: Texas hub spreads averaged $4.66 against $1.77 a year earlier over the first nine months, and returned to historical levels in the fourth quarter. Second, Texas cost deferrals: for the full year the company deferred about $175M of pre-tax costs under Texas Rule 7.7102 (about $100M Distribution, $75M APT; November 5 call transcript), up $149M on FY2025. Management does not expect another increase of that size. These are legitimate regulatory mechanisms rather than accounting gimmicks, but they explain why FY2026 EPS growth (13.8%) ran above the 6–8% the company targets from here: the FY2027 guide of $9.05–$9.25 is +6.6% to +9.0% on $8.49 (our arithmetic), +7.8% at the midpoint. Share count is the offset: forward equity sales pushed average diluted shares up from 161M to a guided 168–171M.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $92.12 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $173.22, ATO gained 88.0% on price. Dividends of roughly $16 per share over the period (estimate, summing $2.72, $2.96, $3.22 and $3.48 for FY2022–FY2025 plus the FY2026 dividend of $4.00) lift that to roughly +106% total return before reinvestment — a strong five years for a utility. The shape: a 2022 rate-shock stall between roughly $100 and $120, a flat 2023, then a steady climb from mid-2024 as rate base and EPS grew and investors paid more for each dollar of earnings, peaking at a $189.98 month-end in April 2026. May 2026 then fell 11.0% in a single month — the largest monthly decline in the window — and the stock slid to a $156.44 close on September 30 before gaining 10.2% in October and ending at $173.22. The company raised FY2026 guidance on May 6, so the May drop was not an earnings disappointment.

The 6-period monthly RSI now sits at 54.3, back near the middle of its range after touching 32.5 at the September 30 month-end — the lowest reading anywhere in the five-year series (the previous low was 36.7 in September 2023) and just above the 30 "oversold" line. The 10-month moving average, about $174.39, is 0.7% above the price: the stock is back within 1% of its recent average. For a stock whose earnings grew every year of the window, the 2026 swing was mostly about how much investors were willing to pay per dollar of earnings, not about the earnings themselves.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 20.4x trailing and about 19.0x next-year earnings for a utility that guides FY2027 EPS up about 8% at the midpoint to $9.05–$9.25 and plans to take rate base from about $25B to about $46B by FY2031, with a 9.8% blended distribution allowance and 11.45% on APT, and a regulatory setup that puts more than 90% of each year's capital into earnings within six months — which is why the earned ROE (9.79%) matches the allowed one. EPS has grown 24 years in a row and the dividend is guided up 9.0% for a 43rd straight increase. The stock is 8.8% off its April month-end, FY2026 EPS landed at the top of the guide, the average analyst target is 5.5% above the price, and about 49,000 new residential customers in FY2026, more than 37,000 of them in Texas, say the Texas growth underneath the plan is real.

Bear Case

This year's 13.8% EPS growth leaned on things that do not repeat on schedule: Texas hub spreads of $4.66 versus $1.77 over nine months (back to historical levels in the fourth quarter), and about $175M of pre-tax cost deferrals under one Texas rule, up $149M on the year. Strip those out and the company itself only promises 6–8%. Meanwhile taking rate base from about $25B to about $46B needs roughly $29B of capital against negative free cash flow of −$2.02B, funded by about $17B of new long-term financing that includes selling new shares. Customers' delivery bills have to rise to pay for it, in a portfolio two-thirds concentrated in one state. At 20x for a 6–8% grower with a 2.5% yield, the stock has to compete with bonds — and analysts, 12 Holds out of 14, have an average target only 5.5% above the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Texas regulatory and legislative concentration. About 65% of distribution rate base and all of APT sit in Texas; the annual mechanisms and the deferral rules are the reason earned returns track allowed returns. A less supportive regime would raise regulatory lag across most of the company at once.
02
Cost of capital on a $29B plan. The plan is funded externally — free cash flow was −$2.02B over the twelve months to June 30 and average diluted shares were guided up ~5% for FY2026. Higher interest rates or a lower share price raise the cost of every dollar of equity issued, and allowed ROEs are reset only slowly.
03
APT spreads back to normal. Through-system gains on $4.66 average spreads over nine months helped push Pipeline & Storage Q3 net income up 32.2%. Spreads returned to historical levels in the fourth quarter, and part of the windfall flows back to customers via Rider REV. FY2027 has to be earned on the regulated base and fixed charges (85–90% of APT revenue, per the call).
Industry Commentary
Affordability vs. the capital plan
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Gas utilities everywhere are in the same trade: replace ageing pipe for safety, earn a regulated return on the replacement, and ask customers to pay for it. Atmos's version is aggressive — rate base going from about $25B to about $46B in five years — and its argument is that the bill stays manageable because the commodity share falls: purchased gas was modelled in May 2026 at ~38% of the customer bill in FY2026 and ~27% by FY2030 under gas-price assumptions of $2.75–$3.75. That assumption is the hinge. If commodity prices run above that band while delivery charges climb, the total bill rises on both lines at once, and that is when regulators start trimming requested increases.

Utilities as bond substitutes
ONGOING HEADWIND

A 2.5% dividend yield growing 6–8% a year is weighed by investors against Treasury yields. The five-year chart shows the pattern: the 2022 rate shock stalled the stock for two years even as earnings kept growing. The 2026 slide from $189.98 in April to $156.44 on September 30 happened with guidance raised and reaffirmed, which is what investors paying less per dollar of earnings, rather than fundamental damage, looks like; the recovery to $173.22 came with results at the top of the guide. Beta of 0.55 describes the stock's relationship to equities; its relationship to long rates is the one that has mattered.

Texas growth and all-fuels policy
EMERGING TAILWIND

Where some states are discouraging new gas hookups, 96% of Atmos's rate base sits in states with "all fuels" policy support, and its Texas territory added more than 37,000 residential customers in FY2026, and the company added 17 industrial or large-load customers across its footprint. The bull framing: Atmos is a regulated growth company in the fastest-growing large state, with a regulator that lets capital earn almost immediately. The bear framing: that same concentration means one legislature and one commission effectively set the equity's value, and the market now pays less per dollar of earnings for it. The test is the rate outcomes still to come: $264M of increases were implemented in the first weeks of FY2027 ($255M of it in Texas), and the Mississippi Supreme Court ruling is pending.
