01 Company Overview

Atmos Energy is the largest pure-play natural gas LDC in the United States: over 3.4 million customers in eight states, about 76,000 miles of distribution and transmission mains, and — the part most people miss — Atmos Pipeline–Texas (APT), a ~5,700-mile intrastate pipeline with five storage facilities that spans the Texas shale basins and connects to the Waha, Katy and Carthage hubs. Roughly 2.1 million of the customers are in Texas, and about 65% of distribution rate base sits there. The company traces to 1906 in the Texas Panhandle; its Energas gas division was spun out of Pioneer Corporation in 1983 and took the Atmos name in October 1988. The 2004 acquisition of TXU Gas added its Texas distribution and pipeline operations.

A regulated utility does not sell gas for profit; it earns an allowed return on the capital it has invested in pipes, called rate base. So the story of Atmos is a capital-spending story. Rate base went from $18.8B at FY2024 to $21.4B at FY2025 (+13.8%), and management's May 2026 plan takes it to $41–43B by FY2030 on roughly $26B of capital spending, more than 85% of it aimed at safety and reliability — replacing old steel and cast-iron pipe. The allowed returns are 9.8% blended on distribution and 11.45% on APT. FY2025 diluted EPS was $7.46; FY2026 guidance, raised in May and reaffirmed in August, is $8.40–$8.50, and the company targets 6–8% annual EPS and dividend growth through FY2030. It has grown EPS for 24 straight years and the dividend for 42.

And yet the stock closed at $160.30, 15.6% below its April 2026 month-end of $189.98, sitting near a 52-week low while the fundamentals set records. That is the question this report is about: nothing in the reported numbers broke, so what is the market pricing — the cost of funding a doubling of rate base, the durability of Texas regulatory support, or simply the price of a bond-like stock in a world of higher rates?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchised monopoly territories Annual rate mechanisms (>90% capex earning ≤6 months) Weather normalization on 98% of dist. revenue APT hub connectivity (Waha, Katy, Carthage) Texas "all fuels" policy support 60% equity cap, $4.6B liquidity

The moat is legal, not competitive. Nobody else can lay a second gas main down the same Dallas street, and the regulator in return caps what Atmos can earn. What makes Atmos unusual among utilities is regulatory lag — or rather the lack of it. Most utilities spend first and wait a year or more for a rate case; Atmos says over 90% of its annual capital starts earning within six months. That is why earned returns sit close to allowed ones: GAAP ROE of 9.79% on book equity against a 9.8% blended distribution allowance. The trade-off is concentration: ~65% of distribution rate base and all of APT are in Texas, so the company's economics are, to a first approximation, a bet on the Texas regulatory compact.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Texas concentration. Two-thirds of distribution rate base and the entire higher-return pipeline sit under one state's regulators and legislature. Texas policy has been supportive — call coverage attributes $132M ($0.63/share) of year-to-date benefit to Texas House Bill 4384 — but what one legislature grants, a later one can revisit. Earnings that depend on a favourable statute carry statute risk.
02
The customer bill. Doubling rate base to $41–43B means the delivery charge customers pay must rise substantially. Atmos argues gas cost falls as a share of the bill (~38% in FY2026 to ~27% by FY2030), but affordability is the constraint every regulator watches, and the political tolerance for rising bills is not a number in any filing.
03
Funding and dilution. FY2025 free cash flow was −$1.51B and TTM is −$2.02B — negative by design, as capex of ~$4B runs far ahead of cash earnings. The gap is filled by debt and equity forwards; average diluted shares rise from 161M to 168–171M in FY2026. EPS growth has to outrun the share count, and the cost of that capital is set by markets, not regulators.
04
Non-repeatable pipeline spreads. APT's through-system gains rode Texas hub spreads of $4.66 versus $1.77 a year earlier. Management already guided the second-half contribution to the low end of its range. When spreads normalise, that slice of earnings goes with them, and some of it is being credited back to customers through Rider REV.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items flatter FY2026 relative to the underlying trend. First, APT through-system gains: wide Texas hub spreads added about $34M ($0.16/share) of revenue in the first nine months, and management expects the second-half contribution near the low end of $0.08–$0.12. Second, Texas legislative and deferral effects: call coverage attributes $132M ($0.63/share) of year-to-date benefit to Texas House Bill 4384 ($71M Distribution, $61M APT), and the company's segment bridges show sizable deferrals of infrastructure costs under Texas Rule 7.7102 ($29.0M Distribution and $33.1M APT in the first half alone). These are legitimate regulatory mechanisms rather than accounting gimmicks, but they explain why FY2026 EPS growth (~13% at the guidance midpoint) runs well above the 6–8% the company targets for later years. Share count is the offset: equity forwards push average diluted shares from 161M to 168–171M.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 19.0x trailing and about 17.8x next-year earnings for a utility with a stated path to double rate base from $21.4B to $41–43B by FY2030, a 9.8% blended distribution allowance and 11.45% on APT, and a regulatory setup that puts more than 90% of each year's capital into earnings within six months — which is why the earned ROE (9.79%) matches the allowed one. EPS has grown 24 years in a row and is guided up ~13% this year; the dividend has grown 42 years and was just raised 14.9%. The stock is 16% off its April level with no earnings miss in sight, every one of 14 analyst targets is above the price, and ~51,000 new customers a year say the Texas growth underneath the plan is real.

Bear Case

This year's 13% EPS growth leans on things that do not repeat on schedule: Texas hub spreads of $4.66 versus $1.77, $132M of year-to-date benefit attributed to one Texas statute, and cost deferrals. Strip those out and the company itself only promises 6–8%. Meanwhile doubling rate base needs roughly $26B of capital against negative free cash flow of −$2.02B, funded by debt and equity that grows the share count from 161M to 168–171M in a single year. Customers' delivery bills have to rise to pay for it, in a portfolio two-thirds concentrated in one state. At 19x for a 6–8% grower with a 2.5% yield, the stock has to compete with bonds — and the Street, 12 Holds out of 14, is cutting targets, not raising them.

Top 3 Risks

01
Texas regulatory and legislative concentration. About 65% of distribution rate base and all of APT sit in Texas; the annual mechanisms, the deferral rules and the benefit attributed to House Bill 4384 are the reason earned returns track allowed returns. A less supportive regime would raise regulatory lag across most of the company at once.
02
Cost of capital on a $26B plan. The plan is funded externally — FCF is −$2.02B TTM and average diluted shares rise ~5% this year. Higher interest rates or a lower share price raise the cost of every dollar of equity issued, and allowed ROEs are reset only slowly.
03
Normalisation of APT spreads. Through-system gains on $4.66 average spreads helped push Pipeline & Storage Q3 net income up 32.2%. Management already guided to the low end for the second half, and part of the windfall flows back to customers via Rider REV. FY2027 has to be earned on the regulated base alone.
Industry Commentary
Affordability vs. the capital plan
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Gas utilities everywhere are in the same trade: replace ageing pipe for safety, earn a regulated return on the replacement, and ask customers to pay for it. Atmos's version is aggressive — rate base roughly doubling in five years — and its argument is that the bill stays manageable because the commodity share falls: purchased gas is modelled at ~38% of the customer bill in FY2026 and ~27% by FY2030 under gas-price assumptions of $2.75–$3.75. That assumption is the hinge. If commodity prices run above that band while delivery charges climb, the total bill rises on both lines at once, and that is when regulators start trimming requested increases.

Utilities as bond proxies
ONGOING HEADWIND

A 2.5% dividend yield growing 6–8% a year is valued, at the margin, against Treasury yields. The five-year chart shows the pattern: the 2022 rate shock stalled the stock for two years even as earnings compounded. The 2026 slide from $189.98 to $160.30 happened with guidance raised and reaffirmed, which is what multiple compression, rather than fundamental damage, looks like. Beta of 0.59 describes the stock's relationship to equities; its relationship to long rates is the one that has mattered.

Texas growth and all-fuels policy
EMERGING TAILWIND

Where some states are discouraging new gas hookups, 96% of Atmos's rate base sits in states with "all fuels" policy support, and its Texas territory is adding ~39,000 customers a year, plus commercial and industrial load (12 new industrial customers year to date, consuming ~950,000 Mcf annually). The bull framing: Atmos is a regulated growth company in the fastest-growing large state, with a regulator that lets capital earn almost immediately. The bear framing: that same concentration means one legislature and one commission effectively set the equity's value, and the market is now charging a lower multiple for it. The way to adjudicate is the Q1 FY2027 rate outcomes against the ~$334M pending — how much of what was filed gets approved.
