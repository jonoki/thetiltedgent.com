Company Overview

Broadcom designs and supplies semiconductors and enterprise infrastructure software, operating as a portfolio of category-leading franchises rather than a single product company. Its semiconductor business spans custom AI accelerators (XPUs) built for hyperscalers, Ethernet switching and routing silicon (Tomahawk, Jericho), optical and DSP components, wireless connectivity chips for smartphones, broadband access, and enterprise storage; its software business is anchored by VMware Cloud Foundation plus mainframe (CA) and security (Symantec) assets. Fiscal 2025 revenue reached $63.9B, up 24% YoY, and the TTM figure through Q2 FY2026 is $75.5B, up 32%. At roughly $1.87 trillion, Broadcom is the world's second-most-valuable semiconductor company behind Nvidia and the eighth-most-valuable public company globally.

The company's defining story in 2026 is custom AI silicon. AI semiconductor revenue hit $10.8B in Q2 FY2026, +143% YoY, and management guided Q3 AI revenue to roughly $16.0B — a figure larger than Broadcom's entire quarterly revenue two years ago. Management has disclosed a combined backlog of roughly $162B (approximately $73B AI plus a comparable software book) extending into 2028–2029, and has publicly targeted north of $100B in annual AI semiconductor revenue by FY2027.

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

Switching Costs IP & Patent Portfolio Scale in Advanced Packaging Co-Design Engineering Depth Foundry Allocation Priority Installed-Base Lock-In (VMware / CA) Capital Allocation Track Record

Broadcom's moat is not one thing — it is the compounding of two very different ones. On the silicon side, designing a custom AI accelerator is a multi-year, multi-hundred-million-dollar commitment in which the customer's software stack, memory architecture, thermals and rack design get co-engineered around Broadcom's SerDes, packaging and networking IP. Once a hyperscaler taped out its third generation with Broadcom, switching design partners means restarting a two-to-three-year clock and re-qualifying an entire fleet — which is why Google has stayed for seven TPU generations. Broadcom and Marvell together hold roughly 95% of the custom ASIC co-design market because almost nobody else can combine leading-edge SerDes, 2.5D/3D packaging know-how and guaranteed CoWoS (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate advanced packaging) allocation at TSMC. On the software side, the moat is simpler and older: mainframe tooling and hypervisors sit beneath mission-critical workloads where the cost of migration dwarfs the cost of the licence, which is precisely what lets Broadcom raise prices aggressively without losing the base. Wrapping both is Hock Tan's operating model — buy entrenched franchises, cut everything that is not the largest 20% of accounts, and redeploy the cash.

Key Vulnerabilities

Customer Concentration Is Extreme and Getting Worse

Six custom-chip customers drive the AI business, and a small number of them — Google, Meta, OpenAI — account for the bulk of it. Management acknowledged on the Q2 FY2026 call that Google is "looking elsewhere," and Google has been reported in talks with Marvell for inference silicon alongside its Broadcom TPU programme. Losing or halving a single flagship programme would be a multi-billion-dollar revenue event with no near-term replacement.

Margin Dilution from the AI Mix Shift

The fastest-growing revenue is the lowest-margin revenue. XPU system-level sales include third-party HBM and other components; Hock Tan stated plainly that "the mix between software and non-AI to the very rapidly growing AI semiconductor is just diluting gross margin." Consolidated gross margin has already stepped down roughly 100bp sequentially in recent quarters. Absolute gross profit dollars are exploding, but multiple compression follows reported margin percentages, not dollars.

Supply Chain Is the Binding Constraint, Not Demand

Broadcom has explicitly flagged TSMC advanced-node and packaging capacity as a limiter into 2026, with CoWoS effectively sold out and Nvidia holding roughly 60% of allocation. HBM and server DRAM are in acute shortage as memory makers reallocate capacity. A $162B backlog is only worth what can actually be shipped, and delivery visibility has already slipped beyond normal planning windows.

VMware Goodwill Erosion and Regulatory Attention

The pricing model that made VMware a $27B software franchise has generated genuine customer resentment, active migration programmes at large enterprises, and scrutiny from European regulators and customer coalitions. Retention is holding today because migration is painful — but every renewal now starts with the customer holding a documented exit plan, and Nutanix, Hyper-V, Proxmox and OpenShift Virtualization are all materially more credible in 2026 than they were in 2023.

Notable One-Off Items

Three distortions matter when reading these numbers. First, VMware purchase accounting: the $69B acquisition brought heavy intangible amortisation and, in FY2024, a $4.5B+ discrete tax charge that crushed GAAP EPS to $1.23 that year — which is why the trailing P/E of 64.8 overstates true valuation and the YoY EPS growth of +124.6% overstates true improvement. Second, share-based compensation runs several billion dollars annually and is excluded from the non-GAAP figures management guides to; the GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap in Q2 FY2026 was $12.07B non-GAAP net income versus $9.31B GAAP. Third, the AI revenue mix shift is structural, not one-off — the ~100bp sequential gross-margin declines are a permanent feature of selling XPU systems with third-party HBM content, not a transitory item that reverses. No material litigation reserves, restructuring charges or asset write-downs are currently distorting results.

Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Broadcom is the only credible full-stack alternative to Nvidia for hyperscalers who refuse to be single-sourced, and it monetises that position twice — once on the XPU, once on the Ethernet fabric around it. A $162B backlog stretching to 2029, a stated $100B+ FY2027 AI revenue target, and 24.7x forward earnings against 70% expected EPS growth (PEG of 0.52) is an unusual combination for a $1.9T company. Meanwhile $27B of high-margin, near-unchurnable software revenue funds the dividend and the debt paydown, giving the story a floor that pure-play AI names do not have.

Bear Case

The valuation now requires flawless execution on a backlog Broadcom does not fully control — TSMC allocates the CoWoS capacity and memory makers set the HBM price. Every incremental AI dollar carries a lower gross margin than the dollar it replaces, so reported margins compress even as the business grows, and multiples follow margins. Concentration is extreme: a handful of customers, one of whom (Google) is openly dual-sourcing to Marvell. If AI capex normalises even to a merely-strong pace, a 64x trailing earnings stock with $65B of debt has a long way to fall — the $215.88 low analyst target implies −45%.

Top 3 Risks

  1. Customer concentration and hyperscaler in-sourcing

    Six customers drive the AI franchise and three drive most of it. Google is in talks with Marvell for inference silicon; hyperscalers also continue to build internal design teams. Any single programme loss is a multi-billion-dollar revenue hole with a two-to-three-year replacement cycle.

  2. Gross-margin compression from AI mix

    XPU system sales bundle third-party HBM and components at far lower margin than software. Management has guided to continued ~100bp sequential gross-margin erosion. Absolute profit dollars rise, but the market prices semiconductor businesses off margin percentages and margin direction — this is the single most likely trigger for de-rating.

  3. Supply-chain dependency and AI capex cyclicality

    Broadcom is fabless: TSMC advanced nodes, CoWoS packaging and HBM supply are all outside its control and all currently constrained. Simultaneously, the demand side depends on hyperscaler capital budgets that have never been tested through a downturn at this scale. A capex digestion phase combined with a supply-driven delivery slip would hit revenue and sentiment at the same time.

Industry Commentary

Silicon Supply Is Now the Ceiling on AI Revenue

Critical constraint

The binding constraint in AI compute has shifted from demand to physical capacity. TSMC's CoWoS advanced packaging is sold out for 2026 with Nvidia holding roughly 60% of allocation; 2nm capacity is fully booked; and total leading-edge wafer demand is running near a million wafers against a supply base that takes years to expand. Broadcom has stated directly that it is "seeing supply constraints driven by TSMC capacity," and that delivery visibility and order fulfilment are already affected. In parallel, memory makers have reallocated fabs toward HBM and server DRAM, producing a shortage severe enough that SK Hynix has warned it may persist past 2030 and pushing DRAM contract prices up sharply. For Broadcom this cuts both ways: it protects pricing and keeps the backlog from being competed away, but it caps how fast $162B of orders can convert to revenue, and rising HBM costs land directly in cost of goods sold on XPU system sales.

Custom ASICs Are Taking Share from Merchant GPUs

Emerging tailwind

The structural shift underpinning Broadcom's re-rating is real and measurable. Custom ASIC-based accelerators are projected to reach roughly 27.8% of AI accelerator shipments in 2026, with custom ASIC unit shipments growing about 44.6% year-over-year versus roughly 16.1% for merchant GPUs. Nvidia still holds around 70% of the AI chip market by revenue, but the marginal dollar of hyperscaler inference spend is increasingly going to purpose-built silicon: OpenAI's Jalapeño, Google's TPU line, Meta's MTIA, Amazon's Trainium and Microsoft's Maia. Broadcom and Marvell together control roughly 95% of the co-design market, with Counterpoint projecting Broadcom at around 60% share of custom AI chips by 2027. The same shift plays out in networking, where Ethernet is displacing InfiniBand as the default AI fabric — the data-center Ethernet switch market grew 39.8% YoY to $15.4B in Q1 2026, and Broadcom's Tomahawk 6 (102.4 Tb/s) sits underneath most non-Nvidia deployments.

The AI Trade Has Become Reflexive — Bull vs Bear Framing

Ongoing headwind

June 4, 2026 was the clearest illustration yet of how fragile positioning has become: Broadcom beat on revenue and EPS, grew AI revenue 143%, and still erased over $250B of its own market value while taking $1.3 trillion out of the semiconductor complex in a single session — Nvidia −6%, AMD −10.9%, Intel −11.3%, Micron −7%, the SOX index down more than 6%. Nothing about the fundamentals changed; the whisper number did. The bull framing: that episode was an expectations reset, not a demand event. Backlog extends to 2029, the forward multiple is now below the NASDAQ-100's, and every disclosed data point on custom silicon share is still moving Broadcom's way — this is what a compounding franchise looks like when sentiment gets ahead of it and then snaps back. The bear framing: when a $1.9T stock can lose 12.6% on a guidance figure that still implies 200% growth, the price already embeds perfection, and the reflexivity works in reverse — capex plans are funded by equity valuations, so a sustained AI drawdown becomes self-reinforcing. The honest answer is that both are true, and the deciding variable is whether hyperscaler capital budgets hold through 2027–2028. That is a macro bet, not a Broadcom bet.
