01 Company Overview

Pick up almost anything in a supermarket, a pharmacy or a clothing store and there is a decent chance an Avery Dennison product is stuck to it. The company makes the pressure-sensitive label stock that printers convert into shampoo, wine and shipping labels, plus the tags, tickets and RFID inlays that give individual garments and grocery items a digital identity. FY2025 revenue was $8.86B, up 1.14%, with GAAP EPS of $8.79 and adjusted EPS of $9.53. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $9.25B (+5.84%).

The story of the last five years is a stock that went nowhere while the business kept compounding its dividend. AVY closed September 2021 at $207.21 and the September 21, 2026 session at $169.33. In between it touched a $227.59 month-end high in May 2024, then spent two years sliding as consumer volumes softened, tariffs arrived and label demand swung with customers' ordering patterns. 2026 has been a round trip in miniature: a $196.35 February close, a 12.1% drop in March, a $159.07 May close — the lowest month-end in the five-year window — and a partial recovery after a strong Q2 print on July 30. The central debate: is this a slow-growth label converter priced fairly at ~18.5x trailing earnings, or a company whose "high-value categories" — which the CEO says are "approximately 45% of our total revenue" — are turning it into a data-and-identification business that the multiple has not caught up with?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in label materials Converter relationships & qualification RFID inlay manufacturing Apparel brand embeddedness Productivity playbook 15 years of dividend growth

The moat is specification and scale, not patents. A label material that has been qualified on a customer's packaging line — adhesion, print quality, dispensing speed — is not swapped lightly for a few basis points of price, and a producer with Avery Dennison's footprint can absorb raw-material swings through what management keeps calling its "productivity playbook". The returns show it: ROE of 31.2% and ROIC of 15.1% on TTM figures. The vulnerability is that the core product is still a coated roll of paper or film. Pricing tracks raw materials in both directions, volumes follow customers' inventory cycles, and the high-value categories that are supposed to change that grew no faster than the base business in Q2 2026.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Customer inventory swings. In StockAnalysis's summary of the Q1 2026 call, "inflation and pre-buying are key themes"; by Q2 the company was flagging a stocking benefit of roughly $0.25 per share that it expects to unwind, mainly in Q3. A strong print built partly on customers buying early borrows from the next one.
02
Price follows raw materials. In Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 Materials Group volume gains were offset by "deflation-related price reductions." Pass-through protects margin in both directions but makes the revenue line noisy, and inflation that outruns price increases shows up before the price increases do.
03
Intelligent Labels has not yet re-accelerated. The business that anchors the higher-multiple case was down low single digits in Q1 2026 (Solutions Group) and, per Investing.com's summary, up only low single digits in Q2. The Wiliot investment widens the product set; it does not by itself restore growth.
04
Leverage and tariffs. Debt-to-equity of 1.59 and $3.69B of total debt against $274.7M of cash. The company's own risk language lists tariffs, trade relations and geopolitical conditions among its most significant near-term factors, and the CEO cited "tariff-related impacts and softer consumer volumes" in describing 2025.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported figures. First, restructuring: the company guides FY2026 reported EPS of $9.40–$9.70 and adjusted EPS of $10.00–$10.30, the gap being an estimated $0.60 per share of "other items and restructuring charges"; in the first half it booked about $34M of pre-tax restructuring charges and realised about $34M of savings. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 18.5x trailing and about 16.6x forward earnings for a business with a 31% ROE, a 15% ROIC, $1.06B of trailing free cash flow (~8.3% yield on market cap), 15 straight years of dividend increases and a share count shrinking by about 2 million a year. Q2 2026 showed what happens when volume returns: +9.7% organic in Materials, margin expansion in both segments, adjusted EPS +19.4%. The stock sits 25% below its 2024 high and below every analyst's target, with even the most cautious house (Jefferies, $181) above the price. If the September 23 showcase convinces investors that Intelligent Labels and Wiliot's Bluetooth tags make the high-value 45% a growth business, the multiple has room to move.

Bear Case

The best quarter in years was partly borrowed. Management itself expects a roughly $0.25-per-share stocking benefit to reverse, mostly in Q3, and guides only 3–4% organic growth for the full year after a 7.6% Q2 — arithmetic that implies a weak second half. The high-value categories grew no faster than the base business in Q2; Intelligent Labels fell in Q1 2026 and grew only low single digits in Q2. Debt-to-equity is 1.59 and pricing follows raw materials in both directions. A PEG near 1.9 is not cheap for a company whose FY2025 revenue grew 1.1% — and the ten-month trend is still down.

Top 3 Risks

01
Destocking deeper or longer than guided. Q3 is expected to carry most of the unwind; if it spills into Q4 or customers' end demand is softer than the stocking suggested, the $10.00–$10.30 adjusted EPS guide is at risk. The October 21 print (date estimated) is the test.
02
High-value growth stalls. The re-rating case depends on Intelligent Labels, Vestcom and apparel embellishment outgrowing the base business. In Q2 2026 they did not in Materials, and only matched it in Solutions; Intelligent Labels fell in Q1 and grew low single digits in Q2.
03
Tariffs, inflation and leverage together. The company lists tariffs and trade among its most significant near-term risk factors; inflation that outruns price increases hits margins before pricing catches up; and $3.69B of debt at 2.3x net debt/EBITDA leaves less room to absorb both at once than an unlevered peer would have.
Industry Commentary
The inventory cycle in label materials
ONGOING HEADWIND

Label stock sits a step or two upstream of the consumer: Avery Dennison sells rolls to converters, who print labels for brand owners, who fill packages for retailers. Every link keeps inventory, and every link changes its mind at the same time when prices are expected to move. That is why this business can post a 7.47% revenue decline in one year (FY2023) and a 9.7% organic jump in one segment in one quarter (Q2 2026) without end demand moving anywhere near that much. StockAnalysis's summary of the Q1 2026 call flagged inflation and pre-buying as the key themes; by Q2 the company was quantifying a stocking benefit it expects to give back. For an investor the lesson is to read any single quarter net of stocking — the organic figure is honest about currency and acquisitions, but not about timing.

From labels to item-level identity
EMERGING TAILWIND

The strategic bet is that every item in a supply chain eventually carries a digital identity — an RFID inlay today, perhaps a battery-free Bluetooth sensor tomorrow — and that the company printing the label is best placed to embed it. Avery Dennison's Intelligent Labels business (about $0.9B of 2025 sales, per Investing.com's summary of the Q2 slides) and its April 2026 Wiliot investment are that bet. The bull framing: apparel adoption is established, food, grocery and logistics are the next markets, and each is far larger. The bear framing: Q2 2026 slide commentary summarised by Investing.com had apparel and general retail up about 10% but food and logistics down double digits, and Intelligent Labels fell in Q1 2026 before growing low single digits in Q2. The adoption curve is real; its slope is what the stock is waiting on.
