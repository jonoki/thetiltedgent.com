01 Company Overview

American Express is the only major payments company that is simultaneously a card network, a card issuer, a merchant acquirer and a lender — the so-called closed-loop model. That structure means Amex earns a merchant discount fee on spend, an annual card fee from the cardholder, and net interest income on revolving balances, all from the same transaction. It is deliberately positioned at the premium/affluent end of the market: 155.4 million cards in force at September 30, 2026 produced $2.03 trillion of network volume over the last four quarters. That is a fraction of what Visa or Mastercard route, but because Amex keeps the merchant fee, the card fee and the interest, it collects far more revenue on each dollar spent than a network that only routes payments for banks.

Fiscal 2025 total revenues net of interest expense were $72.2B, with net income of $10.83B and diluted EPS of $15.38. The third quarter of 2026 (reported October 23) delivered revenue of $20.06B, up 9% YoY, and EPS of $5.42, or $4.68 adjusted (up 13%) once a one-off $982M pre-tax gain on the sale of its stake in Global Business Travel Group and a $336M restructuring charge are stripped out. Management raised full-year 2026 guidance to $18.30–$18.60 of GAAP EPS ($17.60–$17.90 adjusted) and kept revenue growth at about 10%. TTM revenue through September 2026 is $77.6B on TTM EPS of $17.75 (GAAP; $17.02 adjusted).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Closed-Loop Network Premium Brand Equity Affluent Customer Base Recurring Card-Fee Annuity Both-Sides Data Advantage Merchant Acceptance Parity Structural Credit Outperformance

The moat is not the network — Visa and Mastercard have far larger networks. The moat is customer selection compounded by vertical integration. Because Amex issues its own cards and signs its own merchants, it sees both sides of every transaction, which produces underwriting and offer-targeting data no issuing bank on a four-party network can replicate. Because it deliberately selects for affluent, high-spend, high-FICO customers, its credit performance is structurally better through the cycle: in Q3 2026 the net write-off rate (loans written off as uncollectable, net of recoveries) was 2.1% including interest and fees, or 1.8% on principal alone for consumer and small-business cards, and 1.2% of consumer and small-business balances were 30+ days past due — well below mass-market card lenders. And because those customers will pay $895 a year for a piece of metal, Amex has converted a cyclical lending business into something closer to a subscription business: net card fees, up 16% in Q3, are contractual revenue that does not care what happens to unemployment next quarter. That fee stream is now large — $11.2B over the last four quarters, equal to more than half of the $19.8B spent on card member rewards over the same period. The final layer is a loop in the brand: the benefits are expensive because the customers are valuable, and the customers are valuable because the benefits are expensive. That loop is very hard for a bank operating on someone else's rails to copy at the same margin.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. The premium arms race is inflating the cost of the moat. JPMorgan repriced the Chase Sapphire Reserve to a $795 annual fee in mid-2025 with a rebuilt benefit stack explicitly aimed at Amex Platinum holders; Citi launched Strata Elite; Capital One has pushed Venture X hard with a lower fee. Amex's $895 Platinum answer works only if benefit value scales faster than the fee. Card member services expense up 36% YoY in Q3 2026, against 16% growth in net card fees, is the visible cost of defending the position, and each round of escalation raises the breakeven spend per customer.

2. Interchange and merchant-fee regulation. The Credit Card Competition Act has been reintroduced repeatedly and, while Amex's three-party model has historically been carved out of Durbin-style routing rules, any broad compression of US interchange would reset the economics of the whole rewards ecosystem and reduce what competitors can spend — a double-edged outcome. EU/UK interchange caps already constrain Amex's international premium proposition, and merchant-side litigation over swipe fees remains a permanent background risk.

3. Concentration in the US affluent consumer. U.S. Consumer Services is about 48% of segment revenue over the last four quarters and grew 11% in Q3, the fastest of the four segments that quarter. That is a single, correlated exposure to one cohort in one country. It has been the right cohort — affluent spend has been the resilient half of a K-shaped economy — but a genuine wealth-effect shock (a severe equity drawdown, a housing correction, a broad white-collar employment event) would hit spend, card fees and credit simultaneously rather than sequentially.

4. Disintermediation of the card rail itself. Account-to-account payments, pay-by-bank, stablecoin settlement and merchant-led wallets all aim at the same target: removing 2%+ of transaction value that currently accrues to card networks. Adoption in the US has been slow because rewards make cards irrationally attractive to consumers, but the technology and regulatory direction of travel both point toward cheaper rails. Amex, earning the highest take-rate in the industry, has the most to lose per transaction if that pool compresses.

Notable One-Off Items

Q3 2026 carried three one-offs. The sale of Amex's stake in Global Business Travel Group (GBTG) produced a $982M pre-tax gain ($751M after tax, $1.11 a share). A $336M restructuring charge ($253M after tax, $0.37 a share) covers the job cuts management announced. And the effective tax rate fell to 18.1% from 24.1% a year earlier on discrete benefits from settling prior-period tax items, which the company does not adjust out. Removing the gain and the charge turns GAAP EPS of $5.42 into $4.68 adjusted. The company also booked higher legal reserves for previously disclosed matters inside operating expenses.

Credit reserves moved the other way. Provisions for credit losses fell 23% to $985M because Amex released reserves in the quarter, where a year earlier it had built them; card loss reserves ended at $5.63B, 2.5% of card balances, down from 2.9%. Under CECL accounting a release lifts earnings, and it will not repeat every quarter. Platinum refresh spending is the longer-running distortion: card member services expense was up 36% YoY in Q3 as refreshed benefits went live, ahead of the fee revenue that arrives on renewal. That gap should narrow as the repriced base annualises, but how fast is still the open question.

The five-year trajectory. AXP closed October 2021 at $173.78 and October 26, 2026 at $315.05 — a price return of +81.3%, or +92.8% with dividends reinvested ($13.84 a share was paid over the period; Yahoo adjusted closes). Over the same window SPY, the S&P 500 index fund, returned +66.7% on price and +78.2% with dividends reinvested, so Amex beat the index by about 15 percentage points on both measures. Against the two big networks it did not come out ahead this time: Visa rose 90.8% and Mastercard 82.9% on price over the same dates (October 29, 2021 month-end close to the October 26, 2026 close, Yahoo). At the December 2025 month-end peak AXP had been up 113% on the window; the 2026 sell-off gave back much of that.

The path was anything but smooth. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Amex has quietly converted a cyclical lending business into a subscription business. Net card fees, up 16% in Q3 2026 to $11.2B over the last four quarters, are contractual revenue that does not care about the unemployment rate, and Platinum spending retention held at 99% a year after the $895 reprice, per the CFO. Layer that on a 38% ROE (first nine months of 2026, annualised), a share count down 2.6% in a year, write-offs of 2.1%, a raised 2026 guide and a low-single-digit share of a large international market, and double-digit EPS growth looks defensible at 15.6x next year's consensus earnings — with half the analysts still at Hold, there is room for sentiment to turn.

Bear Case

The premium arms race is a prisoner's dilemma and Amex is the incumbent with the most to lose. Chase at $795, Citi Strata Elite and Venture X are all buying the same affluent wallet, and Amex's defence costs — card member services expense up 36% YoY in Q3 — are growing faster than the fees that fund them (16%). The Q3 beat leaned on a lower tax rate (18.1% against 24.1%) and a reserve release, while billed business growth held at 8% rather than accelerating; the market sold the stock on the day. US Consumer is 48% of segment revenue in a split economy that could turn, and interchange regulation plus A2A rails aim directly at the industry's highest take-rate.

Top 3 Risks
01
Premium-competition margin compression. If Chase, Citi and Capital One keep escalating benefits, Amex must match or lose share of the only customer cohort it serves. Each round is funded from the same P&L, and the fee increases that offset it have a ceiling set by what customers will actually pay. Watch card member services and rewards expense growth relative to net card fee growth — if the former stays above the latter for several quarters, the model is leaking.
02
A genuine affluent-consumer shock. The entire thesis rests on the premium customer being structurally different. A severe equity or housing drawdown would attack spend volume, card-fee retention and credit quality simultaneously — the three legs of the model are correlated, not diversifying. With US Consumer Services at 48% of segment revenue, there is no offsetting exposure to hide in.
03
Valuation still above the lenders. At 17.7x trailing earnings and a 1.21% dividend yield, AXP still trades well above consumer-finance peers (~12x in the Industry Avg column). The consensus target of $368.92 implies 17.1% upside, but half the covering analysts (15 of 30) sit at Hold. A quarter in which net card fee growth slows sharply would likely push the multiple toward the consumer-finance average faster than earnings could offset it.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters for Amex Right Now
The K-shaped consumer
EMERGING TAILWIND
Higher-income households have kept spending while lower-income households have pulled back — the "K-shaped" pattern — and their wealth has been lifted by a stock market that has risen about two-thirds in five years (SPY, +66.7% on price over this report's window). Amex sells mostly to the upper arm of the K. This is why Amex has posted steady billed-business growth (8% in Q3 2026) with write-offs at 2.1% and delinquencies at 1.2%. The risk in the tailwind is that it is a wealth-effect phenomenon, not an income phenomenon — it is levered to asset prices, and asset prices mean-revert.
Credit normalisation, not deterioration
ONGOING HEADWIND
Card losses across the industry have normalised from the extraordinary 2021 lows back toward — and in the mass market beyond — pre-2020 levels. Amex's Q3 2026 net write-off rate of 2.1% (including interest and fees) and 30+ day delinquency rate of 1.2% are low, and its write-off rate has eased from 2.3% in Q4 2025 and Q1 2026, allowing a reserve release in Q3. Its monthly filings show the U.S. Consumer write-off rate at 1.7% in July and August, against a June figure of 1.4% that a sale of previously written-off balances lowered by about 0.3 points. Provision expense also grows with a $221B card-balance book under CECL. The headwind is less about realised losses than about perception: every uptick in industry card delinquency data weighs on the whole group's valuation, and Amex's premium multiple has further to fall than peers trading near 12x.
The premium-card fee spiral — bull vs bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Since mid-2025 the industry has repriced the top of the market twice: Chase Sapphire Reserve to $795 in June 2025, Amex Platinum to $895 in September 2025, with Citi and Capital One responding. The bull reading: annual fees are proving to be one of the few genuinely inelastic prices in consumer finance, the premium pool is expanding, and Amex — with the closed loop, the brand and 155M cards in force — captures the most economics per repriced customer; net card fees up 16% with 99% Platinum spending retention is the evidence. The bear reading: this is competitive escalation dressed as pricing power. In Q3 2026 card member services expense grew 36% and rewards 10%; if benefit inflation outruns fee inflation, Amex has raised prices and lowered margins, and the terminal state is a lower-margin business with the same customers. The next four quarters of the net-card-fee versus card-member-services growth spread will settle which reading is right — that single spread is the most informative number in the whole model.
