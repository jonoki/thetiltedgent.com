01 Company Overview

American Express is the only major payments company that is simultaneously a card network, a card issuer, a merchant acquirer and a lender — the so-called closed-loop model. That structure means Amex earns a merchant discount fee on spend, an annual card fee from the cardholder, and net interest income on revolving balances, all from the same transaction. It is deliberately positioned at the premium/affluent end of the market: roughly 152 million cards-in-force generate about $1.94 trillion of annual network volume, versus Visa's ~$16T and Mastercard's ~$10T — Amex has roughly a tenth of Visa's volume but earns more revenue per dollar of spend than any network on earth.

Fiscal 2025 total revenues net of interest expense were approximately $72.2B, up ~9.5% YoY from $65.9B, with net income of $10.83B and EPS of $15.38. Momentum has continued: Q2 2026 (reported July 24, 2026) delivered revenue of roughly $19.6B (+~10% YoY) and EPS of $4.53 against a $4.41 consensus, and management guided full-year 2026 to ~$79.5B of revenue (+~10%) and $17.30–$17.90 of EPS. TTM revenue through June 2026 is approximately $75.9B on TTM EPS of $16.47. Amex is roughly the #76 most valuable listed company globally and among the ten largest US financials by market value.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Closed-Loop Network Premium Brand Equity Affluent Customer Base Recurring Card-Fee Annuity Both-Sides Data Advantage Merchant Acceptance Parity Structural Credit Outperformance

The moat is not the network — Visa and Mastercard have far larger networks. The moat is customer selection compounded by vertical integration. Because Amex issues its own cards and signs its own merchants, it sees both sides of every transaction, which produces underwriting and offer-targeting data no issuing bank on a four-party network can replicate. Because it deliberately selects for affluent, high-spend, high-FICO customers, its credit performance is structurally better through the cycle: net write-off rates around ~2.2% and 30+ day delinquencies around ~1.4% compare with mid-single-digit write-offs at mass-market card lenders — Capital One's card business has run roughly 5–6%. And because those customers will pay $895 a year for a piece of metal, Amex has converted a cyclical lending business into something closer to a subscription business: net card fees compounding around +20% YoY are contractual revenue that does not care what happens to unemployment next quarter. That fee annuity is now large enough — roughly $10B a year — to cover a meaningful share of the rewards cost base on its own. The final layer is the reflexive quality of the brand: the benefits are expensive because the customers are valuable, and the customers are valuable because the benefits are expensive. That loop is very hard for a bank operating on someone else's rails to copy at the same margin.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. The premium arms race is inflating the cost of the moat. JPMorgan repriced the Chase Sapphire Reserve to a $795 annual fee in mid-2025 with a rebuilt benefit stack explicitly aimed at Amex Platinum holders; Citi launched Strata Elite; Capital One has pushed Venture X hard with a lower fee. Amex's $895 Platinum answer works only if benefit value scales faster than the fee. Card member services expense growing ~36% YoY is the visible cost of defending the position, and each round of escalation raises the breakeven spend per customer.

2. Interchange and merchant-fee regulation. The Credit Card Competition Act has been reintroduced repeatedly and, while Amex's three-party model has historically been carved out of Durbin-style routing rules, any broad compression of US interchange would reset the economics of the whole rewards ecosystem and reduce what competitors can spend — a double-edged outcome. EU/UK interchange caps already constrain Amex's international premium proposition, and merchant-side litigation over swipe fees remains a permanent background risk.

3. Concentration in the US affluent consumer. U.S. Consumer Services is roughly 46% of revenue and the fastest-growing part of the model. That is a single, correlated exposure to one cohort in one country. It has been the right cohort — affluent spend has been the resilient half of a K-shaped economy — but a genuine wealth-effect shock (a severe equity drawdown, a housing correction, a broad white-collar employment event) would hit spend, card fees and credit simultaneously rather than sequentially.

4. Disintermediation of the card rail itself. Account-to-account payments, pay-by-bank, stablecoin settlement and merchant-led wallets all aim at the same target: removing 2%+ of transaction value that currently accrues to card networks. Adoption in the US has been slow because rewards make cards irrationally attractive to consumers, but the technology and regulatory direction of travel both point toward cheaper rails. Amex, earning the highest take-rate in the industry, has the most to lose per transaction if that pool compresses.

Notable One-Off Items

Platinum refresh investment spend (2H 2025 through 2026) is the largest distortion in the current numbers. The September 2025 product relaunch front-loaded marketing and benefit costs ahead of the fee revenue that arrives on renewal, which is why Q4 2025 earnings ($3.53 EPS, a rare small miss against a $3.54 consensus) looked weak against a revenue line that grew 10%. Card member services expense running roughly +36% YoY is the visible expression of this; it should decelerate as the repriced base annualises through 2027, mechanically widening margins even if nothing else changes. Investors reading FY2025 and 1H 2026 margins as the steady state are probably reading a trough.

Reserve builds have been a recurring modest drag rather than an event: provisions have risen roughly in line with a growing ~$145B loan book plus a small cushion for macro uncertainty, not because realised losses deteriorated — write-offs have stayed near ~2.2%. This is the standard CECL mechanic where growth itself creates provision expense. The FY2024 comparison base is inflated by the roughly $0.5B pre-tax gain on the sale of Accertify in Q1 2024, which flatters 2024 margins and understates 2025's underlying growth by roughly a point. On the legal and regulatory side, Amex resolved federal inquiries into historical small-business card and wire-product sales practices during 2025 at a scale that was not financially material relative to a $10B+ annual earnings base; no litigation charge of consequence sits in the current run-rate. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Amex has quietly converted a cyclical lending business into a subscription business. Net card fees compounding around +20% YoY toward a ~$10B annual run-rate are contractual revenue that does not care about the unemployment rate, and the $895 Platinum reprice has barely begun to flow through the existing base — most of the renewal cohort reprices through 2026–27. Layer that on a 34% ROE, a ~2.9% annual share-count reduction, best-in-class credit at ~2.2% write-offs, and a low-single-digit share of a $10T international market, and mid-teens EPS growth looks defensible for several years at a forward multiple 20% below the index.

Bear Case

The premium arms race is a prisoner's dilemma and Amex is the incumbent with the most to lose. Chase at $795, Citi Strata Elite and Venture X are all buying the same affluent wallet, and Amex's defence costs — card member services expense up ~36% YoY — are growing faster than the fees that fund them. Net margin at ~15% is below the consumer-finance average precisely because of this. Meanwhile the stock at 20.9x trailing has already re-rated from 12x, US Consumer is 46% of revenue in a K-shaped economy that could invert, and interchange regulation plus A2A rails aim directly at the industry's highest take-rate.

Top 3 Risks
01
Premium-competition margin compression. If Chase, Citi and Capital One keep escalating benefits, Amex must match or lose share of the only customer cohort it serves. Each round is funded from the same P&L, and the fee increases that offset it have a ceiling set by what customers will actually pay. Watch card member services and rewards expense growth relative to net card fee growth — if the former stays above the latter for several quarters, the model is leaking.
02
A genuine affluent-consumer shock. The entire thesis rests on the premium customer being structurally different. A severe equity or housing drawdown would attack spend volume, card-fee retention and credit quality simultaneously — the three legs of the model are correlated, not diversifying. With US Consumer Services at 46% of revenue, there is no offsetting exposure to hide in.
03
Valuation with no margin of safety. At 20.9x trailing earnings and a 1.10% dividend yield, roughly nine years of guided mid-teens EPS growth is already embedded. The consensus target of $375.86 implies only 9.2% upside and nearly half the covering analysts sit at Hold. A single quarter in which net card fee growth decelerates below the mid-teens would likely compress the multiple back toward the consumer-finance average faster than earnings could offset it.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters for Amex Right Now
The K-shaped consumer
EMERGING TAILWIND
The US consumer has bifurcated more sharply than at any point since the 1990s. The top income quintile now accounts for roughly half of all consumer spending, and that cohort's balance sheet has been inflated by an equity market that has roughly doubled in five years. Amex sells almost exclusively to that half. This is why Amex has posted high-single-digit billed-business growth while mass-market lenders report soft volumes and rising delinquencies, and why write-offs at ~2.2% sit less than half the industry average. The risk in the tailwind is that it is a wealth-effect phenomenon, not an income phenomenon — it is levered to asset prices, and asset prices mean-revert.
Credit normalisation, not deterioration
ONGOING HEADWIND
Card losses across the industry have normalised from the extraordinary 2021 lows back toward — and in the mass market beyond — pre-2020 levels. Amex's ~2.2% net write-off rate and ~1.4% 30+ day delinquency rate remain the best in the peer set, against roughly 5–6% at Capital One's card book, but the direction of travel has been mildly upward and provision expense grows mechanically with a ~$145B loan book under CECL. The headwind is less about realised losses than about narrative: every uptick in card delinquency data compresses the whole subsector's multiple, and Amex trades at a premium that is more sensitive to that narrative than peers trading at 12x.
The premium-card fee spiral — bull vs bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Since mid-2025 the industry has repriced the top of the market twice: Chase Sapphire Reserve to $795 in June 2025, Amex Platinum to $895 in September 2025, with Citi and Capital One responding. The bull reading: annual fees are proving to be one of the few genuinely inelastic prices in consumer finance, the premium pool is expanding, and Amex — with the closed loop, the brand and 152M cards in force — captures the most economics per repriced customer; net card fees compounding ~20% while the fee base is only partly repriced is the evidence. The bear reading: this is competitive escalation dressed as pricing power. Card member services expense is growing ~36% and rewards ~10%; if benefit inflation outruns fee inflation, Amex has raised prices and lowered margins, and the terminal state is a lower-margin business with the same customers. The next four quarters of the net-card-fee versus card-member-services growth spread will settle which reading is right — that single spread is the most informative number in the whole model.
