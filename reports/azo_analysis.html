01 Company Overview

AutoZone sells automotive replacement parts, maintenance items and accessories through 7,856 stores across the United States, Mexico and Brazil, and is the largest specialty parts retailer in the American aftermarket by store count. Fiscal 2025 (ended August 30, 2025) revenue was $18.94B, up 2.4% on FY2024's $18.49B; the trailing-twelve-month figure through the third quarter of fiscal 2026 has since accelerated to $19.99B, +5.7% YoY. The company ranks roughly #540 globally by market capitalization at $47.0B.

The central debate is not whether the business works — it plainly does. It is what the business is worth after two years of flat earnings. Diluted EPS peaked at $149.55 in FY2024, fell to $144.87 in FY2025, and sits at $145.61 on a TTM basis through May 9, 2026. Over that same stretch the stock ran to $4,388 and has since given back a third. The bull says a 19.8× multiple on a share count that shrinks every quarter is the cheapest AutoZone has looked in years. The bear says the earnings engine has stalled precisely when the multiple no longer flatters it, and the balance sheet — shareholders' equity of negative $2.78B — leaves no cushion if it keeps stalling.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

The moat is inventory placement, not brand. A failed alternator is a non-discretionary purchase with near-zero price elasticity and near-total urgency — the customer will pay whatever is asked by whoever has the part today. AutoZone's advantage is that it has the part: 6,766 domestic stores backed by 156 mega-hubs carrying the slow-moving, low-turn SKUs that no single store can justify stocking. That network cost decades and roughly $1.6 billion a year of current capital expenditure to build, and it produces a 26.65% ROIC and a 51.75% gross margin — retail economics that look more like a specialty distributor than a big-box chain. The supporting financial trick is the payables float: net inventory per store was negative $107 thousand in Q3 FY2026, meaning vendors finance the shelf. That is how a company with negative book equity still throws off $3.07B of operating cash flow.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Negative equity removes the margin for error. Shareholders' equity is −$2.78 billion against $13.09 billion of total debt and $270.5 million of cash. This is a deliberate outcome of decades of buybacks, not distress — but it means the company depends on uninterrupted access to the debt markets and on investment-grade ratings that are themselves tied to a leverage target. A sustained earnings decline would force the buyback to stop before the balance sheet repairs itself, and the buyback is a meaningful share of EPS growth.

2. Earnings have gone sideways for two years. Diluted EPS: $149.55 (FY2024) → $144.87 (FY2025) → $145.61 (TTM). Net income has actually fallen, from $2.662B in FY2024 to $2.478B on a TTM basis. Revenue growth of 5.7% has not translated into profit growth, because gross margin is compressing (LIFO, commercial mix) and operating profit is flat at roughly $3.6 billion. Buying back stock only manufactures EPS growth while net income holds.

3. O'Reilly is growing faster on both lines. ORLY's TTM revenue is up 8.5%, net income up 9.4% and EPS up 12.9% — against AutoZone's 5.7% revenue and declining EPS. The market has noticed: ORLY trades at 27.1× trailing earnings versus AZO's 19.8×. Persistent relative underperformance in the Commercial channel would validate that spread rather than close it.

4. Currency and international execution. A $74 million FX tailwind turned a +1.6% constant-currency international comp into a reported +16.6%. The same mechanism runs in reverse. With 1,090 stores in Mexico and Brazil and international the fastest-growing slice of the store base, peso and real volatility is now a material driver of reported results rather than a rounding item.

Notable one-off items. Three distortions matter when reading the TTM figures. First, LIFO accounting cost 77 basis points of gross margin in Q3 FY2026 — larger than the 57 basis point total decline, meaning underlying merchandise margin actually improved. Third, fiscal calendars: FY2025 contained a 53rd week in Q4 (16-week quarter ending August 30, 2025, revenue $6.24B), which is why the FY2025-to-FY2026 quarterly comparisons are uneven. Critically, the FY2026 full-year print has not landed — the fiscal year ended Saturday, August 29, 2026, and results are due pre-market on September 22, 2026. RSI compares the size of recent monthly gains to recent monthly losses on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 conventionally mean the stock has risen far and fast enough to be called "overbought"; below 30 means the opposite. It measures momentum, not value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

A first thing to say about this chart: the y-axis is in thousands of dollars. AutoZone has never split its stock, so a single share costs $2,881.47 — more than a share of almost anything else on the NYSE. The consequence is that percentage moves that would look ordinary on a $50 stock show up here as three-figure dollar swings. The $29.50 decline on September 10 is a 1.01% day. The $586.3 million spent on buybacks in Q3 bought just 164,000 shares. And the entire company — 16.33 million shares — is worth $47.0 billion.

Over the five years from the October 2021 close of $1,784.84 to September 10, 2026 at $2,881.47, AZO returned +61.4% with no dividends paid — all of it price, and much of it manufactured by a share count that fell every quarter. But the shape matters more than the total. Four of those five years were a near-uninterrupted grind higher, from $1,785 to an all-time monthly close of $4,290.24 in September 2025: +140% in four years with a beta of 0.34, exactly the low-volatility compounder the market wanted. The last twelve months undid a third of it. From that September 2025 peak the stock is down 32.8%, and the 52-week change of −32.23% is barely distinguishable from the peak-to-trough decline because the peak was twelve months ago.

The de-rating is almost entirely multiple, not earnings. TTM EPS at the September 2025 peak was in the same $145–150 band it occupies now; the stock simply went from roughly 29× to 19.8×. Two step-downs did most of the damage: a 23% drop across October–December 2025, and a −20.8% month in May 2026 that took the stock from $3,704 to $2,935 around the Q3 report — a quarter that grew sales 8.4% and still triggered six target cuts.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A 26.65% ROIC business with 52% gross margins, non-discretionary demand and a 7,856-store moat now trades at 19.8× trailing earnings — a seven-turn discount to O'Reilly and the cheapest it has been in years — while Commercial grows 10.4% and DIY comps accelerate. The board just added $1.5 billion of buyback capacity into the decline, and with 16.33 million shares outstanding every dollar repurchased moves EPS hard. If the September 22 FY2026 print shows the margin pressure was LIFO accounting rather than competitive erosion, the entire 33% de-rating was a multiple story with nothing broken underneath it.

Bear Case

Earnings have not grown in two years: EPS $149.55 → $144.87 → $145.61 TTM, with net income actually down from $2.66B to $2.48B. Revenue growth of 5.7% trails O'Reilly's 8.5%, gross margin is compressing, and the buyback that props up EPS runs on a balance sheet with negative $2.78 billion of equity and $13.09 billion of debt. A 19.8× multiple on flat earnings is not obviously cheap — and six target cuts since May, all clustering near $3,500, suggest the sell side is still walking down rather than defending a floor.

Top 3 Risks

01
The September 22 print confirms the earnings stall. FY2026 results land in eleven days into a stock already at its 52-week low. If full-year EPS again fails to clear FY2024's $149.55, the market will have three consecutive years of no earnings growth to price, and the current 19.8× becomes the starting point rather than the floor.
02
Negative equity constrains the response to weakness. With −$2.78B of shareholders' equity, $13.09B of debt, $270.5M of cash and a current ratio of 0.89, AutoZone has no book cushion. Debt is capped by leverage targets tied to its credit ratings, so a sustained profit decline forces the buyback down before the balance sheet can repair — removing the main support under EPS at exactly the wrong moment.
03
Share loss to O'Reilly in the Commercial channel. DIFM is where the growth is (+10.4%, 29% of revenue) and where ORLY competes hardest. ORLY is compounding revenue 8.5% and EPS 12.9% versus AZO's 5.7% and flat — if that gap persists, the 7-turn valuation discount is the market pricing a real competitive outcome, not an opportunity.

Industry Commentary

CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Sector-wide multiple compression
Auto-parts retail has de-rated as a group, not as individual stories. Trailing twelve months: AZO −32.2%, AAP −32.2%, ORLY −23.8%. ORLY at $85.47 sits near its own 52-week low of $82.59; AAP's market cap is down 25.3% on revenue that has turned negative (−1.3%). When three companies with very different growth rates and balance sheets fall together, the market is repricing the category's terminal growth assumption rather than reacting to any one operator. For AZO specifically this means the discount to ORLY can persist even if AutoZone executes — the whole shelf has been marked down.
ONGOING HEADWIND
Input inflation and the LIFO drag
Steel tariffs, in management's words, "have been in place for quite some time," and the accounting consequence is visible: LIFO cost 77 basis points of gross margin in Q3 FY2026 against a total decline of 57 basis points. The cash economics are unchanged — LIFO is a reporting effect — but it has masked underlying merchandise-margin improvement for several quarters and made the P&L read worse than the business. Management expects inflation to be "slightly muted" going forward as they lap higher prior-year rates, which would let the reported margin line catch up to reality. That reversal, if it shows up on September 22, is the most underappreciated near-term swing factor in the numbers.
EMERGING TAILWIND
Bull vs bear: what the print decides
The bull framing: an aging vehicle fleet and expensive new cars push repair volume into the aftermarket, mega-hub density converts that volume into commercial share (+10.4%, $18.5k weekly per program, +4.5%), international adds 25 stores a quarter, and a $1.5B refreshed buyback compounds it across only 16.33 million shares. The bear framing: two years of flat EPS, a 3-point revenue growth deficit to ORLY, negative book equity, and a sell side that has cut targets six times since May without cutting a single rating. Both cases are fully consistent with the same set of facts — which is why a stock sitting $5.22 above its 52-week low, eleven days before its fiscal-year report, is a genuinely two-sided proposition rather than an obvious one.
