01 Company Overview

Boeing designs, builds and supports commercial jetliners, military aircraft, satellites, launch systems and defense electronics, and is one half of the global large-commercial-aircraft duopoly alongside Airbus. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $89.5 billion, up 34.5% YoY from $66.5 billion in 2024, as 600 commercial deliveries marked the company's highest annual total since 2018 and the December 2025 close of the Spirit AeroSystems reacquisition folded a major structures supplier back in-house. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 stands at $94.0 billion (+24.8%), and Boeing ranks roughly #121 among the world's most valuable public companies by market capitalization.

The investment case is not a growth story — it is a balance-sheet and execution repair story. Boeing has posted GAAP operating losses in five of the last six fiscal years, carries $48.4 billion of debt against $7.7 billion of book equity per share basis, and only turned quarterly FCF positive again in Q2 2026 (+$631 million). Everything below should be read against that frame: the operating results are still poor, and the equity is priced on a recovery that is underway but incomplete.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Vertical Integration (Spirit)

Boeing's moat is structural rather than operational, which is precisely why the stock has survived a decade that would have destroyed most industrial franchises. Building a certified large commercial jet requires a multi-decade regulatory relationship, tens of billions in sunk development, a global supplier network, and worldwide spares and training infrastructure — the reason the field has consisted of two credible players for thirty years. Airlines compound that with fleet commonality: a carrier with 200 737s faces enormous switching costs in pilot training, maintenance tooling, and spares to move to the A320. The $715.3 billion total backlog — $596.7B commercial (6,200+ jets), $85B defense, $33B services — represents roughly a decade of committed demand, and Airbus is sold out on narrowbodies well into the 2030s, meaning a frustrated Boeing customer often has nowhere to go. On the defense side, prime-contractor status on the F-47, F-15EX, KC-46A and P-8, plus the classified franchise, is effectively unwinnable by a new entrant. The December 8, 2025 reacquisition of Spirit AeroSystems pulled 737 and 787 fuselage production back in-house, restoring control over the quality chain that produced the door-plug failure. The critical caveat: a moat protects revenue, not margins. Boeing has demonstrated for six years that it can hold near-half of a duopoly and still lose money.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Regulatory dependency on production rate. Boeing's earnings power is gated by the FAA, not by demand. The 38/month cap imposed after January 2024 cost the company years of cash generation. The cap is gone and rates have moved 38 → 42 (Oct 2025) → 47 (May 2026), but a single significant quality escape could reinstate it. No other large-cap industrial has its revenue ceiling set by a regulator on a rolling basis.

2. Balance sheet fragility. $48.4B of total debt against $19.3B of cash, a Debt-to-Equity ratio of 7.91×, and an Altman Z-Score of 1.35 (the distress zone). Book value per share is just $7.72 against a $223 stock. Boeing has almost no margin for a further operational setback without returning to the capital markets — and it has already diluted shareholders heavily.

3. Fixed-price development contracts. VC-25B, KC-46A, T-7A, MQ-25 and Starliner have generated recurring reach-forward losses with no clear terminal date — $600M on KC-46A in Q4 2025 and $280M on VC-25B in Q2 2026 are simply the two most recent. BDS ran a −0.5% operating margin for all of FY2025 and −0.2% in Q2 2026 despite 13% revenue growth. Until these programs are delivered, the defense segment is a cash sink attached to a growing revenue line.

4. Backlog pricing and customer concentration. A large share of the commercial backlog was priced under pre-inflation, pre-strike cost assumptions, while the 2024 IAM contract added a 38% wage increase over four years. Escalation clauses only partially offset this. Separately, order concentration among a handful of mega-carriers and lessors, plus tariff exposure on a product that is ~70%+ export-destined, makes revenue sensitive to trade policy and to any single airline's capex retrenchment.

Notable One-Off Items

Boeing's reported results are unusually distorted, and taking any headline number at face value will mislead you.

Digital Aviation Solutions gain (+$9.6B, FY2025). Boeing sold its DAS software business (Jeppesen, ForeFlight, and related assets) for ~$10.6B in proceeds, booking a $9.6 billion pre-tax gain. This single item is why FY2025 GAAP EPS was +$2.48 while operating income was −$5.19B and core EPS was $1.19. It also explains the absurd 64.4% "operating margin" reported for Global Services in FY2025. Every trailing profitability metric on this page inherits that distortion.

777X and 767 reach-forward losses (−$5.3B, FY2025). Booked as non-cash charges reflecting cumulative expected program losses; 777X first delivery has slipped repeatedly and is now targeted for 2027. Further slippage triggers further charges.

Fixed-price defense charges. A $600M KC-46A charge in Q4 2025 and a $280M VC-25B (Air Force One) loss in Q2 2026, the latter for additional production and certification resources with first delivery pushed to 2028. These recur.

Spirit AeroSystems acquisition accounting (closed Dec 8, 2025). Spirit results consolidate into segment reporting from Q4 2025, which inflates BCA revenue and the cost base and makes YoY comparisons non-organic. Belfast operations were retained and rebranded Short Brothers; a separate Spirit Defense subsidiary was established. Purchase accounting will continue to affect comparability through 2026.

2024 legacy: strike and dilution. The 53-day IAM District 751 strike (mid-September to November 4, 2024, 26,000+ workers, resolved with a 38% wage increase and $12,000 ratification bonus) cost Boeing and its suppliers an estimated $9.7 billion, at roughly $1 billion/month to Boeing. To defend its investment-grade rating Boeing raised $24.3 billion in October 2024 — 112.5 million common shares at $143 (~$16.1B) plus $5B of mandatory convertible preferred. Shares outstanding are now 790.4 million, up 8.7% YoY as the converts season through. Shareholders were not wiped out, but per-share recovery math is permanently worse.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The regulatory ceiling is gone. 737 output has moved 38 → 42 → 47/month with the FAA publicly comfortable going higher, the 787 is heading to 10/month, and Spirit AeroSystems is back in-house closing the quality loop that caused the crisis. Q2 2026 delivered +$631M free cash flow against a record $715.3B backlog — roughly a decade of committed demand in a duopoly where the alternative is sold out. Incremental deliveries drop through a largely fixed cost base, so the operating leverage from here is extreme: consensus sees FY2026 FCF of ~$2.34B on the way to multiples of that. Add the F-47 anchoring defense into the 2040s and a Global Services business earning ~19% margins, and a normalized Boeing generating $8–10B of FCF supports a materially higher equity value than $176B.

Bear Case

You are paying 2.20× EV/Sales and 84× trailing earnings for a company with −12.7% ROIC, −5.4% operating margins, 7.91× Debt-to-Equity, an Altman Z-Score of 1.35, and a consensus FY2026 EPS of −$1.08. Trailing profitability exists only because of a one-time $9.6B divestiture gain. Boeing has promised a cash-flow inflection in five of the last six years and delivered charges instead — $5.3B on 777X/767 in FY2025, $600M on KC-46A, $280M on VC-25B in Q2 2026, with VC-25B first delivery now 2028. Shareholders absorbed $24.3B of dilution at the bottom, and 23 of 28 analysts are already bullish, so positioning offers no cushion. One quality escape reinstates the cap and the thesis resets to zero.

Top 3 Risks
01 Production ramp failure or a new quality escape. The entire equity story is the delivery ramp. A significant safety or quality incident would reinstate FAA rate restrictions, and Boeing has neither the balance sheet nor the shareholder goodwill to absorb another 21-month cap. This is a binary, non-diversifiable risk sitting on top of a 7.91× leveraged capital structure.
02 Recurring fixed-price development charges. VC-25B, KC-46A, T-7A, MQ-25 and Starliner have each re-charged multiple times, holding BDS at a −0.5% FY2025 and −0.2% Q2 2026 operating margin despite 13% revenue growth. If the F-47 follows the same pattern, Boeing's best defense win in decades becomes another multi-year cash drain.
03 Balance sheet with no slack. $48.4B debt, $29.1B net debt, $7.72 book value per share and an Altman Z-Score of 1.35 leave essentially no capacity for a downturn in airline capex, a tariff shock on an export-weighted product, or another operational surprise. Any of these forces a return to capital markets — and the last raise diluted holders by more than 8%.
  Industry Commentary
Production Ramp Execution Risk Is the Whole Industry Bottleneck
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Commercial aerospace in 2026 is supply-constrained, not demand-constrained. Boeing's $596.7B commercial backlog covers 6,200+ aircraft — roughly a decade at 2025's 600-delivery pace — and Airbus, which delivered 793 aircraft in 2025, is similarly sold out on narrowbodies into the next decade. Neither OEM can convert that book faster than its supply chain and its regulator allow. Boeing's 787 rate was held at 8/month by a GE engine supply disruption before recovering; castings, forgings, structures and skilled labor remain tight industry-wide; and the 38/month FAA cap that governed 737 output from January 2024 to October 2025 demonstrated that a regulator can unilaterally cap a $180B company's revenue. Every incremental aircraft per month is worth several hundred million dollars of annualized contribution margin against a fixed cost base — which is why the 38 → 42 → 47 sequence, and the 52/month target for early 2027, matters more to the equity than any demand datapoint.
Airline Capex and Fleet Replacement Demand Remain Robust
EMERGING TAILWIND
Order intake has not been the problem. Boeing booked 1,173 net commercial orders in 2025 and outsold Airbus for the first time since 2018, pushing total backlog to a record $715.3 billion by Q2 2026 — $596.7B commercial, $85B defense, $33B services. The demand drivers are structural rather than cyclical: aging narrowbody fleets, fuel-efficiency economics that make replacement NPV-positive at almost any oil price, and Middle East and Asian carriers scaling long-haul networks. Meanwhile defense budgets across NATO and the Indo-Pacific are elevated, and 26% of Boeing's FY2025 defense orders were international. The practical implication is that Boeing's revenue trajectory over the next five years is close to underwritten; the open question is entirely whether it converts that revenue to profit.
Duopoly Pricing Power — Real, But Boeing Hasn't Been Able to Use It
ONGOING HEADWIND
In theory a two-player market with a decade-long joint backlog is the ideal pricing environment. In practice Boeing has spent six years negotiating from weakness: it took roughly 43% of 2025's duopoly deliveries (600 vs. Airbus's 793) while running a −17.1% Commercial Airplanes operating margin. A large share of the backlog was priced before the inflation of 2021–2024 and before the 2024 IAM contract added a 38% wage increase over four years; escalation clauses only partially cover the gap, and customers whose deliveries slipped extracted concessions. Bull framing: as delivery reliability is restored, new orders get priced at current economics and Boeing recaptures the pricing power the duopoly structure should confer — this is the single largest source of margin upside and is not in trailing numbers. Bear framing: the low-priced backlog takes years to work through, Airbus is compounding scale and share advantage while Boeing repairs itself, and a company that cannot earn a positive margin at 43% share in a duopoly may have a cost problem that pricing cannot fix.
