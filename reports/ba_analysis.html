01 Company Overview

Boeing designs, builds and supports commercial jetliners, military aircraft, satellites, launch systems and defense electronics, and is one half of the global large-commercial-aircraft duopoly alongside Airbus. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $89.5 billion, up 34.5% YoY from $66.5 billion in 2024, as 600 commercial deliveries marked the company's highest annual total since 2018 and the December 2025 close of the Spirit AeroSystems reacquisition folded a major structures supplier back in-house. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through September 2026 stands at $94.9 billion (+17.5%), and Boeing ranks #91 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization.

The investment case is not a growth story — it is a balance-sheet and execution repair story. Boeing lost $10.7 billion at the GAAP operating line in 2024, and its $4.3 billion operating profit in 2025 came entirely from selling its Digital Aviation Solutions software unit (a $9.6 billion gain). It carries $45.9 billion of debt against $6.5 billion of shareholders' equity (September 30, 2026). Quarterly FCF has been positive in four of the last five quarters, most recently +$464 million in Q3 2026, but on October 27 management pushed its cash timetable back about a year: roughly $2 billion of free cash flow in 2026 and the long-promised $10 billion a year now in 2029. Everything below should be read against that frame: the recovery is underway, slower than the company said it would be.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Boeing's moat is structural rather than operational, which is precisely why the stock has survived a decade that would have destroyed most industrial franchises. Building a certified large commercial jet requires a multi-decade regulatory relationship, tens of billions in sunk development, a global supplier network, and worldwide spares and training infrastructure — the reason the field has consisted of two credible players for thirty years. Airlines compound that with fleet commonality: a carrier with 200 737s faces enormous switching costs in pilot training, maintenance tooling, and spares to move to the A320. The $745.4 billion total backlog at September 30, 2026 — $611.8B commercial (over 6,300 jets), $100.5B defense, $32.7B services — represents roughly a decade of committed demand, and Airbus is sold out on narrowbodies well into the 2030s, meaning a frustrated Boeing customer often has nowhere to go. On the defense side, Boeing now holds both US crewed sixth-generation fighter programs (the Air Force's F-47 and the Navy's F/A-XX) alongside the F-15EX, KC-46A and P-8 — positions a new entrant cannot realistically win. The December 8, 2025 reacquisition of Spirit AeroSystems pulled 737 and 787 fuselage production back in-house, restoring control over the quality chain that produced the door-plug failure. The critical caveat: a moat protects revenue, not margins. Boeing has shown for years that it can hold near-half of a duopoly and still lose money.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Regulatory dependency on production rate. Boeing's earnings power is gated by the FAA, not by demand. The 38/month cap imposed after January 2024 cost the company years of cash generation. The cap is gone and rates have moved 38 → 42 (Oct 2025) → 47 (May 2026), but Q3 showed the next limit is Boeing's own factory and suppliers: stable output at 47 now runs into 2027 and 52 into the back half of 2027. A single significant quality escape could bring the cap back.

2. Balance sheet fragility. $45.9B of debt against $20.7B of cash and marketable securities (September 30, 2026), a Debt-to-Equity ratio of 7.01×, and an Altman Z-Score of 1.41 (StockAnalysis; under 1.8 is read as the distress zone). Book value per share is just $8.27 against a $167 stock. Boeing has almost no margin for a further operational setback without returning to the capital markets — and it has already diluted shareholders heavily.

3. Fixed-price development contracts. VC-25B, KC-46A, T-7A, MQ-25 and Starliner have generated recurring reach-forward losses with no clear terminal date — $600M on KC-46A in Q4 2025 and $280M on VC-25B in Q2 2026 are the two most recent. BDS ran a −0.5% operating margin for all of FY2025; Q3 2026's 6.2% is about 3% without the HRL Laboratories gain. Until these programs are delivered, they hold back a defense segment whose revenue is growing.

4. Backlog pricing and customer concentration. A large share of the commercial backlog was priced under pre-inflation, pre-strike cost assumptions, while the 2024 IAM contract added a 38% wage increase over four years. Escalation clauses only partially offset this. Separately, order concentration among a handful of mega-carriers and lessors, plus tariff exposure on a product sold mostly abroad (non-US customers were 60% of Commercial Airplanes revenue in 2025, per the 10-K), makes revenue sensitive to trade policy and to any single airline's capex retrenchment.

Notable One-Off Items

Boeing's reported results are unusually distorted, and taking any headline number at face value will mislead you.

Digital Aviation Solutions gain (+$9.6B, FY2025). Boeing sold its DAS software business (Jeppesen, ForeFlight, and related assets) for ~$10.6B in proceeds, booking a $9.6 billion pre-tax gain. This single item is why FY2025 GAAP EPS was +$2.48 and GAAP operating earnings +$4.28B; without the year's $9.67B of disposition gains, operating earnings were about −$5.4B. Core (non-GAAP) EPS was $1.19. It also explains the absurd 64.4% "operating margin" reported for Global Services in FY2025. Every trailing profitability metric on this page inherits that distortion.

777X and 767 reach-forward losses ($5.14B in the first nine months of 2025, $86M in the first nine months of 2026). A reach-forward loss books a program's whole expected future loss at once. 777X first delivery has slipped repeatedly and is now targeted for 2027; management's cash targets assume certification by mid-2027. Further slippage triggers further charges.

Q3 2026 items. A $252M gain on the sale of HRL Laboratories lifted the defense segment's margin, and operating cash flow absorbed about $0.7B of Justice Department settlement payments (release).

Fixed-price defense charges. A $600M KC-46A charge in Q4 2025 and a $280M VC-25B (Air Force One) loss in Q2 2026, the latter for additional production and certification resources with first delivery pushed to 2028. These recur.

Spirit AeroSystems acquisition accounting (closed Dec 8, 2025). Spirit results consolidate into segment reporting from Q4 2025, which inflates BCA revenue and the cost base and makes YoY comparisons non-organic. Belfast operations were retained and rebranded Short Brothers; a separate Spirit Defense subsidiary was established. Purchase accounting will continue to affect comparability through 2026.

2024 legacy: strike and dilution. The 53-day IAM District 751 strike (mid-September to November 4, 2024, by a union of about 33,000 workers, resolved with a 38% wage increase and a $12,000 ratification bonus) stopped 737 and 767 production and weighed on 2024 margins and cash (FY2025 10-K). To defend its investment-grade rating Boeing raised $24.3 billion in October 2024 before costs ($23.9B net, per the 10-K): 112.5 million common shares at $143 plus $5B of mandatory convertible preferred, both enlarged when the underwriters took up their extra allotments. Shares outstanding were 791.9 million at September 30, 2026; Q3 weighted diluted shares were 792.7 million, up 4.3% from 759.9 million a year earlier. Shareholders were not wiped out, but per-share recovery math is permanently worse.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The regulatory ceiling is gone and demand is not the problem: a record $745.4B backlog, over 6,300 jets in a duopoly where the alternative is sold out, and the 737-7 and 737-10 finally certified. Free cash flow was positive in Q2 and Q3 2026 (+$464M in Q3, after a one-off $0.7B Justice Department payment), and management still sees $10B a year — only a year later, in 2029, a delay Ortberg called “more of a one-year shift to the right.” Each added 737 a month drops through a largely fixed cost base, so the payoff from stabilising at 47 and reaching 52 is large. Boeing now holds both US sixth-generation fighters, with the F-47 on a cost-plus contract, and Global Services earns about 18% margins. At $132B of market value, a normalized Boeing generating $10B of free cash flow would be priced at about 13× that figure.

Bear Case

Boeing missed on profit again (core −$0.34 a share against −$0.21 consensus) and moved its cash goals back a year, after the ramp to 47 a month ran slower than planned. You are paying 1.66× EV/Sales for a company with −9.5% ROIC, roughly breakeven operating profit without asset-sale gains, 7.01× Debt-to-Equity, an Altman Z-Score of 1.41 and consensus FY2026 EPS of −$1.67. Trailing profit exists only because of a one-time $9.6B divestiture gain. Charges keep coming — $5.1B on 777X/767 in the first nine months of 2025, $600M on KC-46A, $280M on VC-25B — and the 2027 cash target assumes 777X certification by mid-2027. Shareholders absorbed $24.3B of dilution at the bottom, and 25 of 28 analysts are still bullish, so positioning offers little cushion. One quality escape brings back the FAA cap.

Top 3 Risks
01 Production ramp failure or a new quality escape. The entire equity story is the delivery ramp, and Q3 showed it running behind plan: stable output at 47 a month now runs into 2027. A significant safety or quality incident would reinstate FAA rate restrictions, and Boeing has neither the balance sheet nor the shareholder goodwill to absorb another 21-month cap. This risk sits on top of 7.01× Debt-to-Equity.
02 Recurring fixed-price development charges. VC-25B, KC-46A, T-7A, MQ-25 and Starliner have each re-charged multiple times, holding BDS at a −0.5% FY2025 operating margin; Q3 2026's 6.2% is about 3% without the HRL Laboratories gain. The F-47 is cost-plus, per Ortberg, but the F/A-XX contract type has not been disclosed.
03 Balance sheet with no slack. $45.9B debt, $25.2B net debt, $8.27 book value per share and an Altman Z-Score of 1.41 leave essentially no capacity for a downturn in airline capex, a tariff shock on an export-weighted product, or another operational surprise. Any of these forces a return to capital markets — and the 2024 raise sold $18.2 billion (net) of new common stock, diluting existing holders.
  Industry Commentary
Production Ramp Execution Risk Is the Whole Industry Bottleneck
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Commercial aerospace in 2026 is supply-constrained, not demand-constrained. Boeing's $611.8B commercial backlog covers over 6,300 aircraft — roughly a decade at 2025's 600-delivery pace — and Airbus, which delivered 793 aircraft in 2025, is similarly sold out on narrowbodies into the next decade. Neither OEM can convert that book faster than its supply chain and its regulator allow. Castings, forgings, structures and skilled labor remain tight industry-wide; and the 38/month FAA cap that governed 737 output from January 2024 to October 2025 demonstrated that a regulator can unilaterally cap the revenue of one of the two companies that build large jets. Every incremental aircraft per month is worth several hundred million dollars of annualized contribution margin against a fixed cost base — which is why the 38 → 42 → 47 sequence, and the 52/month target now set for the back half of 2027, matters more to the equity than any demand datapoint.
Airline Capex and Fleet Replacement Demand Remain Robust
EMERGING TAILWIND
Order intake has not been the problem. Boeing booked 1,173 net commercial orders in 2025, and total backlog reached a record $745.4 billion at September 30, 2026 — $611.8B commercial, $100.5B defense, $32.7B services. The demand drivers are structural rather than cyclical: aging narrowbody fleets, fuel savings that make a new jet pay for itself at almost any oil price, and Middle East and Asian carriers scaling long-haul networks. Meanwhile defense budgets across NATO and the Indo-Pacific are elevated, and 26% of Boeing's defense backlog at the end of 2025 came from customers outside the US. The practical implication is that Boeing's revenue trajectory over the next five years is close to underwritten; the open question is entirely whether it converts that revenue to profit.
Duopoly Pricing Power — Real, But Boeing Hasn't Been Able to Use It
ONGOING HEADWIND
In theory a two-player market with a decade-long joint backlog is the ideal pricing environment. In practice Boeing has spent years negotiating from weakness: it took roughly 43% of 2025's duopoly deliveries (600 vs. Airbus's 793) while running a −17.1% Commercial Airplanes operating margin. A large share of the backlog was priced before the inflation of 2021–2024 and before the 2024 IAM contract added a 38% wage increase over four years; escalation clauses only partially cover the gap, and customers whose deliveries slipped extracted concessions. Bull framing: as delivery reliability is restored, new orders get priced at current economics and Boeing recaptures the pricing power the duopoly structure should confer — this is the single largest source of margin upside and is not in trailing numbers. Bear framing: the low-priced backlog takes years to work through, Airbus is compounding scale and share advantage while Boeing repairs itself, and a company that cannot earn a positive margin at 43% share in a duopoly may have a cost problem that pricing cannot fix.
