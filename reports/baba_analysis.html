01 Company Overview

Alibaba runs China's largest online marketplaces — Taobao and Tmall — alongside Alibaba Cloud, a quick-commerce delivery business, international marketplaces and a growing AI model and app operation (Qwen). It reports in renminbi (RMB) on a fiscal year ending March 31. FY2026 (April 2025 – March 2026) revenue was RMB1,023.7B, up 3% — 11% on a like-for-like basis once the disposed Sun Art and Intime retail businesses are stripped out. The most recent reported period is Q2 FY2027, the September quarter 2026 (July–September 2026), reported November 19, 2026: revenue RMB271.52B, +10% YoY. At a $201.71B market value (our calculation: 19.90B ordinary shares in issue at October 31, 2026 ÷ 8 per ADS × the $81.08 close) it ranks #10 among Chinese companies and #81 in the world by market capitalisation (companiesmarketcap, Nov 20, 2026 close). The home line trades in Hong Kong as 9988; this report covers the NYSE ADS.

Call it "broken growth turned AI-cloud turnaround". After the September quarter the filings support more of it than they did, but not all. The cloud half keeps accelerating: Cloud Intelligence revenue grew 34% in FY2026, the March 2026 quarter 38%, the June quarter 45%, and the September quarter — under the segment now called AI Cloud and Compute Services — 53%, with revenue from outside customers up 64% and AI-related product revenue posting a thirteenth straight quarter of triple-digit growth. The commerce half has not turned. Customer management revenue (CMR) fell 5% YoY; adding back a new merchant-subsidy accounting change it grew 1%, the same as in the June quarter. Operating profit recovered from a low base: adjusted EBITA rose 260% to RMB32.6B, from RMB9.1B a year earlier. Net income rose 213% to RMB64.4B, but mostly because of gains on the market value of Alibaba's stakes in other companies, which the company leaves out of its own adjusted figures. Free cash flow was still an outflow of RMB34.5B, with capex up 60%. So cloud is now a revenue and profit story; the group as a whole is still spending more cash than it brings in.

What an ADS holder actually owns

The share you buy in New York is an ADS of Alibaba Group Holding Limited, a company incorporated in the Cayman Islands (stated on its own results announcement) — not a share of a Chinese company. Chinese law restricts foreign ownership of internet businesses, so the licensed operating companies in China are owned by Chinese citizens, and the Cayman parent controls them and takes their economics through a web of contracts: exclusive service agreements, loan agreements, equity pledges and voting proxies. That is the VIE structure. The accounts consolidate these entities (the release refers to "the company, its subsidiaries and consolidated entities"), so the revenue and profit you see are real on paper. What you hold, though, is a claim that depends on Chinese authorities continuing to tolerate those contracts and on Chinese courts enforcing them. Nothing has been challenged so far, but no ADS holder has ever tested the claim in a Chinese court.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Taobao/Tmall merchant & consumer network 88VIP (~66M members) Full-stack AI: chips-to-cloud-to-model Open-source Qwen model family RMB487.9B cash & liquid investments Logistics & instant-delivery footprint

The old moat is the two-sided marketplace. Merchants pay for traffic on Taobao and Tmall because that is where China's highest-spending shoppers are, and the growing 88VIP base shows the top of that funnel is intact. The new moat Alibaba is trying to build is vertical integration in AI: its own cloud, compute, and the Qwen models, which the company's FY2026 results announcement says form "a fully integrated technology stack that significantly accelerates both training and inference efficiency." In a market where access to leading US accelerators is restricted, and where a new US rule from November 2026 reaches into overseas data centres too, owning the whole stack down to its own chip unit (T-Head) is a real advantage. The weakness is that the old moat protects traffic, not monetisation. Like-for-like CMR growth has sat at 1% for two quarters while the company spent heavily on quick-commerce subsidies to keep users. The marketplace is holding share by paying for it.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The capex bill is still running ahead of the cash. Q2 FY2027 capex of RMB50.5B was 0.83× the AI Cloud segment's quarterly revenue of RMB61.0B (it was 1.4× in the June quarter). Free cash flow was −RMB34.5B in one quarter and −RMB90.0B on a trailing basis (StockAnalysis). On the call, per published transcripts, CEO Eddie Wu said Alibaba should reach positive cash flow overall in the second half of 2028. The cash pile covers years of this, but the return on it has to show up in cloud margins, which are 14.5% for now.
02
Core commerce has stalled under subsidy. CMR went from +8% like-for-like in the March quarter to +1% in the June quarter and +1% again in the September quarter. The contra-revenue program means Alibaba is effectively rebating merchants to keep them spending. If like-for-like CMR turns negative, the profit pool funding the AI build shrinks while the build is still running.
03
Structural: VIE, regulation and geopolitics. ADS holders own a Cayman holding company whose control of the Chinese operating businesses rests on contracts (Section 01). Changes in Chinese rules on those structures, US–China restrictions on chips or listings, or delisting pressure on US-listed Chinese issuers would hit the ADS directly. The HKEX line exists partly as a hedge against the last of those. The chip risk became concrete in November 2026: on Nov 12 the shares fell as much as 7.3% in Hong Kong as turmoil at Nvidia stoked fears over access to chips; Alibaba rents Nvidia chips through Singapore-based Megaspeed International (The Edge Malaysia); and on Nov 16 the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued a rule, effective on publication (expected Nov 19), requiring a licence to ship advanced chips to any data centre that gives companies headquartered in China large-scale access to computing power, with existing providers given 90 days to obtain one (Nixon Peabody summary of the rule).
04
Litigation. Law-firm notices from August 2026, Hagens Berman's among them, announced a US securities class action, Wistisen v. Alibaba Group Holding Limited (Southern District of New York), on behalf of buyers of Alibaba securities from June 26, 2025 to June 24, 2026; the lead-plaintiff deadline was October 5, 2026. Alibaba recorded a one-time class-action provision in FY2025, per the FY2026 announcement.

Notable One-Off Items

Several items distort GAAP profit. Investment gains: interest and investment income rose 265% to RMB73,305M in Q2 FY27, "primarily attributable to the increase in net gain from mark-to-market changes of our equity investments," partly offset by impairments of investments. In the June quarter it had fallen 48% to RMB9,004M. That swing is the main reason net income rose 213% in the September quarter after falling 75% in the June quarter, and the company excludes these gains from non-GAAP earnings. Goodwill impairment: none in Q2 FY27; RMB4,458M in Q1 FY27, which the release ties to businesses in All Others; FY2026 carried RMB9,515M. Disposals: the Sun Art and Intime sales cut reported FY2026 revenue growth from 11% like-for-like to 3%. Accounting reclassification: the contra-revenue treatment of merchant subsidies lowers reported CMR and sales and marketing expense by the same amount, so the revenue line looks weaker than the underlying economics.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $127.53 to the November 20, 2026 settled close of $81.08, the ADS lost 36.4% of its price; over the same dates SPY gained 51.4% and QQQ 54.5% (price only, dividends excluded). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Alibaba owns the only fully integrated AI stack in China at scale — its own chips (T-Head), cloud, compute and a leading open model family — and it is monetising: AI Cloud revenue +53%, with outside customers +64%, AI-related product revenue RMB17.9B a quarter with thirteen straight triple-digit quarters, and segment EBITA +121% at a 14.5% margin. The AI Labs loss narrowed from RMB13.9B to RMB10.5B in a quarter, and management says quick-commerce losses will keep shrinking. On forward earnings the stock is at ~9.6x (StockAnalysis), with RMB487.9B of cash and liquid investments, and 38 of 40 analysts rate it Buy at an average target double the price. A T-Head listing would put a public price on the chip unit. If cloud margins keep rising and the subsidy war cools, earnings recover from a low base while the capex is already committed.

Bear Case

The turnaround is in cloud, not in the company. Core CMR fell 5% reported and has grown 1% like-for-like for two straight quarters. Most of the 213% jump in net income came from gains on the market value of investments, not from operations, so the 11.5x trailing P/E flatters the business. Free cash flow was −RMB34.5B in a single quarter, and on the call, per published transcripts, management pointed to the second half of 2028 for positive cash flow and floated a 30–45% cloud growth target for next fiscal year, below the 53% just reported. US chip rules are tightening around the overseas data centres Chinese AI firms rent. The buyback that supported the stock in FY2025 ($11.9B, 5.1% of shares) has shrunk to about 0.1% of market cap a quarter, and the share count is rising. ROIC is 4%. On top of that, ADS holders own a Cayman shell with contractual — not equity — control of the Chinese business, and the company faces a US securities class action filed in August 2026.

Top 3 Risks

01
AI capex without matching returns. Capex of RMB50.5B in one quarter (+60%) against AI Cloud revenue of RMB61.0B and ROIC of 4.28%. If cloud growth slows toward the 30–45% range management floated before margins scale, free cash flow stays negative (−RMB79.1B in the six months to September) with too little to show for it.
02
Commerce stall. Like-for-like CMR went from +8% to +1% in the June quarter and stayed at +1% in the September quarter. Commerce funds everything else: E-commerce Group EBITA of RMB38.9B against a group total of RMB32.6B. A negative like-for-like print would put the funding model itself under pressure.
03
Structure and jurisdiction. The VIE structure, US–China technology and listing policy (now including the November 2026 rule on chips in overseas data centres), and securities litigation are risks the income statement cannot offset. They are also why a 40-analyst Strong Buy with 100% implied upside can sit unresolved for months.
Industry Commentary
The AI capex race — revenue now, returns later
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Alibaba's September quarter is a clean case of the global hyperscaler pattern at a smaller scale and a thinner margin. Capex was +60% YoY to RMB50.5B, operating cash flow +55% to RMB15.7B, and free cash flow −RMB34.5B. The AI Labs segment lost RMB10.5B, "primarily due to our increased investment in AI capabilities, and higher inference cost related to Qwen app." Against that, AI Cloud revenue grew 53% and its EBITA 121%. The question for every AI spender is the same: does the infrastructure earn its cost of capital before it has to be replaced? Alibaba's version is harder in two respects — free cash flow is negative, not merely lower, and US rules now reach the overseas data centres where Chinese firms rent advanced chips — and easier in another: it holds RMB487.9B of cash and liquid investments, and its own chip unit, T-Head, gives it a domestic supply line.

China consumption and the quick-commerce subsidy war
ONGOING HEADWIND

Management put the June-quarter slowdown in CMR down to "weaker transaction activities," and the September quarter brought no pick-up: CMR −5% reported, +1% like-for-like. Merchant subsidies, now booked as contra revenue, show the platform paying to keep merchants' ad spend going. In quick commerce Alibaba grew revenue 22% in the September quarter, after 45% in June, and says it improved unit economics "while maintaining market share," meaning competitors are spending too. The filings show a core marketplace growing like-for-like at +1% while adjacent businesses are bought with subsidy.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The sell side's average target of $162.28 sits 100% above the $81.08 close. The bull framing: Alibaba is priced as a slowing Chinese retailer with an expensive hobby, when on the evidence it is the leading Chinese AI infrastructure company attached to a cash-generating marketplace. Forward P/E of ~9.6 on recovering earnings, a cloud unit growing 53% with rising margins, and a still-unused ~$18.7B buyback authorisation (derived) are the ingredients for investors to pay a higher multiple of earnings. The bear framing: the discount is for jurisdiction and capital allocation, not for growth. A company that cut its buyback from $11.9B to $1.05B to fund AI capex, whose core ad revenue fell 5% in the latest quarter, and whose ADS is a contractual claim on Chinese assets, has not earned a US-tech multiple. The way to judge it is not the P/E but three reported numbers over the next four quarters: like-for-like CMR, AI Cloud EBITA margin, and free cash flow.
