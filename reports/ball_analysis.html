01 Company Overview

Ball started in 1880 in Buffalo, New York, when Frank and Edmund Ball set up the Wooden Jacket Can Company; by 1889 the brothers had moved their glass works to Muncie, Indiana, and the name on a century of home-canning jars was theirs. Several reinventions later, the company is almost nothing but one product: the aluminium beverage can, plus aluminium containers for personal-care and household goods, made across North and Central America, Europe/Middle East/Africa and South America. TTM revenue is $14.33B, up 15.6%, with GAAP EPS of $3.51. At the $59.71 close the market values it at about $15.8B, roughly #1,414 among the world's listed companies.

The last five years were a deliberate narrowing. Ball sold its aerospace division to BAE Systems for about $5.6B in February 2024 (the cash-flow statement books $5,422M of divestiture proceeds that year), deconsolidated its aluminium-cups business in March 2025, sold its 41% stake in its Saudi Arabian business in August 2025, and bought 80% of Benepack's European beverage-can business in January 2026. The aerospace cash went mostly back to shareholders — $1.71B of buybacks in FY2024 and $1.32B in FY2025, with the share count down 7.77% year over year. What is left is a pure-play can maker whose economics are simple to state: fill the plants, pass the metal cost through to customers, and turn a 10% operating margin into cash. The stock has not rewarded the tidy-up yet: the $59.71 close is 33.6% below the September 2021 month-end of $89.97. The live question is whether a leaner can business deserves a higher multiple than the conglomerate did, or whether it is just a slower-growing, more indebted one.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in a concentrated industry Multi-year volume contracts Plants located near fillers Metal cost pass-through Three-continent footprint 146 years of customer relationships

The moat is physical. Empty cans are mostly air, so they are expensive to ship; a can plant sits close to the brewery or bottler it serves, and building a new one takes years of capital. That keeps the industry to a handful of large suppliers and makes long contracts normal — the "more than 50% sold out through the end of the decade" line is the moat in one sentence. The weakness is the other side of the same coin: the customers are giant beverage companies with their own buying power, the product is a commodity, and margins are earned on utilisation. A half-empty plant loses money; a full one needs expensive new capacity. Ball's operating margin has stayed in a narrow 8–11% band for five years (10.38% in FY2021, 8.23% in FY2022, 10.56% in FY2025). It is a toll bridge, not a growth engine.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
North America is short of capacity. The biggest segment's earnings fell 2.4% in Q2 even as sales rose, because the network is too full to run efficiently. Until Millersburg ramps, each extra can costs more to make. Both executives said so on the call.
02
Leverage is higher than you would expect after a $5.6B asset sale. Total debt is $7.58B against $500M of cash; debt-to-equity is 1.31. The aerospace proceeds went mainly to buybacks rather than debt repayment, so the balance sheet now carries a pure packaging business at 3.16x net debt/EBITDA.
03
Free cash flow has been lumpy. $34M in FY2021, −$1,350M in FY2022 (a heavy capex year), $818M in FY2023, −$369M in FY2024 and $788M in FY2025. The 2026 guide of more than $900M would be the best in the window, and it is what funds the buyback.
04
Trade policy on the metal. The release books tariff contingency expenses in its charges. The Q2 call summary noted no near-term impact from Section 232 tariffs, but aluminium is most of what Ball buys and trade policy is outside its control.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things bend the reported numbers. First, the aerospace sale: FY2024 net income of $4,008M and EPS of $13.00 are not operating results — operating income that year was $1,183M — so any EPS growth rate that touches 2024 or early 2025 means little. Second, the gap between GAAP and "comparable" earnings: H1 2026 GAAP diluted EPS was $1.59 against comparable $1.97, and Ball's guidance ("10-plus percent" comparable EPS growth) is on the comparable basis; the adjustments include business-consolidation charges and tariff contingency expenses. Third, portfolio changes — the cups deconsolidation (March 2025), the Saudi stake sale (August 2025) and the Benepack acquisition (January 2026) — mean segment growth rates are not perfectly like-for-like.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Ball is now a pure-play can maker with more than half its volume contracted through the end of the decade, volumes growing 4.3%, comparable EPS growing 14.4%, and management guiding to 10%-plus comparable EPS growth, more than $900M of free cash flow and at least $600M of buybacks this year. At 14.9x consensus 2026 earnings, with every one of 15 analysts' targets above the price, the market is still valuing it like the disappointing business of 2022. Once Millersburg is running, this year's North American friction becomes next year's capacity, and a share count already down 7.77% in a year keeps shrinking.

Bear Case

A can is a commodity, the customers are bigger than the supplier, and the operating margin has sat in an 8–11% band for five years. The aerospace proceeds went mainly to buybacks rather than debt, leaving a 3.16x-levered packaging company with a 1.07 current ratio and a free-cash-flow record with two negative years out of five. Much of the revenue growth is metal passed through. South America's 64% earnings jump was a World Cup quarter, and the largest segment's earnings fell. Five years of flat-to-down share price say the market has heard the "pure-play re-rating" story before.

Top 3 Risks

01
Execution on Millersburg and the North American network. Half the company's sales come from a region whose earnings fell in Q2 on start-up costs and a constrained network. A slow ramp stretches the ~$35M start-up bill and the friction into 2027.
02
Cash promises that compete with each other. Buybacks of at least $600M (about $100M done by mid-year), ~$800M of total returns and deleveraging from 3.16x to ~2.7x all depend on more than $900M of free cash flow — a level Ball did not reach in any of FY2021–FY2025.
03
Aluminium and trade policy. Metal is the product. The company is already booking tariff contingency expenses, and although the Q2 call flagged no near-term Section 232 impact, a change in trade rules can move costs faster than contracts reprice.
Industry Commentary
Capacity: from overbuild to tight in four years
CYCLE TURN

Ball's own numbers trace a capacity cycle. It spent $1,726M on capex in FY2021 and $1,651M in FY2022, building for a demand boom; free cash flow went to −$1,350M in 2022 and the stock halved. Capex was then cut to $484M in FY2024 and $474M in FY2025. Now North America is short of capacity again — "notably tight," in the CEO's words — and a new plant has just started. A can maker earns its best returns when plants are full and new capacity is scarce. The risk is that the industry repeats 2021 and overbuilds.

Growth is coming from outside the US
EMERGING TAILWIND

In Q2 2026, EMEA volumes grew mid-single digits and South America mid-teens, against low-single digits in North America. Management expects EMEA full-year growth above its 3–5% range, has added Benepack in Europe, and has announced a plant in India. Half the company's sales now come from outside North and Central America, and that half is growing faster.
