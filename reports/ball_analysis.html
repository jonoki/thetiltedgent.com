01 Company Overview

Ball started in 1880 in Buffalo, New York, when Frank and Edmund Ball set up the Wooden Jacket Can Company; by 1889 the brothers had moved their glass works to Muncie, Indiana, and the name on a century of home-canning jars was theirs. Several reinventions later, the company is almost nothing but one product: the aluminium beverage can, plus aluminium containers for personal-care and household goods, made across North and Central America, Europe/Middle East/Africa and South America. TTM revenue is $15.14B, up 19.2%, with GAAP EPS of $1.99, cut by a $475M non-cash charge in the third quarter for handing its U.K. pension plan to an insurer. At the $67.38 close the market values it at about $17.5B.

The last five years were a deliberate narrowing. Ball sold its aerospace division to BAE Systems for about $5.6B in February 2024 (the cash-flow statement books $5,422M of divestiture proceeds that year), took its aluminium-cups business off its own books in March 2025, sold its 41% stake in its Saudi Arabian business in August 2025, and bought 80% of Benepack's European beverage-can business in January 2026. The aerospace cash went mostly back to shareholders — $1.71B of buybacks in FY2024 and $1.32B in FY2025, with the share count down 5.77% year over year. What is left is almost purely a can maker, whose economics are simple to state: fill the plants, pass the metal cost through to customers, and turn a roughly 10% operating margin into cash. The stock has only half rewarded the tidy-up: the $67.38 close is up 40.9% over the past year but still 27.9% below the November 2021 month-end of $93.45. The live question is whether a leaner can business deserves a higher price for each dollar of earnings than the conglomerate did, or whether it is just a slower-growing, more indebted one.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in a concentrated industry Multi-year volume contracts Plants located near fillers Metal cost pass-through Three-continent footprint 146 years of customer relationships

The moat is physical. Empty cans are mostly air, so they are expensive to ship; a can plant sits close to the brewery or bottler it serves, and building a new one takes years of capital. That keeps the industry to a handful of large suppliers and makes long contracts normal — the "more than 50% sold out through the end of the decade" line is the moat in one sentence. The weakness is the other side of the same coin: the customers are giant beverage companies with their own buying power, the product is a commodity, and margins are earned on utilisation. A half-empty plant loses money; a full one needs expensive new capacity. Ball's operating margin has stayed in a narrow 8–11% band for five years (10.38% in FY2021, 8.23% in FY2022, 10.56% in FY2025). It is a toll bridge, not a growth engine.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
North America is short of capacity. The biggest segment's earnings fell 2.4% in Q2 and 2.3% in Q3 even as sales rose, because the network is too full to run efficiently. Until Millersburg ramps, each extra can costs more to make; the CFO said so again on the Q3 call.
02
Leverage is higher than you would expect after a $5.6B asset sale. Total debt was $7,284M against $410M of cash at September 30, 2026 (release); debt-to-equity is 1.33. Over FY2024 and FY2025 Ball spent $3.03B on buybacks and cut its debt by a net $1.63B (StockAnalysis cash-flow statements), so the balance sheet now carries a pure packaging business at 3.21x net debt to EBITDA.
03
Free cash flow has been lumpy. Operating cash flow minus capex was $34M in FY2021, −$1,350M in FY2022 (a heavy capex year), $818M in FY2023, −$369M in FY2024 and $788M in FY2025. Ball's own adjusted figure for 2025, which adds back $168M of cash taxes on the aerospace sale, was a record $956M. The 2026 guide is now "in the range of $900 million," and nine months produced −$439M, so the fourth quarter has to carry the year.
04
Trade policy on the metal. Ball had set money aside for possible tariff costs and reversed those accruals in Q3 after a favourable final ruling from the U.S. Department of Commerce, per the release. Aluminium is most of what Ball buys, and trade policy is outside its control.

Notable One-Off Items

Four things bend the reported numbers. First, the U.K. pension buy-out: in Q3 Ball paid an insurer to take over its U.K. defined-benefit pension plan and booked a $475M non-cash settlement charge, which turned the quarter into a GAAP loss and pulled trailing GAAP EPS down to $1.99 from $3.51 at the last edition. Second, the aerospace sale: FY2024 net income of $4,008M and EPS of $13.00 are not operating results — operating income that year was $1,183M — so any EPS growth rate that touches 2024 or early 2025 means little. Third, the gap between GAAP and "comparable" earnings: nine-month 2026 GAAP diluted EPS was $1.25 against comparable EPS of $3.08 (release), and Ball's guidance ("10-plus percent" comparable EPS growth) is on the comparable basis. Fourth, portfolio changes — the cups business leaving the books (March 2025), the Saudi stake sale (August 2025) and the Benepack acquisition (January 2026) — mean segment growth rates are not perfectly like-for-like.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Ball is now almost purely a can maker, with more than half its volume contracted through the end of the decade and, in Lewis's words, "effectively contracted for 2027." Volumes grew 4.7% in Q3, comparable EPS 6.8%, and management still guides to 10-plus percent comparable EPS growth, about $900M of free cash flow and at least $600M of buybacks this year. At 16.8x consensus 2026 earnings and 15.0x 2027, the shares are still 27.9% below their November 2021 month-end. Millersburg's start-up bill is now expected below the August plan, this year's North American friction becomes next year's capacity, and a share count already down 5.77% in a year keeps shrinking.

Bear Case

A can is a commodity, the customers are bigger than the supplier, and the operating margin has sat in an 8–11% band for five years. More of the aerospace proceeds went to buybacks ($3.03B in FY2024–FY2025) than to net debt repayment ($1.63B), leaving a packaging company at 3.21x net debt to EBITDA with a 1.04 current ratio. Nine months of 2026 produced −$439M of free cash flow, the guide has slipped from "greater than" to "in the range of" $900M as capex rose, and the largest segment's earnings fell again. Much of the revenue growth is metal passed through. After a 40.9% gain in a year the stock sits 1.3% below its 52-week high, the average target is only 6.3% above the price, and J.P. Morgan stepped back to Neutral the day after the print.

Top 3 Risks

01
Execution on Millersburg and the North American network. Half the company's sales come from a region whose earnings fell in Q2 and Q3 on start-up costs and a full network, now under a new segment head (Carey Causey, from October 19). A slow ramp stretches the friction into 2027, even with the 2026 start-up bill cut to about $25M.
02
Cash promises that compete with each other. Buybacks of at least $600M ($272M done in nine months), at least $800M of total returns and deleveraging from 3.21x to 2.7x all depend on about $900M of 2026 free cash flow, after −$439M in the first nine months. Industry Commentary
Capacity: from overbuild to tight in four years
CYCLE TURN

Ball's own numbers trace a capacity cycle. It spent $1,726M on capex in FY2021 and $1,651M in FY2022, building for a demand boom; free cash flow went to −$1,350M in 2022 and the stock halved. Capex was then cut to $484M in FY2024 and $474M in FY2025, and is guided back up to $650M for 2026. Now North America is short of capacity again — "notably tight," in the CEO's words in August — Millersburg started in July and a German plant at Baruth is due to ramp up in late 2028. A can maker earns its best returns when plants are full and new capacity is scarce. The risk is that the industry repeats 2021 and overbuilds.

Growth is coming from outside the US
EMERGING TAILWIND

In Q3 2026, EMEA volumes grew mid-single digits and South America low-double digits, against low-single digits in North America. Management expects EMEA full-year growth above its 3–5% range and South America in the middle of its 4–6% range, has added Benepack in Europe, and has announced plants in Germany and India. About half the company's sales now come from outside North and Central America (50.8% in Q3), and that half is growing faster.
