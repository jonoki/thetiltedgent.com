01 Company Overview

Bloom Energy builds solid oxide fuel cells — refrigerator-sized "Energy Servers" that sit on a customer's site and turn natural gas, biogas or hydrogen into electricity without burning it. For most of its public life that was a hard sell: a clean-ish power box competing with a grid that was cheaper and already there. Revenue grew at 10–23% a year from FY2021 to FY2024 and the company lost money every one of those years — GAAP net losses of $164M, $301M, $302M and $29M.

Then the grid stopped being "already there". AI data centers need hundreds of megawatts now, and a utility interconnection takes years. Bloom's pitch became time to power: installation in "3 to 4 months versus 4–7 years for grid connections", with an Oracle project delivered in 55 days. The orders followed — up to 1 GW with AEP (Nov 2024), a $5B Brookfield partnership (Oct 2025) expanded to a $25B framework (Jun 30, 2026), and a master services agreement for up to 2.8 GW with Oracle (mid-April 2026). Q2 2026 revenue was $1,065.4M, up 165.5%, the first billion-dollar quarter in the company's history, and TTM GAAP EPS turned positive at $0.89. The stock went from a $9.60 month-end close in October 2024 to $265.63, and on September 21, 2026 it entered the S&P 500, replacing Molson Coors.

The central debate is not whether the demand is real — the income statement says it is. It is what a 298x trailing multiple is paying for: a durable new power-generation category, or a bridge technology whose window closes once the grid and turbines catch up. And underneath the growth sit two facts investors should hold in the same hand: one customer was about 73% of H1 2026 revenue, and the company faces a securities class action over its disclosure of Chinese scandium in its supply chain.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Time to power (months, not years) Proven SOFC at scale US manufacturing base Brookfield $25B financing framework Oracle / AEP reference deployments Installed-base service annuity

Bloom's moat is not that fuel cells are the cheapest electrons — historically they have not been. It is that they are the fastest electrons you can get at scale. When a data center's alternative is waiting years for a grid connection, the value of power is set by the revenue of the idle GPUs, not by the utility tariff. Bloom has spent two decades manufacturing the one on-site technology that can be dropped in modularly, and the Brookfield framework solves the customer's other problem — who pays for it up front. The vulnerability is the mirror image: a moat built on scarcity lasts only as long as the scarcity.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One customer is most of the company. About 73% of H1 2026 revenue came from a single customer. Whether that is an end-user or a financing channel through which many end-users buy is not stated in the sources fetched — but either way, a change in that relationship would move group revenue more than anything else in this report.
02
Scandium and the class action. On July 8, 2026 Hunterbrook Media published "Bloom's Big Lie", alleging Bloom sourced Chinese scandium through intermediaries in Thailand, Japan and South Korea; the stock fell nearly 6% that day. A securities class action (Nevins v. Bloom Energy Corporation, N.D. Cal., No. 26-cv-07944) covers purchases from February 27, 2025 to July 8, 2026. Management says it is not dependent on China. These are allegations, not findings.
03
The window can close. Bloom sells speed. If utilities, gas turbines and grid build-out catch up with data-center demand, the premium for on-site power in months shrinks — and so does the case for paying fuel-cell prices.
04
Dilution from convertibles. Shares outstanding rose 24.65% YoY (StockAnalysis). The 0% notes due November 2030 ($2,500.0M principal per the 10-Q summary) are potentially convertible into 19,554,000 shares — another ~6.6% of today's count — and $147.0M converted in H1 2026. Long-run per-share series understate how much the company has grown in absolute terms and overstate how much of it belongs to each share.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a stock in a genuine re-rating can stay overbought for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $18.72 to the September 18, 2026 settled close of $265.63, BE rose 1,319% on price (no dividends). But almost none of that happened in the first three years. The stock fell from $18.72 to a $9.60 month-end low in October 2024 — a 49% loss over three years of cash burn — before the AI-power story arrived. Everything since is a different company in the same ticker: +186% in November 2024 on the AEP deal, a near-vertical run from $18.32 in April 2025 to $132.16 in October 2025, then +109% in April 2026 on Oracle.

The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 96.7 in October 2025 — about as overbought as the measure gets — and stayed above 76 through the first half of 2026. July's 32% fall reset it to 54.8; it now reads 65.9, elevated but no longer extreme. The 10-month moving average sits at roughly $207.82, well below the price: the long-term trend is still up, and the July break did not reverse it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Power is the binding constraint on AI, and Bloom sells the one thing a data center cannot buy from a utility: power in months. The evidence is in the numbers, not the narrative — Q2 revenue +165.5%, gross margin up 668 bps to 33.4%, TTM free cash flow of $624.7M from a company that burned $456M in 2023, a ~$20B backlog at end-2025, and financing partners (Brookfield's $25B framework, the Nebius structure) that take the capital burden off customers. Consensus has EPS going from $2.71 in 2026 to $4.93 in 2027; if the growth holds, a 98x forward multiple falls to 54x in a year. S&P 500 membership adds a permanent passive holder base.

Bear Case

At 298x trailing GAAP earnings, 25x sales and 187x EV/EBITDA, the price assumes the boom lasts — for a business where one customer was about 73% of H1 revenue. The moat is scarcity of grid power, and scarcity is what markets are built to fix. A securities class action alleges misleading disclosure of Chinese scandium in the supply chain. Share count rose 24.65% in a year, and another ~19.6M shares sit behind the 2030 convertibles. The stock has already shown what a bad month looks like — −32% in July 2026 — and with a 3.81 beta it will show it again. Two of 29 analysts rate it a Sell, with a low target of $97.

Top 3 Risks

01
Customer concentration. ~73% of H1 2026 revenue from one customer, and receivables 87% concentrated in three. Any delay, renegotiation or financing hiccup at that counterparty flows straight into product revenue.
02
Valuation leaves no room for a stumble. At 98x FY2026 consensus EPS, a miss against the $3.9–$4.2B guide — or even a lumpy quarter from product shipments — reprices the stock by far more than the earnings change. July 2026's −32% is the template.
03
Supply chain and litigation. The scandium allegations touch both a critical input for solid oxide cells and the credibility of management disclosure. The class action is at an early stage (lead-plaintiff deadline September 28, 2026); outcomes and any supply-chain changes are unknown.
Industry Commentary
Time to power: the grid gap for AI data centers
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

AEP said in November 2024 that it expected 20 GW of new load across its footprint by the end of the decade. Grid connections are quoted at 4–7 years; Bloom installs in 3–4 months. That gap is the entire market. Brookfield's head of AI infrastructure framed it as "behind-the-meter power solutions are essential to closing the grid gap for AI factories." As long as the gap exists, the buyer's comparison is not fuel cell versus grid tariff — it is fuel cell versus idle GPUs.

Critical minerals and supply-chain scrutiny
ONGOING HEADWIND

Solid oxide fuel cells use scandium, and the July 2026 Hunterbrook report put Bloom's sourcing under a spotlight at the same time that US policy is focused on Chinese critical-mineral dependence. Bloom's position, stated on the Q2 call, is that it is not dependent on China and that sufficient scandium can be economically recovered. The market will want that shown, not stated.

The price of being the answer
VALUATION RISK

In twelve months Bloom went from a perennial money-loser to an S&P 500 member priced at 298x trailing earnings. The bull framing: trailing multiples are the wrong lens for a company doubling revenue with rising margins and positive cash flow; on 2027 consensus the multiple roughly halves. The bear framing: every successful bridge technology gets priced as if the bridge were permanent, and the three variables that decide this — the concentrated customer, the scandium question and the pace of grid build-out — are all outside the income statement. The adjudication is the order book: backlog and new-customer diversification, reported quarter by quarter.
