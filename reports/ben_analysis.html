01 Company Overview

For most of its life the company was called Franklin Resources and the funds were called Franklin Templeton. On August 17, 2026 the holding company took the brand's name — Franklin Templeton, Inc. — keeping the ticker BEN, the CUSIP and every shareholder right unchanged. It is a global asset manager: it runs mutual funds, ETFs, separately managed accounts, institutional mandates and private-market funds, and it is paid a percentage of what it manages. At June 30, 2026 that was a record $1,791.6B of AUM, up 11% in a year; the company's preliminary figure for August 31, 2026 was $1.83 trillion. Trailing-twelve-month revenue was $9.32B, up 7.9%, and GAAP EPS was $1.47.

An asset manager is a toll booth on other people's money, and the toll depends on two things it doesn't fully control: what markets do to the assets, and whether clients stay. For two years, the second one went badly. In August 2024 its bond unit, Western Asset Management, put its co-chief investment officer Ken Leech on leave; he was indicted in November 2024 on charges of "cherry-picking" winning trades for favoured portfolios. Clients pulled money: by March 2026 Western's AUM was $228.9B, down 40% from June 2024 (CNBC). The stock drifted from $28.11 at the end of March 2024 to an $18.76 month-end low in April 2025. Then the story turned. Western settled with the SEC for $100M on June 5, 2026, which the company says also closed the parallel Department of Justice inquiry. Leech pleaded guilty to obstruction on June 12, and the rest of the firm is now bringing in money: $63.3B of long-term net inflows in the first nine months of fiscal 2026, against $85.5B of net outflows in the same nine months a year earlier. The stock closed at $33.64, up 37.8% over 52 weeks. The question now is whether a firm that has swapped a bond franchise for private markets, ETFs and custom portfolios can hold a ~30% adjusted margin with fees still drifting lower.

The two operating metrics that actually matter

An asset manager's revenue is roughly average AUM × effective fee rate. AUM grows two ways: markets (which the firm doesn't control) and net flows (which it does). Watch the flows and the fee rate. Everything else follows from them.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$1.79T scale Global distribution (~$525B intl AUM) Private-markets franchise (Lexington) Canvas tax-overlay stickiness Locked-up alternative capital 43% insider ownership 46 years of dividend growth

Asset management has weaker moats than most industries. The product is performance, and past performance really is no guarantee. Franklin's advantages are in distribution and in the kind of money it holds, not in stock-picking. Distribution means relationships with the wealth platforms and intermediaries that decide which funds advisers see, in the U.S. and in the international markets that now hold ~$525B. The money is also changing character. A dollar in a ten-year secondaries fund or in a tax-managed Canvas account is much harder to redeem than a dollar in a bond mutual fund, and the two businesses taking a growing share of Franklin's flows are exactly those. The Western episode showed the flip side. A reputation built by one star manager can walk out of the door in a few months, and $18.4B of good quarterly flows can be undone by one bad headline in a business where clients can redeem daily.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Revenue is a leveraged bet on markets. In Q3, $98.0B of AUM growth came from market movement and $18.4B from flows. With equity at 42% of AUM and a five-year beta of 1.57, a bear market cuts revenue and flows together, while compensation, technology and occupancy (over $1.19B a quarter combined) do not fall at the same pace.
02
Western isn't finished. The SEC and DOJ matters for the firm are closed, but Western still saw $1.1B of long-term outflows in Q3. Leech's sentencing on his obstruction plea was scheduled for September 21, 2026 (per coverage of the June 12 plea); the outcome was not confirmed for this report. Any renewed headline risk falls on a franchise that has already lost 40% of its assets since June 2024.
03
GAAP earnings and cash flow look much weaker than "adjusted". Q3 GAAP operating margin was 9.2%, against an adjusted 28.0%. The gap is intangible amortisation, a $33.0M impairment, acquisition-related items, consolidated investment products and a general, administrative and other line up 70% YoY. Trailing free cash flow per S&P Global data is only $231.6M, down 84%, against $1.32 per share of dividends (~$670M a year on 508M shares). StockAnalysis's quarterly cash-flow table shows operating cash flow is lumpy: $1,282M in the June 2025 quarter against $581M in June 2026, and −$255M in the December 2025 quarter. The sources fetched don't say what drove those swings, and it needs watching.
04
Fee compression. The fee rate guide sits in the high-37 bps range. Growth in ETFs (61% of ETF net flows into active ETFs), cash and large institutional mandates tends to bring in AUM at lower fees than the legacy mutual-fund book. Private markets push the other way, and the net effect is the number to track.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the GAAP-to-adjusted gap is unusually wide. Q3 GAAP operating income was $215.8M against adjusted $508.9M, and GAAP EPS was $0.31 against adjusted $0.72. The adjustments include $50.7M of intangible amortisation, a $33.0M intangible impairment, acquisition-related retention pay, and the consolidation of investment products. General, administrative and other expense also rose 70% YoY to $312.8M. The release tables fetched don't itemise that line, so we can't say whether it holds the $100M Western Asset SEC penalty agreed on June 5. Second, other income of $155.1M in Q3 (vs $22.5M a year earlier) boosted GAAP pre-tax income; it includes gains on the firm's own investments and consolidated funds, which don't repeat reliably. Third, buybacks: $348.1M repurchased in Q3 (10.4M shares), part of it an opportunistic block from Great-West Lifeco, so per-share figures benefit slightly from a falling share count (−0.42% quarter on quarter).

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 month-end close of $29.72 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $33.64, BEN gained 13.2% on price. Adding back the $5.87 per share of dividends that went ex over the period (sum of the quarterly amounts on StockAnalysis, not reinvested) gives an estimated ~33% total return. The dividend did most of the work. But the endpoints hide the shape: the first four years went down, and nearly all of the gain came in the last seventeen months. The stock peaked at $33.49 in December 2021, fell to $21.52 in September 2022, spent 2023 chopping between $22 and $31, then slid through the Western Asset scandal to $18.76 in April 2025. From there it nearly doubled, to a $34.15 August 2026 month-end, the highest of the window.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows the change of regime. Its low was 28.3 in April 2022, and it sat near 30 again through August–September 2024 (31.0 / 30.8) as the Western news broke. It then climbed to 80.0 at the August 2026 close, the most overbought reading in the five-year series, and eased to 75.3 at the September 21 close. The 10-month moving average is about $29.66, roughly 12% below the price, so the long-term trend is clearly up. The short-term warning is the RSI itself: it has been above 70 for five consecutive month-end readings (April–August 2026). Momentum that stretched usually cools, in time or in price.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The legal overhang is gone, and the flows turned before the settlement did. $63.3B of long-term net inflows in nine months, after $85.5B of outflows a year earlier, is a swing of about $149B. The new money is going into the stickiest, best-paid parts of the business: private markets fundraising is running toward $40B, against a $25–30B target, and Canvas has gone from $2B to $30B. Adjusted operating margin rose from 23.7% to 28.0% in a year and is guided to ~29–30% for fiscal 2027 at flat markets. At ~11x forward adjusted earnings, with a 3.9% dividend yield backed by 46 years of increases and a 43% insider stake, the stock is still priced as though the Western years might return. If fees hold near 37 bps and flows stay positive, earnings grow faster than AUM, and the multiple has room to rise.

Bear Case

Most of the rally is market beta. In Q3, $98.0B of AUM growth came from markets and $18.4B from flows, and $4.1B of those flows were reinvested distributions. The stock has risen roughly 79% from its April 2025 low, the monthly RSI has been above 70 for five straight month-ends, and the average target is only 5% higher. The GAAP picture is much weaker than the adjusted one: a 22.9x trailing P/E, a 9.2% GAAP operating margin, an 89.8% payout ratio, and trailing free cash flow of $231.6M that doesn't cover a dividend of ~$670M a year. Evercore rates it Sell. Western still bleeds, and fees are still compressing. If markets fall, a 1.57-beta asset manager falls further.

Top 3 Risks

01
Market drawdown. Fees are charged on market value, and 42% of AUM is equity. A bear market hits AUM, revenue, flows and the margin guide together, and the fiscal-2027 target explicitly assumes flat markets.
02
Flow reversal. Q3 net flows of +$18.4B are the difference between $122.0B in and $103.6B out. A modest drop in gross sales, a bad performance year in a flagship strategy, or fresh Western headlines around the Leech sentencing could flip the sign quickly, as fiscal 2025 showed.

Cash-flow and payout coverage. Trailing free cash flow of $231.6M (S&P Global data) against a ~$670M annual dividend and $348.1M of buybacks in one quarter. Industry Commentary
From public funds to private markets
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Management's own framing in the Q3 release is that "investors are increasingly seeking partners that can deliver integrated solutions across public and private assets." Franklin's numbers show it. Alternatives are 16% of AUM but produced $9.1B of the quarter's $18.4B long-term net flows. More than 30 private-market strategies contributed to fundraising. The wealth channel raised $6.6B for alternatives year to date, and 29% of alternative sales came from outside the U.S. A target-date strategy (Retirement Advantage Plus) is being repositioned to hold 2–8% in private real estate and private credit. The economics are straightforward: locked-up capital with higher fees makes revenue more durable and less exposed to redemptions. The cost is complexity, marks that lag reality, and a much more crowded fundraising market.

Key-person risk in active management
LESSON LEARNED

The Western Asset episode is the industry's standing lesson about star-manager concentration. Per the SEC order, Leech started trading as early as 5:00 a.m. Pacific but routinely didn't enter allocations until after noon Pacific, when the exchange had set settlement prices. Western's own chief compliance officer had warned in August 2021 that delaying allocations created exactly this risk. Once it became public, clients redeemed first and asked questions later: Western lost 40% of its AUM in under two years. The $100M penalty is small next to the fee revenue lost on that money. For Franklin, the diversification built through acquisitions was what kept the firm intact. Excluding Western, fixed income has had ten straight quarters of inflows.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

BEN trades at 22.9x trailing GAAP earnings but ~10.9x forward adjusted earnings, a 1.45x price-to-book and a 10.0x EV/EBITDA (StockAnalysis). The bull framing: GAAP understates earning power because of acquisition amortisation that will run off (it already fell 55% YoY in Q3) and one-off charges tied to Western, and adjusted EPS of $0.72 a quarter annualises to about $2.88, close to the $2.89 FY2026 consensus. The bear framing: acquisitions are how this company grows, so their costs are recurring in substance, and the adjusted figures leave them out. GAAP free cash flow is the check, and on trailing data it covers about a third of the dividend. The resolution is not the multiple. It is whether FY2026 free cash flow rebounds toward the ~$1.21B that S&P Global's consensus shows.
