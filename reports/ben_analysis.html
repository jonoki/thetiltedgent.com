01 Company Overview

For most of its life the company was called Franklin Resources and the funds were called Franklin Templeton. On August 17, 2026 the holding company took the brand's name — Franklin Templeton, Inc. — keeping the ticker BEN, the CUSIP and every shareholder right unchanged. It is a global asset manager: it runs mutual funds, ETFs, separately managed accounts, accounts for institutions and private-market funds, and it is paid a percentage of what it manages. At September 30, 2026, the end of its fiscal year, that was $1,795.1B of AUM, up 8% in a year. Fiscal 2026 revenue was $9.42B, up 7.3%, and GAAP EPS was $1.97, up from $0.91; on the company's adjusted basis it was $3.07, up from $2.22.

An asset manager is a toll booth on other people's money, and the toll depends on two things it doesn't fully control: what markets do to the assets, and whether clients stay. For two years, the second one went badly. In August 2024 its bond unit, Western Asset Management, put its co-chief investment officer Ken Leech on leave; he was indicted in November 2024 on charges of "cherry-picking" winning trades for favoured portfolios. Clients pulled money: by March 2026 Western's AUM was $228.9B, down 40% from June 2024 (Reuters). The stock drifted from $28.11 at the end of March 2024 to an $18.76 month-end low in April 2025. Then the story turned. Western settled with the SEC for $100M on June 5, 2026, which the company says also closed the parallel Department of Justice inquiry, and Leech pleaded guilty to obstruction on June 12. The rest of the firm is bringing in money: $84.1B of long-term net inflows in fiscal 2026, against $97.4B of net outflows in fiscal 2025, even after Western lost another $11.7B. On November 6 the company beat forecasts for its fiscal fourth quarter and raised its long-term goals, and the stock closed at $35.87 on November 9, up 61.4% over 52 weeks. Management now guides to an adjusted operating margin of about 30% in fiscal 2027 and aims for 35% in the quarter ending September 2029, both assuming flat markets. The question is whether a firm that has swapped a bond franchise for private markets, ETFs and custom portfolios can get there with fees drifting lower.

The two operating metrics that actually matter

An asset manager's revenue is roughly average AUM × effective fee rate. AUM grows two ways: markets (which the firm doesn't control) and net flows (which it does). Watch the flows and the fee rate. Everything else follows from them.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$1.80T scale Global distribution (~$525B intl AUM) Private-markets franchise (Lexington) Canvas tax-overlay stickiness Locked-up alternative capital 42% insider ownership 46 years of dividend growth

Asset management has weaker moats than most industries. The product is performance, and past performance really is no guarantee. Franklin's advantages are in distribution and in the kind of money it holds, not in stock-picking. Distribution means relationships with the wealth platforms and intermediaries that decide which funds advisers see, in the U.S. and in the international markets that now hold ~$525B. The money is also changing character. A dollar in a ten-year secondaries fund or in a tax-managed Canvas account is much harder to redeem than a dollar in a bond mutual fund, and the two businesses taking a growing share of Franklin's flows are exactly those. The Western episode showed the flip side. A reputation built by one star manager can walk out of the door in a few months, and $20.8B of good quarterly flows can be undone by one bad headline in a business where clients can redeem daily.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Revenue rides on markets. In the June quarter markets added $98.0B to AUM against $18.4B from flows. The September quarter went the other way: $20.8B of long-term net inflows, but $11.3B lost to market moves, distributions and other changes and $6.0B of cash-management outflows, so AUM rose only $3.5B. With equity at 43% of AUM and a five-year beta of 1.60, a bear market cuts revenue and flows together, while compensation, technology and occupancy (about $1.25B combined in the September quarter) do not fall at the same pace.
02
Western isn't finished. The SEC and DOJ matters for the firm are closed, but Western had $11.7B of long-term net outflows in fiscal 2026. Leech's sentencing on his obstruction plea, first set for September 21, 2026, was still pending in November: on November 5 MLex reported that prosecutors want a prison term above the sentencing guidelines. On October 6 he agreed to a $3M SEC penalty and a bar from serving as an officer or director of a public company, subject to court approval (SEC). Any renewed headline risk falls on a franchise whose AUM fell 40% between June 2024 and March 2026.
03
GAAP earnings and cash flow look much weaker than "adjusted". Q4 GAAP operating margin was 14.6%, against an adjusted 31.8%; for fiscal 2026 the two were 12.5% and 28.0%. The gap is intangible amortisation ($42.0M in Q4), special termination benefits (severance: $81.1M in Q4, $204.5M for the year), the $33.0M impairment and $100.0M regulatory settlement charge booked in Q3, acquisition-related items and consolidated investment products. Trailing free cash flow to June 30, 2026 per S&P Global data is only $231.6M, about 75% below fiscal 2025's $911.6M, against $1.32 per share of dividends (~$677M a year on 512.5M shares); the fiscal-year cash-flow statement comes with the 10-K, not yet filed. StockAnalysis's quarterly cash-flow table shows operating cash flow is lumpy: $1,282M in the June 2025 quarter against $581M in June 2026, and −$255M in the December 2025 quarter.
04
Fee pressure. The adjusted fee rate slipped to 37.5 bps in Q4 from 37.9 in Q3. Growth in ETFs ($27.5B of fiscal 2026 net inflows), cash and large institutional accounts tends to bring in AUM at lower fees than the legacy mutual-fund book. Private markets push the other way, and the net effect is the number to track.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the GAAP-to-adjusted gap is wide. Q4 GAAP operating income was $355.8M against adjusted $598.6M, and GAAP EPS was $0.72 against adjusted $0.94. The adjustments include $42.0M of intangible amortisation, $81.1M of special termination benefits (severance; $204.5M for the year), acquisition-related items and the consolidation of investment products. The release also lists a $100.0M regulatory settlement charge in Q3, the same amount as the Western Asset SEC penalty agreed on June 5. Second, other income of $278.2M in Q4 lifted GAAP pre-tax income; it includes gains on the firm's own investments and consolidated funds, which don't repeat reliably. Third, buybacks: $86.1M in Q4 (2.5M shares), and $1.2B returned through dividends and buybacks over fiscal 2026.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 9, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

Over the five years from the November 2021 month-end close of $32.40 to the November 9, 2026 settled close of $35.87, BEN gained 10.7% on price, against 65.2% for the S&P 500 (SPY) and 72.5% for the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) over the same dates. Adding back the $6.20 per share of dividends that went ex over the period (the sum of twenty quarterly amounts, not reinvested) gives an estimated ~30% total return. The dividend did most of the work. But the endpoints hide the shape: the first three and a half years went down, and all of the gain came in the last nineteen months. The stock peaked at $33.49 in December 2021, fell to $21.52 in September 2022, spent 2023 chopping between $22 and $31, then slid through the Western Asset scandal to $18.76 in April 2025. From there it nearly doubled: $34.15 at the August 2026 month-end, a pullback to $31.64 at the end of October, then $35.87 after the November print, the highest close on the chart.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows the change of regime. Its low was 21.1 in June 2022, and it sat near 30 again in August–September 2024 (30.9 / 30.7) as the Western news broke. It climbed to 80.0 at the August 2026 close, the most overbought reading in the series, cooled to 64.0 and 60.6 at the September and October month-ends, and is back at 74.9 at the November 9 close. The 10-month moving average is about $31.21, roughly 13% below the price, so the long-term trend is clearly up. The short-term warning is the RSI itself: a 13.4% jump from the October month-end in six sessions has pushed it back above 70. Momentum that stretched usually cools, in time or in price.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The legal overhang is gone, and the flows turned before the settlement did. $84.1B of long-term net inflows in fiscal 2026, after $97.4B of outflows in fiscal 2025, is a swing of about $181B. The new money is going into the stickiest, best-paid parts of the business: private-markets fundraising reached $43.2B, well past a full-year target the company had already beaten by June, and Canvas has grown to $32.6B. Adjusted operating margin was 31.8% in Q4, up from 26.0% a year earlier; management guides to about 30% for fiscal 2027 and has raised its goal to 35% for the September 2029 quarter, both at flat markets. At ~11x fiscal 2027 consensus adjusted earnings, with a 3.7% dividend yield backed by 46 years of increases and a 42% insider stake, the stock is still priced as though the Western years might return. If fees hold near 37.5 bps and flows stay positive, earnings grow faster than AUM, and the valuation has room to rise.

Bear Case

A lot is already priced in. The stock has risen 91% from its April 2025 month-end low, the monthly RSI is back at 74.9, and the average target ($36.92) is only 3% above the price. The GAAP picture is much weaker than the adjusted one: an 18.2x trailing P/E, a 12.5% GAAP operating margin for fiscal 2026, a Q4 GAAP profit lifted by $278.2M of other income, and trailing free cash flow (to June) of $231.6M that doesn't cover a dividend of ~$677M a year. Bank of America and Evercore rate it Sell. Western lost another $11.7B in fiscal 2026, the fee rate slipped to 37.5 bps, and in the September quarter market moves, distributions and other changes took $11.3B off AUM. If markets fall, a 1.60-beta asset manager falls further.

Top 3 Risks

01
Market drawdown. Fees are charged on market value, and 43% of AUM is equity. A bear market hits AUM, revenue, flows and the margin guide together, and the fiscal-2027 target explicitly assumes flat markets.
02
Flow reversal. Q4 net flows of +$20.8B are the difference between $114.7B in and $93.9B out. A modest drop in gross sales, a bad performance year in a flagship strategy, or fresh Western headlines around the Leech sentencing could flip the sign quickly, as fiscal 2025 showed.
03
Cash-flow and payout coverage. Trailing free cash flow of $231.6M to June 30, 2026 (S&P Global data) against a ~$677M annual dividend and $1.2B returned through dividends and buybacks in fiscal 2026. The fall from $911.6M in FY2025 comes from lumpy quarterly operating cash flow ($1,282M in the June 2025 quarter against $581M a year later, and −$255M in the December 2025 quarter); the fiscal-year cash-flow statement comes with the 10-K. If the weakness is not timing, the 46-year dividend record depends on the balance sheet (a notes offering was priced in August 2026) more than on cash generation.
Industry Commentary
From public funds to private markets
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

In the Q3 release (July 31) Jenny Johnson said investors increasingly want managers that can combine public and private assets in one solution. Franklin's numbers show it. Alternatives are 17% of AUM but produced $9.5B of the September quarter's $20.8B long-term net flows, and $48.7B of fiscal 2026 fundraising, $43.2B of it in private markets. The economics are straightforward: locked-up capital with higher fees makes revenue more durable and less exposed to redemptions. The cost is complexity, valuations that lag reality, and a much more crowded fundraising market.

Key-person risk in active management
LESSON LEARNED

The Western Asset episode is the industry's standing lesson about star-manager concentration. Per the SEC order, Leech started trading as early as 5:00 a.m. Pacific but routinely didn't enter allocations until after noon Pacific, when the exchange had set settlement prices. Western's own chief compliance officer had warned in August 2021 that delaying allocations created exactly this risk. Once it became public, clients redeemed first and asked questions later: Western lost 40% of its AUM in under two years. The $100M penalty is small next to the fee revenue lost on that money. For Franklin, the diversification built through acquisitions was what kept the firm intact: in fiscal 2026 Western had $11.7B of net outflows, while fixed income as a whole lost only about $0.7B (CEO, November 6 call).

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

BEN trades at 18.2x trailing GAAP earnings but ~11.0x fiscal 2027 consensus adjusted earnings, a 1.55x price-to-book and a 12.5x EV/EBITDA (StockAnalysis). The bull framing: GAAP understates earning power because of acquisition amortisation that is running off (it fell 55% YoY in the June quarter), severance tied to the cost programme and one-off charges tied to Western; fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS was $3.07, and consensus expects $3.27 for fiscal 2027. The bear framing: acquisitions are how this company grows, so their costs are recurring in substance, and the adjusted figures leave them out. GAAP free cash flow is the check, and on trailing data to June it covers about a third of the dividend. The resolution is not the valuation. It is whether the fiscal 2026 cash-flow statement, due with the 10-K, shows the weak June-quarter figures were timing.
