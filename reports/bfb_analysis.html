01 Company Overview

Brown-Forman is the house that Jack Daniel's built — a 156-year-old Louisville distiller whose portfolio runs from Jack Daniel's, Woodford Reserve and Old Forester to el Jimador and Herradura tequila, the New Mix ready-to-drink cocktails, Scotch single malts (The Glendronach, Benriach, Glenglassaugh), Diplomático rum and Gin Mare. It sells in more than 170 countries with about 5,000 employees (FY2026 release). Two share classes trade: Class A (BF.A), where the votes and the family's control sit, and Class B (BF.B), the line in the S&P 500 and the subject of this report. Fiscal 2026 (year to April 30, 2026) net sales were $3.93B, down 1% (flat organic), with diluted EPS of $1.53, down 17%.

This is a story about a family that will not sell at the bottom. The stock closed September 2021 at $67.01; it closed September 21, 2026 at $26.15 — 61% lower — as American whiskey went from growth category to problem category: softer consumer demand, tariffs, and Canadian provinces pulling US spirits from their shelves. Then, in 2026, the table got interesting. On March 26 Brown-Forman and Pernod Ricard confirmed talks on a possible combination; they ended without a deal on April 28. Sazerac, the private Buffalo Trace owner, made a cash offer that Reuters reported at $32 a share, roughly $15B, and reported in May had been rejected; a further unsolicited Sazerac proposal was declared "not actionable" on July 26 after a Brown-family group holding a majority of the Class A shares said it did not align with their vision. In between, on July 13, CEO Lawson Whiting announced his retirement. So the stock sits about 18% below the cash price Reuters reported someone was willing to pay, controlled by holders who have said no twice. The question for a BF.B holder is simple: what is the business worth if nobody is ever allowed to buy it?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Jack Daniel's global brand Aged-whiskey inventory 60%+ gross margin Owned distribution in key markets Family control (no forced sale) 42 years of dividend increases

The moat is time. Whiskey sold today was distilled years ago, which is why Brown-Forman's balance sheet looks the way it does: a current ratio of 3.26 but a quick ratio of 0.93, and inventory that turns about 0.6 times a year (StockAnalysis). A new entrant can't buy that age. Add a brand sold in more than 170 countries and you get a gross margin that has stayed around 59–61% for five years even as sales fell. The weakness is the other side of the same coin: a brand this old is also a brand that younger drinkers think of as their parents' drink, and aged inventory laid down for a growth market becomes a cost when the market stops growing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One family of brands. The first risk the company lists in its release cautionary statements is "Our substantial dependence upon the continued growth of the Jack Daniel's family of brands." In Q1 FY2027 that family was flat, and its flagship whiskey was flat. When the anchor stalls, New Mix and Blackberry have to run just to keep the total level.
02
Trade policy lands on American whiskey first. FY2026 Developed International sales were held back by the absence of American-made spirits from shelves in most Canadian provinces. Tariffs and import rules are an explicit risk factor in every recent release, and American whiskey is an obvious retaliation target in any trade dispute.
03
Control cuts both ways. The family's control stopped a cash offer reported by Reuters at $32 a share. That protects the brands, but it also removes the takeover floor that often supports a de-rated consumer stock. Outside BF.B holders get the dividend and the operating result — nothing else.
04
Leadership gap. The CEO has announced his retirement with no successor named. Strategy is unlikely to change while the search runs, and a new CEO usually "resets" guidance in their first year.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported numbers. First, portfolio exits: the end of the Korbel relationship and the Sonoma-Cutrer sale reduce reported sales, which is why reported growth runs below organic. Second, FY2026 operating income (down 10% to $1.0B) carried higher non-cash impairment charges, $19M of restructuring charges, and SG&A costs linked to the "contemplated business transaction discussions" — the Pernod talks cost money even though they produced no deal. Fourth-quarter operating income fell 53% to $96M. Third, buybacks: $400M of repurchases, completed in December 2025, cut the share count 2.03% year on year and helped Q1 FY2027 EPS rise 6% while operating income fell 3%.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $67.01 to the September 21, 2026 close of $26.15, BF.B lost 61.0% of its price. Dividends paid over the window add up to about $5.28 a share, including a $1.00 special in December 2021; adding them back without reinvestment still leaves a loss of roughly 53% (our estimate). The shape is two acts. From 2021 to mid-2023 the stock went sideways between about $62 and $74 — the five-year high month-end close was $74.22 in July 2022. From August 2023 it fell, with only brief pauses, to $26.91 in June 2025. Since then it has barely moved: every month-end since June 2025 has closed between $25.72 and $29.94, with the five-year low of $25.72 in May 2026.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 15.7 in June 2025, the lowest reading in the series, and spent most of the following year between the mid-20s and mid-40s. It now reads about 40 — neutral to soft. The 10-month moving average is about $26.83, just above the price: after a year of takeover headlines in both directions, the stock is back where it started. The market has given the deal talk no lasting credit.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 16.9x trailing earnings and 15.5x forward for one of the world's best-known spirits brands, with a 60%+ gross margin, 14% ROIC, a 7.6% free-cash-flow yield, a 3.5% dividend raised for 42 straight years, and a capex budget cut to $60–70M. Emerging markets are growing double digits and New Mix is growing faster still. A trade buyer has already put a number on the business — roughly $32 a share as reported by Reuters, 22% above the close — which suggests the private-market value is well above the public one. A new CEO, lower tariffs or a family decision to sell could all close that gap, and the 11% short float adds fuel if one does.

Bear Case

Sales have fallen in each of the last three fiscal years, the flagship whiskey is flat, tequila is down double digits, and guidance calls for another flat year with falling operating income. The takeover floor is not real: the family has said no twice, and the Pernod talks cost money and produced nothing. EPS growth now comes from buybacks and cost cuts rather than demand, and 16.9x is not cheap for that. A leaderless company, a developed-market drinker cutting back, and trade disputes aimed squarely at American whiskey make the four Sell ratings — Morgan Stanley at $23 — look reasonable.

Top 3 Risks

01
Secular demand decline in developed markets. The company itself expects FY2027 to stay "challenging" as macro pressure hits beverage-alcohol consumption, "particularly within developed markets." Developed International fell 8% organically in Q1 FY2027. If this is structural rather than cyclical, flat is the best case.
02
Tariffs and trade retaliation. Canadian provinces pulling US spirits off shelves has already hurt FY2026. Trade policy, tariffs and import rules are an explicit risk factor, and Jack Daniel's is one of the most visible American exports on any retaliation list.
03
Governance and succession. Family control makes a sale unlikely at any price the family dislikes, and the CEO seat is in transition. Class B holders carry the economic risk without the vote.
Industry Commentary
The spirits hangover
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Brown-Forman's own outlook blames "macroeconomic pressures and geopolitical instability" for weaker "consumer behavior and beverage alcohol consumption." A Reuters headline on September 2 (via the StockAnalysis news feed) described the quarter as a sales miss. The evidence inside Brown-Forman's numbers: non-branded and bulk sales, driven by used barrels, down 68% in FY2026, Herradura down 17% in Q1, flagship Jack Daniel's flat. Brown-Forman's sales rose 13.6% in FY2022 and 7.5% in FY2023; the three years since have been the payback.

Consolidation pressure
ONGOING THEME

When a category stops growing, the large players look at merging. In the space of four months Brown-Forman discussed a combination with Pernod Ricard, described in the March 26 announcement coverage as "a partnership akin to a merger of equals" (per Just Drinks) — the two own 15 of the top 100 global spirits brands by volume, per The Brand Champions 2025 report cited by The Spirits Business — and received two approaches from Sazerac. None happened. Deal talk tends to come back when valuations stay low, but at Brown-Forman the family decides. TD Cowen's reading after the July statement, quoted by Just Drinks, was that family control remains "the key obstacle to any change-of-control transaction."

Where growth still exists
EMERGING TAILWIND

The growth is in formats and places, not in the old core: ready-to-drink cans (New Mix +41% in FY2026), flavour extensions (Jack Daniel's Tennessee Blackberry), and emerging markets such as Türkiye, the UAE, Brazil and Mexico. The bull framing: Brown-Forman has the brands and distribution to take that growth, and a cheaper cost base after restructuring. The bear framing: cans and flavours carry less of the aged-whiskey premium that built the 60% gross margin, so the mix shift may keep sales level while profit falls, which is exactly what FY2027 guidance says.
