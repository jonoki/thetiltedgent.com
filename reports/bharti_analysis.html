01 Company Overview

Bharti Airtel is India's mobile-and-broadband heavyweight with a second engine in Africa. At June 30, 2026 it served 681 million customers across 15 countries: 491.9 million in India (376.5 million of them on mobile) and 189.0 million across 14 African markets through Airtel Africa plc, which is listed in London. It also owns the tower company Indus Towers, the data-centre arm Nxtra, a fibre-to-the-home and enterprise business, and Airtel Payments Bank. In the year to March 2026 (FY26) consolidated revenue was ₹2,10,973 crore, up 22.0%, and net income before exceptional items rose 53.1% to ₹26,904 crore. At ₹1,741.10 the company is valued at US$113.3B and is a Nifty 50 constituent.

The investment case rests on one number: ARPU. Indian mobile is effectively a three-player market in which Airtel's average customer paid ₹264 a month in the June 2026 quarter, up from ₹250 a year earlier, in a business that turns 60.8% of its revenue into EBITDA margin. Management's own view is that the industry's tariff structure — cheap plans with unlimited data — caps that number, and that Airtel cannot fix it alone. Meanwhile the stock has had a poor 2026: from ₹2,105.60 at the end of December 2025 to ₹1,741.10 on October 1, a 17.3% fall that ends at the lowest close of the past 52 weeks, even though the June 2026 quarter (reported August 4) grew revenue 18.4% and net income before exceptional items 35.5%. Five years out, the picture is the reverse: +153.0% from the September 2021 close against +27.3% for the Nifty 50.

The latest quarter (Q1 FY27, to June 30, 2026), released after the close on August 4: revenue ₹58,539 crore (+18.4% YoY, +5.7% QoQ); India revenue ₹41,214 crore (+9.7%); EBITDA ₹33,599 crore at a 57.4% margin; net income before exceptional items ₹8,057 crore vs ₹5,948 crore; diluted EPS ₹13.37 vs ₹9.90; net debt to annualised EBITDA down to 1.17x from 1.70x a year earlier; capex ₹13,386 crore. The quarter also closed a share swap that lifted Airtel's effective stake in Airtel Africa from 62.62% to 78.93% (79.11% after Airtel Africa's own buyback). Executive Vice Chairman Gopal Vittal in the release: "We delivered yet another quarter of strong performance, supported by the resilience of our diversified portfolio and sharp execution across businesses." The market's verdict was muted: +0.40% on August 5 (₹1,970.10 → ₹1,978.00, the first session after the release) and −1.52% on August 6, and the stock is down 11.6% from the pre-release close since.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Spectrum + licences in a 3-player market 376.5M India mobile customers ₹264 ARPU, rising on mix 5,42,570 route-km of fibre Indus Towers ownership Airtel Money in Africa BBB+ balance sheet

Telecom moats are built from things that cannot be copied quickly: spectrum bought at auction, licences, towers and fibre in the ground, and a customer base large enough to spread those fixed costs. Airtel has all of them, plus something its domestic rival Vodafone Idea lacks — the cash to keep investing. Consolidated net debt to EBITDA fell to 1.17x in June 2026 from 1.70x a year earlier, and on June 24, 2026 S&P Global upgraded the company to BBB+ (stable) from BBB (positive), citing growth "in high-growth markets of India and Africa" and expecting the company "to prudently manage its balance sheet and operate at lower leverage than before." The weakness is that the moat protects market share, not price: ARPU depends on what the whole industry charges, and Airtel's management says the industry's tariff design has to change before ARPU can grow durably.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
ARPU growth without tariff increases has a ceiling. Management's case is that cheap unlimited-data plans cap ARPU, and Shashwat Sharma said on August 5 that fixing it "needs, like, we cannot do it single handedly." Until the industry moves together, ARPU grows only through mix — postpaid, smartphone upgrades, bigger plans — which is slower than a tariff round.
02
Capital intensity is about to rise again. FY26 consolidated capex was ₹47,522 crore. Vittal told the August 5 call that data centres will bring "a period of rapid capex spend" and that "as a Company, we will not hold back on capex." Data centres, cloud, home fibre and lending all need capital at once.
03
Africa adds growth and currency risk. A 21.1% constant-currency quarter became 45.4% in rupees in Q1 FY27; the same arithmetic works in reverse when African currencies fall against the rupee. After the June share swap, roughly 79% of every African profit or loss now belongs to Airtel shareholders, against about 63% before.
04
Regulation is constant. Since July 2026 the company has disclosed a stream of small Department of Telecommunications and TRAI penalties (₹1.0–7.4 lakh each) over subscriber-verification, service-quality and number-portability rules. The large disputes are about spectrum charges: on June 8, 2026 the Bombay High Court set aside a ₹8,414 crore one-time spectrum charge demand, a win the company disclosed on June 9.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items move the per-share figures. First, the share count grew: Airtel collected the call money on most of the partly paid shares from its 2021 rights issue (₹15,695 crore of share-issue proceeds in the March 2026 quarter's cash flow), then issued 146,761,335 new shares for the Africa swap in June. Basic EPS uses a weighted share count that rose through the year, which is why reported EPS grew more slowly (+30.4% in Q1 on basic EPS) than net income before exceptional items (+35.5%). Second, exceptional items: pre-tax charges of ₹3,161 crore in the March 2026 quarter and ₹353 crore in the June quarter (a provision for settling a commercial dispute at an African subsidiary). After tax and the minority holders' share, the exceptional line added ₹80 crore and ₹110 crore to owners' profit in those quarters (and cut ₹290 crore in the December quarter), so the reported TTM EPS used for the P/E sits close to the figure before exceptional items. Third, the Africa stake increase changes the profit split from here on: minority interests took ₹1,844 crore of Q1 FY27 profit, and the larger stake means more of Africa's profit is now attributed to Airtel shareholders.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 30, 2021 close of ₹688.30 to the October 1, 2026 close of ₹1,741.10, Airtel's price rose 153.0%, against +27.3% for the Nifty 50 (17,618.15 → 22,421.95) over the same dates; dividends are not included in either. The shape has three phases: two flat years between about ₹680 and ₹930 at month-end, a near-straight climb from ₹1,014.70 in November 2023 to ₹1,709.55 in September 2024, and a broad top between ₹1,570 and ₹2,106 since. The highest month-end was ₹2,105.60 in December 2025; the stock has fallen 17.3% since, in a year when the Nifty 50 has fallen 14.2%.

The 6-period monthly RSI reached 95.4 in September 2024, the most overbought reading in the window, and now stands at 37.0 — second only to March 2026's 36.7 as the lowest in five years, though not oversold. The price sits below its 10-month moving average of ₹1,848.02, and has been below it at each of the last three chart points (August, September and October 1): the long-term trend has turned down. Over one year the stock is down 7.3% (₹1,878.40 at end-September 2025), slightly better than the Nifty 50's −8.9%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operator with what it calls India's industry-leading mobile ARPU is growing that ARPU without a tariff increase — ₹250 to ₹264 in a year — while adding a record 1.0 million postpaid customers a quarter and pushing mobile data consumption up 36%. Africa, now 79% owned, is growing above 20% in constant currency and carries an Airtel Money listing that could put a public price on the fintech arm. Leverage is falling (net debt/EBITDA 1.17x from 1.70x), S&P has upgraded the credit to BBB+, the dividend rose 50%, and the business throws off ₹75,901 crore of free cash flow a year before interest and leases. Any industry tariff round would lift ARPU faster than mix can. At ₹1,741.10 the average analyst target sits 33% above the price, and the stock trades at 27.8x a consensus FY27 EPS that expects 31% growth.

Bear Case

At 36.4x trailing earnings the stock is priced for growth that now depends on things Airtel does not control: an industry-wide change to tariff design that management itself says it "cannot do it single handedly", and African currencies. Homes, the growth story of 2025, has slowed (473,000 additions vs 1.1 million the quarter before, ARPU down 2.7%) and loses cash after capex. Capex is about to rise again for data centres, where Airtel has about 12% share by management's estimate. The June share swap issued 146.8 million new shares to buy more of the most currency-exposed part of the group. And the market has spoken: the stock is at its 52-week closing low, below its 10-month average, after its worst month in five years — with an analyst consensus that has not moved.

Top 3 Risks

01
ARPU stalls without a tariff move. The June quarter's ₹264 included an extra day; mix-led growth is slower than a tariff round, and management says the pricing structure can only be fixed by the whole industry. A flat ARPU at 36x earnings is the main way this valuation disappoints.
02
The capex cycle restarts. FY26 capex of ₹47,522 crore was expected to repeat "give or take a little bit" (Vittal, May 14, 2026), but the one-gigawatt data-centre plan, home fibre and cloud all need capital, and management says it "will not hold back on capex." Free cash flow is the casualty if returns on the new bets are slow.
03
Africa currency and execution. More than a quarter of segment revenue and, after the swap, about 79% of its profit now belongs to Airtel shareholders. A 21.1% constant-currency quarter can turn into weak rupee growth if African currencies fall against the rupee.
Industry Commentary
Indian telecom: the "pricing architecture" problem
STRUCTURAL QUESTION

Most Indian mobile plans bundle unlimited data at a flat price. Airtel's management made the same argument on both of its last two earnings calls: the problem is not the entry price but the lack of tiers. Vittal on August 5: "for a very low level of pricing you get unlimited data and that means ARPU is capped." Asked by Goldman Sachs analyst Manish Adukia whether the industry has entered a period of less frequent tariff increases, Sharma answered that "how this plays out in the future is very difficult to comment on, honestly." The consequence for investors is that ARPU growth now comes from moving customers up the plan ladder, which Airtel does well (₹264, which the company calls industry-leading), but which compounds slowly. NSE's Nifty Telecommunications index traded at 14.56x earnings and 7.83x book on October 1 by NSE's own calculation, against Airtel's 36.4x and 6.71x.

Africa: "India nearly 10 years ago"
GROWTH TAILWIND

Vittal's August 5 case for buying more of Airtel Africa rested on penetration: tele-density of 45%, smartphone penetration of 52%, home broadband at 2%, a median age under 18 across Airtel's 14 markets, and 64% of adults without a formal bank account. The business delivers "an annualized EBITDA of over Rs.35,000 Crores," which he called "a very, very rare achievement for an Indian based company." The trade-off is that African earnings come in naira, shillings and kwacha; constant-currency growth of 21.1% in the June quarter was 30.9% in dollars only because African currencies were firmer against the dollar than a year earlier, and 45.4% in rupees because the rupee itself had weakened.

Data centres and AI: the next capex wave
EMERGING OPPORTUNITY

Indian operators are repositioning as infrastructure companies for cloud and AI workloads. Airtel's plan is to go from 120–130 MW of data-centre capacity to one gigawatt "over the next few years," much of it in Mumbai, where Vittal said a very large part of hyperscaler workloads run; an analyst on the call put India's total capacity at about 1.6 GW. Nxtra has raised outside capital for the build (an analyst referred to a $1 billion raise; Soumen Ray: "There was opportunity which presented itself in Nxtra and we have used that opportunity"). The opportunity is real, but data centres are capital-hungry and lower-margin than mobile, and Vittal expects the Airtel Business margin to "trend slightly downwards" as the digital portfolio grows.
