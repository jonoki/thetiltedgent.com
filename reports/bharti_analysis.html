01 Company Overview

Bharti Airtel is India's mobile-and-broadband heavyweight with a second engine in Africa. At September 30, 2026 it served 693 million customers across 15 countries, among them 377.2 million on mobile in India and 193.9 million in 14 African markets through Airtel Africa plc, which is listed in London. Its mobile-money arm, Airtel Money, listed in London in its own right in October 2026. Airtel also owns the tower company Indus Towers, the data-centre arm Nxtra, a fibre-to-the-home and enterprise business, and Airtel Payments Bank. In the year to March 2026 (FY26) consolidated revenue was ₹2,10,973 crore. At ₹2,106.00 the company is valued at US$139.8B and is a Nifty 50 constituent.

The investment case rests on one number: ARPU. Indian mobile is effectively a three-player market in which Airtel's average customer paid ₹271 a month in the September 2026 quarter, up from ₹264 in June and ₹256 a year earlier, in a mobile business with a 61.0% EBITDA margin. Part of the rise came from Airtel's own price changes in August, which withdrew the ₹299 entry-level prepaid plan among others; Citi, as reported by Business Today on November 6, tied them to higher customer churn, and Airtel has since restored a ₹299 plan with 1 GB a day instead of 1.5 GB. Management's view is unchanged: cheap plans with unlimited data cap ARPU, and fixing that takes the whole industry. The stock's 2026 has been a round trip: from ₹2,105.60 at the end of December 2025 to ₹1,741.10 on October 1, the lowest close in 52 weeks, and back to ₹2,106.00 on November 13, 21.0% above the previous edition. Five years out: +207.3% from the October 29, 2021 close against +42.3% for the Nifty 50.

The latest quarter (Q2 FY27, to September 30, 2026), approved by the board and released on November 5: revenue ₹60,878 crore (+16.7% YoY, +4.0% QoQ); India revenue ₹42,308 crore (+9.4%); EBITDA ₹35,240 crore at a 57.9% margin; net income before exceptional items ₹9,015 crore vs ₹6,792 crore (+32.7%) and, after a ₹180 crore pre-tax charge for the costs of the Airtel Money listing, ₹8,888 crore of profit attributable to owners; diluted EPS ₹14.24 vs ₹11.30; net debt to annualised EBITDA 1.18x vs 1.63x a year earlier; capex ₹12,467 crore, plus ₹2,826 crore for a land parcel for data centres. Executive Vice Chairman Gopal Vittal in the release: "We delivered another quarter of consistent performance with consolidated revenues of Rs 60,878 crore, increasing 4.0% QoQ". Shashwat Sharma said on the November 6 call that the ₹271 ARPU "benefited by one extra day in the quarter as well". The market's verdict: +2.45% on November 6 (₹2,056.90 → ₹2,107.20, the first session after the release) and −1.67% on November 9 (₹2,072.10); the stock closed November 13 at ₹2,106.00, 2.4% above the pre-release close.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Spectrum + licences in a 3-player market 377.2M India mobile customers ₹271 ARPU, up ₹15 in a year 5,52,373 route-km of fibre Indus Towers ownership Airtel Money, listed in London BBB+ balance sheet

Telecom moats are built from things that cannot be copied quickly: spectrum bought at auction, licences, towers and fibre in the ground, and a customer base large enough to spread those fixed costs. Airtel has all of them, plus something its domestic rival Vodafone Idea lacks — the cash to keep investing. Consolidated net debt to EBITDA fell to 1.18x in September 2026 from 1.63x a year earlier, and on June 24, 2026 S&P Global upgraded the company to BBB+ (stable) from BBB (positive), citing growth "in high-growth markets of India and Africa" and expecting the company "to prudently manage its balance sheet and operate at lower leverage than before." The weakness is that the moat protects market share, not price: ARPU depends on what the whole industry charges, and Airtel's management says the industry's tariff design has to change before ARPU can grow durably — the August price changes show how a step up in price costs customers.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
ARPU growth without industry tariff increases has a ceiling, and Airtel's own price step cost customers. Sharma said on November 6 that the ₹299 intervention was "more of experimentation on our own base" and that "the architecture correction is a much broader correction that is required in the industry to charge for data in many ways." After Airtel withdrew the ₹299 plan in August, revenue-earning customers rose only 0.7 million and churn went up; by November 6 Airtel had restored a ₹299 plan with 1 GB a day (Business Today). Until the industry moves together, ARPU grows through mix and occasional price steps, which is slower than a tariff round.
02
Capital intensity keeps rising. FY26 consolidated capex was ₹47,522 crore. In the September quarter capex was ₹12,467 crore plus ₹2,826 crore for the data-centre land parcel. Ray said on November 6 that the core business continues "as per guidance" but "we will not shy away from investing in the new businesses to drive growth", naming data centres and cloud; Nxtra's one-gigawatt plan, home fibre and lending all need capital at once.
03
Africa adds growth and currency risk. A 20.6% constant-currency quarter became 37.6% in rupees in Q2 FY27 because the rupee weakened (an average ₹95.49 per dollar against ₹87.28 a year earlier, RBI reference rates in the quarterly report); the same arithmetic works in reverse when African currencies fall against the rupee. After the buyback, about 79.4% of Airtel Africa's profit or loss belongs to Airtel shareholders.
04
Regulation is constant. The Q2 quarterly report lists new rules in the quarter: biometric subscriber verification (August 21, 2026), and a September 21, 2026 amendment to TRAI's consumer-protection rules requiring operators to offer voice-and-SMS-only plans at a lower price, to be in place within 30 days. The large disputes are about spectrum charges: on June 8, 2026 the Bombay High Court set aside a ₹8,414 crore one-time spectrum charge demand, a win the company disclosed on June 9.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items move the per-share figures. First, the share count grew: Airtel collected the call money on most of the partly paid shares from its 2021 rights issue, then issued 146,761,335 new shares for the Africa swap in June. Basic EPS uses a weighted share count that rose through the year, which is why reported EPS grew 21.6% on a basic basis (₹14.25 vs ₹11.72) and 26.0% on a diluted basis (₹14.24 vs ₹11.30), while profit attributable to owners grew 30.9% and net income before exceptional items 32.7%. Second, exceptional items: a pre-tax charge of ₹180 crore in the September quarter for the costs of the Airtel Money listing (a ₹7 crore tax benefit and ₹46 crore borne by minority holders), ₹353 crore in the June quarter and ₹3,161 crore in the March 2026 quarter. After tax and the minority holders' share, the September-quarter charge cut ₹128 crore from owners' profit. Third, the Africa stake increase changes the profit split: minority interests took ₹1,823 crore of Q2 FY27 profit, and with Airtel's effective stake in Airtel Africa at 79.44% after the buyback, more of Africa's profit is attributed to Airtel shareholders.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operator with what it calls India's industry-leading mobile ARPU lifted it from ₹256 to ₹271 in a year, partly through its own August price changes, while adding a record 1.2 million postpaid customers in a quarter and pushing mobile data traffic up 36.5%. Africa, now about 79% owned, grew 20.6% in constant currency, and Airtel Money's London listing put a market price on the fintech arm. Leverage is falling (net debt/EBITDA 1.18x from 1.63x), S&P has upgraded the credit to BBB+, and the business throws off ₹75,429 crore of free cash flow a year before interest and leases. Any industry tariff round would lift ARPU faster than mix can. At ₹2,106.00 the average analyst target sits 12.5% above the price, and the stock trades at 33.7x a consensus FY27 EPS that expects 23% growth.

Bear Case

At 41.5x trailing earnings the stock is priced for growth that depends on things Airtel does not control: an industry-wide change to tariff design that management itself says it cannot do alone, and African currencies. Airtel's own price step lifted ARPU but customer additions fell to 0.7 million and churn rose. Homes, the growth story of 2025, needed fixed wireless sales scaled back, and its EBIT margin has fallen to 6.7% from 16.2% a year ago. Capex keeps rising for data centres, with ₹2,826 crore spent on land in one quarter. And the shares have rallied 21% in six weeks, so the average target now leaves 12.5% upside rather than 33%, with the stock 2.6% below its November 2025 closing high.

Top 3 Risks

01
ARPU stalls without a tariff move. The September quarter's ₹271 included an extra day and a one-time price step; mix-led growth is slower than a tariff round, and management says the pricing structure can only be fixed by the whole industry. A flat ARPU at 41x earnings is the main way this valuation disappoints.
02

03
Africa currency and execution. More than a quarter of segment revenue and, after the buyback, about 79% of its profit now belongs to Airtel shareholders. A 20.6% constant-currency quarter can turn into weak rupee growth if African currencies fall against the rupee.
Industry Commentary
Indian telecom: the "pricing architecture" problem
STRUCTURAL QUESTION

Most Indian mobile plans bundle unlimited data at a flat price. Airtel's management made the same argument on its last three earnings calls: the problem is not the entry price but the lack of tiers. On November 6 Sharma said the industry's structure is "very flat" and that "you are giving away unlimited usage and data, and you are not monetizing consumption in any way"; in his words the ₹299 step was an experiment on Airtel's own base, and the correction "is a much broader correction that is required in the industry". Asked by JP Morgan how Airtel would respond if rivals raised tariffs, he said he does not see a constraint in the headroom for tariff growth. The consequence for investors is that ARPU growth now comes from moving customers up the plan ladder and from occasional price steps, which compounds slowly and, as August showed, can cost customers. NSE's Nifty Telecommunications index traded at 14.20x earnings and 8.02x book on November 13 by NSE's own calculation, against Airtel's 41.5x and 8.32x.

Africa: a listed fintech and a currency question
GROWTH TAILWIND

Africa is about 30.5% of group revenue on management's own basis (Ray, November 6). In October Airtel Money listed in London at £1.96 a share, about £5.3 billion at the offer price; Ray told the call it was valued at about $7 billion and "reported as one of London's largest IPOs". Airtel Africa kept its 77.85% stake, so Airtel shareholders now own a large part of a business with a daily market price. The trade-off is that African earnings come in naira, shillings and kwacha: constant-currency growth of 20.6% in the September quarter was 25.7% in dollars as reported and 37.6% in rupees, because the rupee itself weakened (an average ₹95.49 per dollar against ₹87.28 a year earlier, RBI reference rates).

Data centres and AI: the next capex wave
EMERGING OPPORTUNITY

Indian operators are repositioning as infrastructure companies for cloud and AI workloads. Airtel's plan is to build from 200–250 MW of data-centre capacity (Ray's figure on the November 6 call) to one gigawatt "over the next few years"; in the September quarter Nxtra spent ₹2,826 crore on a land parcel that Vittal said could hold at least 400–500 MW. Ray said the group had just done about $800 million of equity raising, in June and July, and that debt will fund part of the build-out; asked how much of the capacity serves Airtel itself and how much outside customers, he answered "Nothing is external capacity" and "We have not leased capacity to sell it to somebody else." On renting out AI chips, Vittal separated bare-metal leasing (renting out the chips themselves), which he called a commoditised "capex to opex game", from software-led GPU services, which Airtel is studying. The opportunity is real, but data centres are capital-hungry and lower-margin than mobile.
