01 Company Overview

BHP is Australia's largest company and, by its own FY2026 20-F, the world's largest copper producer. It runs three businesses — Copper (Escondida and Pampa Norte in Chile, Copper South Australia, a non-operated share of Antamina in Peru), Iron Ore (Western Australia Iron Ore, "WAIO", in the Pilbara) and Coal (the BHP Mitsubishi Alliance steelmaking-coal mines in Queensland and New South Wales Energy Coal) — plus the Jansen potash project under construction in Canada. It is the largest company in Australia by market capitalisation (#1, country ranking via companiesmarketcap) and a heavyweight of the ASX 200, and ranks #86 globally. On the NYSE it trades as an ADR: one ADR equals two ASX ordinary shares.

The latest reported period is the full fiscal year FY2026 (July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026), published August 18, 2026 (BHP reports half-yearly; there is no quarterly P&L). Revenue was US$58.76B, up 14.6%; Underlying EBITDA was US$32.95B (+26.8%); Underlying attributable profit was US$13.20B (+30.0%); statutory attributable profit was US$9.83B (+9.0%) after US$3.37B of exceptional charges.

The premise this report was asked to test — "iron ore as a proxy for Chinese construction" — is out of date on BHP's own numbers. In FY2026, for the first time, Copper contributed more than half of Underlying EBITDA (US$18.93B of segment EBITDA versus US$14.53B from Iron Ore). China still bought 58.2% of revenue (US$34.21B), but that is down from 62.6% in FY2025, and much of it is now copper concentrate and cathode rather than ore for steel. BHP's own view, stated in the 20-F, is that "China's real steel production will plateau around the 1 Bt level for the rest of the decade", with scrap displacing pig iron over time. So the stock is now two bets: a cost-curve bet on iron ore (WAIO unit cost US$19.66/t, the lever that keeps a flat-demand business highly profitable) and a volume-and-price bet on copper (realised US$5.74/lb, +35%). China construction still matters to the first; it matters much less to the second.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Sub-US$20/t WAIO unit cost Escondida — world's largest copper mine Integrated Pilbara rail & port Long-life, high-grade resources Balance sheet (net debt $8.7B) Copper-SA gold by-product credits Scale in project execution

In commodities the only lasting moat is position on the cost curve, and BHP holds bottom-of-curve positions in its two biggest businesses. WAIO turns ore into US$14.7B of EBITDA at under US$20/t, through a closed system of five mines, four processing hubs and more than 1,000 km of rail that it owns. A high-cost producer is squeezed out first when the price falls; WAIO is the last to be squeezed. Escondida at US$1.07/lb earns a 73% EBITDA margin at current copper prices. BHP says its group EBITDA margin has averaged more than 50% for over 25 years. The weakness is that the moat protects margins, not volumes or prices: FY2027 guidance has copper volume falling and every major asset's unit cost rising at once.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
FY2027 is a volume air-pocket in copper. Group copper guidance of 1,650–1,800 kt is 8–16% below FY2026's 1,953 kt, driven by Escondida grade decline (to 1,000–1,100 kt from 1,261 kt). The pipeline that makes up for it — Escondida New Concentrator, Vicuña, Resolution, the Spence projects — mostly comes after FY2028. For the next two years, copper earnings depend on the copper price, not on growth.
02
Costs are rising at every major asset at once. WAIO guide US$20.25–21.75/t (vs 19.66), Escondida US$1.20–1.50/lb (vs 1.07), Spence US$2.40–2.70/lb (vs 2.15). Part of this is grade and diesel (the Singapore gasoil sensitivity is explicit), part is AUD strength — the WAIO guide assumes AUD/USD 0.70 versus 0.68 realised in FY2026.
03
Capital allocation record on new commodities. The US$2.3B Jansen impairment came before a tonne of potash was sold. Samarco keeps absorbing cash — US$2.0B of settlement payments in FY2026 and a further US$1.1B exceptional charge. Western Australia Nickel is in temporary suspension.
04
China is still 58% of revenue. The premise is weaker than it was, but not gone: US$34.21B of FY2026 revenue came from Chinese customers, and every US$1/t move in iron ore changes Underlying EBITDA by US$230M. A US$10/t fall is roughly US$2.3B, about 7% of group Underlying EBITDA.

Notable One-Off Items

FY2026 exceptional items totalled US$3.37B after tax (66.4¢ per ordinary share): a US$2.3B impairment of the Jansen potash project and US$1.1B for Samarco dam-failure impacts. FY2025's exceptional items were US$1.14B (Samarco US$0.9B, WA Nickel suspension US$0.2B). This is why statutory profit grew 9% while underlying profit grew 30%. Two other items matter for the cash flow: US$4.3B of streaming proceeds (cash received up front for future metal deliveries), which flattered the fall in net debt, and US$2.0B of Samarco settlement payments, which will continue. In the price chart, the June 2022 Woodside distribution — an in-specie dividend worth 386.4 US cents per ordinary share (about $7.73 per ADR) when BHP merged its petroleum arm into Woodside (completed June 1, 2022) — shows up as a price fall in the unadjusted series.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

BHP is now mainly a copper company with a low-cost iron ore business attached, and it has the balance sheet to fund growth. Copper made more than half of FY2026 Underlying EBITDA at a 73% margin at Escondida, and the pipeline — Escondida New Concentrator, Vicuña, Resolution, two sanctioned Spence projects — supports ~5% a year copper growth to FY2035, into a market BHP expects to grow from ~34 Mtpa to over 50 Mtpa by 2050 on electrification and AI data centres. WAIO at under US$20/t keeps earning cash even if Chinese steel demand plateaus. Net debt is down to US$8.7B, underlying ROCE is 26.1%, and the dividend yields 4.0% at a 66% payout. On underlying earnings the ADR trades at 16.6x.

Bear Case

The stock is already pricing in the copper story; the operations are about to go the other way. FY2027 guidance cuts copper volume to 1,650–1,800 kt (from 1,953 kt) and raises unit costs at every major asset — WAIO to US$20.25–21.75/t, Escondida to US$1.20–1.50/lb. Consensus EPS for FY2027 is down 1%, the average target of $76.25 is 11% below the price, and only one of eight US-line analysts rates it a buy. The FY2026 earnings jump came from a 35% copper price rise that BHP does not control. Meanwhile the capital record on new ventures is poor — a US$2.3B Jansen impairment before first production, and more years of Samarco cash outflows — and 58% of revenue still depends on Chinese customers.

Top 3 Risks

01
Copper price reversal during a volume trough. At US$39M of Underlying EBITDA per 1¢/lb, a US$1/lb fall in copper would remove about US$3.9B — while FY2027 volume is also falling. The two effects compound.
02
Iron ore unit-cost creep meets a demand plateau. WAIO costs are guided up ~7% at the midpoint, and BHP itself expects Chinese steel output to flatline around 1 Bt. Flat demand plus rising costs narrows the per-tonne margin from both sides; each US$1/t on price is worth US$230M of EBITDA.
03
Project execution and legacy liabilities. Jansen (US$2.3B already impaired, first production mid-CY2027), Samarco settlement payments (US$2.0B in FY2026), and a new CEO's first big capital decisions (Vicuña FID, Escondida New Concentrator) all land in the next 24 months.
Industry Commentary
Is iron ore still a proxy for Chinese construction?
PREMISE CHECKED — PARTLY OUTDATED

For BHP's earnings, less and less. Iron ore produced 40.6% of FY2026 revenue and about 44% of segment Underlying EBITDA, down from the majority it once was, while Chinese customers' share of revenue fell from 62.6% to 58.2% in one year. BHP's own outlook in the 20-F does not model a construction rebound. It expects Chinese steel output to plateau around 1 Bt, scrap to push pig iron down, and seaborne demand to shift toward emerging markets such as India (12 Mt of imports in CY2025). In that world the investment question for iron ore is not "will China build?" but "who is lowest on the cost curve when demand stops growing?" WAIO at US$19.66/t — guided to US$20.25–21.75/t — is BHP's answer, and its 61.8% EBITDA margin is what the market should watch, not Chinese housing starts.

Copper: demand growth against a thin supply pipeline
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

BHP's stated view: global copper demand rises from ~34 Mtpa to over 50 Mtpa by 2050 through ordinary economic growth, the energy transition and AI-driven data-centre build-out, while ageing mines and a weaker project pipeline than in past cycles create a "looming global copper supply challenge." FY2026 showed what that means for earnings: a 35% rise in realised copper price lifted segment EBITDA 48% even though BHP's copper output fell 3%. The same logic makes the stock sensitive to the copper price. The 20-F lists the peers BHP benchmarks against: Anglo American, Antofagasta, Codelco, Freeport, Glencore, Rio Tinto, Southern Copper and Teck.

Costs: diesel, currency and grade
ONGOING HEADWIND

Three forces drive unit costs up across the sector, and all three appear in BHP's FY2027 guidance. Diesel: WAIO's guide carries an explicit Singapore gasoil sensitivity, and BHP said in September 2026 that its emissions and diesel use will stay higher for longer. Currency: guidance assumes AUD/USD 0.70 and USD/CLP 890, a stronger Australian dollar and Chilean peso than FY2026's 0.68 and 920 — costs rise in US-dollar terms without any real change on site. Grade: Escondida's concentrator feed grade fell to 0.90% from 1.02%, and lower grade means more rock moved per pound of copper. The operator with the most control over these is the one with the best ore bodies, which is BHP's real edge — but guidance shows even the best ore bodies cost more to mine each year.
