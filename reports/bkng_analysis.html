01 Company Overview

Booking Holdings is the largest online travel agency (OTA) group in the world, operating Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK, OpenTable and Rentalcars.com. The company is an asset-light marketplace: it takes a commission on accommodation, flight, car-rental and restaurant transactions rather than owning inventory, which produces an gross margin of 87.2% and FCF of $9.54B on $28.24B of trailing revenue.

Fiscal 2025 revenue was $26.92B, up 13.4% YoY, with operating income of $9.49B (35.2% operating margin). Trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 30, 2026 is $28.24B (+12.9%) and TTM net income $7.21B (+49.8%). At $153.85B of market value the company ranks roughly #107 among US-listed companies and #143 globally. In April 2026 Booking executed its first-ever forward stock split — 25-for-1, effective April 6 — cutting a share price that had peaked above $5,800 down to the low-$200s.

The engine of the group and the largest accommodation marketplace on earth, with particular density in Europe where fragmented, independent and boutique properties have no realistic direct-booking alternative. Booking's moat is supply density compounded by conversion efficiency. Every incremental independent property listed on Booking.com makes the site marginally more useful to a traveler, and every incremental traveler makes the listing marginally more valuable to the property — a textbook two-sided network, but the strength of it varies enormously by geography. In Europe, where the lodging base is fragmented across small independents with no loyalty program and no meaningful direct channel, the network is close to unassailable and take rates hold. In North America, where Marriott and Hilton concentrate supply behind loyalty walls, the same network is materially weaker. Layered on top is a second, less obvious advantage: Booking is one of the largest buyers of performance advertising on the planet, and its conversion data lets it bid more accurately per click than sub-scale rivals — so the marketing channel that looks like a cost is actually a barrier to entry. The merchant-model transition adds a third layer, converting Booking from a referral business into a payments business that holds the customer's card, the itinerary and the cash float. The financial signature of all this is an ROIC of 92.45% and $9.54B of trailing FCF on essentially no owned hard assets.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. AI agents as a new front door. This is the central bear argument and the reason the multiple compressed in 2026. If travellers begin their journey inside a general-purpose LLM assistant rather than a search box, the discovery layer that Booking currently rents from Google could be replaced by an agent that Booking neither owns nor advertises on. KAYAK is the most directly exposed asset; Booking.com is exposed through its customer-acquisition cost. Management's counter — that AI can plan the trip while Booking captures the transaction, and that it has been "deploying AI at scale for more than a decade" — is plausible but so far unquantified in disclosure. There is currently no reported metric that lets an investor measure agent-sourced traffic, which is itself a risk.

2. European regulatory concentration. Europe is simultaneously Booking's profit pool and its regulatory risk. The company operates under the EU's Digital Markets Act gatekeeper regime and has faced national competition and tax proceedings in multiple European jurisdictions. Remedies that weaken rate parity, mandate choice screens, or restrict data reuse hit exactly the geography where economics are best. Specific current DMA obligations, fine amounts and the status of Dutch and Italian tax matters were not verifiable from live sources in this snapshot — confirm in the latest 10-K/10-Q before relying on them.

3. Decelerating unit growth. Q2 2026 room nights grew just +5% against gross bookings +9% and revenue +8.1%. The gap between units and dollars means price and mix — and a weaker dollar — are doing the work. Q3 2026 revenue guidance of $9.4–9.5B came in below the ~$9.7B consensus, and the stock's derating this year is largely a room-night-growth story rather than a margin story. Travel is cyclical and post-pandemic normalization removes the easy comparisons.

4. Capital structure distortion. Relentless buybacks have driven stockholders' equity to −$10.78B and book value per share to −$14.23. That is a deliberate choice, not distress — the company has $17.21B of cash against $20.92B of debt and generates $9.54B of FCF — but it renders ROE, D/E and price-to-book uncomputable, removes balance-sheet cushion against a genuine travel shock, and makes screens and factor models treat the stock erratically.

Notable One-Off Items

A large below-the-line charge distorted FY2025. Operating income rose 25.0% in FY2025 to $9.49B, yet GAAP net income fell 8.1% to $5.40B and EPS fell 4.1% to $6.62. That is roughly a $1.9B swing occurring below the operating line — far too large to be interest expense. FY2025 net margin therefore collapsed to 20.1% from 24.8% in FY2024, and the subsequent TTM "recovery" to 25.5% margin and +56.1% EPS growth is substantially an artifact of lapping that charge rather than a genuine step-change in profitability. The specific driver is not verifiable from the live sources used here — the FY2025 10-K tax footnote and legal-proceedings note are the place to confirm it. Anyone underwriting the +56% TTM EPS growth figure at face value is likely double-counting.

Buyback-driven negative equity. Stockholders' equity of −$10.78B and book value per share of −$14.23 are the accumulated result of repurchasing far more stock, at far higher prices, than retained earnings support. Buyback yield is 4.16% and shares outstanding fell 4.16% over the past year; total shareholder yield including the dividend is 4.98%. This is the single most important reconciliation item on the page: ROE, D/E and P/B are all mechanically meaningless for BKNG, and any screen or peer comparison that uses them will misrank the stock in both directions.

Elevated 2026 investment spend. Management committed roughly $700M above baseline in 2026 for generative AI, the Connected Trip platform, market expansion and advertising — a deliberate margin drag in the current year that partially offsets the ~$250M of Transformation Program savings that lifted adjusted EBITDA margin to ~36.9%.

Currency and geopolitics. With the majority of gross bookings originating outside the United States, reported growth carries a meaningful FX translation component — the 4-point gap between Q2 gross bookings (+9%) and room nights (+5%) is partly price, partly mix, partly currency. Management explicitly cited Middle East "volatility" as a demand headwind in the Q2 2026 report.

Regulatory and tax matters — flagged, not quantified. Booking has historically carried European competition, Digital Markets Act compliance and multi-jurisdiction tax exposures (including long-running Dutch and Italian matters). Current amounts, accruals and case status could not be verified from live sources in this snapshot and are deliberately omitted rather than estimated.

Five-year trajectory. On a split-adjusted basis BKNG went from roughly $92 in September 2021 to $204.76 on August 17, 2026 — a total price return of about +123% over the five years, or roughly 17% annualized before dividends. TradingView's verified five-year reading taken on July 7, 2026 was +104.45%. The path was anything but smooth: a 2022 drawdown to roughly $65 as rate shock and recession fear hit travel, a violent 2023–2024 recovery as international travel normalized and margins expanded, an all-time high of $233.58 on July 8, 2025, and then a 2026 derating that carried the stock to a 52-week low of $150.14 before the August recovery to $204.76. The stock remains 11.7% below its 52-week high and 12.3% below its all-time high.

What changed in 2026. The 2026 decline was a multiple event, not an earnings event. TTM revenue still grew 12.9% and TTM free cash flow is $9.54B, yet the forward multiple compressed to 18.4x from levels in the high 20s. Two forces did the work: room-night growth decelerating to +5% in Q2, and the market's growing conviction that generative-AI assistants could take the discovery layer of travel. The April 25-for-1 split was cosmetic and did nothing to arrest the derating — the stock fell from roughly $186 in March to a low near $150 in April/May before the Q2 beat turned sentiment.

Relative performance. Against the travel peer set BKNG has been the quality trade: it carries a 25.5% net margin versus Airbnb's 20.5% and Expedia's 13.0%, and trades at 22.6x trailing earnings versus Hilton at 47.3x and Marriott at 37.3x. Its $153.85B market cap exceeds Airbnb ($91.4B) and Expedia ($37.6B) combined. A precise BKNG-versus-S&P-500 and BKNG-versus-NASDAQ-100 total-return comparison could not be sourced live in this snapshot and is deliberately omitted rather than estimated — but the direction is clear enough: a −15% year-to-date move in 2026 against a broadly higher large-cap index means the stock has given back a meaningful chunk of relative performance.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the world's dominant travel marketplace — 87.2% gross margin, 25.5% net margin, 92.45% ROIC and $9.54B of free cash flow — at 18.4x forward earnings and a 1.15 PEG, cheaper than both its travel peers and the NASDAQ-100. Europe's fragmented independent-lodging base is a supply moat no chain and no agent can replicate quickly, the merchant-payments transition converts Booking from a referral business into one that owns the transaction, and a 4.98% total shareholder yield retires 5–6% of the float per year. Thirty of 38 analysts rate it Buy with an average target of $237.81 (+16.1%). If room-night growth stabilizes anywhere near mid-single digits, the multiple has more room to re-rate than to compress.

Bear Case

Room nights grew just 5% in Q2 2026 and Q3 revenue was guided to $9.4–9.5B against $9.7B consensus — the deceleration is real and predates any proof that AI agents are the cause. Booking's economics depend on renting the discovery layer from Google; if that layer migrates to LLM assistants the company does not control, customer-acquisition cost rises structurally and the take rate is squeezed from both ends. Negative $10.78B of stockholders' equity means there is no balance-sheet cushion for a genuine travel shock, European regulation targets the exact geography where margins are best, and the +56% TTM EPS growth headline is largely lapping a $1.9B FY2025 below-the-line charge rather than genuine acceleration.

Top 3 Risks
01
Disintermediation by AI agents. If generative-AI assistants become the default entry point for trip planning, Booking loses control of demand acquisition and either pays more for traffic or accepts lower conversion. There is currently no disclosed metric that lets investors track this, which means the market will keep discounting the possibility until the company quantifies it. KAYAK is the first casualty; Booking.com's marketing efficiency is the second.
02
Sustained room-night deceleration. +5% unit growth against +9% gross bookings means dollars are outrunning units. If price, mix and FX stop cooperating, revenue growth converges toward the unit rate and the 18.4x forward multiple stops looking cheap. Q3 guidance already points that way, and travel demand is genuinely cyclical — a consumer slowdown or renewed geopolitical disruption (management flagged Middle East volatility) compounds it.
03
European regulatory and tax exposure. Booking earns its best economics in the market with the most aggressive platform regulation. Digital Markets Act obligations, rate-parity challenges and multi-jurisdiction tax proceedings can each impair the European take rate. Because the company's equity base is negative, there is limited accounting cushion to absorb a large one-time settlement without further distorting the balance sheet.
  Industry Commentary
AI agents and the travel discovery layer
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the debate setting BKNG's multiple, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. Online travel has always been a two-stage business: discovery (where do I go, what's available, what does it cost) and transaction (book it, pay for it, service it). Booking's economics historically came from owning stage two while renting stage one from Google. A capable LLM assistant that plans an itinerary end-to-end attacks stage one directly. Booking's response has three parts: roughly $700M of above-baseline 2026 spend on generative AI and the Connected Trip; the argument that it has been "deploying AI at scale for more than a decade"; and the structural bet that whoever settles the payment, holds the cancellation policy and answers the 2 a.m. customer-service call owns the customer. That last point is not trivial — Connected Trip transactions grew in the high-20% range and are deliberately hard to unbundle. But the counter-evidence is equally concrete: Q2 room nights grew only +5%, and the forward multiple has compressed to 18.4x from the high 20s. Until Booking discloses agent-sourced traffic or customer-acquisition cost trends, this stays unresolved and the discount persists.
Global travel demand normalization
ONGOING HEADWIND
The post-pandemic revenge-travel surge is fully lapped. Booking's revenue growth has stepped down cleanly each year — +56.0% (FY22), +25.0% (FY23), +11.1% (FY24), +13.4% (FY25) — and Q2 2026 came in at just +8.1%, with Q3 guided to $9.4–9.5B against ~$9.7B consensus. Room nights at +5% against gross bookings at +9% tell you the composition: price, mix and currency are carrying growth while unit volumes normalize toward GDP-plus. Management flagged Middle East volatility as a specific Q2 demand drag. This is not a thesis-breaking dynamic — a marketplace compounding units at mid-single digits with a 4.98% shareholder yield and mid-teens EPS growth guidance is a perfectly good business — but it removes the easy comparisons that supported a 30x multiple and it makes every incremental quarter a referendum on whether deceleration is cyclical or structural.
European regulation vs. the merchant-payments build — bull and bear framed
EMERGING TAILWIND
Europe is the hinge. It is where Booking's supply moat is strongest, where its take rates are best, and where platform regulation is most aggressive — Digital Markets Act obligations, rate-parity challenges and national tax proceedings all concentrate in the same geography that generates the profit. Specific current DMA remedies, fine amounts and the status of Dutch and Italian tax matters were not verifiable from live sources in this snapshot and are flagged rather than quantified. The bear read: forced choice screens, parity bans and data-portability remedies erode the European take rate at exactly the moment unit growth is decelerating and AI is attacking demand acquisition — a two-front war on the profit pool, with no equity cushion (−$10.78B) to absorb a settlement. The bull read: regulation is a fixed-cost barrier that a $154B platform absorbs far more easily than a sub-scale rival, while the merchant-model transition and the Agoda–Checkout.com payments buildout convert Booking from a commission collector into the entity that holds the card, the itinerary and the float. If that lands, the company owns the part of the value chain regulators are least able to unbundle and agents are least able to replicate — and 18.4x forward earnings for a 92% ROIC business will look like the mistake of the cycle.
