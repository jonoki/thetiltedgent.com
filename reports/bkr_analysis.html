01 Company Overview

Baker Hughes is one of the "big three" oilfield-services companies alongside SLB and Halliburton, except that well under half of it is now oilfield services. Since buying Chart Industries in July 2026 it reports three segments: Oilfield Services & Equipment (OFSE), the drilling, completions, artificial-lift and subsea business that rises and falls with oil companies' spending; Industrial & Energy Technology (IET), which builds the gas turbines, compressors and liquefaction equipment behind LNG (liquefied natural gas) plants and, increasingly, behind gas-fired power for data centers; and Chart, the cryogenic tanks, heat exchangers and gas-handling equipment it just bought. FY2025 revenue was $27.7B, down 0.3% from $27.8B in FY2024, but that flat headline hid the swap underneath: IET revenue grew +10% to $13.4B while OFSE fell −8% to $14.3B. In Q3 2026 OFSE was 46% of revenue, IET 43% and Chart 11%. Market cap: $54.13B at the October 29, 2026 close.

The debate on this page is a valuation identity crisis. In the twelve months to October 29, 2026 BKR rose 12.8%, in line with the S&P 500 fund SPY (+11.9%) and the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ (+12.5%), price only. It trades at 18.7× trailing GAAP earnings, between Halliburton's 15.0× and SLB's 26.1×, and at 18.9× analysts' FY2027 estimate. What the market is paying for is IET's record $39.4B backlog and order flow of $7.1B in Q2 and $5.9B in Q3 2026, not the rig count. On July 16, 2026 the company closed its all-cash purchase of Chart Industries ($13.6B including debt when it was announced; $14.8B of total consideration in the Q3 10-Q, including $4.5B to repay Chart's debt), moving further from the oil patch and toward industrial gas handling. The Q3 report showed the bill: $18.3B of debt against $4.1B of cash at September 30. The question is whether a company still earning close to half its revenue from oil and gas drilling deserves to be priced like an industrial with a power-equipment story, and whether it paid up, with borrowed money, to make that argument stick.

Chart (formerly NYSE: GTLS) makes cryogenic tanks, heat exchangers, air and gas handling and compression equipment: the cooling and gas-handling plumbing that sits next to Baker Hughes' turbines and compressors in an LNG plant, a hydrogen project or a data-center cooling loop. The deal closed on July 16, 2026. The Q3 10-Q puts total consideration at $14,809M, including $10,054M paid to Chart shareholders and $4,451M to repay Chart's debt, funded by $6.5B of U.S.-dollar notes and €3.0B of euro notes sold in March 2026 and two $1.0B two-year term loans taken in July. Its first part-quarter, from July 16: orders $831M, revenue $888M ($623M original equipment, $265M aftermarket parts and service), segment EBITDA $156M at a 17.6% margin and a $3.6B backlog. For Q4 management guided Chart to about $1.1B of revenue and $195M of EBITDA. On the call Moghal said: "We remain on track to achieve $325 million of annualized cost synergies within three years of closing the acquisition." The company's September slides target a Chart margin of 22–23% by the second half of 2028.

Also in motion: Waygate Technologies (industrial inspection), agreed for sale to Hexagon for ~$1.45B cash, was still pending at September 30 and is classified as held for sale in the 10-Q. Portfolio churn is high, and it distorts GAAP earnings (see Section 04).
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

LNG liquefaction franchise $39.4B IET backlog Service income from installed machines Gas turbine IP (ex-GE) National oil company ties (Middle East / LatAm) Subsea & artificial-lift technology Only big oil-service firm with an industrial arm

The core advantage is that Baker Hughes owns the equipment layer of the LNG build-out and the service contracts that follow it. Once a liquefaction train is designed around a specific Baker Hughes compressor and turbine set, the plant owner is buying parts, upgrades and field service from Baker Hughes for 25 years. Gas Technology Services revenue was $897M in Q3 2026, and unlike equipment it does not jump around with new-project approvals. The company's gas-turbine IP is the GE Oil & Gas heritage that SLB and Halliburton do not have, which is why the data-center power opportunity landed with Baker Hughes rather than them. And $46.4B of total remaining performance obligations at September 30, about 1.6 years of trailing revenue, is forward visibility that a pure oilfield-services company, priced off the next quarter's rig count, cannot offer.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The data-center turbine market has bigger incumbents. GE Vernova ($233.8B market cap, $44.0B TTM revenue) and Siemens Energy (€116.6B, €41.8B) own the large-frame heavy-duty gas turbine market. Baker Hughes' NovaLT and aeroderivative machines are smaller, faster-to-deploy units — a real niche for behind-the-meter data-center power, but a niche. If hyperscalers standardise on utility-scale plants, the order flow goes to GEV and Siemens.
▸
Close to half the company is in a flat-to-down cycle. OFSE revenue fell 8% in FY2025, and the company's September guide of $13.5–14.2B for 2026 implies another decline of 1–6%. Q3 2026 OFSE revenue was +0.7% year over year and EBITDA −1.8%. Without Chart, total Q3 revenue rose 1.8%; the +14.5% headline is mostly the acquisition.
▸
Leverage went from zero to real. At June 30, with the Chart money raised but not yet spent, Baker Hughes' $15.73B of cash nearly matched its $16.25B of debt: net debt of $526M. At September 30 it had $18.26B of debt against $4.07B of cash: $14.2B of net debt (our calculation from the Q3 release). Net interest expense was $177M in Q3, against $56M a year earlier. There were no buybacks in the first nine months of 2026 ($384M in the same period of 2025), and management says paying down debt comes first.
▸
LNG order lumpiness. Gas Technology Equipment orders were $4.9B in Q2 2026 and $3.1B in Q3, swings driven by a handful of large LNG project approvals (Q2's included Venture Global and Cheniere). LNG FIDs (final investment decisions) cluster; a quarter with none of them looks like a collapse even when the backlog is intact. The stock's June 2026 monthly close of $55.50, down 20% from April, shows how the market treats that lumpiness.

Notable One-Off Items. The trailing numbers are distorted in both directions. First-half 2026 GAAP earnings included roughly $697M of gains on business sales (chiefly the PSI sale and the Surface Pressure Control JV), about $0.70 a share of TTM EPS that will not recur; that is why analysts' FY2026 estimate ($2.59) sits below trailing GAAP EPS ($2.91). Net interest expense rose to $177M from $56M a year earlier: the Chart debt is now in the profit and loss. In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $25.08 to $54.51 on October 29, 2026, BKR returned +117% in price alone, about 16.8% a year compounded over five years, before a dividend that rose from about $0.72 to $0.92 a year. Over the same dates the S&P 500 fund SPY gained 67.5% and the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ 85.3% (price only). The path had two very different halves. From late 2021 through the end of 2023 the stock traded as a bet on the oil price: a spike to $36.41 in March 2022, a slide to the 5-year monthly low of $20.96 in September 2022, then most of the next two years between $27 and $36 while the company sorted out the post-GE cost base. From October 2024 investors started valuing it on a different story (IET orders, LNG project approvals and, from 2025, data-center power) and it rose 83% from $38.08 to the April 2026 monthly-close high of $69.67. It has since given back 22% of that close: a June 2026 dip, a recovery on the Q2 orders, then the September oil-services slide and the post-Q3 drop. Over the last twelve months BKR is up 12.8% from the October 29, 2025 close of $48.32, about level with SPY (+11.9%) and QQQ (+12.5%). A 1.04 beta and a five-year gain well ahead of SPY's mean much of the return came from the company's own story rather than the market's rise.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What the market is actually paying for. Strip the company into its parts. OFSE ($14.3B of FY2025 revenue at an 18.3% EBITDA margin, guided lower for 2026) is the kind of business investors value like Halliburton, at about 15× trailing earnings. IET ($13.4B in FY2025 at 18.5%, 19.7% in Q3 2026, with $39.4B of backlog) is the kind they value like GE Vernova, at 23× trailing and 38× forward (stockanalysis.com, October 29 close). Baker Hughes trades at 18.7× trailing GAAP earnings and 18.9× FY2027 estimates: on trailing earnings the market has placed it a little under halfway from the oil-services multiple toward the power-equipment one, and Chart is now a third business inside that blend. Every quarter in which IET orders stay above $5B and OFSE merely holds flat pulls its price-to-earnings multiple toward the power-equipment end. Every quarter without big LNG project approvals pulls it back.

The backlog arithmetic. $46.4B of remaining performance obligations against $28.74B of trailing revenue is about 1.6 years of revenue already contracted; for IET alone, $39.4B against FY2025's $13.4B is 2.9 years. IET's book-to-bill (orders divided by revenue) in Q3 2026 was $5,886M on $3,477M: 1.69×, down from 2.15× in Q2 but still adding to the backlog. That is what "the market values it as an industrial" means in practice: visibility that oilfield services has never had. But equipment backlog converts at equipment margins, and Gas Technology Equipment was $1,576M of the $3,477M IET revenue in Q3. The IET margin slipped to 19.7% from Q2's 20.6%, still above 18.8% a year earlier; Chart reports separately at 17.6%, so the company-wide margin now carries a lower-margin third segment until its synergies arrive.

The balance sheet changed on July 16, and now it shows. At September 30: $18.26B of debt, $4.07B of cash, $14.2B of net debt (our calculation) and a 1.24 current ratio, against $526M of net debt and a 2.09 current ratio at June 30. Buybacks stopped (none in the first nine months of 2026, against $384M in the same period of 2025). Business-sale proceeds ($1,450M in nine months, with Waygate's ~$1.45B pending) and free cash flow are the plan for paying down debt; management targets net leverage below 1.5× by the second half of 2028 and said debt reduction starts in Q4. On the call CFO Ahmed Moghal said leverage had risen to 2.5 times at quarter end; that gap is what the stock is now being judged on.

Bull Case

IET puts LNG liquefaction equipment and small gas turbines for data-center power in one ticker, and the order book shows the demand: $7.1B and $5.9B of IET orders in the last two quarters, $4.9B of data-center orders so far this year, a record $39.4B backlog, a full-year order guide raised to $21.5–23.5B and a 2026–2028 outlook of $50B-plus. Chart adds the cryogenic and gas-handling layer that makes Baker Hughes a fuller supplier to LNG, hydrogen and data-center cooling, with $325M of yearly cost savings targeted. If IET keeps growing on a rising margin, the company earns a GE Vernova-style multiple on its biggest growth business, and 18.9× FY2027 earnings looks cheap. Meanwhile 74% of OFSE revenue is international, the U.S. rig count is up 10% in a year, ROIC of 11.7% beats the 8.0% cost of capital even after Chart, and 25 analysts land at a $70 average target.

Bear Case

OFSE is guided to shrink again in 2026 and the Middle East, its biggest region, is in conflict. IET orders stepped down from $7.1B to $5.9B, and they depend on a handful of LNG customers whose project approvals cluster. Trailing GAAP EPS of $2.91 still includes ~$0.70 of disposal gains; on the $2.59 FY2026 estimate the stock is 21× earnings for a company whose revenue grew 1.8% in Q3 without Chart. Spending the Chart money took net debt from $526M at June 30 to $14.2B, more than tripled quarterly net interest expense ($177M against $56M a year earlier) and paused buybacks. And in data-center power the incumbents are GE Vernova and Siemens Energy at $234B and €117B: Baker Hughes is the niche player, priced as if it were not.

Top 3 Risks

01
IET order normalisation. Q3's $5.9B was below Q2's record $7.1B, though +42% on a year earlier. Order quarters will swing with LNG approvals (that is arithmetic, not a thesis failure), but a stock priced on order momentum reacts to whether growth is speeding up or slowing down. The June 2026 drawdown (−20% in two months) is the template.
02
Chart integration and leverage. A $14.8B all-cash deal (total consideration including Chart's debt) financed with $6.5B of U.S.-dollar notes, €3.0B of euro notes and $2.0B of term loans. Net debt was $14.2B at September 30 and net interest expense $177M in Q3. Management is counting on $325M of yearly cost savings within three years and a Chart margin of 22–23% by the second half of 2028, up from 17.6%; the October 28 drop shows how little patience the market has if those slip.
03
OFSE cycle depth. The company's September guide for 2026 OFSE revenue ($13.5–14.2B) is below FY2025's $14.3B, and Middle East/Asia revenue fell 13% year over year in Q3 (mostly the Surface Pressure Control sale, per the company). The release lists the Middle East conflict and the Strait of Hormuz among its risks. If international spending, 74% of OFSE revenue, turns into a genuine downturn, the segment that still brings in close to half of revenue becomes a drag large enough to offset IET growth, and the "industrial" valuation argument stalls.
  Industry Commentary
Oil-Company Spending Is Under Pressure
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
All three large oilfield-services companies posted lower FY2025 revenue (SLB −1.6%, Halliburton −3.3%, Baker Hughes OFSE −8%), and Baker Hughes guides its OFSE revenue lower again for 2026. The U.S. rig count has turned up, to 603 in the week to October 23 from 550 a year earlier, but the risk has moved to the Middle East: all three stocks fell 13–15% between September 4 and September 30, a stretch that included Saudi Arabia's pipeline shutdown, and Baker Hughes lists the regional conflict and the Strait of Hormuz among its risks. This is not a Baker Hughes problem alone; it is why the big three have been buying their way into nearby industrial markets: SLB's $3.4B Kelvion deal and Baker Hughes' Chart deal are the same instinct at different scales. The constraint on the whole group is that the legacy business funds the shift, and the legacy business is not growing.
LNG Orders Come in Bunches
ONGOING HEADWIND
A Venture Global six-block award or a Cheniere Sabine Pass Train 7 order is a multi-billion-dollar event that lands in one quarter. Baker Hughes' Gas Technology Equipment orders of $4.9B in Q2 2026 versus $1.5B of GTE revenue in the same quarter is what a cluster of project approvals looks like; Q3's $3.1B of GTE orders was a more normal quarter. The headwind is not demand — the backlog says demand is fine — it is that quarterly order prints become the stock's heartbeat, and a quiet quarter looks like a heart attack. Investors who bought after the record quarter should expect to be tested by a normal one.
Data Centers Want Gas Turbines, Now
EMERGING TAILWIND
Grid interconnection queues are years long; a gas turbine installed behind the meter is not. That is why Dynamis ordered 76 NovaLT16s (~1.3 GW) and Kodiak signed for up to 1.8 GW, and why Simonelli put Baker Hughes' data-center orders at $4.9B so far in 2026. GE Vernova's forward P/E of 38× (stockanalysis.com, October 29 close) says the market believes the cycle is long. Bull framing: Baker Hughes' small-frame, fast-deploy turbines are exactly what a hyperscaler needs for bridging power, and every unit sold adds 25 years of service revenue — the more the grid lags, the better. Bear framing: the heavy-duty incumbents are ten times Baker Hughes' size in power equipment; when utilities catch up with large-frame plants, "bridging" turbines get idled, and Baker Hughes discloses data-center orders but not data-center revenue, so how much of the tailwind reaches the income statement, and when, is not yet a reported number.
