01 Company Overview

BlackRock is the world's largest asset manager, running $15.35 trillion of client assets as of June 30, 2026 across index and active strategies, the iShares exchange-traded fund (ETF) franchise, cash management, and a fast-growing private-markets platform. Revenue is overwhelmingly a function of assets under management (AUM) multiplied by an effective fee rate, so results are structurally levered to market levels as well as to net flows. Full-year 2025 revenue was $24.22 billion, up 19% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 reached $27.30 billion (+26.5%) as the GIP, HPS and Preqin acquisitions annualized into the base — a growth rate that ranks BlackRock as the fastest-compounding of the world's mega-scale asset managers by a wide margin.

The company sits at roughly #115 globally and #66 in the US by market capitalization ($187.6B). Notably, its market cap is only about 1.2% of the assets it manages — a reminder that BlackRock is a fee-taker on other people's capital, not a balance-sheet risk-taker. That asset-light model produces a 24.1% net profit margin and a 45.9% as-adjusted operating margin, but it also means the earnings stream inherits the volatility of global equity and bond markets.

BlackRock's moat is a compounding scale advantage that is difficult to attack from below. In index products, cost per dollar managed falls almost linearly with AUM, and the largest ETFs attract the tightest bid-ask spreads and deepest options markets — which draws institutional users, which deepens liquidity further. iShares crossing $6 trillion and roughly doubling in three years is that flywheel in numbers. Layered on top is Aladdin: once a client runs its risk, trading and reporting on BlackRock's operating system, switching means re-plumbing the entire investment process, which produces subscription revenue with very low churn and 15% ACV growth. The private-markets build — GIP for infrastructure, HPS for private credit, Preqin for data — extends both advantages into the fastest-growing, highest-fee corner of asset management, and the resulting mix shift is pushing the blended fee rate up rather than down, with 8% organic base fee growth in Q2 2026 and 10% on a trailing-twelve-month basis. The result is a firm that is simultaneously the low-cost provider in commodity beta and a credible fee-rate expander in alternatives — a combination no competitor currently holds.

Key Vulnerabilities

Market-beta dependence. Roughly 79% of revenue is a basis-point fee on AUM, and AUM moves with global equity and bond markets. A 20% drawdown in world markets mechanically removes a large share of base fees with almost no offsetting cost flexibility in the short run, and the equity beta of Beta 1.42 reflects exactly that operating gearing. The March 2026 close of $956.32 — down 13.6% from the September 2025 peak — shows how quickly the multiple compresses when markets wobble.

Fee compression in core passive. Vanguard, State Street and Fidelity compete directly on expense ratio, and zero-fee or near-zero-fee index products have repeatedly reset the price floor. BlackRock's defence is mix — active ETFs, private markets, technology — but if flows stay concentrated in the cheapest core beta, revenue growth decouples from AUM growth. FY2025 distribution fees growing just 6% against 19% total revenue growth is a reminder that not every line participates in the story.

Acquisition integration and goodwill risk. The GIP, HPS and Preqin deals transformed the growth profile but also injected substantial purchase accounting: Q2 2026 intangible amortization alone was $276M, up $139M YoY, and FY2025 GAAP operating income fell 7% while as-adjusted operating income rose 18%. The $12.78 gap between FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS of $35.31 and as-adjusted EPS of $48.09 is the size of the adjustment investors are being asked to look through. If private-markets fundraising disappoints, that goodwill becomes an impairment candidate.

Political and regulatory exposure. As the largest holder of a substantial share of the US equity market, BlackRock is a standing target for anti-ESG state-level action, common-ownership antitrust theory, and index-fund voting-power scrutiny. These rarely damage earnings directly but they generate mandate-loss headlines, constrain product positioning, and raise the cost of doing business in politicised jurisdictions. The advisory relationship with regulators (FMA) simultaneously deepens the conflict-of-interest critique.

Notable One-Off Items

One-off distortions are material here and dominate the GAAP-versus-adjusted gap. First, acquisition-related intangible amortization: Q2 2026 amortization of intangible assets was $276M, an increase of $139M YoY, driven primarily by the HPS transaction that closed in July 2025. Second, integration and transaction costs from GIP, HPS and Preqin, which are the main reason FY2025 GAAP operating income declined 7% to $7.05B (29.1% GAAP margin) while as-adjusted operating income rose 18% to $9.60B (44.1% adjusted margin) — a 15-percentage-point margin wedge. Third, contingent consideration remeasurement, which swung from a $76M expense in the prior-year quarter to an $11M benefit in Q2 2026, a $87M year-over-year earnings tailwind that is pure accounting and will not repeat. Fourth, performance fee lumpiness: Q2 2026 performance fees of $305M versus $94M a year earlier (+224%) reflect crystallization timing, not a new run-rate. Finally, seed and co-investment marks on balance-sheet capital deployed alongside clients flow through non-operating income and add quarter-to-quarter noise — most visibly in digital assets, where AUM fell to $48.8B at June 30, 2026 from $79.6B a year earlier. Investors should anchor on as-adjusted EPS ($48.09 FY2025; $13.91 in Q2 2026) while remembering that the amortization being adjusted out is a real economic cost of having bought the growth.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

BlackRock is executing a genuine business-model upgrade: $329B of private-markets AUM growing at $24.5B year-to-date, performance fees up 224% YoY, technology ACV up 15%, and a blended fee rate that is rising rather than compressing — 8% organic base fee growth in Q2 and 10% trailing-twelve-month. That sits on top of an iShares franchise that has crossed $6T and roughly doubled in three years, with $178B of ETF inflows in a single quarter. If GAAP converges toward the 45.9% as-adjusted operating margin as GIP/HPS/Preqin amortization rolls off, the 19.2x forward P/E on consensus FY26 EPS of $56.23 looks undemanding for a business compounding revenue above 20% with a 0.23 debt-to-equity ratio and a rising buyback.

Bear Case

Roughly 79% of revenue is a basis-point fee on market values, and beta of 1.42 means a market drawdown hits earnings and multiple simultaneously — as March 2026's $956.32 close demonstrated. GAAP results are considerably weaker than the adjusted narrative: FY2025 GAAP operating income fell 7%, GAAP margin was 29.1% versus 44.1% adjusted, ROE has sagged to 12.28% on a goodwill-inflated equity base, and free cash flow declined to $3.52B from $4.70B in FY2024. The stock is up just 1.70% over 52 weeks despite the strongest fundamental period in the firm's history, which suggests much of the private-markets optionality is already in the 27.4x trailing multiple. If fundraising slows or markets fall, both the E and the multiple compress together.

Top 3 Risks

01
Market-level dependence with no natural hedge. With ~79% of revenue derived from AUM-linked fees across both equity and fixed income, a correlated drawdown in both asset classes — as in 2022, when the stock fell 38.6% from peak to trough — removes base fees with essentially no cost offset. Beta of 1.42 quantifies the transmission.
02
Acquisition integration and goodwill impairment. The GIP/HPS/Preqin build cost real capital and is now embedded in a $15/share annual gap between GAAP and as-adjusted EPS, $276M of quarterly intangible amortization, and a diluted ROE of 12.28%. If private-markets fundraising or fee realization disappoints, the write-down risk lands on the equity base and the growth story simultaneously.
03
Fee compression and political pressure on the index franchise. Vanguard, State Street and Fidelity compete on price in the core beta products that still constitute the majority of AUM, while anti-ESG mandate withdrawals, common-ownership antitrust theories and index-voting scrutiny impose an ongoing tax on the firm's largest and most visible business. The mix shift into private markets is the answer, but it currently offsets rather than escapes the pressure.
  Industry Commentary
The Migration from Public to Private Markets EMERGING TAILWIND

Capital formation is shifting away from public listings toward private credit, infrastructure and private equity, and fee rates in those channels are a multiple of index fee rates. BlackRock's response has been the most aggressive of any traditional manager: GIP for infrastructure, HPS for private credit, Preqin for the data layer. The result is $329B of private-markets AUM (infrastructure ~$113B, private credit ~$151B, private equity ~$32B), $15.4B of Q2 net inflows and $24.5B year-to-date, and performance fees of $305M in Q2 versus $94M a year earlier. HPS alone contributed roughly $230M of fees in the quarter. This is the single reason BlackRock's organic base fee growth is running at 8–10% while the traditional industry grows mid-single digits.

Passive Fee Compression vs. Mix Shift Up ONGOING HEADWIND

The structural deflation in core beta pricing has not stopped — Vanguard (~$12.0T), Fidelity ($7.0T) and State Street ($5.6T) all compete directly on expense ratio, and the top 20 managers already control ~47% of industry AUM, which concentrates the price war among a handful of giants with the scale to fight it. BlackRock's $6.25T ETF book (40.7% of AUM) sits at the centre of that pressure. The offset is mix: active ETFs, private markets and digital assets carry higher fee rates, so the blended rate has been rising even as individual product prices fall. FY2025 distribution fees growing only 6% against 19% total revenue growth is the clearest evidence that the low-fee legacy channels are no longer the growth engine.

Market-Level Dependence — Bull vs. Bear Framing CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Everything above is contingent on market levels. AUM of $15.35T generates fees as a percentage of value, so a 20% global drawdown removes roughly a fifth of the base fee pool in months, with a cost base that is only partly variable. Bull framing: that same gearing works in reverse — the +129.6% run from the September 2022 trough shows how violently the equity re-rates when AUM recovers, and the private-markets and technology lines now provide a genuinely less market-sensitive floor (technology ACV +15%, contracted and recurring). Bear framing: at 27.4x trailing earnings with beta of 1.42, an investor buying BLK today is buying a leveraged claim on global asset prices at a full multiple, having already paid for the diversification that is supposed to reduce that leverage. The digital-assets AUM collapse from $79.6B to $48.8B in twelve months is a live demonstration of how fast a high-fee-rate, high-beta asset pool can shrink.
