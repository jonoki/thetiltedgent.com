01 Company Overview

A note on the calendar before anything else. Broadridge's fiscal year ends June 30, so "FY2026" on this page means the twelve months ended June 30, 2026 — a year that is already closed, reported and audited, not a forecast. The FY2026 year-end print landed on August 4, 2026. Every trailing-twelve-month figure quoted here is therefore identical to the FY2026 full-year figure; the first calendar-year quarter of the new fiscal year (FQ1 2027, the September quarter) has not yet been reported. Where a calendar year is meant, it is written as "CY".

Broadridge is the plumbing of shareholder communications and post-trade processing. It distributes and tabulates proxy materials for the overwhelming majority of U.S. public-company shareholder meetings, mails and e-delivers interim reports and regulatory documents on behalf of banks and brokers, and runs the securities-processing systems that carry trades from order capture through settlement for capital-markets and wealth-management firms. FY2026 revenue was $7,477M, up 8.5% from $6,889M in FY2025, with recurring revenue of $4.88B growing 8% on a constant-currency basis and 6% organically. GAAP net income was $1,124M, up 33.9%, and diluted EPS reached $9.60. At $19.15B the company ranks roughly #1166 globally by market capitalisation.

The central debate on this page is not about the business. It is about what happened to the price of the business. In the twelve months to September 10, 2026, BR shares fell 33.1% — from a monthly-close peak of $255.62 in August 2025 to a June 2026 trough of $136.95 — while the company grew adjusted EPS 12%, raised its dividend for the twentieth consecutive year, printed record closed sales and grew its backlog. Multiply it out: at the August 2025 peak the stock traded at roughly 36× the then-trailing FY2025 EPS of $7.10; today it trades at 17.5× the FY2026 EPS of $9.60. Earnings went up; the multiple halved. The whole question is whether that de-rating is the market correctly pricing a durability problem — a bond-like compounder being repriced for an AI-era disruption risk and a slowing organic growth rate — or whether a franchise with 40.9% ROE, near-monopoly regulatory infrastructure and 100%+ free-cash-flow conversion has simply been thrown out with the rest of the IT-services sector. Sections 04 and 07 take that apart.

Broadridge's DLR (Distributed Ledger Repo — a blockchain-based platform for repurchase agreements, the overnight collateralised borrowing that funds much of the dealer market) was running roughly $360 billion of daily volume as of June 2026, described on the FY2026 Q4 call as having tripled. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
REGULATED PROXY MONOPOLY BENEFICIAL-OWNER DATA GRAPH MISSION-CRITICAL SWITCHING COST 98%+ CLIENT RETENTION POSITION-GROWTH ANNUITY MULTI-YEAR BACKLOG SCALE IN PRINT + DIGITAL

The quiet engine: position growth. This is the part of Broadridge most coverage skips, and it is the single most important mechanical fact about the business. Broadridge is paid, under an SEC-approved and NYSE-administered fee schedule, per position — that is, per beneficial-owner account holding a given security that must be communicated with. So its revenue base grows when the number of shareholder accounts holding things grows, regardless of whether it sells anything new. In FY2026 that engine delivered equity position growth of 16%, equity revenue position growth of 12% (the subset that actually bills, after the tiered fee schedule and managed-account suppression), and mutual fund and ETF position growth of 6%.

Note the gap between 16% and 12%. Positions grow faster than billable positions because the fee schedule steps down as an account holds more positions, and because managed accounts and certain householded relationships are counted differently. The 12% is the number that matters. And here is the uncomfortable half: Broadridge does not control any of it. Position growth is a function of how many retail brokerage accounts get opened, how many securities each of them holds, how many funds retail investors own — in other words, of retail participation in markets. A brokerage-account boom is a Broadridge revenue tailwind that management neither created nor can sustain; a multi-year retail retrenchment is a headwind it cannot offset with effort. Management's own framing for the first half of FY2027 is "high single-digit" equity revenue position growth and "mid single-digit" fund growth, based on position testing — a deliberate step down from 12% and 6%. That guided deceleration is a large part of why the multiple has compressed, and it is worth being precise: the company is not guiding to a decline, it is guiding to a normalisation from unusually strong levels.

Layered on top of the position annuity is a genuinely high switching cost. A broker replacing Broadridge's proxy and communications stack is not swapping a vendor; it is re-plumbing a regulated obligation with personal liability attached for getting it wrong. The same logic holds in GTO, where a securities-processing conversion is a multi-year project — which is precisely why the $470M backlog and $305M of FY2026 closed sales convert into revenue slowly but almost never reverse. Closed sales, for the avoidance of doubt, are the annualised recurring revenue value of new contracts signed in the period; they are a forward indicator, not revenue, and the lag from signature to revenue runs one to three years.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The fee schedule is set by regulators, not by Broadridge. The economics of the proxy business run on an SEC-approved, NYSE-administered schedule of processing and distribution fees. That schedule is the moat and the ceiling at the same time: it blocks price competition, and it means Broadridge's core pricing power is subject to periodic review by parties — issuers, brokers, the SEC — who would all prefer it to be lower. This is an unusual risk profile: not a competitor taking share, but a regulator adjusting a rate card.
▸
Position growth is exogenous and is guided to slow. FY2026's 12% equity revenue position growth is guided down to "high single digit" for 1H FY2027, and fund growth from 6% to "mid single digit." Because this flows almost straight through to the highest-margin recurring line, a few points of position growth is worth more to the P&L than a few points of anything management directly controls. If retail account formation stalls, the 6–8% recurring growth guide gets hard to hit even with closed sales at record levels.
▸
Digitisation cuts both ways on distribution revenue. Roughly $2.6B of FY2026 revenue sits in distribution and event-driven work — largely print, postage and fulfilment, passed through at thin margin. As communications move to e-delivery, that revenue shrinks. Mathematically this is margin-accretive and revenue-dilutive at the same time, which makes headline revenue growth a poor read on the business. In FY2026 the effect ran the other way: the CFO flagged that higher distribution revenue combined with lower interest rates produced a 40 basis-point headwind to adjusted operating margin, which finished flat at 20.5%.
▸
The AI de-rating of "IT services." Broadridge is classified by stockanalysis.com under Technology / Information Technology Services — a sector that has been repriced hard on the thesis that large language models compress the value of outsourced processing and services headcount. Whether that thesis applies to a regulated, position-billed proxy monopoly is exactly the argument; but classification drives flows, and a stock in a de-rating bucket gets sold by people who never read the segment disclosure. A 33% twelve-month decline against 12% adjusted EPS growth is what that looks like.

Notable one-off items. Three distortions are worth flagging, and one non-distortion. (1) The GAAP-to-adjusted convergence is coincidental. FY2026 GAAP diluted EPS of $9.60 rose 35% while adjusted EPS of roughly the same value rose 12%. The two figures landing on the same number is arithmetic coincidence, not a sign that adjustments have disappeared — the GAAP line grew faster because the prior-year GAAP base ($7.10) carried charges the adjusted base did not. Use the 12% adjusted growth as the run-rate read, not the 35%. (2) The interest-rate and distribution-mix drag. The CFO quantified a 40 basis-point headwind to adjusted operating margin from lower interest rates plus higher distribution revenue; margin finished flat at 20.5% only because operating leverage elsewhere offset it. Falling rates are a genuine, recurring headwind for a company that earns float on client balances — not a one-off. (3) Q4 seasonality is extreme and intentional. FQ4 (April–June) is proxy season: $2,220M of revenue and $546M of operating income landed in that single quarter, versus $1,589M and $189M in FQ1. Any quarter-on-quarter comparison in this name is meaningless; only year-over-year works. (4) The non-distortion: no large acquisition, impairment or restructuring charge was identified in the FY2026 results from the sources fetched. The capital story is plain — a 12% dividend raise, record buybacks, and a fresh $1.5B repurchase authorisation.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and on monthly data each point represents a month, so signals are slow and rare.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 monthly close of $178.41 to the September 10, 2026 close of $168.24, BR's price return over five years is −5.7%, a compound annual rate of about −1.2%. Dividends — currently $4.36 a share, a 2.60% yield, raised every one of those years — turn that into a modestly positive total return; the exact cumulative dividend over the window was not verified from the sources fetched and is therefore not stated as a number. What the flat five-year line conceals is a round trip of real violence for a 0.91-beta stock: BR compounded steadily from a December 2022 low of $134.13 to an August 2025 peak of $255.62 — a +90.6% run over 32 months — and then gave essentially all of it back, falling 46.4% to a June 2026 close of $136.95 before recovering to today's level.

The de-rating, in arithmetic. This is worth doing explicitly because it is the whole story. At the August 2025 peak of $255.62, trailing FY2025 EPS was $7.10 — a multiple of 36.0×. Today at $168.24 on trailing FY2026 EPS of $9.60, the multiple is 17.5×. Earnings per share rose 35% over those thirteen months and the share price fell 34%. Essentially none of the decline is an earnings story; all of it is multiple. That is an unusual and clean setup: the bear case has to argue that 36× was always wrong and that 17.5× is now roughly right for a business whose organic recurring growth is 6% and whose exogenous position-growth driver is guided to decelerate. The bull case has to argue that a 17.5× trailing, 15.95× forward multiple on a 17% ROIC, 100%-FCF-conversion, twenty-year dividend-raiser with a regulated near-monopoly is a price that will not persist.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The four numbers management is actually judged on. Broadridge guides on a specific quartet, and every one of them landed well in FY2026. (1) Recurring revenue: $4.88B, +8% constant currency, +6% organic. This is the franchise — the position-billed, contractually-renewing base, roughly 65% of total revenue. The two-point gap between 8% and 6% is acquisition contribution; the 6% is what the business does on its own. (2) Closed sales: $305M, up from $288M, with a record $158M in FQ4 alone — that is the annualised recurring value of contracts signed this year, a forward indicator that typically converts to revenue over one to three years. (3) Revenue backlog: $470M, up $40M year over year — signed-but-not-yet-live revenue, which management explicitly framed as providing visibility into FY2027 and FY2028 growth. (4) Adjusted EPS: $9.60, +12%, with adjusted operating margin flat at 20.5% against a 40 bp headwind from lower rates and richer distribution mix. For FY2027 the guide is 6–8% constant-currency recurring revenue growth, 8–12% adjusted EPS growth, $290–330M of closed sales and 100%+ free-cash-flow conversion. Note the closed-sales guide brackets the FY2026 result on both sides — management is explicitly not promising another record.

The engine nobody controls. Underneath all four of those guided metrics sits the driver this report has flagged twice already, because it is the thing that most determines whether the guide is met. Broadridge bills per beneficial-owner position under a regulated fee schedule. In FY2026, equity positions grew 16%, equity revenue positions — the billable subset after the tiered schedule steps down — grew 12%, and mutual fund and ETF positions grew 6%. Those increments flow into the highest-margin recurring line with almost no incremental cost: the proxy for a marginal position is the same document, sent to one more account. It is, functionally, an annuity indexed to retail market participation. And it is entirely exogenous. Broadridge does not open brokerage accounts, does not decide how many funds a household holds, and cannot market its way to more positions. When retail engagement is strong, the company looks like a compounding machine that beats its own guide; when it normalises, the same operating effort produces a visibly lower growth rate and the market assumes something has broken. Management's guide for the first half of FY2027 — "high single-digit" equity revenue position growth and "mid single-digit" fund growth, from 12% and 6% — is a step down of roughly three to four points on equities. On a $2.96B implied ICS recurring base, three points of position growth is real money, and it explains a meaningful share of why forward estimates decelerate from 8.5% reported revenue growth to a 5.1% consensus for FY2027.

Why the gross margin is a trap. Anyone screening this name on a 31.78% gross margin against a ~40% sector average will conclude it is a low-quality processor. That is the wrong read, and it matters because it is probably part of the de-rating. Of $7,477M of FY2026 revenue, only $4.88B is recurring; the remaining ~$2.6B is distribution and event-driven — postage, print, physical fulfilment — carried largely as pass-through at minimal margin. Those dollars inflate the revenue line and crush the gross margin percentage simultaneously. The clean tell is on the other side of the P&L: a business with genuinely thin economics does not produce 17.03% ROIC, $1.28B of free cash flow on $7.48B of revenue, and 100%+ cash conversion. The margin structure is a reporting artefact of a pass-through business bolted onto a high-margin annuity. Over time, e-delivery shrinks the pass-through, which will make reported revenue growth look worse and the gross margin look better — a combination almost designed to confuse a screen.

Valuation — and the one thing that has to be true. At $168.24, BR trades at 17.53× trailing FY2026 GAAP earnings, 15.95× forward (FY2027 consensus EPS $10.55), 15.0× free cash flow, 12.33× EV/EBITDA on a $22.22B enterprise value, and yields 2.60% on a payout ratio of 45.4% with a twenty-year raise streak. Against a business earning 17% on invested capital, those are not demanding numbers — they are roughly what the market pays for a stable industrial. For the stock to work from here, one thing has to be true: recurring revenue growth has to hold in the guided 6–8% band rather than sliding to 4%. If it holds, 8–12% adjusted EPS growth plus a 2.6% yield plus 7.8% of authorised buyback gets an investor to a low-to-mid-teens annual return with no multiple expansion at all, and any re-rating toward even 20× is upside on top. If recurring growth slips below the band because positions normalise harder than management expects, then 15.95× forward is not cheap, it is a fair price for a 4%-grower — and the average analyst target of $213 is a number from a different regime. Everything else on this page is secondary to that one variable.

Bull Case

The business is intact and the price is not. FY2026 delivered 8% constant-currency recurring revenue growth, 12% adjusted EPS growth, record $305M closed sales, a backlog up $40M to $470M, a 12% dividend raise (20th straight) and a new $1.5B buyback — while the stock fell 33%. The de-rating from 36× to 17.5× is pure multiple: EPS rose 35% over the same window. At 15.95× forward on a regulated near-monopoly earning 17% ROIC with 100%+ cash conversion, an investor is paid 2.6% to wait, with 7.8% of the share count authorised for repurchase and every one of nine analyst targets sitting above the current price.

Bear Case

Organic recurring growth is 6%, not 8%, and the engine driving it is outside management's control and guided to decelerate — equity revenue position growth from 12% to "high single digit," fund growth from 6% to "mid single digit." Consensus already models FY2027 revenue growth of just 5.1%. The 31.78% gross margin reflects $2.6B of structurally shrinking pass-through revenue; 1.24 debt-to-equity flatters a 40.9% ROE that ROA of 9.30% does not support; lower rates are a recurring, not one-off, margin headwind. If the market has correctly decided that a mid-single-digit grower in a sector being repriced for AI disruption deserves 16×, there is no re-rating coming, and four of nine analysts already say Hold.

Top 3 Risks

01
Position growth normalises harder than guided. This is the dominant variable and it is exogenous. FY2026's 12% equity revenue position growth and 6% fund growth are guided down to high-single-digit and mid-single-digit for 1H FY2027. Because these increments land in the highest-margin recurring line with almost no incremental cost, a shortfall flows almost undiluted to earnings. A genuine retail retrenchment — fewer new brokerage accounts, fewer positions per account — would take recurring growth below the 6–8% guide with nothing management could do about it.
02
Regulatory revision of the proxy fee schedule. The core economics of ICS rest on an SEC-approved, NYSE-administered fee schedule. That schedule is simultaneously the barrier that keeps competitors out and the ceiling that caps pricing — and it is periodically reviewed by constituencies (issuers, brokers, the regulator) with an interest in lowering it. A downward revision would compress the highest-margin revenue in the company without any competitive event occurring. This is a low-frequency, high-severity risk that no operating metric will warn you about in advance.
03
Sector classification and the AI de-rating persisting. Broadridge is screened as Technology / Information Technology Services, a bucket being repriced on the thesis that AI compresses the value of outsourced processing. The specific 33% twelve-month decline in a 0.91-beta stock, against 12% adjusted EPS growth, suggests the selling has been categorical rather than analytical. That is an opportunity only if it reverses; if the market sustains the view that regulated processing infrastructure is disruptable, the multiple stays at 16× indefinitely and the entire return comes from EPS growth, dividends and buybacks — a perfectly respectable 10–13%, but not the 27% the average price target implies.
  Industry Commentary
Retail Participation Is the Sector's Volume Input
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every shareholder-communications and post-trade business is ultimately a derivative of how many people own how many securities. Broadridge's FY2026 numbers show the input running hot — 16% equity position growth, 12% equity revenue position growth, 15% internal trade growth — and management's 1H FY2027 guide shows it cooling to high-single-digit and mid-single-digit. Unlike a software company that can sell more seats, Broadridge cannot manufacture positions. This is the constraint that defines the sector's growth ceiling, and it is set by household behaviour, brokerage account formation and fund flows, not by any vendor's sales force. It is also why the sector's revenue has a cyclical component that its "recurring" label tends to obscure.
Falling Rates and the E-Delivery Squeeze
ONGOING HEADWIND
Two slow grinds are running at once. First, rates: the CFO quantified 40 basis points of adjusted operating margin headwind in FY2026 from lower interest rates combined with higher distribution revenue — processors earn float on client balances, and that income shrinks as rates fall. Second, e-delivery: roughly $2.6B of Broadridge's revenue is distribution and event-driven work whose physical component erodes every year as communications go digital. The net effect is confusing by design — it is margin-accretive and revenue-dilutive simultaneously, meaning reported revenue growth understates franchise health in some years and overstates it in others. FY2026 was the latter: 8.5% reported growth against 6% organic recurring growth.
Tokenization: Existential Threat or the Next Rail to Own?
EMERGING TAILWIND
If securities settlement and custody migrate onto distributed ledgers, the incumbent that runs the legacy communications and post-trade layer either owns the new layer or gets disintermediated by it — there is no comfortable middle. Broadridge has chosen to run at it: Distributed Ledger Repo volumes tripled to roughly $360B daily as of June 2026, and the DLX platform is being extended to carry proxy and governance capability into tokenized securities via partnerships with Ondo, Alpaca and Galaxy across synthetic, custodial and native issuance models. Bull framing: the one thing tokenized securities will still need is a regulated way to communicate with and collect votes from their holders — and Broadridge is building exactly that, giving it a second life on new rails while the market prices it as a print-and-mail business at 16× forward earnings. Bear framing: the company discloses volume and not revenue for any of it. $360B of daily repo volume is a headline, not a P&L line, and until a dollar figure appears this is a story investors are asked to take on trust — in a stock where the verifiable numbers show 6% organic growth decelerating.
