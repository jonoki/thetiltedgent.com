01 Company Overview

A note on the calendar before anything else. Broadridge's fiscal year ends June 30, so "FY2026" on this page means the twelve months ended June 30, 2026 — a year that is closed, reported and audited, not a forecast — and "FY2027" is the year now running. The first quarter of FY2027 (FQ1, July–September 2026) was reported on November 3, 2026. Trailing-twelve-month (TTM) figures here are therefore FY2026 with the September 2025 quarter swapped for the September 2026 one. Where a calendar year is meant, it is written as "CY".

Broadridge is the plumbing of shareholder communications and post-trade processing. It distributes and tabulates proxy materials for the overwhelming majority of U.S. public-company shareholder meetings, mails and e-delivers interim reports and regulatory documents on behalf of banks and brokers, and runs the securities-processing systems that carry trades from order capture through settlement for capital-markets and wealth-management firms. FY2026 revenue was $7,477M, up 8.5% from $6,889M in FY2025, with recurring revenue of $4.88B growing 8% on a constant-currency basis and 6% organically. GAAP net income was $1,124M, up 33.9%, and diluted EPS reached $9.60. In the September 2026 quarter revenue rose 5% to $1,669M and recurring revenue — the contracted, repeating fees at the heart of the business — rose 11% to $1,083M, 9 points of it organic (from businesses Broadridge already owned, not acquisitions). Adjusted EPS rose 3% to $1.56, ahead of the $1.37 Nasdaq consensus, while GAAP diluted EPS fell 41% to $0.83, mostly because a $40M non-cash loss on the company's digital assets replaced a $46M gain a year earlier. At $21.41B the company ranks #391 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation (our calculation, November 4, 2026).

The central debate on this page is still about the price of the business more than the business. In the twelve months to November 4, 2026, BR shares fell 15.1% ($222.86 → $189.29) while the S&P 500 fund SPY rose 13.6%. The bigger swing sits inside that stretch: from an August 2025 month-end peak of $255.62 down 46.4% to a June 2026 month-end low of $136.95, then back up 38.2% after the August full-year results and the November quarter. Through all of it the company kept growing: adjusted EPS rose 12% in FY2026 and 3% in the September quarter, the dividend went up for the twentieth year in a row and fourth-quarter closed sales set a record. Multiply it out: at the August 2025 peak the stock traded at roughly 36× the then-trailing FY2025 GAAP EPS of $7.10; today it trades at 21.0× TTM GAAP EPS of $9.03 (19.6× adjusted TTM EPS of $9.65). Earnings went up; the price paid for each dollar of them fell by about two-fifths. The question is whether the market was right to mark it down that far — a steady, slower-growing business priced for the risk that AI erodes outsourced processing and that growth in shareholder accounts cools — or whether a company with a ~40% ROE, near-monopoly regulated infrastructure and 100%+ free-cash-flow conversion was simply sold along with the rest of the IT-services group. Sections 04 and 07 take that apart.

This is the proxy business and everything around it. When a U.S. public company holds an annual meeting, the shares held in "street name" — that is, held by your broker on your behalf rather than registered directly in your name — must still be voted by their beneficial owners. Banks and brokers are legally required to forward the materials and collect the votes, and essentially all of them outsource that job to Broadridge. The segment also covers interim communications (fund prospectuses, annual and semi-annual reports, statements), customer communications (statements and confirms, increasingly digital), and corporate issuer services including registrar and virtual-meeting work. The regulatory product line grew 12% in FY2026 — the fastest-growing line in the segment and the one most tightly coupled to the position-growth driver described below. Margin profile: 19.8% pre-tax on reported revenue, but that number is diluted by roughly $2.6B of pass-through distribution and event-driven revenue; on the recurring base the underlying margin is materially higher. September 2026 quarter: ICS revenue rose 2% to $1,153M and its recurring revenue 10% to $568M, but pre-tax margin fell to 9.0% from 11.2% as company-wide event-driven revenue (one-off mailings around proxy contests, fund meetings and similar events) dropped 41% to $67M and operating costs rose.

Industry size: Broadridge does not publish a TAM for proxy and investor communications. The usable size proxy is the disclosed position-growth data: +16% equity positions, +12% equity revenue positions, +6% mutual fund/ETF positions in FY2026; in the September 2026 quarter, +16%, +11% and +16%.

Named alternatives are Computershare (ASX: CPU), Equiniti, Say Technologies and brokers doing the work in-house, named here without figures. Broadridge has no rival of similar scale in U.S. beneficial-owner proxy distribution; the competitive pressure comes from issuers and brokers pushing for lower regulated fee schedules, not from a rival vendor.

The back office. GTO licenses and operates the systems that take a trade from order capture through clearance, settlement, asset servicing and regulatory reporting across equities, fixed income, mutual funds and derivatives — and, in wealth management, the advisor-facing platform layer on top. It splits into two lines with visibly different momentum: Capital Markets grew 5% in constant currency in FY2026, while Wealth Management grew 10%. That gap matters. Capital Markets is the mature, competitive, trade-volume-linked half; Wealth is where Broadridge is displacing incumbent advisor platforms and where the multi-year implementation contracts that build backlog get signed. Internal trade growth ran 15% in FY2026 — a volume tailwind for the processing business, though one that converts to revenue far less than one-for-one because most contracts are tiered. Pre-tax margin of 15.5% is structurally below ICS, reflecting the implementation and conversion costs that sit in front of each new client's recurring stream. September 2026 quarter: GTO revenue, all of it recurring, rose 12% to $516M (13% in constant currency, 3 points of it from the CQG acquisition, which the CEO said on the call is strengthening the futures and options offering), and pre-tax margin rose to 19.1% from 14.6%. Capital Markets grew 17% and Wealth and Investment Management 6% in constant currency (5% as reported), each with help from software term licences (multi-year licences whose revenue is booked largely up front).

Industry size: not disclosed by the company. The company-level demand signal is the $470M revenue backlog at the end of FY2026 (up $40M YoY), which is largely GTO implementation work not yet in revenue.

Not a reported segment, but a line management keeps returning to. Broadridge's DLR (Distributed Ledger Repo — a blockchain-based platform for repurchase agreements, the overnight collateralised borrowing that funds much of the dealer market) processed an average of $359 billion a day in September 2026, $7.5 trillion over the month (company release, October 8, 2026), and on the November 3 call the CEO said he expects further significant increases in DLR volumes over the next six months. The company is extending this into a broader tokenization platform it calls DLX, with announced partnerships including Ondo, Alpaca and Galaxy to carry governance and proxy capability into tokenized securities. The logic is defensive as much as offensive: if securities move onto distributed ledgers, the company that already runs the communications and post-trade layer for today's systems wants to run the same layer on the new ones. No revenue, margin or contribution figure is disclosed for this line, so it is best treated as a possible future business, not an earnings contributor. Separately, the company holds digital assets on its balance sheet whose value now swings GAAP earnings: a $40M non-cash loss in the September 2026 quarter against a $46M gain a year earlier.

Disclosure gap: volume is disclosed, revenue is not. $359B of daily repo volume tells you nothing about the fee Broadridge earns on it.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
REGULATED PROXY MONOPOLY BENEFICIAL-OWNER DATA GRAPH MISSION-CRITICAL SWITCHING COST 98% CLIENT RETENTION POSITION-GROWTH INCOME MULTI-YEAR BACKLOG SCALE IN PRINT + DIGITAL

The quiet engine: position growth. This is the part of Broadridge most coverage skips, and it is the single most important mechanical fact about the business. Broadridge is paid, under an SEC-approved and NYSE-administered fee schedule, per position — that is, per beneficial-owner account holding a given security that must be communicated with. So its revenue base grows when the number of shareholder accounts holding things grows, regardless of whether it sells anything new. In FY2026 that engine delivered equity position growth of 16%, equity revenue position growth of 12% (the subset that actually bills, after the fee schedule's step-downs and leaving out managed accounts, which are billed differently), and mutual fund and ETF position growth of 6%. The first quarter of FY2027 stayed strong: equity positions +16%, equity revenue positions +11% (7% a year earlier) and fund positions +16% (2% a year earlier).

Note the gap between 16% and 12%. Positions grow faster than billable positions because the fee schedule steps down as an account holds more positions, and because managed accounts and accounts grouped under one household are counted differently. The 12% is the number that matters. And here is the uncomfortable half: Broadridge does not control any of it. Position growth is a function of how many retail brokerage accounts get opened, how many securities each of them holds, how many funds retail investors own — in other words, of retail participation in markets. A brokerage-account boom is a Broadridge revenue tailwind that management neither created nor can sustain; a multi-year retail retrenchment is a headwind it cannot offset with effort. In August, management framed the first half of FY2027 as high-single-digit equity revenue position growth and mid-single-digit fund growth, based on position testing (the company's early read of share-position counts for upcoming jobs) — a step down from 12% and 6%. The first quarter came in above both (11% and 16%); on the November 3 call the CFO said testing shows equity position growth in the mid-teens in the second quarter and in the low double digits for the full year, and that part of the fund figure reflected the timing of certain jobs: set that aside and testing points to high-single-digit fund growth for the rest of the year, up from 6% in FY2026. The company is not guiding to a decline; equity growth is expected to cool from unusually strong levels, and that expected slowdown is part of why investors pay less for each dollar of earnings than they did in 2025.

Layered on top of that position-driven income is a genuinely high switching cost. A broker replacing Broadridge's proxy and communications stack is not swapping a vendor; it is re-plumbing a regulated obligation with personal liability attached for getting it wrong. The same logic holds in GTO, where a securities-processing conversion is a multi-year project — which is precisely why the $470M backlog and $305M of FY2026 closed sales convert into revenue slowly but almost never reverse. Closed sales, for the avoidance of doubt, are the annualised recurring revenue value of new contracts signed in the period; they are a forward indicator, not revenue, and the lag from signature to revenue runs one to three years.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The fee schedule is set by regulators, not by Broadridge. The economics of the proxy business run on an SEC-approved, NYSE-administered schedule of processing and distribution fees. That schedule is the moat and the ceiling at the same time: it blocks price competition, and it means Broadridge's core pricing power is subject to periodic review by parties — issuers, brokers, the SEC — who would all prefer it to be lower. This is an unusual risk profile: not a competitor taking share, but a regulator adjusting a rate card.
▸
Position growth is outside the company's control and expected to cool on equities. The August outlook stepped FY2026's 12% equity revenue position growth down to high single digits for the first half of FY2027, and fund growth from 6% to mid single digits; the September quarter ran above that (11% and 16%), and in November the CFO's outlook was low-double-digit equity position growth for the full year and high-single-digit fund growth for the rest of it. Because this flows almost straight through to the highest-margin recurring line, a few points of position growth are worth more to profit than a few points of anything management directly controls. If new retail brokerage accounts dry up, the 6–8% recurring growth guide gets hard to hit even with closed sales at record levels.
▸
Digitisation cuts both ways on distribution revenue. Roughly $2.6B of FY2026 revenue sits in distribution and event-driven work — largely print, postage and fulfilment, passed through at thin margin. As communications move to e-delivery, that revenue shrinks. Mathematically this raises the margin and lowers revenue at the same time, which makes headline revenue growth a poor read on the business. In FY2026 the effect ran the other way: the CFO flagged that higher distribution revenue combined with lower interest rates produced a 40 basis-point (0.40 percentage point) headwind to adjusted operating margin, which finished flat at 20.5%.
▸
The AI sell-off in "IT services." Broadridge is classified by stockanalysis.com under Technology / Information Technology Services — a group whose share prices have been marked down hard on the idea that AI models cut the value of outsourced processing and services staff. Whether that applies to a regulated, position-billed proxy monopoly is exactly the argument; but funds that buy and sell by sector sell every stock in the group, including those whose owners never read the segment detail. A 46% fall from the August 2025 peak to the June 2026 low, while adjusted EPS grew 12%, is what that looked like. Broadridge is using AI itself: on the November call the CFO put the quarter's AI savings at $6M, on track for $25M for the year, and the quarter carried a $15M restructuring charge tied in part to AI and productivity work.

Notable one-off items. Four distortions are worth flagging. (1) Digital assets now move GAAP earnings. The September 2026 quarter carried a $40M non-cash loss on digital assets, about $0.35 a share, against a $46M gain (about $0.39) a year earlier — a swing of roughly $0.74 that explains most of the fall in GAAP diluted EPS from $1.40 to $0.83. Adjusted EPS leaves it out, which is why it still rose 3% to $1.56. (2) Restructuring and tax. A $15M restructuring charge (about $0.13 a share), tied in part to AI and productivity work, hit GAAP results; a lower tax rate (13.6% vs 22.4%, from one-time tax benefits) partly offset it. (3) FY2026's GAAP and adjusted EPS were both $9.60 by coincidence: GAAP rose 35% from a prior-year base ($7.10) that carried charges the adjusted base ($8.55) did not, so the 12% adjusted growth is the better read of the run rate. (4) Seasonality is extreme. FQ4 (April–June) is proxy season: $2,220M of revenue and $546M of operating income landed in that single quarter of FY2026, versus $1,669M and $174M in the September 2026 quarter. Quarter-on-quarter comparisons in this name mean little; only year-over-year works. In FY2026 the CFO also quantified a 40 basis-point (0.40-point) drag on adjusted operating margin from lower interest rates plus higher distribution revenue — falling rates cut what the company earns on client balances, a recurring headwind rather than a one-off. Capital return: $200M of buybacks (1.1M shares) and $111M of dividends in the quarter, under the $1.5B repurchase authorisation approved in August.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and on monthly data each point represents a month, so signals are slow and rare.

The fall in the multiple, in arithmetic. The P/E (price ÷ earnings per share) is worth doing explicitly because it is the whole story. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The four numbers management is actually judged on. Broadridge guides on a specific quartet, and every one of them landed well in FY2026. In the first quarter of FY2027: recurring revenue +11% (9% organic) and closed sales $36M, +10%, but adjusted EPS only +3% to $1.56 as adjusted operating margin fell 2.0 points to 13.8% on lower event-driven revenue and growth spending. Management reaffirmed every FY2027 target. (1) Recurring revenue: $4.88B, +8% constant currency, +6% organic. This is the franchise — the position-billed, contractually-renewing base, roughly 65% of total revenue. The two-point gap between 8% and 6% is acquisition contribution; the 6% is what the business does on its own. (2) Closed sales: $305M, up from $288M, with a record $158M in FQ4 alone — that is the annualised recurring value of contracts signed this year, a forward indicator that typically converts to revenue over one to three years. (3) Revenue backlog: $470M, up $40M year over year — signed-but-not-yet-live revenue, which management explicitly framed as providing visibility into FY2027 and FY2028 growth. (4) Adjusted EPS: $9.60, +12%, with adjusted operating margin flat at 20.5% despite a 0.40-point drag from lower rates and more distribution revenue. For FY2027 the guide, reaffirmed on November 3, is 6–8% constant-currency recurring revenue growth, an adjusted operating margin of about 21%, 8–12% adjusted EPS growth, $290–330M of closed sales and 100%+ free-cash-flow conversion. Note the closed-sales guide brackets the FY2026 result on both sides — management is not promising growth on FY2026's $305M.

The engine nobody controls. Underneath all four of those guided metrics sits the driver this report has flagged twice already, because it is the thing that most determines whether the guide is met. Broadridge bills per beneficial-owner position under a regulated fee schedule. In FY2026, equity positions grew 16%, equity revenue positions — the billable subset after the tiered schedule steps down — grew 12%, and mutual fund and ETF positions grew 6%; in the September 2026 quarter the three ran at 16%, 11% and 16%. Those increments flow into the highest-margin recurring line with almost no added cost: the proxy for one extra position is the same document, sent to one more account. It works like an income stream tied to how many people own stocks and funds. And it is entirely outside the company's control. Broadridge does not open brokerage accounts, does not decide how many funds a household holds, and cannot market its way to more positions. When retail engagement is strong, the company beats its own guide; when it normalises, the same operating effort produces a visibly lower growth rate and the market assumes something has broken. Management's August outlook for the first half of FY2027 — high-single-digit equity revenue position growth and mid-single-digit fund growth, from 12% and 6% — was a step down of roughly three to four points on equities; the first quarter beat it, and in November the CFO's outlook was low-double-digit equity position growth for the full year and high-single-digit fund growth for the rest of it. On a $2.96B implied ICS recurring base, three points of position growth is real money, and it is a large part of why analysts expect revenue growth to slow from 8.5% in FY2026 to a 5.25% consensus for FY2027 ($7.87B, stockanalysis.com).

Why the gross margin is a trap. Anyone screening this name on a 31.52% gross margin against a ~40% sector average will conclude it is a low-quality processor. That is the wrong read, and it matters because it may be part of why the stock was sold. Of $7,477M of FY2026 revenue, only $4.88B is recurring; the remaining ~$2.6B is distribution and event-driven — postage, print, physical fulfilment — carried largely as pass-through at minimal margin. Those dollars inflate the revenue line and crush the gross margin percentage simultaneously. The clean tell is on the other side of the P&L: a business with genuinely thin economics does not produce 16.92% ROIC, $1.33B of TTM free cash flow on $7.56B of revenue, and 100%+ cash conversion. The margin structure is a reporting artefact of a pass-through business bolted onto a high-margin recurring business. Over time, e-delivery shrinks the pass-through, which will make reported revenue growth look worse and the gross margin look better — a combination almost designed to confuse a screen.

Valuation — and the one thing that has to be true. At $189.29, BR trades at 20.96× TTM GAAP earnings (19.6× adjusted), 17.94× FY2027 consensus EPS of $10.55, 16.1× free cash flow, 13.81× EV/EBITDA (enterprise value — market cap plus net debt — divided by operating earnings before depreciation and amortisation) on a $24.76B enterprise value, and yields 2.30% on a payout ratio of 48.07% with a twenty-year raise streak. For a business earning about 17% on invested capital, those are not demanding numbers. For the stock to work from here, one thing has to be true: recurring revenue growth has to hold in the guided 6–8% band rather than sliding to 4%. The first quarter's 11% is a good start, but it was helped by acquisitions (2 points) and by software term licences in GTO. If the band holds, 8–12% adjusted EPS growth plus a 2.3% yield adds up to roughly 10–14% a year with no change in the price paid per dollar of earnings. If recurring growth slips below the band because positions slow harder than management expects, then about 18× forward is a fair price for a 4% grower — and the average analyst target of $209 is too high. Everything else on this page is secondary to that one variable.

Bull Case

The business is intact and the price is still well below its peak. FY2026 delivered 8% constant-currency recurring revenue growth, 12% adjusted EPS growth, $305M of closed sales (a record $158M in the fourth quarter), a backlog up $40M to $470M, a 12% dividend raise (20th straight) and a new $1.5B buyback; the September quarter added 11% recurring growth, position growth above the company's own outlook and reaffirmed guidance. The fall from 36× to 21× trailing earnings is all price: GAAP EPS rose 27% over the same stretch. At 17.94× FY2027 consensus for a regulated near-monopoly earning about 17% on invested capital with 100%+ cash conversion, an investor is paid 2.3% to wait, with about 7% of the market value authorised for repurchase and the average analyst target 10% above the price.

Bear Case

Organic recurring growth was 6% in FY2026, and the engine driving it is outside management's control: equity position growth is expected to cool from 16% in FY2026 to the low double digits in FY2027. The first quarter's adjusted EPS grew only 3% as margins fell two points, and GAAP EPS now swings with the value of the company's digital assets. Consensus models FY2027 revenue growth of just 5.25%. The 31.52% gross margin reflects $2.6B of shrinking pass-through revenue; 1.39 debt-to-equity flatters a 40% ROE that a 9.50% ROA does not support; lower rates are a recurring, not one-off, margin headwind. If the market has correctly decided that a mid-single-digit grower in a group being sold on AI-disruption fears deserves about 18× forward earnings, the stock has already recovered most of what it will, four of nine analysts say Hold, and two targets sit below the price.

01
Position growth slows harder than expected. This is the dominant variable and the company does not control it. 02
Regulatory revision of the proxy fee schedule. The core economics of ICS rest on an SEC-approved, NYSE-administered fee schedule. That schedule is simultaneously the barrier that keeps competitors out and the ceiling that caps pricing — and it is periodically reviewed by parties (issuers, brokers, the regulator) with an interest in lowering it. A downward revision would compress the highest-margin revenue in the company without any competitive event occurring. This is a low-frequency, high-severity risk that no operating metric will warn you about in advance.
03
The sector label and the AI sell-off persisting. Broadridge is screened as Technology / Information Technology Services, a group marked down on the idea that AI cuts the value of outsourced processing. The 46% fall from the August 2025 peak to the June 2026 low in a 0.90-beta stock, while adjusted EPS grew 12%, suggests the selling was about the label more than the numbers. The 38% recovery since shows how quickly that can turn, but if the market keeps treating regulated processing as something AI can replace, the multiple stays around 18× forward earnings and the whole return comes from EPS growth, dividends and buybacks — a respectable 10–14% a year, with no lift from investors paying more for each dollar of earnings.
  Industry Commentary
Retail Participation Is the Sector's Volume Input
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every shareholder-communications and post-trade business is ultimately a derivative of how many people own how many securities. Broadridge's FY2026 numbers show the input running hot — 16% equity position growth, 12% equity revenue position growth, 15% internal trade growth. In the September 2026 quarter positions stayed hot (equity revenue positions +11%, funds +16%) but internal trade growth slowed to 7% from 17% a year earlier, and management's testing points to low-double-digit equity position growth for the full year and high-single-digit fund growth for the rest of it. Unlike a software company that can sell more seats, Broadridge cannot manufacture positions. This is the constraint that defines the sector's growth ceiling, and it is set by household behaviour, brokerage account formation and fund flows, not by any vendor's sales force. It is also why the sector's revenue has a cyclical component that its "recurring" label tends to obscure.
Falling Rates and the E-Delivery Squeeze
ONGOING HEADWIND
Two slow grinds are running at once. First, rates: the CFO quantified 40 basis points of adjusted operating margin headwind in FY2026 from lower interest rates combined with higher distribution revenue — processors earn interest on client balances they hold, and that income shrinks as rates fall. Second, e-delivery: roughly $2.6B of Broadridge's revenue is distribution and event-driven work whose physical component erodes every year as communications go digital. The net effect is confusing by design — it raises the margin and lowers revenue at the same time, meaning reported revenue growth understates franchise health in some years and overstates it in others. FY2026 was the latter: 8.5% reported growth against 6% organic recurring growth.
Tokenization: Existential Threat or the Next Rail to Own?
EMERGING TAILWIND
If securities settlement and custody migrate onto distributed ledgers, the incumbent that runs the legacy communications and post-trade layer either owns the new layer or gets disintermediated by it — there is no comfortable middle. Broadridge has chosen to run at it: Distributed Ledger Repo averaged $359B a day in September 2026, and the DLX platform is being extended to carry proxy and governance capability into tokenized securities via partnerships with Ondo, Alpaca and Galaxy. Bull framing: the one thing tokenized securities will still need is a regulated way to communicate with and collect votes from their holders — and Broadridge is building exactly that, giving it a second life on new systems while the market prices it as a print-and-mail business at about 18× forward earnings. Bear framing: the company discloses volume and not revenue for any of it. $359B of daily repo volume is a headline, not a profit line, and until a dollar figure appears this is a story investors are asked to take on trust. Its digital-asset holdings also now move reported earnings: a $40M non-cash loss in the September 2026 quarter.
