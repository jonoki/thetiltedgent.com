01 Company Overview

Brown & Brown is an insurance broker, not an insurer. It sells property & casualty, employee-benefits and specialty cover to businesses, public entities and individuals, and earns a commission on the premium it places — it never pays a claim. Founded in Florida in 1939 and still run from Daytona Beach by a Brown (CEO J. Powell Brown), it has spent decades growing by acquisition. FY2025 revenue was $5.76B, up 22.49%, and revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was $5.30B, up 23.4%.

That growth rate is the trap in this report. Most of it is not organic. On August 1, 2025 Brown & Brown closed its largest-ever deal, the $9.83B acquisition of Accession Risk Management Group — parent of retail broker Risk Strategies and wholesaler One80 Intermediaries — paid for largely with a June 2025 share sale and new bonds, and adding about $1.7B of annual revenue (Accession’s 2024 pro forma figure). Strip the acquisitions out and organic revenue grew 1.2% in Q3 2026, after falling 0.7% in Q2; for the nine months it is up 0.3%. The price tells you how the market scored that: from a $124.40 month-end close in March 2025 to $71.88 on November 2, 2026, down 42%. The question is simple to state and hard to answer — is a broker whose organic growth has barely moved on softening property rates, carrying $7.8B of debt and about 12% more shares than a year ago, cheap at 22.6x trailing earnings, or merely no longer expensive?

The Q3 print (October 26, 2026). Revenue was $1.725B, up 7.4%, and adjusted EPS $1.07, up from $1.05. Both were a little below what analysts expected, and the adjusted EBITDAC margin fell 200 basis points (two percentage points) to 34.6%, more than half of it because the company earned about $29M less interest income than a year earlier, when it was holding cash raised for Accession. The stock fell 4.97% on the first session after the release. Management raised its Accession savings target and, for the first time, gave ranges for Q4 and for 2027 (Section 02).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

No underwriting risk Recurring renewal commissions Decentralised acquisition machine Niche program franchises (Arrowhead) Wholesale access for independent agents Founder-family ownership (14.0% insiders) 33 years of dividend growth

Brokerage is a good business: clients renew every year, the commission is a percentage of a premium that tends to rise with inflation and asset values, and the broker never pays a claim. The moat is distribution, meaning thousands of producers with local relationships plus wholesale and program franchises that carriers rely on to reach niche risks. The weakness is the flip side. That distribution walks out of the door with its people, and the revenue line moves with premium rates set by a market the broker does not control. When catastrophe-property rates rise, brokers look like growth companies. When they fall, as in 2026, brokers look like what they are, which is a leveraged bet on the insurance pricing cycle.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Premium-rate cycle. Specialty Distribution organic growth was −3.5% in Q2 2026 and −0.8% in Q3, and management guides to a 2–4% decline in Q4 as catastrophe-property rates fall. A broker earns a share of the premium, so lower rates mean lower revenue even when every client renews.
02
Leverage after Accession. Debt was $7.76B at September 30, 2026 against $1.10B of cash (10-Q); StockAnalysis, counting leases, puts debt at $8.10B, which is 3.31x EBITDA, with interest covered 4.58x. Q3 interest expense was $100M, and that money is not available for acquisitions or buybacks.
03
Dilution. The Accession equity raise left 331.7M shares outstanding on October 23, 2026, which StockAnalysis puts 11.9% above a year earlier. Nine-month GAAP EPS of $2.57 is flat even though net income attributable rose 20.8% to $954M. The company bought back $750M of stock in the nine months, which is slowly shrinking the count.
04
People risk. The company expects $50–60M of lost revenue in 2026 from employees leaving for a start-up competitor, and its organic growth figure leaves out $16M of prior-year revenue from accounts lost to that competitor, which is the subject of litigation. In brokerage, producers take their books with them, and non-competes only slow that down.

Notable One-Off Items

Four items distort the reported figures. First, the Accession consolidation. Accession closed on August 1, 2025, so year-over-year revenue growth has already dropped from 30% in Q2 to 7.4% in Q3 and will fall further once the full third and fourth quarters of 2025 are in the comparison; consensus FY2027 revenue growth is 3.7%. Second, GAAP vs adjusted EPS: Q3 2026 diluted EPS was $0.67 and adjusted EPS was $1.07, a gap made up mostly of acquisition-related amortization and integration costs. Third, interest income: in the third quarter of 2025 the company earned about $29M of interest on cash raised before the deal closed, which flatters the prior-year margin comparison. Fourth, accounts lost to a competitor: the organic growth figure leaves out $16M of Q3 2025 revenue from client accounts lost to a competitor that is the subject of litigation; counted in, Q3 organic growth would have been about 0.1% rather than 1.2% (our calculation from the release's reconciliation). On the other side, Brown & Brown bought back $750M of stock in the first nine months of 2026, about $250M of it in Q3.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"). Readings below 30 mean it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value, and a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 close of $64.41 to the November 2, 2026 settled close of $71.88, BRO gained 11.6% on price. That number hides two opposite moves. The stock nearly doubled to $124.40 at the end of March 2025, when brokers were priced as businesses whose earnings rise steadily year after year, during a hard insurance market (one where premium rates are rising). It then lost 54.8% to a $56.25 May 2026 close, as organic growth faded, the Accession deal added shares and debt, and property rates softened. There were no share splits in the window (the last was 2:1 in March 2018). The chart uses unadjusted closes.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how long the decline lasted. It stayed below 30 for eight consecutive month-ends, from October 2025 to May 2026, and bottomed at 13.0 in May 2026, the most oversold reading of the five-year window. It recovered to 48.8 by August, fell to 33.7 after September and is now 51.8 after the October rebound. The 10-month moving average is about $66.27, below the current price.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At $71.88 you pay 16.2x FY2026 consensus adjusted EPS and get a 6.3% free-cash-flow yield from a broker with no underwriting risk, a 34–37% adjusted EBITDAC margin, 33 straight years of dividend growth (the quarterly payout is going from $0.165 to $0.18) and insiders who own about 14% of the shares. The stock is still 42% below its peak. Organic growth is back above zero (+1.2% in Q3), Retail is accelerating (+2.6%), management guides to 2–4% in Retail and 1–3% in Specialty Distribution for 2027, the Accession savings target was raised to about $50M this year and $175M run-rate by 2028, and the company bought back $750M of stock in nine months. If property pricing stabilises, a return to mid-single-digit organic growth on a much larger revenue base could lift the price investors pay for each dollar of earnings; the stock traded at about 1.7 times today's price at the March 2025 peak.

Bear Case

Headline growth is bought: organic growth was +1.2% in Q3 after −0.7% in Q2, and Q3 revenue and adjusted EPS both came in a little under expectations. The purchase took debt to $7.76B, kept quarterly interest expense at $100M and lifted the share count by about 12%, so nine-month GAAP EPS is flat at $2.57 while net income grew 20.8%. Specialty Distribution, the high-margin half, is guided to shrink 2–4% in Q4, and management expects a lower Q4 margin than last year. Producers are leaving for a start-up competitor, with an estimated $50–60M of 2026 revenue at stake; counting the $16M of lost accounts the company leaves out of its organic figure, Q3 organic growth would have been about 0.1%. At 22.6x trailing GAAP earnings this is not a distressed valuation, and 13 of 21 analysts are at Hold. Brokers were priced for a hard market, and pricing for a soft one may not be finished.

Industry Commentary
The broker's hidden bet: premium rates
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Brokers are often described as safe businesses that take no insurance risk, and on the balance sheet that is true. The income statement is different. A commission is a percentage of the premium, so when rates rise the broker grows without winning a single new client, and when rates fall the broker shrinks while renewing every one. Brown & Brown's 2026 figures show this. Retail, which relies more on new business and exposure growth, managed +2.6% organic in Q3. Specialty Distribution, which places property catastrophe risk, fell 0.8% as those rates came down, and the company expects a 2–4% fall in Q4. In poker terms, the broker takes a rake on every pot and never plays a hand, but the rake is a percentage, and the pots are getting smaller.

Consolidation and the war for producers
ONGOING HEADWIND

Broker growth by acquisition, buying agencies at one price while being valued at a higher one, depends on the acquirer's own valuation staying high. BRO's forward P/E has fallen from 25.34 (FY2024 basis) to about 16 today, per StockAnalysis's forecast table, which narrows that gap. At the same time, well-funded start-up brokers are hiring producers away from established firms, and the company has put a $50–60M figure on its 2026 exposure. On the Q3 call management said some sellers' price expectations are unrealistic and that growth will need hiring and selective acquisitions. The competition is now for producers as much as for clients.

The valuation reset — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

BRO trades at about 13.1x EV/EBITDA (our calculation: market cap $23.84B plus $8.10B of debt less $1.10B of cash, divided by $2.35B of TTM EBITDA, StockAnalysis), a 6.25% FCF yield and 16.2x forward adjusted earnings, against a forward multiple above 23x in every year from FY2022 to FY2024 on StockAnalysis's basis. The bull framing: the market has moved from pricing a hard market to pricing a soft one, and at these levels even flat organic growth plus buybacks and debt paydown produces a mid-single-digit per-share return before any change in the multiple. The bear framing: the old multiple assumed organic growth of high single digits, which the business is not producing now. Until organic growth is clearly positive without contingents for several quarters, the discount reflects what the business is earning today.
