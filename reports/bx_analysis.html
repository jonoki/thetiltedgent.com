01 Company Overview

Blackstone is the world's largest alternative asset manager, running $1.368 trillion of AUM across four segments: Real Estate, Private Equity, Credit & Insurance, and Multi-Asset Investing. The economics are two-layered — a recurring management-fee stream on $989.7B of fee-earning AUM, plus performance revenue that is realised only when investments are sold, which is why the GAAP income statement swings violently from year to year.

Most recent full year (FY2025): revenue of $14.21B, up 12.2% YoY, with GAAP net income of $3.02B and GAAP EPS of $3.87. But the numbers management and the sell side actually run the business on were Fee-Related Earnings (FRE) of $5.7B ($4.67/share) and Distributable Earnings (DE) of $7.1B ($5.57/share). Blackstone ranks #82 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation on October 23, 2026 (added to the index on 18 September 2023, per S&P Dow Jones Indices).

The most recent quarter (3Q26, reported before the open on 22 October 2026) beat the forecast but showed cash earnings going backwards: DE of $1.715B ($1.32/share), down 13% per share from a year earlier yet above the Nasdaq consensus of $1.26, because realised gains from selling investments fell 34%. The fee side kept growing: FRE of $1.572B ($1.26/share, +5% per share), base management fees of $1.99B, AUM of $1,368.3B (+10%), perpetual capital of $562.3B (+12%), and quarterly inflows of $63.3B. GAAP net income attributable to Blackstone was $662M ($0.83/share). The stock rose 8.4% on results day but is still down 23.9% over the past year, after a 21.7% fall in September alone.

It spans opportunistic funds (BREP), core-plus, the real estate debt business (BREDS) and the non-traded retail vehicle BREIT. AUM fell 4% YoY to $309.0B — still the only segment shrinking — and in 3Q26 segment distributable earnings fell 10% to $554M, with management fees down 9% to $629M. The portfolio has been deliberately rotated: on the 2Q26 call management said data centres, logistics and rental housing make up nearly 80% of the global property equity portfolio. In 3Q26 Gray said the firm raised the cap rates (the yields used to turn a property's income into a value) across its holdings as base interest rates rose, which led to depreciation in the opportunistic funds: Chae put the BREP opportunistic funds at −3.2% for the quarter and the Core+ funds at +1.6%. Inflows were $6.3B, including $1.3B raised in BREIT.
The BREIT redemption episode, stated plainly. BREIT is a semi-liquid, non-traded REIT sold largely to wealth-channel investors. It is structured with repurchase caps — roughly 2% of net asset value per month and 5% per quarter. When rates spiked and listed REITs fell far below BREIT's appraised marks in late 2022, redemption requests exceeded those caps and Blackstone prorated withdrawals for months into 2023. Investors got some money back each month, but not all of what they asked for. The mechanism worked exactly as documented, and no gate was "broken" — but it made the central bear-case point about semi-liquid retail vehicles concrete: liquidity in these products is a promise conditioned on other people not asking at the same time, and appraisal-based NAVs can lag observable public-market prices for a long time. BREIT has since recovered: on the 3Q26 call Gray said it grew 9% year over year to $58B, had its best regular-way net flows in four years, and has posted 21 consecutive months of positive returns, about 11% net over the last twelve months for its largest share class. The same mechanism is now at work in credit instead: BCRED has filled only about half of its repurchase requests in each of the last two quarters (see Credit & Insurance above).
Data centres / QTS. The single largest value driver at the firm right now is the digital-infrastructure platform anchored by QTS. On the 2Q26 call management put total data-centre platform value — including facilities under construction — at $185B, up from roughly $130B earlier in the year, and named QTS the largest single source of appreciation firm-wide. On the 3Q26 call Gray said they expect a threefold increase in data-centre leasing this year across the global platform. Blackstone also listed BXDC (Blackstone Digital Infrastructure Trust), a data-centre REIT, on the NYSE in May 2026, in an offering of up to $2.0B. In the 3Q26 release Schwarzman names AI and energy infrastructure among the themes the firm has built leading platforms for. That framing is a double-edged sword: it is the clearest bull catalyst and simultaneously the clearest source of concentration risk if AI capex expectations reset.

Blackstone's moat is not a product — it is the cost of capital on the fundraising side. At $1.368T of AUM and $271.6B of trailing-twelve-month inflows, it is the default allocation for pension funds, sovereign wealth funds and insurers that need to write a $500M–$2B ticket without doing diligence on an unproven manager. That scale compounds in three ways. First, it lets Blackstone underwrite transactions almost no one else can finance alone — a $185B data-centre platform is not assembled by a mid-sized sponsor. Second, it converts into pricing power on the liability side: $562.3B of perpetual capital, up 12% in a year, of which the fee-earning $469.5B is 47% of fee-earning AUM, means roughly half the fee base does not have to be re-raised every few years. Third, it gives Blackstone the distribution to reach retail and insurance money that traditional drawdown funds structurally cannot: BREIT, BCRED, BXDC and the insurance mandates are all attempts to convert a wholesale business into something closer to an annuity.

The financial fingerprint of that moat is visible in the metrics table below: ROE of 32.38% and a 22.99% net profit margin against a median of roughly 5% for the listed alternative-manager peer group. Blackstone converts revenue to profit at four times the rate of its closest comparables because it does not need much balance sheet to do it — total assets are only $49.6B against $1.37T of managed money. But the moat protects the fee stream far better than it protects the earnings multiple, and the last twelve months are the proof: FRE for the first nine months of 2026 rose 17% and the stock still fell 23.9% over the year.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Real estate is shrinking while everything else grows. Real Estate AUM fell 4% YoY to $309.0B — the only segment in decline — even as it still supplies 29.1% of management fees, and its fees fell 9% in 3Q26. That gap means fee revenue in the segment is being carried by a base that is not replenishing. Commercial real estate marks are appraisal-driven and lag observable transaction prices; in 3Q26 management raised the cap rates it values the book on as base rates rose, and the opportunistic BREP funds marked down 3.2%. If data-centre demand cools while rates stay high, the segment's fee base compresses from both ends.

2. Funds sold to individual investors promise more liquidity than their assets have. BREIT and BCRED offer periodic withdrawals on portfolios of assets that take months or years to sell. The 2022–23 BREIT episode showed what happens when requests exceed the 2%-monthly / 5%-quarterly caps: proration. It is happening again, in credit: BCRED received requests for 10% of its net asset value in 3Q26 against its 5% quarterly limit, mostly carried over from 2Q26, filled about half, and had $1.4B of net outflows (Gray, 3Q26 call); Reuters reported the second consecutive cap on 3 September. BREIT, by contrast, had its best regular-way net flows in four years. The headline risk is out of proportion to the AUM at stake because it is the retail channel that generates press coverage.

3. Private credit is now the growth engine — and the most crowded trade in finance. Credit & Insurance is $480.0B and growing 11%. The reported 7% trailing-twelve-month gross return in non-investment-grade private credit is fine, but it has been earned in a benign default environment with wide spreads. Spread compression from competing capital lowers future returns; a genuine default cycle tests marks that are, again, model-derived rather than traded. Regulators and the IMF/BIS have repeatedly flagged the opacity of the asset class. Blackstone is better positioned than most, but it is not insulated from a sector-wide de-rating.

4. Earnings quality is a presentation choice, and the dividend rides on it. GAAP EPS was $3.87 in FY2025 while DE was $5.57 and FRE $4.67. The $5.06/share declared over the last four quarters works out to a payout ratio of 113% of trailing GAAP EPS ($4.49) but about 85% of DE (the 3Q26 dividend of $1.12 is 85% of DE of $1.32 a share). That is defensible — DE is the right denominator — but it means the dividend is variable by design and falls when realisations fall: the 3Q26 dividend was 13% below the $1.29 paid for 3Q25. Investors who bought BX for a 4.3% yield should understand they own a distribution that management has explicitly structured to fluctuate with the exit environment, not a fixed coupon.

Notable One-Off Items

Unrealised performance-fee marks are the dominant distortion. In 3Q26, GAAP total revenue was $3.063B while total segment revenue — the operating measure — was $3.217B; a quarter earlier GAAP revenue had run $1.24B above segment revenue. GAAP revenue carries unrealised gains and losses on fund investments and accrued carry, so the gap swings with the marks. Cumulatively, $7.3B of net accrued performance revenue ($5.84/share, +12% YoY) sits unrealised on the balance sheet. None of it is cash yet.

The FY2021→FY2022 revenue collapse was not operational. Revenue fell from $22.18B to $8.02B (−63.9%) and GAAP EPS from $8.13 to $2.36 (−71.0%). Fee-earning AUM did not fall by anything like that; the swing was reversal of 2021's enormous unrealised marks. Any screen that ranks BX on multi-year GAAP revenue or EPS growth is measuring mark-to-market noise.

Realisations fell, as guided. Net realisations (the profit Blackstone books when funds sell investments) were $332M in 3Q26, down 34% from $505M a year earlier, which is why DE fell 9% while FRE rose 6%. Realisation-driven DE is lumpy in both directions: Chae said carry realisations already under contract to close in 4Q26 alone are expected to exceed the firm's total net realisations for 3Q26.

Real-estate valuation marks. In 3Q26 management raised the cap rates used to value its real estate as base rates rose; the opportunistic BREP funds fell 3.2% and Core+ rose 1.6%. These marks are appraisal-based, not transaction-based, and are the single most subjective input in the reported numbers.

Balance-sheet and buyback items. Share repurchases were only 0.2M shares in the quarter (0.8M over the trailing twelve months) — buybacks are immaterial here; $1.5B is to be distributed to shareholders for the quarter. Total assets of $49.6B at 30 September 2026 (StockAnalysis).

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF OCT 23, 2026
In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 means it has been sold off hard and may be washed out. It says nothing about whether the stock is cheap — it is a momentum gauge, not a valuation one.

The five-year trajectory has been a round trip that ended below where it started. From the October 2021 month-end close of $138.42 to the October 23, 2026 close of $117.97, the price return is −14.8% — roughly −3.2% a year. Dividends offset part of that (the last four quarters alone declared $5.06/share, a 4.3% yield at the current price), but they are not in the chart. What the price path shows is three distinct regimes: a peak in November 2021, a brutal 48% de-rating into December 2022 as rates rose and real-estate marks came under attack, and then a two-year recovery to a November 2024 high of $191.09 that has since been more than given back — the September 2026 close was 41.3% below it.

The last twelve months have been the hard part. The stock is down 23.9% over the year (Oct 23, 2025 close $154.98 → $117.97), and traded between an intraday high of $165.14 on 16 January 2026 and a low of $101.73 on 12 March 2026, on a business whose fee-related earnings rose 17% over the first nine months of 2026. That divergence between operating results and share price is the central fact of the current setup. Price sits about 6% below both the 50-day ($125.04) and 200-day ($125.10) moving averages even after the results-day jump, and the 6-period monthly RSI on the chart is 42 — in the middle of its range, recovering from 37 at the end of September, after touching 25.5 in February 2026.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The recurring engine is working: FRE rose to $1.572B in 3Q26 (+6%, and +17% for the first nine months), base management fees reached $1.99B, AUM $1.368T, perpetual capital $562.3B (+12%), and $63.3B of inflows in a single quarter show the fundraising franchise is intact. Management has guided to a step-up in realisations in 4Q26 and double-digit base management fee growth in 2027. Meanwhile the stock is down 23.9% over twelve months, so an investor today buys at 17.9× forward earnings with a 4.3% variable yield, plus $7.3B ($5.84/share) of unrealised carry as a call option on the exit window reopening — and a $185B data-centre platform (2Q26 figure).

Bear Case

Cash earnings are going the wrong way: DE per share fell 13% in 3Q26 and the dividend fell with it ($1.12 against $1.29). 46% of analysts are on Hold, five of the eight firms that moved on 23 October sit at Hold, and Deutsche Bank cut its target from $149 to $138. The growth is concentrated in private credit — the most crowded trade in finance, priced for a benign default environment — and BCRED has had to ration withdrawals for two quarters running. Real Estate, still 29% of management fees, is the only shrinking segment at −4% AUM, and its opportunistic funds marked down 3.2% as cap rates rose. Marks in both businesses are model- and appraisal-derived rather than traded. GAAP EPS of $4.49 covers the $5.06 of dividends declared over the last four quarters at only 89%, and the payout rises and falls with realisations.

Top 3 Risks
01
Private credit cycle risk. Credit & Insurance is $480.0B and the main source of new money, but its 7% trailing-twelve-month gross return was earned in a low-default environment. A genuine credit cycle would hit marks that are model-derived, compress spreads across the industry, and slow the credit inflows that supplied 57% of firm inflows in 3Q26; BCRED's capped withdrawals show the retail side is already under strain. A sector-wide de-rating would hit BX regardless of its own credit performance.
02
Real-estate marks and the rate path. Real Estate AUM is down 4% YoY at $309.0B while still generating 29.1% of management fees. Appraisal-based valuations lag transactions; the rise in base rates already pushed management to raise cap rates in 3Q26, and the opportunistic funds marked down 3.2%. Higher-for-longer rates simultaneously depress marks, slow realisations (which drive the dividend) and could revive redemption pressure in BREIT.
03
Concentration in the AI/data-centre trade. The $185B data-centre platform is the largest single driver of appreciation firm-wide, QTS is explicitly named as such, and management names AI as one of the main themes it has built platforms for. That is a real asset with real leases — but it means a reset in AI capex expectations would hit Blackstone's real-estate marks, its realisation pipeline, its retail-vehicle NAVs and its narrative simultaneously. Diversification across four segments is less protective than it looks when one theme drives the appreciation in the largest of them.
  Industry Commentary
The Public-to-Private Capital Shift
EMERGING TAILWIND
Capital keeps migrating out of public markets and into private vehicles, and Blackstone is the largest single beneficiary. The scale is now hard to argue with: $1,368.3B of AUM, $271.6B of inflows over the trailing twelve months, and $228.9B of dry powder waiting to be deployed. The strategically important part is not the headline number but the composition — $562.3B of perpetual capital, up 12% YoY, with its fee-earning part 47% of fee-earning AUM. Perpetual capital does not have to be re-raised, which converts a lumpy fundraising business into something with subscription-like characteristics. That is the mechanism behind management's guidance for double-digit base management fee growth in 2027. It is also the reason FRE deserves a higher multiple than the GAAP earnings the screens show: $5.7B of FY2025 FRE against $3.02B of GAAP net income is not an accounting quirk, it is the durable half of the business being obscured by the volatile half.
Private Credit at Scale — Growth Engine and Systemic Question Mark
ONGOING HEADWIND
Private credit has become the largest segment at Blackstone: $480.0B of AUM, +11% YoY, and $35.8B of inflows in 3Q26 alone, more than half the firm's total. The bull argument is that banks retrenched and someone has to lend; the bear argument is that so much capital has arrived so quickly that spreads have compressed and underwriting standards have loosened across the industry, and that nobody can independently verify marks on loans that never trade. Blackstone's own reported numbers are reassuring — 2.6% gross return in the quarter, 7% over twelve months — but they are self-reported, model-based, and drawn from a period with very few defaults. The pressure has shown up in liquidity rather than losses so far: BCRED's individual investors asked to withdraw 10% of the fund in each of the last two quarters and got about half. The valuation risk here is not that Blackstone's book goes bad; it is that the market decides the entire asset class deserves a lower multiple, which is arguably part of what the last twelve months of share-price action already reflect.
Commercial Real Estate, Rates, and the Semi-Liquid Retail Vehicle — Bull vs Bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is where the bull and bear cases actually meet. The bull framing: Blackstone has already rotated the portfolio out of the damaged parts of commercial real estate — data centres, logistics and rental housing make up nearly 80% of the global property equity portfolio (2Q26 call), and data-centre leasing is expected to triple this year. BREIT has demonstrably healed: up 9% to $58B, its best regular-way net flows in four years, and 21 straight months of positive returns, about 11% net over twelve months for its largest share class. Rate cuts from here would lift marks, accelerate realisations and revive flows all at once.
The bear framing: segment AUM still fell 4% YoY — the only segment shrinking — while supplying 29.1% of management fees, and segment DE fell 10% in 3Q26, so the fee base is eroding underneath the earnings. Valuations remain appraisal-based, and the rate rise has already forced higher cap rates and a 3.2% markdown in the opportunistic funds. And the structural point from 2022–23 has not been engineered away: BREIT's liquidity is capped at roughly 2% of NAV monthly and 5% quarterly, and when requests exceeded those caps investors were prorated for months. That worked as designed, but "worked as designed" is precisely the bear case — a semi-liquid vehicle sold to wealth-channel investors delivers full liquidity only when few people want it. Higher-for-longer rates would compress marks, stall realisations and re-test that mechanism simultaneously. Which of these two framings is right is, more than anything else, a view on the path of long-term interest rates and on whether AI-driven data-centre demand holds.
