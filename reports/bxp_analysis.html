01 Company Overview

BXP is the landlord of the American trophy office. Founded in Boston in 1970 by Mortimer Zuckerman and Edward Linde, listed in June 1997 as Boston Properties and renamed BXP, Inc. on July 1, 2024 — a name that no longer ties it to just one of its six regions (Boston, Los Angeles, New York, San Francisco, Seattle and Washington, DC). At September 30, 2026 it owned or had interests in 162 properties totalling about 50.6 million square feet: 141 office, 13 retail, 7 residential and one hotel (Q3 2026 supplemental). It is a REIT, so the number that matters is not GAAP earnings but FFO: $1.89 per diluted share in Q3 2026, up from $1.74 and $0.08 above the midpoint of the company's own guidance, with full-year 2026 guidance raised again, to $7.06–$7.08.

Here is the hand the market has been dealt. Five years ago this stock closed November 2021 at $107.84; it now sits at $63.47. Along the way the company took $1.22 per share of non-cash impairments in Q3 2025, cut its quarterly dividend from $0.98 to $0.70 in September 2025, and put roughly 30 non-core properties up for sale to fund development. And yet the operating line has turned: occupancy has risen four quarters running, from 86.0% (Sep 2025) to 86.7% (Dec 2025), 87.4% (Mar 2026), 88.4% (Jun 2026) and 89.4% (Sep 2026), leasing ran at about 1.2 million square feet in Q3 on a 10.3-year average term, and the flagship 343 Madison Avenue tower is now 56% leased with a $1.2B construction loan in hand and the sale of a 10.6% stake to a partner announced in October. The stock trades at about 9.0x forward FFO with a 4.41% yield that FFO covers about 2.5 times. The debate is simple to state: is office occupancy recovering fast enough to out-run the cost of refinancing cheap 2010s-era debt at 2026 rates — and the company itself says asset sales and refinancing will slow FFO growth in 2027?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Six-city gateway CBD footprint ~10-year lease terms In-house development platform Unsecured bond market access Scale: 50.6M sq ft Flight-to-quality tenant demand

The office market after 2020 split in two: commodity buildings that nobody wants, and the newest, best-located towers that tenants consolidate into. BXP's moat is being on the right side of that split at scale — its CBD portfolio runs 91.8% occupied against 89.4% for the whole book, and new leases are being signed for roughly a decade. The development platform compounds the advantage: a landlord that can build the next trophy tower (343 Madison) and lease more than half of it before delivery is selling a product that older stock cannot match. The moat protects occupancy and rent. It does not protect the cost of capital — and for a landlord carrying $16.59B of debt, that is the other half of the equation.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage in a higher-rate world. Consolidated debt of $16.59B at September 30, 2026 (Q3 supplemental) against an $11.27B market cap; debt/EBITDA of 9.27x on StockAnalysis's basis and 7.52x net debt/EBITDAre on the company's. Every maturity that rolls from a 2010s coupon to a 2026 coupon takes FFO with it — the 2.75% → 6.05% refinancing is the template.
02
Asset values have already been marked down. Non-cash impairments of $1.22 per share in Q3 2025 and $0.10 per share in Q2 2026 (Sumner Square, Washington, DC, ahead of its sale in Q3); $18.0M of impairments in the first nine months of 2026 and none in Q3 (Q3 release). Impairments do not touch FFO, but they are the accounting admission that some buildings are worth less than they cost — and the disposition programme sells into that market.
03
The dividend has been cut once already. $0.98 to $0.70 a quarter in September 2025 (−28.6%), with the company saying it would keep roughly $50M more cash each quarter. The new dividend is well covered by FFO (about 40%), but income investors who owned it for the payout were the ones who paid for the reset.
04
Development risk is concentrated. 343 Madison is a $2B project, 56% leased after the Q3 call and not due for delivery until 2029. The rest has to be leased into whatever the Manhattan office market looks like in a few years, with a floating-rate construction loan at SOFR (a benchmark overnight lending rate) + 2.50% until milestones cut it to + 2.25%. Selling a 10.6% stake cut BXP's equity commitment but not the leasing risk.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay about 9.0x guided FFO for a CBD-weighted portfolio of premier office buildings, at a point where every operating signal has turned: occupancy up four quarters in a row to 89.4%, leased at 91.9% (tenants already signed but not yet paying), about 1.2 million square feet signed in Q3 on ten-year terms, FFO guidance raised for the third quarter running, and a 4.41% dividend covered about 2.5 times by FFO. The dividend reset and the impairments are behind it; the disposition programme is cutting leverage (net debt/EBITDAre 7.52x); 290 Binney is delivering an 8.9% cash yield (per the Q2 call) and 343 Madison is 56% leased. If occupancy reaches 91% by end-2027 as targeted, FFO grows into a multiple that is still near its trough.

Bear Case

The bear case is arithmetic: $16.59B of debt is being refinanced from 2–3% coupons into 6%, and each roll takes FFO with it — $700M at 330 bps wider is about $23M a year before anything else happens. FFO per share has gone from $7.10 in 2024 to $6.85 in 2025 and is guided back only to about $7.07, and the CFO has told investors to expect about $0.08 per share of dilution from asset sales in 2027; the dividend has already been cut 28.6%; and $18.0M of impairments in the first nine months of 2026 say some buildings being sold are worth less than they cost. A 7.86% short interest is money betting that occupancy gains stall near 90% while interest costs keep climbing, and that 343 Madison has to lease its remaining 44% into an uncertain Manhattan in 2029.

Refinancing cost. The 2.75% → 6.05% roll on the 2026 notes is the pattern for the rest of the maturity ladder. With net debt at 7.52x EBITDAre, interest expense can absorb much of the occupancy gain.
02
Occupancy stalls. The path is about 90% at end-2026 and a goal of 91% at end-2027. Q3 delivered 89.4%; the Q4 print, with 2027 guidance, is the next test, and a miss would hit the bull case at its core.
03
Development and disposition execution. 343 Madison ($2B, 56% leased, due in 2029) and a multi-billion-dollar disposition programme both depend on office capital markets staying open — for leasing on one side and for buyers on the other.
Industry Commentary
Rates are the office landlord's second tenant
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For a leveraged landlord, the bond market sets two prices at once: what the buildings are worth and what it costs to hold them. The worst month in BXP's five-year series (June 2022, −20.0%) came in the year rates rose. The August 2026 financing puts a number on today's level: 6.05% for ten-year unsecured money, versus 2.75% on the notes it replaces. Until that gap closes, higher occupancy is partly offset by interest expense, and the CFO's 2027 list of headwinds starts with asset sales and refinancing.

Flight to quality — the split office market
ONGOING HEADWIND

Office demand has not recovered evenly; it has concentrated. BXP's CBD portfolio at 91.8% occupied versus 89.4% overall shows the spread inside one landlord, and the company is selling the weaker end — suburban office, land and non-core assets — to fund the stronger end. On the Q2 call, management described demand picking up from technology, AI, defence and cybersecurity clients across its markets (paraphrased); the Q3 release names a technology company as the tenant of its biggest new lease in Washington, DC. The headwind is for everyone else's buildings; the question for BXP is how much of its own portfolio sits on the wrong side of the line — and the impairments suggest it is not zero.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $63.47 the stock is priced at about 9.0x guided FFO with a 4.41% yield covered roughly 2.5x. The bull framing: this is trough pricing for a portfolio whose occupancy is rising and whose best development has just come in at an 8.9% cash yield; every point of occupancy on 50.6 million square feet is real rent. The bear framing: a single-digit FFO multiple is fair for a landlord whose earnings are being eaten by refinancing, whose FFO per share is still below 2024, and whose dividend was cut a year ago. The way to decide is not the P/FFO today; it is two numbers reported every quarter — occupancy against the 90%/91% path, and net debt/EBITDAre against the lower-7x goal.
