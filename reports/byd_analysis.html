01 Company Overview

BYD started in 1995 as a rechargeable-battery maker in Shenzhen and is now the world's largest seller of new energy vehicles (NEVs): 4,602,436 of them in 2025, up 7.73%, which the company says made it the world's best-selling NEV brand for a fourth straight year and the fifth-largest car group of any kind. It builds almost everything inside the car itself — batteries, electric motors, power chips — and also runs a large contract-electronics arm (phone parts and assembly, now AI-server cooling and power) and an energy-storage business. FY2025 revenue was RMB803.96B, up 3.46%, but profit attributable to shareholders fell 18.97% to RMB32.62B (EPS RMB3.58), which the company puts down to a change in product mix that lowered gross margin. The H shares trade in Hong Kong in Hong Kong dollars; the larger A-share line trades in Shenzhen as 002594 in renminbi.

The 2026 story is two companies inside one. At home, sales are shrinking: first-half deliveries fell 15.72% to 1,808,511 and revenue from cars sold in mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan dropped 33.6% as a price war met cautious buyers and the phase-out of China's purchase-tax exemption for NEVs. Abroad, it is booming: exports rose 67.8% to 792,000 vehicles, overseas car revenue grew 55.8%, and more than half of group revenue (52.6%, against 38.65% for 2025 as a whole) came from outside China. Overseas cars also carry far better margins — about 28% gross versus about 17% at home on the segment note's figures (our calculation). Group H1 revenue still fell 7.13% and profit fell 20.54%, but the second quarter alone showed profit up roughly 30% on revenue down 3% (our calculation from the Q1 and interim filings) — less than brokers expected: Morgan Stanley, UBS, Citi, Deutsche Bank and CMBI had together pencilled in a second-quarter profit gain of roughly 48%, as Quartz reported on results day. Management's answer is the export target, raised three times this year: 1.3 million overseas sales for 2026 in January, 1.5 million on the March 30 post-results call (Reuters), and 1.9 to 2.0 million, with more than 2.5 million for 2027, in comments to analysts reported on September 7 from a Deutsche Bank research note. Overseas sales reached 1,342,960 in the first nine months (+92.66%), so the new range needs about 557,000 to 657,000 more in the fourth quarter — 186,000 to 219,000 a month, against September's 180,700 (our calculation). The market is not buying the turn yet: at HK$73.90 the H shares are 43.6% below their March and May 2025 month-end peak of HK$130.93 and 35.1% below the October 2, 2025 close of HK$113.90. Valued at the H-share price, all 9.117B shares are worth HK$673.8B (US$85.9B); the Shenzhen A shares trade at a premium (RMB83.31 at the September 30 close, HK$97.52, 29.0% above the H share's HK$75.60 that day), so valuing each class at its own last price gives HK$802.0B (US$102.2B) — our calculations. The central debate: can the export engine and a new battery cycle outrun a home market where, in the chairman's words, competition "has reached a fever pitch"?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

In-house batteries, motors & chips Scale (4.6M NEVs in 2025) BEV + plug-in hybrid range Blade Battery & FLASH Charging Overseas plants (Brazil, Thailand) Energy-storage leadership Founder control

The moat is vertical integration at scale. Most carmakers buy their battery cells, motors and power chips; BYD makes them, and even in the 2025 price war its gross margin held at 17.74%. Selling both pure-electric and plug-in hybrid cars lets it serve buyers who are not ready to give up a fuel tank, in China and in emerging markets with thin charging networks. Local plants — the Brazil factory built its 100,000th car in July 2026, and Thailand is also producing — turn an exporter into a local manufacturer, outside the reach of duties that apply to cars shipped from China. The vulnerability is that the same scale needs constant volume: an integrated factory network is a fixed cost that punishes every month of weak domestic demand.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The home market is a price war. China car revenue fell a third in H1 2026. China's total vehicle sales fell 4.1% in the half (CAAM, cited by BYD) and the purchase-tax exemption for NEVs has been phased out. Q1 2026 profit fell 55.4%; even the stronger Q2 left H1 profit 20.5% lower.
02
Trade walls are rising where the growth is. The European Commission imposed a 17.0% countervailing duty on BYD's China-built electric cars for five years from October 2024. On June 8, 2026 the US Department of Defense added BYD to its list of "Chinese Military Companies"; BYD says it is neither, that the list is not a sanctions list and that it does not restrict trading in its shares.
03
Expansion is consuming cash. Operating cash flow fell from RMB133.45B in 2024 to RMB59.14B in 2025 while spending on plants and equipment rose to RMB156.81B, so free cash flow was about −RMB97.7B (our calculation). Borrowings rose from RMB28.58B at end-2024 to RMB120.06B at June 30, 2026, and the company's own gearing ratio (net debt to equity) rose to 31% from 25% at end-2025.
04
The dividend was cut hard. The FY2025 final dividend is RMB0.358 per share, about RMB3.26B in total or roughly 10% of profit, against about RMB12.08B (30% of profit) for FY2024 (our calculations). The chairman tied it to "the Company's actual operating cash flow and future development needs".

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported figures. First, currency: BYD explains its H1 2026 profit decline by the shrinking NEV business and foreign-exchange losses; in Q1 alone the finance-expense line swung from net income of RMB1.91B a year earlier to a charge of RMB2.10B, which the Q1 report attributes mainly to exchange losses this year against exchange gains last year. With half of revenue now abroad, currency is a recurring swing factor, not a one-off. Second, the 2025 bonus and capitalisation issue: on approval at the June 6, 2025 AGM, holders received eight bonus shares and twelve capitalisation shares for every ten held, so all per-share history before mid-2025 — earnings, dividends and the price chart below — is divided by three. Third, the March 2025 placement: BYD completed a US$5.6B top-up placement, which the chairman says drew sovereign wealth funds and Middle East strategic investors; it lifted cash and the share count at the same time.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of HK$81.00 (split-adjusted) to the October 2, 2026 close of HK$73.90, the H shares lost 8.8% of their price. Adding back the roughly HK$3.45 per share of dividends paid over the window (Yahoo's split-adjusted dividend record) brings the total return to about −4.5%. That is five years of going nowhere for the company that became the world's largest NEV seller — but the path was anything but flat: two full round trips, from about HK$100 to under HK$60 (2022–24), then up to HK$130.93 (March and May 2025 month-ends) and back down to the low seventies.

The 6-period monthly RSI printed 21.9 at the June 2026 close, the most oversold reading in the series, then bounced with July's 30% rally and has slid back to 33.7 — close to oversold again. The peak reading was 86.6 in March 2025. The 10-month moving average sits at about HK$89.56, 21% above the price: the long-term trend is still down, and the July bounce failed below it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 19.6 times trough-ish trailing earnings, and about 14 times the consensus 2026 estimate, for the world's largest NEV maker at the moment its business is rotating toward higher-margin exports. Overseas car revenue grew 55.8% in H1 2026 at a gross margin near 28% versus 17% at home, Q2 profit rose about 30%, and September deliveries were up 17% with nearly two in five cars going abroad. Management has lifted its 2026 overseas target to 1.9–2.0 million and aims for more than 2.5 million in 2027. Local plants in Brazil and Thailand reduce tariff exposure, the second-generation Blade Battery gives the range a fresh technical edge, and 24 of 28 analysts rate it a buy with an average target 68% above the price. If domestic volume merely stabilises, earnings can recover faster than the market assumes.

Bear Case

The home market — still the largest part of revenue — is shrinking in a price war the chairman himself calls a "knockout stage", and exports are heading straight into rising trade walls: a 17.0% EU countervailing duty, a US military-company listing, and whatever comes next. Expansion has turned free cash flow deeply negative (about −RMB97.7B in 2025), borrowings have quadrupled since end-2024, inventory days are up 30, and the dividend was cut to about 10% of profit. Even the Q2 rebound (about +30%) fell short of the roughly 48% brokers had pencilled in. J.P. Morgan cut its rating to Hold on September 29 and its target from HK$124 to HK$88. The shares have fallen 43.6% from the 2025 peak with no clear floor.

Top 3 Risks

01
Domestic price war. China car revenue fell 33.6% in H1 2026 and group profit 20.5%. Every further price cut by rivals forces a choice between volume and margin on the largest part of the business.
02
Trade and political barriers abroad. More than half of revenue is now overseas — the part of the business that is growing and earning the higher margins. Tariffs such as the EU's 17.0% duty, and US designations such as the June 2026 Chinese Military Companies listing, fall exactly where the growth is.
03
Cash and working capital. Free cash flow of about −RMB97.7B in 2025, borrowings up to RMB120.06B, inventories up to RMB190.0B and a current ratio of 0.87 leave less room for error if sales disappoint, and foreign-exchange losses are now a real swing factor in reported profit.

China's NEV price war — the "knockout stage"
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

China is the world's largest car market — over 34 million vehicles produced and sold in 2025, per BYD's annual report — and more than half of new cars sold there are now NEVs. That success has created overcapacity. In his 2025 annual statement Wang Chuan-fu wrote that "competition in the NEV industry has reached a fever pitch, and is undergoing a brutal 'knockout stage'". The first half of 2026 made it concrete: total China vehicle sales fell 4.1% while NEV sales still grew 7.3% (CAAM, cited in the interim report), the NEV purchase-tax exemption was phased out, and raw-material and chip prices rose. BYD's own domestic car revenue fell a third. Its interim report's outlook stays measured: "Although the momentum of domestic demand recovery in the second half of the year still needs to be consolidated, and the stability of the supply chain may also be tested by recurring disruptions, the overall fundamentals of the industry remain stable and positive." For an integrated manufacturer, a shrinking home market is doubly painful because its battery and component plants are sized for growth.

The export boom meets trade walls
ONGOING HEADWIND

Chinese carmakers exported 5.096 million vehicles in H1 2026 (+65.3%), and NEV exports alone reached 2.355 million (+120%), per the CAAM figures in BYD's interim report. Importing countries are responding. The European Commission's October 29, 2024 decision set five-year countervailing duties of 17.0% on BYD, 18.8% on Geely, 35.3% on SAIC and 7.8% on Tesla's China-built cars. In June 2026 the US Defense Department added BYD to its Chinese Military Companies list. BYD's answer is to build locally — Brazil reached 100,000 vehicles in July 2026, and the interim report cites plants in Thailand and other regions — which trades tariff risk for capital spending.

Exports and the battery cycle — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: BYD is quietly becoming a global company. Overseas revenue rose from 38.65% of the total in 2025 to 52.6% in H1 2026, overseas cars earn margins about eleven points higher than domestic ones, and high oil prices — which the interim report links to Middle East tensions — widen the running-cost gap between electric and petrol cars. Add a new battery generation that charges in minutes and the Q2 profit rebound looks like the start of a mix shift, not a blip. The bear framing: the overseas premium exists because markets are still open and competitors are still arriving; tariffs and local rivals can compress it just as China's price war compressed domestic margins. Meanwhile the company is spending heavily on plants abroad while cutting its dividend. The way to adjudicate is the monthly export number and the overseas share of gross profit in each half-year — both are published.
