01 Company Overview

Cardinal Health is the third of the three companies that move essentially all of the pharmaceuticals consumed in the United States. In fiscal 2026, ended June 30, it reported $254.25B of revenue, up 14.2%, and $1.71B of net income. Divide the second number by the first and you get a net profit margin of 0.67% — sixty-seven basis points. Gross margin for the whole enterprise is 3.84%; operating margin is 1.29%. Against its two peers, McKesson turns $410.98B of revenue into $4.59B of net income (1.12%) and Cencora turns $332.77B into $2.62B (0.79%). Together the three of them ran roughly $998B of revenue through the system over the last twelve months on combined net income of under $9B. This is not a margin business. It is a logistics utility that happens to be priced in dollars of drug.

The central point of this report, and the reason a reader who looks at the revenue line will misread the company entirely: revenue growth here is mostly a pass-through of what branded drug manufacturers charge, and it is nearly uncorrelated with profit. Cardinal buys a branded drug and resells it for a contractually fixed, low single-digit percentage — so a $10,000 specialty oncology vial produces roughly the same handful of dollars as a much cheaper one. Earnings come from somewhere else entirely: the generics program, where Cardinal buys generic molecules through the Red Oak Sourcing joint venture with CVS Health and captures the spread, and manufacturer brand price increases, where inventory already on the shelf marks up when a manufacturer raises list price. Fiscal 2026 makes the point cleanly. Revenue grew 14.2%. Non-GAAP operating earnings grew 30% and non-GAAP EPS grew 37%, to $11.26. The two lines are not describing the same thing.

The debate this page takes apart is whether the last two years represent a durable re-rating or a favourable point in a cycle. The stock has compounded at roughly 39% a year for five years and is up 64.2% over the last twelve months, which is an extraordinary outcome for a 0.67%-margin distributor. Three things drove it: the company absorbed the loss of a customer worth 17% of revenue without breaking, the long-broken Medical segment finally stopped bleeding, and specialty distribution — bought, not built — became the growth engine. Whether that justifies 34.2× trailing earnings against McKesson's 24.2× and Cencora's 24.6× is the open question.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
TRIOPOLY STRUCTURE RED OAK GENERIC SOURCING NEGATIVE WORKING CAPITAL DEA-LICENSED NETWORK NUCLEAR PHARMACY FOOTPRINT SWITCHING-COST INERTIA SCALE-ONLY ECONOMICS

The moat is structural, not proprietary. Three companies distribute almost all US pharmaceuticals because the economics only work at nine-figure unit volumes: at a 1.19% segment margin, there is no room for a fourth entrant to under-price anyone into existence, and the regulatory overhead — DEA licensing, pedigree tracking, controlled-substance monitoring, cold chain — is a fixed cost that only enormous throughput can absorb. That is a real barrier, and it is also the ceiling: the same math that keeps competitors out keeps returns thin.

Two features are worth isolating. First, Red Oak Sourcing, the generic-sourcing joint venture with CVS Health, aggregates generic purchasing across both parents and is the mechanism through which the generics program — the actual earnings engine — is negotiated. Second, and less discussed, the working capital model is the business. Cardinal's current ratio is 0.88 and working capital is negative $5.15B. In most industries those numbers would read as distress. Here they mean Cardinal collects from customers before it pays manufacturers — suppliers finance the inventory. That is why a company with 0.67% net margins produced $4.53B of free cash flow in FY2026, roughly 2.6× GAAP net income, and why ROIC is 18.36% despite razor-thin margins. Very little capital is actually invested.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer concentration — and this is the correction to the usual framing. Cardinal's fiscal 2026 annual report discloses that CVS Health accounted for 28% of consolidated revenue and the five largest customers 43%; members of the Vizient and Premier group purchasing organisations together represented a further 29%. OptumRx, often cited as the concentration risk here, is no longer one: Cardinal announced in April 2024 that its pharmaceutical distribution contracts with OptumRx would expire at the end of June 2024, and OptumRx represented 17% of consolidated revenue in fiscal 2024. That loss is already in the numbers — it is why FY2025 revenue fell 1.9% while earnings rose. The live risk today is CVS at 28%, not Optum, and the Red Oak joint venture means the largest customer and the generic-sourcing partner are the same counterparty.
▸
Opioid settlement payments are a permanent cash-flow claim. Cardinal paid $417M in fiscal 2026, including the fifth annual payment of $366M under the National Opioid Settlement Agreement, and made the sixth annual payment of $374M in July 2026, just after year-end. The remaining accrual at June 30, 2026 was $4.3B, with most payments running through 2038. FY2025 payments were $798M. This is roughly 8–9% of annual free cash flow, contractually committed for another twelve years, and it is a large part of why shareholders' equity is negative.
▸
Earnings depend on things Cardinal does not control. Manufacturer brand price increases and generic deflation rates are set by pharmaceutical manufacturers and by competitive dynamics in the generic market. When brand inflation is strong, inventory on the shelf appreciates and the quarter looks good; when generic deflation accelerates, the sourcing spread compresses. Neither is a management decision. The FY2026 result was helped by a favourable configuration of both — and management's own disclosure that GLP-1s and IRA pricing each move revenue by ~500bps in opposite directions shows how exposed the reported numbers are to policy and product mix.
▸
The specialty growth is bought, and the balance sheet shows it. Goodwill rose from $4.73B (June 2024) to $11.44B (June 2026) on $7.24B of acquisition spend across FY2025–FY2026, funded partly with debt: total debt went from $5.70B to $10.00B over the same window. Buying physician practices and specialty distributors is a different business from moving pallets, with different integration risk, different reimbursement exposure and different regulatory scrutiny. A goodwill impairment in this segment would rhyme uncomfortably with what happened to GMPD in fiscal 2022.

Notable One-Off Items

There are three, and they all matter to how the headline numbers should be read.

1. The GAAP / non-GAAP gap of $4.03 per share. FY2026 GAAP diluted EPS was $7.23; non-GAAP was $11.26. On 232.58M shares that is roughly $940M of excluded pre-tax-equivalent items — amortisation of acquired intangibles from the specialty roll-up, restructuring, litigation, and state opioid assessment adjustments (the last of these was explicitly identified in the release's non-GAAP reconciliation). Amortisation of purchased intangibles is a real economic cost of a roll-up strategy, even though it is non-cash. The trailing P/E of 34.19 is computed on the GAAP number and the forward P/E of 19.67 on the adjusted one — which is why the two multiples look like they belong to different companies.

2. The ~$100M GMPD tariff refund in Q4 FY2026. Reported Q4 GMPD segment profit was $150M against $70M a year earlier. Management disclosed the refund and put normalised Q4 profit at roughly $50M. That single item is about 39% of the segment's entire full-year reported profit of $258M. FY2027 guidance of $200–220M is set against the normalised base, not the reported one.

3. The OptumRx base effect. FY2025 revenue declined 1.9% to $222.58B because contracts representing 17% of FY2024 consolidated revenue expired on June 30, 2024. FY2026's +14.2% growth is measured off that depressed base and includes a full year of the acquired specialty businesses. Neither year's growth rate describes the underlying run-rate; FY2027 guidance of +3–5% Pharma revenue growth probably does.

Buyback and dividend. Cardinal repurchased $1,437M of stock in FY2026 (against $778M in FY2025 and $2,000M in FY2023) and on August 4, 2026 the board authorised a further $5.0B, taking total authority to $6.4B — over 11% of the current market capitalisation. FY2027 guidance assumes at least $1B of repurchase. Dividends paid were $491M. Note the ordering: buyback spend is roughly 3× dividend spend, and the quarterly dividend of $0.5158 yields 0.83%. This is a share-count-reduction story with a token income component, and the negative equity balance is a direct consequence of running it for a decade.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and on a monthly series like this one, a stock in a long uptrend can sit near 70 for years without that meaning anything is wrong.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $47.81 to the September 4, 2026 close of $247.18, CAH returned +417.0% on price alone — a compound annual rate of about 38.9% before dividends, which added roughly another point a year. For a company with a 0.67% net margin and a beta of 0.52, that is one of the more improbable five-year charts on the New York Stock Exchange, and it is almost entirely a multiple story layered on a genuine earnings recovery: FY2022 was a $938M net loss and FY2026 was $1.71B of net income, with GAAP EPS going from −$3.37 to +$7.23.

The path was not smooth in the middle. The stock bottomed at $46.23 in November 2021, when opioid litigation was unresolved and GMPD was actively destroying value, and did not clear $100 until late 2023. It then compounded relentlessly through 2024 and 2025 with only shallow pullbacks, and re-priced violently upward in autumn 2025 — from $156.96 in September 2025 to $212.26 in November 2025, +35% in two months. The only meaningful drawdown of the last two years was February to April 2026, $229.23 to $192.88, −15.9%, fully recovered by June. The current print of $247.18 is 4.3% below the 52-week high of $258.30.

Relative performance. A precise five-year total-return comparison against the S&P 500 and the NASDAQ-100 could not be verified from the sources fetched and is deliberately not stated. What is verified, over the trailing twelve months, is the comparison that this report exists to make: CAH's market capitalisation rose 59.9%, McKesson's rose 23.2%, and Cencora's rose 10.4%. Cardinal has been the best-performing of the three big distributors by a wide margin over the past year — after being the worst for most of the preceding decade. That is a mean-reversion trade that has already happened, and it is why the valuation gap has inverted: the laggard now trades at 34.2× trailing earnings against 24.2× and 24.6× for the two companies that never broke.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The earnings algorithm no longer depends on revenue. Cardinal grew non-GAAP EPS 37% in a year when it was still lapping the loss of a customer worth 17% of revenue, because generics and specialty — not volume — drive profit. Specialty revenue grew over 25%, "Other" earns a 10.4% segment margin and is guided to 15–18% profit growth, and GMPD has finally been de-risked to a $200–220M floor. Behind it sits $4.53B of free cash flow, an 18.4% ROIC, a supplier-financed working-capital model that requires almost no invested capital, and a $6.4B buyback authorisation against a $57.5B market cap. Guidance of $12.40–$12.60 puts the stock at under 20× forward earnings.

Bear Case

You are paying a 40% premium to McKesson and Cencora for the weakest net margin of the three (0.67% vs 1.12% and 0.79%) and the only negative equity balance. The FY2026 result flatters itself: a ~$100M one-time tariff refund inflated GMPD, a depressed FY2025 base inflated revenue growth, and $4.03 per share of the earnings story lives outside GAAP — much of it amortisation from a $7.2B acquisition spree that took goodwill to $11.44B and debt to $10.0B. The generics spread and brand price inflation that drive profit are set by other people. Meanwhile $4.3B of opioid payments run to 2038, CVS is 28% of revenue, the average price target sits 9.6% away, and every analyst raised at once.

Top 3 Risks

01
Customer concentration at CVS Health — 28% of revenue. The five largest customers are 43% of revenue and GPO members another 29%. Cardinal has already demonstrated what a contract loss looks like: OptumRx, 17% of FY2024 revenue, walked in June 2024. The company survived that because the lost revenue carried almost no margin — but a renegotiation of the CVS relationship would touch both the distribution contract and the Red Oak generic-sourcing joint venture that is the actual earnings engine. That is a different kind of exposure.
02
The specialty roll-up is now the growth story, and it is unproven. $7.24B of acquisitions across two years, goodwill up from $4.73B to $11.44B, debt up from $5.70B to $10.00B. Physician-practice management carries reimbursement risk, integration risk and regulatory scrutiny that pallet-moving does not. Cardinal has impaired a segment before — GMPD in fiscal 2022, which produced a $938M loss year and a decade of discount.
03
Valuation has no cushion left. 34.2× trailing earnings against peers at ~24×, 90% of the way up the 52-week range, an average price target 9.6% above spot with a low target below spot, and a covering panel that raised targets unanimously in a single week in August. Nothing about the operating business has to go wrong for a multiple this far above its own five-year history to compress.
  Industry Commentary
Drug pricing policy is now a direct input to the P&L
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Management quantified this on the Q4 call in a way most companies do not: Inflation Reduction Act price changes and GLP-1 growth are each worth approximately 500 basis points of Pharma segment revenue, pulling in opposite directions. The IRA's negotiated Medicare prices cut the list price of exactly the high-dollar branded drugs that generate the most revenue per unit shipped — and because distributor compensation on branded product is partly a percentage of price, a lower list price is a smaller fee for identical logistics work. The offsetting force, GLP-1 obesity and diabetes drugs, is enormous in dollars and thin in margin, which inflates revenue while doing comparatively little for profit. The net effect is that Cardinal's top line is now partly a policy variable, and a reader watching revenue is watching Washington and Novo Nordisk rather than watching Cardinal.

The opioid settlement is a twelve-year annuity paid by the distributors
ONGOING HEADWIND

All three distributors carry this, and it is a structural feature of the industry rather than a Cardinal problem. For Cardinal specifically: $417M paid in fiscal 2026 (including the $366M fifth annual National Opioid Settlement payment), a $374M sixth payment in July 2026, and $4.3B still accrued with most of it running through 2038. FY2025 payments were $798M. Against $4.53B of free cash flow, roughly 8–9% of annual cash generation is spoken for before a dollar reaches shareholders — and it is senior to both the dividend and the $6.4B buyback authorisation. It is also a direct contributor to the negative $2.72B equity balance, which is why conventional leverage and return-on-equity ratios simply do not compute for this company.

Specialty and community oncology: the whole industry is buying the same thing
EMERGING TAILWIND

All three distributors are pushing capital into specialty distribution and physician-practice services, because that is where a distributor can earn a fee for something other than moving a box: data, practice management, reimbursement support, site-of-care services. Cardinal grew specialty revenue over 25% in FY2026 and has spent $7.24B across two fiscal years buying into urology (Solaris Health), diabetes supply (Advanced Diabetes Supply, Strive Medical, the announced AdaptHealth Diabetes Health business) and adjacent categories. McKesson has been doing the same — it announced a $2.25B acquisition of Precision Medicine Group — and Cencora has its own oncology franchise.

Bull versus bear on this point. The bull reading: specialty carries structurally better margins than commodity distribution, the volume growth is demographic and therefore durable, and Cardinal is buying into it with cash flow that costs almost nothing to generate. The bear reading: three well-capitalised buyers competing for the same finite set of specialty practices is how acquisition multiples get bid up, and Cardinal — the smallest of the three, with the weakest balance sheet and the most goodwill added relative to its size — is the one that can least afford to overpay. The FY2027 print on Pharma segment profit growth of 8–11%, and specifically Solaris Health's guided 2–3 point contribution to it, is the first real evidence either way.
