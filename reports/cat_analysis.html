01 Company Overview

Caterpillar is the world's largest manufacturer of construction and mining equipment, and — increasingly — one of the largest suppliers of on-site electrical power generation on the planet. It sells excavators, dozers, wheel loaders, mining trucks, reciprocating engines, gas turbines and locomotives through a network of 150 independent dealers (41 in the U.S., 109 abroad) serving 190 countries, and finances a large share of those machines through its own captive lender, Cat Financial. FY2025 revenue was $67.6B, up 4.3% YoY, but that headline badly understates the current pace: trailing-twelve-month revenue is now $77.8B (+20.3%), and Q3 2026’s $20.7B was the biggest quarter in company history, just ahead of Q2’s $20.5B. The company holds roughly 15.2% of the $246.5B global construction-equipment market — nearly 40% more share than second-place Komatsu — and at a $286B market cap ranks #38 in the S&P 500 (our calculation at the Oct 30 close; #28 in August).

What investors will pay for each dollar of Caterpillar’s profit is the story. Two years ago it was a late-cycle machinery stock: its October 2024 close of $376.20 was about 17x its 2024 earnings per share of $22.05. By June 2026 the price had nearly tripled, because the Power & Energy segment — large reciprocating engines and turbines that data centers install on site to make their own power, off the grid (“behind the meter”) — had turned a cyclical industrial into an AI-infrastructure supplier. Since then the stock has fallen 42% from its June high to $622.75, and the price-to-earnings ratio has come down from 36.9x in our August edition to 24.4x. The business did not shrink: order backlog hit a record $78.1B in Q3 2026, which CEO Joe Creed put at “an increase of approximately $38 billion or 96% from the third quarter last year.” What fell was the price of growth that is limited by factory capacity and, on management’s own guide, comes with margins near the bottom of its target range this year. Whether the remaining premium holds is the investment debate.

Capacity Reservation Lock-In

Caterpillar's moat is not the machine — it is everything around the machine. 150 independent dealers, most of them multi-generational family businesses, operate thousands of service locations across 190 countries; a mine in Chile or a contractor in Indonesia buys Cat partly because a technician and a part can reach the site within hours, and downtime on a $6M mining truck costs far more than the price premium. That network sustains about $24B a year of services revenue (2025, per management on its Q4 2025 call) at structurally higher margins than new equipment, and it is close to unreplicable — Chinese entrants can match the steel, not thirty years of dealer capital. The Power & Energy franchise adds a second, newer moat: emissions-certified large engines are slow to design and slower to certify, and Caterpillar is now taking large-engine orders as far out as 2029 and turbine orders into 2030, so buyers are reserving capacity years ahead rather than shopping on price. Backlog at a record $78.1B at the end of Q3 2026 (58% expected to ship within twelve months, per the call) is the moat made visible.

Key Vulnerabilities

Cyclicality — the oldest problem, now hidden. Construction and mining capex are among the most cyclical spending lines in the economy, and because so much of Caterpillar's cost is fixed, profit swings harder than sales in both directions: FY2025 adjusted EPS fell 13% to $19.06 on a mere volume dip plus tariffs. The record backlog masks this, but a backlog is a promise, not a contract in the strict sense — orders placed for 2029–2030 delivery can be deferred if the AI capex cycle cools.

Chinese competition and export share. XCMG ($14.2B) has passed Deere to become the world's #3 construction-equipment maker and Sany is close behind; both compete aggressively on price across Asia, Africa, Latin America and the Middle East — markets where the dealer-network premium is thinnest. Caterpillar has already ceded meaningful share in China itself, and each cycle of Chinese overcapacity exports deflation into Caterpillar's non-U.S. pricing.

Data-center concentration and permitting backlash. The segment carrying the valuation is exposed to a small number of hyperscaler customers and to a policy environment turning hostile: New York enacted a data-center moratorium, Texas froze new project approvals pending a state audit in August 2026, and water, zoning and tax-incentive fights are spreading. Baird's July 29 downgrade cited this as a reason investors would pay less for each dollar of earnings, and the stock fell ~7% that day; reporting the Q3 print, Reuters noted that sentiment has weakened amid growing opposition to data centers. Concentration risk plus regulatory risk in the same revenue line is a genuinely poor combination.

Technology disruption and trade policy. Two slower-burning threats sit under the moat. First, electrification and autonomy are re-opening a product category that has been closed for decades: battery-electric compact machines invite new entrants who do not need Caterpillar's engine expertise, and autonomous haulage shifts value from iron toward software and fleet-management platforms where Komatsu, Epiroc and pure-play technology vendors compete on different terms. Caterpillar leads today with Cat MineStar and a large autonomous fleet, but leadership in a software category is a very different defensive proposition than leadership in diesel engines. Second, trade policy is an ongoing structural cost: roughly $1.8B of tariffs in 2025 and ~$2.2B guided for 2026 against FY2025 operating profit near $11B. Pricing has recovered part of it ($663M of favorable price realization in Q3 2026 alone), but pass-through depends on a demand environment strong enough to absorb it — and demand environments change faster than tariff schedules.

Notable One-Off Items: Trailing figures are unusually distorted. Tariffs are the dominant item: roughly $1.8B of cost in 2025 (raised through the year from an initial $1.3–1.5B estimate) and guided at approximately $2.2B gross in 2026 (unchanged at the Q3 print), partially offset by IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) tariff recoveries of $392M in Q2 and $128M in Q3 2026 — refunds the company does not count on: the outlook assumes none in the fourth quarter. The $128M lifted Q3’s 20.6% adjusted operating margin by about 0.6 percentage points. Restructuring cost $41M in Q3 2026 ($0.07 a share, excluded from adjusted profit). In FY2025 restructuring cost $444M, of which $319M landed in Q4, including write-downs in the Rail division. Caterpillar also books an annual Q4 mark-to-market remeasurement of pension and OPEB plans, which routinely swings fourth-quarter GAAP results by hundreds of millions in either direction and is the main reason GAAP and adjusted EPS diverge most in Q4. From $204.01 at the end of October 2021, CAT spent 2022 as a rate-shock casualty, bottoming at $164.08 in September 2022 before the industrial re-opening trade lifted it through 2023. Phase two (2023 through late 2024) was a conventional, well-executed machinery bull market: pricing power, buybacks and margin expansion carried the stock from ~$230 to a November 2024 peak of $406.11 — and then it broke. April 2025 saw a tariff-shock low of $309.27, down 24% from the peak, as the market handicapped a $1.5–1.8B cost hit against decelerating machine volumes. Phase three began in the autumn of 2025 and is unlike anything in the company's history: as hyperscalers discovered that grid interconnection queues were longer than their build schedules, Caterpillar's reciprocating engines became a scarce AI input. The stock went $477 (Sep 25) → $577 (Oct 25) → $657 (Jan 26) → $890 (Apr 26) → $1,064.90 (Jun 26), printing an intraday high of $1,073.46 on June 30. Since then it has given back much of it: −23.5% in July on the Baird downgrade and data-center permitting backlash, a roughly flat August and September around $800, and −23.2% in October, when it slid 12% into the Q3 print and 12.7% in the two sessions after it. Total 5-year price return: +205% ($204.01 on Oct 29, 2021 → $622.75 on Oct 30, 2026, dividends excluded), versus +64.6% for SPY (an S&P 500 index fund) and +78.8% for QQQ (a Nasdaq-100 fund) over the same dates. CAT has still returned about three times the S&P 500 fund — but the stock now sits 42% below its high, back near its early-2026 levels.

Momentum read. The 6-period monthly RSI spent ten straight months above 70, from September 2025 through June 2026 — a long overbought stretch and a sign of a crowded trade. July's drawdown pulled it back to about 56, where it sat through September; October's fall took it to 38, close to but not in oversold territory. The last monthly readings below 35 came at the April 2025 tariff low (34) and the September 2022 trough (34); the April 2025 close of $309.27 has still doubled since. Daily data look more stretched: the 14-day RSI was 23.6 at the Oct 30 close, below the oversold line of 30, with the price 20.5% below its 50-day average ($783.30) and 23.1% below its 200-day average ($809.91).

Relative performance. Over five years CAT is about 141 percentage points ahead of SPY, but the last twelve months have reversed the picture: CAT is up 6.8% (Oct 30, 2025 close $583.15 → $622.75), behind SPY's +11.2% and QQQ's +10.3% over the same dates. Measured from the end of September 2025, when the data-center thesis took hold, CAT is still up 30.5%; from the June 2026 close it is down 41.5%. The premium investors pay for CAT's earnings has come down by a third since August, but at 24.4x it is still well above the ~17x of late 2024 — a buyer today is still paying for the power business to keep growing.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

Caterpillar has become the physical bottleneck in AI infrastructure: grid interconnection queues run years long, so hyperscalers buy their own power, and large reciprocating engines and turbines are capacity-constrained through 2030. A record $78.1B backlog (up 96% in a year, 58% expected to ship within twelve months) plus six separate gigawatt-scale customer agreements gives Caterpillar unusual visibility on its revenue for a machinery company. Meanwhile the cyclical businesses are growing at the same time — Construction +18% and Resource +15% in Q3 — while large-engine capacity heads toward nearly 3x its 2024 level and the program is funded from $9.6B of trailing free cash flow. After a 42% fall from the high, you pay 24.4x trailing and 19.3x 2027 consensus earnings, with a 1.13 PEG, 56% ROE and 32 straight years of dividend increases: a much lower price than in June for an entrenched franchise with a fortress distribution moat.

▼ Bear Case

You are still paying 24.4x trailing earnings for a company that gets about half its segment sales from construction and mining cycles, at a beta of 1.64. 2026 showed how fast this unwinds: one downgrade on permitting policy — not orders, not earnings — took 7% out in a day and July closed down 23%; then a quarter that beat on sales, profit and backlog cost 12.7% in two sessions because the full-year guide did not rise. Data-center backlash is spreading from New York to Texas, roughly $2.2B of annual tariff cost is structural, and the 2027–2029 capacity build commits capital years before the revenue arrives. If AI capex normalizes even modestly, CAT is left with nearly tripled engine capacity, a lower multiple, and two cyclical segments facing tough comparisons. Eight firms cut their targets the day after the print, Morgan Stanley to $675 — barely above the price.

Top 3 Risks
01
An AI spending slowdown and a lower multiple. The gap between CAT at 24.4x and the ~17x it traded at in late 2024 is about 30% of the share price. Any credible sign that the big cloud companies are buying less power equipment — order deferrals, a capex cut, or simply cooling enthusiasm for AI stocks — removes the premium far faster than backlog can be delivered. A beta of 1.64 means the stock has moved about 1.6 times as much as the market, and in 2026 its biggest falls came on data-center news and its own outlook, not on housing starts.
02
Data-center policy and permitting. New York's moratorium and Texas's August 2026 approval freeze pending a state audit are the leading edge of a nationwide fight over water, grid capacity, zoning and tax incentives. Baird's downgrade thesis is that this lowers what investors will pay for each dollar of earnings regardless of near-term orders — and the July price action proved the market agrees. Concentration in a handful of hyperscaler customers amplifies it.
03
Tariffs, trade policy and cost pass-through. Roughly $1.8B of tariff cost in 2025 and ~$2.2B guided for 2026 against ~$11B of FY2025 operating profit. Q2 and Q3 margins were helped by $520M of IEEPA tariff recoveries ($392M and $128M), and the outlook assumes none in the fourth quarter. Pass-through has worked while demand is red-hot; in a softer order environment the same tariffs would hit margins directly — and further trade escalation, or an adverse legal outcome on tariff refunds, would widen the hit.
  Industry Commentary: The Macro Backdrop
Data-Center Power Demand: A Genuinely New Growth Vector
EMERGING TAILWIND
U.S. electricity demand is rising for the first time in roughly two decades, and the constraint is not generation capacity in aggregate — it is time. Utility interconnection queues stretch years, so hyperscalers increasingly build behind-the-meter generation: gas reciprocating engines and turbines sited at the data center. That is Caterpillar's product. Power Generation sales rose 30% in Q3 2026 to $3.42B; as of the Q2 call, large-engine backlog was up more than 3.5x since January 2024, six customer agreements each covered 1 GW or more, and on the Q3 call management said it is taking large-engine orders as far out as 2029 and turbine orders into 2030. Peers confirm the same picture from different angles — GE Vernova's gas-turbine backlog and slot reservations reached 116 GW at the end of June (it expects at least 125 GW by year-end) as its total orders rose 88% organically, and Cummins said on its Q2 call that it is generally selling new power-generation equipment into the second half of 2028. This is not a Caterpillar-specific narrative; it is an industry-wide capacity shortage. The analytical caution: an industry-wide shortage invites industry-wide capacity addition, and Caterpillar, Cummins, GE Vernova, Mitsubishi and Wärtsilä are all expanding at once into a demand curve nobody can forecast past 2028.
Tariffs: A $2B+ Annual Tax on a Globally-Sourced Manufacturer
ONGOING HEADWIND
Caterpillar builds in the U.S., Europe, Japan, China, India, Brazil and Mexico, and moves components across all of them — which makes it one of the most tariff-exposed large-cap industrials in America. The 2025 hit escalated through the year from an initial $1.3–1.5B estimate to roughly $1.8B, and drove the $1.03B of unfavorable manufacturing costs that crushed Q4 2025 margins (13.9% GAAP operating margin, down from 18.0%). For 2026, guidance is approximately $2.2B gross — trimmed from $2.5B — with $392M of IEEPA recoveries booked in Q2, $128M more in Q3, and none assumed for the fourth quarter. Section 232 duties on construction equipment and parts, at rates up to 50%, sit on top of that. Pricing has offset a meaningful share ($663M of favorable price realization in Q3 2026), and adjusted operating margin still rose 3.1 percentage points to 20.6% in Q3 — but that only works while the order book is this strong. The honest read: tariffs are currently a manageable, disclosed, quantified headwind; in a demand downturn they become an unhedged margin problem.
The Old Cycle Is Turning Too — and That Is the Real Bull/Bear Divide
CYCLICAL INFLECTION — FRAMES THE DEBATE
Lost in the AI story: the traditional businesses are inflecting simultaneously. Global construction equipment hit a record $246.5B in 2025 (+3.8%), Construction Industries sales rose 18% in Q3 2026 after 35% in Q2, and Resource Industries grew 15% as mining fleets bought in the 2011–2013 supercycle finally age out against copper and critical-minerals demand. Infrastructure spending and data-center site work are reinforcing each other — every gigawatt of compute needs earthmoving before it needs engines. The frame for investors: the bull argues you are getting a cyclical recovery and a secular power franchise at the same time, with a $78B backlog, 19.6% ROIC and 32 years of dividend growth underwriting the wait. The bear argues you are buying two peaks at once — a construction cycle already well into its upswing and an AI capex cycle whose first policy speed bump cost the stock 23% in a month — at 24x trailing earnings and a 1.64 beta, with margins guided near the bottom of the company’s range. Both are describing the same $286B company. The difference is not the analysis of Caterpillar; it is what you assume about hyperscaler capital spending in 2028, and that assumption, not the stock selection, is the actual wager.
