01 Company Overview

Chubb Limited is the world's largest publicly traded property and casualty (P&C) insurer by market capitalization, underwriting commercial and personal property, casualty, specialty, accident & health, agricultural and life insurance in 54 countries. Full-year 2025 revenue was $59.63B, up 6.5% YoY, on consolidated net premiums written of $54.84B (+6.6%), producing record net income of $10.31B and a record-low combined ratio of 85.7%. At $131.5B of market value it ranks roughly #175 among all listed companies globally, and it is the largest constituent of the U.S. P&C insurance complex by market cap.

The business model is deliberately two-engined. Engine one is underwriting: Chubb has run a combined ratio in the mid-80s while the U.S. industry average sat near 92.9%, a roughly nine-point structural advantage that compounds. Engine two is investing: an invested asset base that generated $6.95B of adjusted net investment income in 2025 (+9%) and a record $1.88B in Q2 2026 (+11.4%) as maturing low-coupon bonds are reinvested at materially higher yields. The Asia-weighted life insurance franchise — an unusual asset for a P&C carrier — grew net premiums 15.1% in 2025 and is the fastest-growing piece of the company.

The moat is not a product — it is the ability to price risk better than the next carrier and to prove it over decades. Chubb's Q2 2026 P&C combined ratio of 83.8% versus a U.S. industry average near 92.9% is roughly nine points of structural advantage; on ~$48B of annual P&C net premiums that gap is worth billions of pre-tax dollars a year that competitors simply do not earn. Three things sustain it. First, licensing and local paper in 54 countries — a multinational manufacturer needs admitted coverage in every jurisdiction it operates, and only a handful of carriers can issue it, which is why Chubb wins large global programmes on capability rather than price. Second, a $80.9B equity base and 18.2x interest coverage that make Chubb an acceptable counterparty for the longest-tail liabilities, where a claim may not be paid for twenty years. Third, in high-net-worth personal lines, a claims and appraisal organisation that affluent clients will pay a premium for — evidenced by the segment's 67.3% combined ratio, a number that is impossible to earn on price competition alone. Underpinning all of it is a proven willingness to shrink: North America Commercial premiums fell 2.3% and Global Reinsurance fell 6.7% in the most recent quarter because management would not match market pricing. Insurers that lack that discipline grow into soft markets and pay for it three years later in reserve strengthening.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
Catastrophe severity and climate concentration. FY2025 pre-tax catastrophe losses were $2.92B, up from $2.39B in 2024, and the January 2025 California wildfires alone cost $1.47B pre-tax — a single event equal to roughly 14% of a year's net income. Chubb's affluent-homeowner book is concentrated in exactly the coastal and wildland-urban-interface geographies where insured values are highest and loss frequency is rising. Reinsurance transfers some of this, but at a price that itself rises after every bad year.
B
A softening commercial pricing cycle. Marsh's global commercial composite fell 6% in Q2 2026, the eighth straight quarterly decline, with property down 12% globally and 13% in the U.S. Chubb's response is to shrink — North America Commercial premiums fell 2.3% — which protects margin but caps top-line growth. If soft conditions persist for several more years, either premium growth stays negative or the company eventually writes business at ratios well above today's mid-80s.
C
U.S. casualty reserve risk and social inflation. U.S. casualty was the only line still taking rate in Q2 2026 (+7%, and +11% excluding workers' compensation) precisely because loss severity is running hot on litigation funding, nuclear verdicts and medical inflation. Chubb has reported favorable prior-period development every recent period ($1.13B in FY2025, $283M in Q2 2026), but that is a backward-looking comfort: the accident years most exposed to social inflation are still developing, and the industry has a long history of discovering casualty deficiencies late.
D
Key-man and succession concentration. Evan Greenberg has been CEO since 2004 and Chairman since 2007 — a 22-year tenure over which the ACE/Chubb combination and the current underwriting culture were built, and a substantial part of the stock's premium-to-book valuation is attributed to him personally. Chubb has not publicly named a designated successor, so succession remains an unresolved governance question rather than a disclosed plan; any announcement is likely to be a first-order share-price event in either direction.

Notable One-Off Items

Four categories of non-recurring or volatile items distort the reported numbers, and all four matter for CB.

1. Catastrophe losses. FY2025 pre-tax catastrophe losses of $2.92B (vs $2.39B in FY2024) included $1.47B from the January 2025 California wildfires — $1.30B after tax, or $3.21 per share, and 15.9 combined-ratio points in Q1 2025 alone. That single quarter is the reason trailing EPS growth now looks like +24.7%: the comparison base is artificially depressed. Q2 2026 catastrophe losses fell to $475M from $630M a year earlier, a genuinely benign quarter that should not be extrapolated through hurricane season.

2. Prior-year reserve development. Chubb released $1.13B of favorable prior-period development in FY2025 (vs $856M in FY2024) and $283M in Q2 2026. Reserve releases flow straight to underwriting income and flatter the combined ratio; excluding them, the underlying result is several points worse than the headline. The direction has been consistently favorable, which is a genuine positive, but investors should treat releases as a lower-quality earnings component than current-accident-year margin.

3. Realized investment gains and losses. The gap between GAAP net income and core operating income isolates these. In FY2025 net income of $10.31B exceeded core operating income of $9.95B by roughly $360M, i.e. realized/mark-to-market items added about 3.5% to reported earnings. In Q2 2026 the gap was negligible — net income $2.854B versus core operating income of approximately $2.84B ($7.30 vs $7.26 per share). Core operating income is the number to track.

4. Currency translation. With roughly 40% of net premiums written outside North America, published growth and constant-dollar growth diverge. Chubb discloses both — in Q1 2025, for instance, consolidated premium growth was reported at 5.7% in constant dollars — and the difference is the FX effect. The specific constant-dollar figures for Q2 2026 could not be verified from a live source here, so no FX adjustment is quantified in this page; readers comparing quarters should check the constant-dollar column in Chubb's release.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Above 70 means the stock has risen far and fast and may be due a pause; below 30 means the opposite. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Reading the momentum panel. The 6-period monthly RSI currently sits near 64 — elevated but short of the 70 overbought threshold, consistent with a stock in an established uptrend that has paused rather than one that has become stretched. The two most informative readings of the period were the sub-30 prints around the September 2022 and May 2023 lows, both of which preceded multi-month rallies, and the deterioration through mid-2025 as the wildfire and soft-pricing narratives compounded. Momentum has been recovering steadily since the July 2025 low without yet reaching the extremes that historically preceded consolidation.

Over the 60 months from September 2021 to August 2026 CB went from $173.48 to $340.92, a gain of +96.5% on a split- and dividend-adjusted basis, or roughly 14.5% annualised. That is a striking result for a stock with a beta of 0.39 — the return profile of an equity-market compounder with the volatility profile of something closer to credit. The path, however, was not smooth, and it maps almost perfectly onto the insurance underwriting cycle rather than onto the broader market.

The 2022 stretch was flat-to-down in absolute terms but sharply positive in relative terms: while the S&P 500 fell through the year, CB troughed at $181.88 in September 2022 and then rallied hard into year-end as investors rotated into hard-market insurers with rising rates and rising reinvestment yields. 2023 was a consolidation year — the stock round-tripped from $227.49 in January to $185.80 in May amid the regional-bank stress before recovering to $226.00 in December. The genuine breakout came in early 2024, when the stock cleared $245 in January and never looked back, driven by record underwriting margins and an investment portfolio repricing into higher yields. 2025 delivered the sharpest drawdown of the period: from $301.99 in March to $266.04 in July, a 12% fall as the January wildfire losses, softening commercial rates and a competitive property market compressed sentiment. The recovery from that low has been almost uninterrupted — up more than 28% to $340.92 — as FY2025 delivered a record 85.7% combined ratio and Q2 2026 an 83.8%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Chubb is compounding book value at double digits — BVPS $195.45 (+12.3%) and TBVPS $131.93 (+17.1%) — while running an 83.8% combined ratio roughly nine points better than the U.S. industry average, and it is doing so while shrinking the business it judges underpriced. The second engine is accelerating independently of the pricing cycle: adjusted net investment income hit a record $1.88B in Q2 2026 (+11.4%) as low-coupon bonds roll into higher yields, a tailwind that persists for years regardless of premium rates. Add a 21.2% core operating ROTE, 34 consecutive years of dividend growth on a 14% payout, $1.37B of quarterly capital return, Berkshire Hathaway as a ~$10.7B holder, and a 1.74x book multiple below the peer average, and the setup is a defensive compounder trading at 12x earnings.

Bear Case

The growth engine has stalled where it matters most: North America Commercial net premiums written fell 2.3% and Global Reinsurance fell 6.7%, with Marsh's global commercial composite down for eight straight quarters (property −12%). Margin this good is cyclically peak, not structurally permanent — 83.8% follows an unusually light $475M catastrophe quarter and is flattered by $283M of favorable reserve releases, neither of which is repeatable at will. Reported ROE of 14.8% badly lags peers, U.S. casualty severity is running hot enough that rates still rise 11% ex-comp, and the wildfire quarter demonstrated that a single event can cost 16 combined-ratio points. At 12.1x trailing earnings and 1.74x book, with the sell side majority-Hold and a $366 average target implying just 7% upside, much of the good news is priced.

Top 3 Risks

01
A prolonged soft commercial market that forces the choice between growth and margin. Eight consecutive quarters of global rate decline with property down 12% means the current mid-80s combined ratio is being defended by shrinking the book. If softening runs another two or three years, either premium contraction accelerates and the earnings base erodes, or Chubb writes at materially worse ratios. Neither outcome supports the current multiple.
02
Catastrophe concentration in high-value property. $2.92B of catastrophe losses in FY2025 versus $2.39B in 2024, with $1.47B from one wildfire event, in a book deliberately concentrated in the most expensive homes in the most exposed geographies. Rising climate loss frequency raises both the expected loss and the cost of the reinsurance that caps it. A single major landfall in the current hurricane season would reset the Q3 narrative immediately.
03
Casualty reserve adequacy against social inflation — compounded by succession risk. U.S. casualty rates rose 7% (11% ex-workers' comp) because loss severity is outrunning expectations industry-wide; Chubb's recent favorable development of $1.13B in FY2025 reflects older accident years, not the ones currently at risk. Reserve strengthening at any large casualty writer would hit both earnings and the sector multiple. Layered on top, a 22-year CEO tenure with no publicly designated successor means the culture that produces the industry's best combined ratio is not yet demonstrably institutionalised.
  Industry Commentary
The commercial P&C cycle has turned soft
ONGOING HEADWIND
Marsh's Global Insurance Market Index shows the global commercial composite rate falling 6% in Q2 2026, the eighth consecutive quarterly decline, after a 5% fall in Q1 and following seven years of increases that ended around mid-2024. Property led the way down at −12% globally and −13% in the U.S., a third straight quarter of double-digit declines; cyber fell 4% for its twelfth consecutive decline; financial and professional lines fell 3%. Regionally, IMEA fell 16%, Pacific 13%, LAC 9%, UK 8%, Europe 6%, Asia 5% and the U.S. just 2%. The cause is abundant capacity, strong insurer profitability and cheap reinsurance — the classic self-correcting mechanism of the cycle. The one exception is U.S. casualty, up 7% (and 11% excluding workers' compensation), where litigation funding and nuclear verdicts are still pushing severity higher. For Chubb this cuts both ways: property softening explains the −2.3% North America Commercial premium decline, while casualty firming supports its large liability book.
Catastrophe frequency and the climate loss trend
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Chubb's own catastrophe load rose from $2.39B in FY2024 to $2.92B in FY2025 — a 22% increase in a single year — and the January 2025 California wildfires alone produced $1.47B of pre-tax losses ($1.30B after tax, $3.21 per share, 15.9 combined-ratio points in Q1 2025). That event was not a coastal windstorm but a wildland-urban-interface fire in an affluent suburb, which is precisely the exposure profile of Chubb's high-net-worth homeowners franchise. The structural problem is that insured values in these geographies are rising faster than general inflation while loss frequency is rising too, so the same physical event costs more each cycle. Chubb has repriced and tightened concentration limits — the segment's 67.3% Q2 2026 combined ratio and 6.0% premium growth suggest the repositioning is working — but the company has not published a segment-level exposure-reduction disclosure, so the durability of that fix cannot be independently verified. Q2 2026 catastrophe losses of $475M versus $630M a year earlier reflect a benign quarter, not a solved problem.
Higher-for-longer rates are repricing the float
EMERGING TAILWIND
This is the offset the bear case tends to underweight. Adjusted net investment income reached a record $6.95B in FY2025, up 9%, and a record $1.88B in Q2 2026, up 11.4%, with pre-tax net investment income up 12.3% to $1.76B. The mechanism is mechanical and slow-moving: bonds bought during the zero-rate era mature at par and are reinvested at today's materially higher yields, so investment income keeps rising for several years even if central-bank policy rates plateau or fall modestly. Because underwriting margin and investment income are largely independent drivers, this stream partially insulates earnings from the soft premium cycle. Bull framing: a growing float ($15.4B of annual free cash flow, $39.85 per share) reinvested at higher yields can carry EPS growth for several years even if net premiums written go flat or negative, which is exactly what makes the 12x multiple defensible. Bear framing: investment income is a lower-quality, more market-dependent earnings source than underwriting profit; if rates fall materially the tailwind fades just as the premium cycle bottoms, leaving both engines decelerating at once — and at 1.74x book with only 7% consensus upside, the stock has limited cushion for that scenario.
