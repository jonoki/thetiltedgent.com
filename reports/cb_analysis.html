01 Company Overview

Chubb Limited is a Zurich-based property and casualty (P&C) insurer that describes itself as a world leader in insurance, underwriting commercial and personal property, casualty, specialty, accident & health, agricultural and life insurance in 54 countries and territories. Full-year 2025 revenue was $59.63B, up 6.5% YoY, on consolidated net premiums written of $54.84B (+6.6%), producing record net income of $10.31B and a record-low combined ratio of 85.7%. In the third quarter of 2026 (reported October 20) it earned a record $7.66 of core operating income per share (Chubb's measure that leaves out investment gains and losses), against a Nasdaq consensus of $6.53, on an 84.1% combined ratio. At $139.2B of market value it ranks #87 in the S&P 500.

The business model is deliberately two-engined. Engine one is underwriting: Chubb has run a combined ratio in the mid-80s while the U.S. industry came in at about 93% for 2025 (AM Best), a roughly nine-point advantage that compounds. Engine two is investing: an invested asset base that generated $6.95B of adjusted net investment income in 2025 (+9%) and a record $1.89B in Q3 2026 (+6.3%) as maturing low-coupon bonds are reinvested at higher yields. The Asia-weighted life insurance franchise — an unusual asset for a P&C carrier — grew net premiums 15.1% in 2025; in Q3 2026 its net premiums rose 1.4% (3.7% in constant dollars), or 15.2% counting deposits on universal life and investment contracts, which are not counted as revenue.

The moat is not a product — it is the ability to price risk better than the next carrier and to prove it over decades. Chubb's Q3 2026 P&C combined ratio of 84.1% against a U.S. industry figure of about 93% for 2025 (AM Best) is roughly nine points of advantage; on ~$48B of annual P&C net premiums (FY2025) that gap is worth billions of pre-tax dollars a year that competitors simply do not earn. Three things sustain it. First, licensed local insurers in 54 countries and territories — a multinational manufacturer needs coverage from a locally licensed insurer in every country it operates in, and only a handful of carriers can issue it, which is why Chubb wins large global programmes on capability rather than price. Second, $73.2B of shareholders' equity and interest coverage of about 17x that make Chubb an acceptable counterparty for the longest-tail liabilities, where a claim may not be paid for twenty years. Third, in high-net-worth personal lines, a claims and appraisal organisation that affluent clients will pay a premium for — evidenced by the segment's 60.3% Q3 combined ratio, a number that is impossible to earn on price competition alone. Underpinning all of it is a proven willingness to shrink: North America Commercial premiums fell 2.7% and Global Reinsurance fell 5.6% in the most recent quarter; Greenberg said in the release that the businesses not growing were "flat or purposely shrinking due to inadequate pricing or terms." Insurers that lack that discipline grow into soft markets and pay for it three years later in reserve strengthening.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
Catastrophe severity and climate concentration. FY2025 pre-tax catastrophe losses were $2.92B, up from $2.39B in 2024, and the January 2025 California wildfires alone cost $1.47B pre-tax — a single event equal to roughly 14% of a year's net income. Q3 2026 catastrophe losses were $705M, against $285M a year earlier. Chubb's affluent-homeowner book is concentrated in exactly the coastal areas and wildfire-prone edges of cities where insured values are highest and loss frequency is rising. Reinsurance transfers some of this, but at a price that itself rises after every bad year.
B
A softening commercial pricing cycle. Marsh's global commercial composite fell 5% in Q3 2026, the ninth straight quarterly decline, with property down 10% globally. Chubb's response is to shrink — North America Commercial premiums fell 2.7% — which protects margin but caps top-line growth. If soft conditions persist for several more years, either premium growth stays negative or the company eventually writes business at ratios well above today's mid-80s.
C
U.S. casualty reserve risk and social inflation. Casualty is the only major line where Marsh's global rates still rose in Q3 2026 (+1%, and +6% in the U.S.). On the October 21 call Greenberg said "pricing in numerous areas is failing to keep pace with loss costs, which while steady, continue to well outpace general inflation." Chubb has reported favorable prior-period development (reserve releases on past years' claims) every recent period ($1.13B in FY2025, $511M in Q3 2026), but that is a backward-looking comfort: the accident years most exposed to social inflation are still developing, and the industry has a long history of discovering casualty deficiencies late.
D
Key-man and succession concentration. Evan Greenberg has been CEO since 2004 and Chairman since 2007 — a 22-year tenure over which the ACE/Chubb combination and the current underwriting culture were built. Chubb has not publicly named a designated successor, so succession remains an open question rather than a disclosed plan; an announcement would be a major event for the stock.

Notable One-Off Items

Four categories of non-recurring or volatile items distort the reported numbers, and all four matter for CB.

1. Catastrophe losses. FY2025 pre-tax catastrophe losses of $2.92B (vs $2.39B in FY2024) included $1.47B from the January 2025 California wildfires; that quarter's catastrophes together cost $1.30B after tax, or $3.21 per share, and 15.9 combined-ratio points in Q1 2025 alone. That single quarter is the reason trailing EPS growth looks like +19.4%: the comparison base is artificially depressed. Q3 2026 catastrophe losses rose to $705M from an unusually light $285M a year earlier (5.4 points on the combined ratio), from weather events around the world and an earthquake in Latin America, CFO Peter Enns said on the call.

2. Prior-year reserve development. Chubb released $1.13B of favorable prior-period development in FY2025 (vs $856M in FY2024) and $511M in Q3 2026 (3.9 points on the combined ratio, against $361M a year earlier). Reserve releases flow straight to underwriting income and flatter the combined ratio; excluding them, the underlying result is several points worse than the headline. The direction has been consistently favorable, which is a genuine positive, but investors should treat releases as a lower-quality earnings component than current-accident-year margin.

3. Realized investment gains and losses. The gap between GAAP net income and core operating income isolates these. In FY2025 net income of $10.31B exceeded core operating income of $9.95B by roughly $360M, i.e. realized/mark-to-market items added about 3.5% to reported earnings. In Q3 2026 net income of $2.84B ($7.30 per share) trailed core operating income of $2.98B ($7.66). Coming the other way: Greenberg said in the release that the sale of USI Insurance Services to Aon, held through Chubb's Strategic Holdings partnership with KKR, is expected to produce a realised gain of $460M on a $740M investment, which will show in net income, not core. Core operating income is the number to track.

4. Currency translation. With roughly 40% of net premiums written outside North America, published growth and constant-dollar growth diverge. Chubb discloses both — in Q3 2026 consolidated net premiums rose 1.9% as published and 1.8% in constant dollars — and the difference is the FX effect. Readers comparing quarters should check the constant-dollar column in Chubb's release.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Chubb is still compounding: tangible book value per share rose 15.4% over the year leaving out paper losses on bonds (5.7% including them), and Q3 core operating EPS was a record $7.66 on an 84.1% combined ratio, roughly nine points better than the U.S. industry's 2025 figure — achieved while shrinking business it judges underpriced. The second engine grows on its own: adjusted net investment income hit a record $1.89B in Q3 2026 (+6.3%), which Greenberg tied to rising interest rates, a tailwind that does not depend on premium rates. Add a 21.6% core operating ROTE, 34 consecutive years of dividend growth on a 14% payout, $4.47B of capital returned in nine months, Berkshire Hathaway as a 34.2M-share holder, an expected $460M gain from the USI sale, and Greenberg's stated expectation that North America Commercial growth will improve, and the setup is a defensive compounder at under 13x trailing earnings.

Bear Case

Growth has stalled where it matters most: consolidated net premiums rose just 1.9% in Q3, North America Commercial fell 2.7% and Global Reinsurance 5.6%, and Marsh's global commercial composite has fallen for nine straight quarters (property −10%). On the call Greenberg himself described "a lot of hungry and extremely naive capacity" in the market. Margins this good may be near a cyclical peak: Q3's 84.1% was helped by $511M of favorable reserve releases, which cannot be repeated at will, and in the wildfire quarter catastrophes added 15.9 points to the combined ratio, $1.47B of the losses from the fires alone. Reported ROE of 14.3% badly lags peers, book value per share rose only 5.1% over the year as higher rates marked down the bond portfolio, and at 12.7x trailing earnings and 1.90x book, with the sell side majority-Hold and the price already at the $367 average target, much of the good news is priced.

Top 3 Risks

01
A prolonged soft commercial market that forces the choice between growth and margin. Nine consecutive quarters of global rate decline, with property down 10% in Q3 2026, mean the current mid-80s combined ratio is being defended by shrinking the book. If softening runs another two or three years, either premium contraction accelerates and the earnings base erodes, or Chubb writes at materially worse ratios. Neither outcome supports the current multiple.
02
Catastrophe concentration in high-value property. $2.92B of catastrophe losses in FY2025 versus $2.39B in 2024, with $1.47B from one wildfire event, in a book deliberately concentrated in the most expensive homes in the most exposed geographies. Rising climate loss frequency raises both the expected loss and the cost of the reinsurance that caps it. Q3 2026 catastrophe losses of $705M were about 2.5 times the prior year's $285M even in a season Marsh called benign for U.S. hurricanes; a major landfall would hit a quarter far harder.
03
Casualty reserve adequacy against social inflation — compounded by succession risk. Marsh's U.S. casualty rates rose 6% in Q3 2026, and Greenberg said casualty pricing in numerous areas is failing to keep pace with loss costs; Chubb's recent favorable development of $1.13B in FY2025 reflects older accident years, not the ones currently at risk. Reserve strengthening at any large casualty writer would hit both earnings and the sector multiple. Layered on top, a 22-year CEO tenure with no publicly designated successor means the culture that produces the industry's best combined ratio is not yet demonstrably institutionalised.
  Industry Commentary
The commercial P&C cycle has turned soft
ONGOING HEADWIND
Marsh's Global Insurance Market Index (October 21, 2026) shows the global commercial composite rate falling 5% in Q3 2026, the ninth consecutive quarterly decline, after a 6% fall in Q2. Property led the way down at −10% globally; financial and professional lines fell 3% and cyber 4%. Regionally, IMEA fell 14%, Pacific 12%, LAC 9%, the UK 8%, Canada, Europe and Asia 6% each, and the U.S. just 1%. The one exception is casualty, up 1% globally and 6% in the U.S. On the October 21 call Greenberg gave Chubb's own North America pricing: commercial property down 4.2%, casualty up 5.7% and financial lines up 2.1%. For Chubb this cuts both ways: Greenberg tied the −2.7% North America Commercial premium decline mainly to property and financial lines, while casualty firming supports its large liability book.
Catastrophe frequency and the climate loss trend
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Chubb's own catastrophe load rose from $2.39B in FY2024 to $2.92B in FY2025 — a 22% increase in a single year — and the January 2025 California wildfires alone produced $1.47B of pre-tax losses (all of that quarter's catastrophes together: $1.30B after tax, $3.21 per share, 15.9 combined-ratio points in Q1 2025). That event was not a coastal windstorm but a wildfire where affluent suburbs meet open land, which is precisely the exposure profile of Chubb's high-net-worth homeowners franchise. The segment has since returned to strong margins: a 60.3% combined ratio in Q3 2026 on 5.3% premium growth. Q3 2026 catastrophe losses of $705M against $285M a year earlier show how fast the line can move even in a quiet hurricane season.
Higher-for-longer rates are repricing the float
EMERGING TAILWIND
This is the offset the bear case tends to underweight. Adjusted net investment income reached a record $6.95B in FY2025, up 9%, and a record $1.89B in Q3 2026, up 6.3%, with pre-tax net investment income up 8.6% to $1.79B. Greenberg credited "rising interest rates and an excellent fixed-income investment environment." The mechanism is mechanical and slow-moving: bonds bought during the zero-rate era mature at par and are reinvested at today's materially higher yields, so investment income keeps rising for several years even if central-bank policy rates plateau or fall modestly. Because underwriting margin and investment income are largely independent drivers, this stream partially insulates earnings from the soft premium cycle. Bull framing: a growing float ($17.8B of operating cash flow over the past year, about $46.56 per share) reinvested at higher yields can carry EPS growth for several years even if net premiums written go flat or negative, which is exactly what makes a multiple under 13x defensible. Bear framing: investment income is a lower-quality, more market-dependent earnings source than underwriting profit; if rates fall materially the tailwind fades just as the premium cycle bottoms, leaving both engines decelerating at once — and at 1.90x book with the price already at the average target, the stock has limited cushion for that scenario.
