01 Company Overview

Commonwealth Bank of Australia — CommBank to most Australians — is the country's biggest bank and, after the miner BHP, the second-largest company on the ASX. It holds 25.4% of Australian home loans and 26.4% of household deposits (APRA data, June 2026), and it says it is the main bank of one in three Australians and one in four Australian businesses. In the year to June 30, 2026 (FY26) it earned A$10.98B of cash profit, up 7%, on operating income of A$30.2B (+6%), with a return on equity of 14.0%. The full-year dividend rose 4% to A$5.05 per share, fully franked.

CBA matters beyond Australia for one reason: price. At A$151.45 the shares trade at 23.3 times trailing earnings and 3.6 times tangible book value — rich multiples for a bank earning a 14% return on equity. The premium rests on things that are real: a 26.4% share of the country's household deposits, what it calls Australia's most popular banking app (9.6 million users), and a business bank that grew lending 13% in a year. FY26 was the first year in which CBA grew at or above the market in all five of its core domestic products (home lending, business lending, consumer finance, household and business deposits) — a first for any major Australian bank in 15 years, the company says. The other side: every one of the 14 analysts in S&P Global's panel rates the shares Sell or Strong Sell, and the stock is 18.4% below its 52-week high of April 14, 2026: the May 13 trading update brought the largest one-day fall in five years, and the Reserve Bank of Australia has turned back to raising rates.

Outlook, and what has happened since the results. CBA gives no profit guidance. In the August 12 results release CEO Matt Comyn wrote that "growth is slowing, with higher interest rates and inflation placing uneven pressure on household incomes and economic activity", and that home-loan "Application volumes appear to have stabilised in recent weeks." For FY27 it gives only a few markers: investment spend held at A$2.4B, gross benefits from AI use cases expected to double from about A$200M and to exceed the AI investment, and an effective tax rate of about 30%; its dividend payout target stays 70–80% of cash profit. On margins, CFO Alan Docherty told analysts "We are not going to add to that here and provide specific guidance". Since then, on August 26 CBA agreed in principle to pay A$249M to settle a 2018 class action over cash and deposit options in Colonial First State superannuation products, admitting no liability and subject to Federal Court approval (it says a provision booked in an earlier period covers the sum). On September 15 its New Zealand subsidiary ASB named Sinead Taylor, CBA's head of Institutional Banking & Markets, to succeed Vittoria Shortt as ASB chief executive from December 12, 2026.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

26.4% of household deposits Main bank for 1 in 3 Australians 9.6M+ app users 79% deposit-funded Business-bank scale Investment spend A$2.4B a year

CBA's edge is the transaction account. More than 97% of its home-loan customers and more than 90% of its business-loan customers also hold a CommBank transaction account, the CEO told analysts — so salaries, bills and card spending run through CBA, and that flow becomes cheap deposit funding. Deposits supply 79% of the bank's funding, and the engagement feeds credit decisions: about 70% of home-loan applications through its own channels get an automated decision the same day. The moat protects CBA's volumes; it does not set its margins, which still move with the Reserve Bank and with price competition for mortgages.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Valuation leaves no room for disappointment. At 3.6 times tangible book and 23 times earnings, the shares price in steady growth. The May 13, 2026 trading update — cash profit about 1% below the first-half quarterly run-rate and a A$316M impairment charge — was followed by a 10.4% one-day fall.
02
Housing concentration. Home loans are A$749B of a A$1.45 trillion balance sheet. National dwelling prices are down about 2.8% from their March 2026 peak, and home-loan application volumes fell 15% from May before stabilising, the CEO said. Arrears are rising from low levels: home loans 0.73% and personal loans 1.72% at 90+ days.
03
Margin competition. The CFO attributed second-half pressure on lending margins to cash-rate lag, competition and a shift toward fixed-rate home loans and lower-risk institutional lending, both at thinner spreads. Term-deposit spreads are also compressing.
04
Capital is fully used. CET1 of 12.0% is well above APRA's 10.25% minimum, but strong lending growth absorbed 72bps of capital in FY26 and the A$1B buy-back expired on August 12, 2026 with A$300M done and was not extended. Growth, dividends and buybacks now compete for the same surplus.
05
Legal overhang. Two shareholder class actions filed in 2017 and 2018 allege CBA breached its continuous-disclosure duties over the AUSTRAC money-laundering case it settled in 2018 for a A$700M penalty. CBA won at trial in 2024; in September 2025 the Full Federal Court declared a disclosure breach on or about April 24, 2017 but dismissed the applicants' individual claims. Both sides appealed to the High Court, which heard the case on June 11–12, 2026 and reserved judgment; CBA says it cannot yet determine the impact, if any.

Notable One-Off Items

CBA's headline is cash profit, which excludes hedging and accounting volatility and gains or losses on selling businesses: FY26 cash profit A$10,982M versus statutory A$10,866M including discontinued operations (the difference, A$116M after tax, is A$54M of hedging and IFRS volatility and A$62M of losses on past disposals). Inside cash profit, A$170M of restructuring and notable items (FY25: A$130M), all booked in the first half, cover provisions to settle legal proceedings in New Zealand, an extra goodwill payment to some customers after ASIC's Better Banking review, and domestic customer remediation. Other income was helped by a milestone payment on the earlier sale of CommInsure General Insurance and a fair-value gain on CBA's investment in Gemini after its IPO; it was held back by the sale of the aircraft-leasing portfolio. Capital: no shares were bought back in FY26; the A$1B on-market buy-back (A$300M completed) expired on August 12, 2026 and was not extended.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

CBA is the best-run franchise in a four-bank market and still taking share: at or above market growth in all five core products in FY26, business lending up 13%, retail main-bank share up to 34.2%. A 14% ROE on a 79% deposit-funded balance sheet, a 45.5% cost-to-income ratio, provisions A$2.7B above its central scenario and CET1 of 12.0% make it a defensive earnings base. The CFO sees another year of tailwind from its deposit hedges (the replicating portfolio), and AI is meant to start paying for itself in FY27. The 3.3% fully franked yield grosses up to 4.8% for holders who can use franking credits.

Bear Case

Twenty-three times earnings and 3.6 times tangible book for a bank whose cash earnings grew 7% in FY26. FY27 consensus EPS growth is about 2%. Every analyst in the S&P Global panel says Sell, the average target is 17% below the price, and even the highest is below it. Housing is turning — prices off their March peak, applications down, arrears up — and the three latest cash-rate rises are still working through household budgets. New Zealand profit is falling, institutional margins are thinning, and the buy-back is over. Two divisions change leader in December, with no successor yet named for the institutional bank, and the High Court has still to rule on the shareholder class actions. Two trading updates six months apart produced one-day falls of 6.6% and 10.4%; the stock does not get the benefit of the doubt.

Top 3 Risks

01
De-rating. The premium multiple is the main risk to holders. At today's tangible book, each one-turn fall in the multiple — 3.6x to 2.6x — would take about 28% off the price (our calculation), more than a year of earnings growth could make up.
02
Australian housing and household credit. A$680B of Australian home loans; arrears rising from low levels as the RBA tightens again; dwelling prices falling from a March 2026 peak after a near-70% rise over seven years, per the CEO.
03
Margin squeeze from competition. Lending margins fell in the second half on competition, cash-rate lag and mix, and term-deposit spreads are compressing. Group NIM was down 3bps for the year.
Industry Commentary
Australia's rate cycle turned up again
MIXED

Australian rates are rising again. In February 2026 CBA described interest rates as easing "until recently"; by the FY26 call in August the CEO said house prices had peaked in March, "coinciding with the second of three cash rate rises", and put the cumulative increase in the cash rate since May 2022 at 425 basis points. For a deposit-rich bank this cuts both ways. Hedged deposits earn more as swap rates rise — the CFO's replicating-portfolio tailwind — while mortgage borrowers carry higher repayments; the CEO noted the increase in repayments has fallen mostly on households aged about 25 to 55. That is why CBA's arrears, impairment charges and application volumes are the numbers the market reacts to.

From mortgages to business lending
TAILWIND

Australian banking has long been a mortgage business. CBA is tilting away from it: business lending grew 13% against 7% for retail home lending, Business Banking earns a 3.39% margin against 2.50% in retail, and it now delivers 41% of cash profit. The CFO named the mix shift toward business lending as the main source of margin support ahead. The risk is the mirror image: more business lending means more exposure to the business cycle, and troublesome and non-performing exposures rose to 0.94% of committed exposures, above December though below a year earlier, the CEO said.

The valuation argument
KEY DEBATE

A simple rule of thumb prices a bank at its return on equity divided by its cost of equity. On a 14% ROE and an assumed 9–10% cost of equity, that gives roughly 1.4–1.6 times book value — an illustrative estimate, against the 3.2 times book (3.6 times tangible book) CBA trades at. The gap is the market paying for durability: steady share gains, a dominant app, fully franked dividends that are worth more to Australian taxpayers than the cash yield shows. The stock rose through mid-2025; since then it has given back 18% from its June 2025 month-end. Whether the premium compresses further depends on whether the 7% earnings growth of FY26 survives FY27.
