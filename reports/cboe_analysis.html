01 Company Overview

In April 1973 a handful of traders in Chicago started swapping standardised stock options on a floor carved out of the Chicago Board of Trade. Fifty-three years later that experiment is Cboe Global Markets: the home of SPX options, the operator of the VIX, and one of the largest US options and equities exchange groups. The company went public in June 2010, bought Bats Global Markets for roughly $3.2 billion in 2017, renamed itself Cboe Global Markets in October 2017, and joined the S&P 500 at the open on March 1, 2017.

The business is a toll road on risk. Every time someone hedges a portfolio, sells a covered call or trades a same-day SPX contract, Cboe collects a fee. In Q2 2026 that toll road produced record net revenue of $731.6M, up 25%, with diluted EPS of $3.35, up 50%. Options alone were 65% of net revenue. And yet the stock closed June at $242.67 — down 27% in a single month — after the futures regulator approved a new kind of crypto contract that the market read as a threat to retail options volume. It then rallied 28% in July and has since given back more than half of that, closing at $271.43 on September 21, 2026. The central question: is the SPX franchise a moat that new product wrappers cannot cross, or is the retail short-dated-options boom that powers the numbers a flow that can move elsewhere?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive SPX options license VIX franchise Liquidity network effect Proprietary market data Regulated clearing & exchange licenses Net cash balance sheet ($774M)

The moat is a contract and a crowd. The contract: a 2013 agreement with S&P Dow Jones Indices gave Cboe exclusive rights to list options on the S&P 500 and S&P 100 through 2032, non-exclusive through 2033 (whether it has since been amended was not checked). The crowd: liquidity attracts liquidity. A market maker quoting SPX needs to be where every other hedger already is, and nobody else can list the contract anyway. That is why a 30% share of multi-listed options is a fight while the index complex is a franchise. The vulnerability is that exclusivity covers a contract, not a need. A trader who wants cheap leveraged exposure to the S&P 500 for the afternoon can, in principle, get it from a different wrapper on a different venue — and that is exactly the fear the market priced in June.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
New wrappers for the same exposure. On June 2, 2026 Cboe fell 7.6% after the CFTC approved the first US-regulated bitcoin perpetual futures contract on another exchange and published a policy statement on perpetual listings. Raymond James analyst Patrick O'Shaughnessy: "the market reaction indicates U.S. exchange shareholders are concerned that products with significant retail participation are at risk of losing market share to new competitors with perpetual futures contracts." CME has sued the CFTC over the order (filed June 18, 2026). Cboe's derivatives head Rob Hocking argues perpetuals solve leverage and shorting, while options offer "capped risk downside" — different problems, different products.
02
Retail and 0DTE concentration. 0DTE was about 65% of SPX volume by May 2026, and Donohue's team puts the SPX retail share at roughly 55–60%. Retail flow is the most cyclical and the most mobile participant in any market. If it cools — or migrates — the fastest-growing revenue line reverses first.
03
Volatility dependence. On June 16, 2026 the stock fell 7.05% as a tentative US–Iran agreement drove a risk-on rally and compressed the VIX. Calm markets mean less hedging. Record 2026 volumes were earned in a volatile tape; the base rate for how long that lasts is not in Cboe's control.
04
Execution on the reset. Exiting listings, CEDX and Japanese equities, selling Australia and Canada, and — per Trefis — cutting about 20% of the workforce, while simultaneously launching event contracts, KPI contracts, clearing and longer trading hours. Shrinking and building at the same time is where plans slip.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 month-end close of $123.86 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $271.43, CBOE rose 119.1% on price — more than doubling — with no stock splits in the period. Dividends add to that: FY2022–FY2025 alone paid $9.12 per share. The shape is two regimes. From 2021 to mid-2023 the stock went nowhere, bottoming at $112.31 in May 2022. Then came the 0DTE era: from mid-2023 the monthly RSI lived above 70 for most of three years, peaking at 91.6 in November 2023, as same-day SPX options turned Cboe into a growth stock.

2026 broke the pattern. The stock hit its best month-end of the window at $333.56 in May, lost 27.2% in June, gained 27.8% in July and has fallen 9.6% in September to date. RSI is now 47.6, squarely neutral — and it did not get close to oversold even in June (41.0), which tells you how far above trend the stock had run first. The 10-month moving average, about $285.50, sits above the current price: the long-term trend has rolled over for the first time since 2024.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 21.2x trailing and about 19x forward earnings for a business with an exclusive license on the world's most important index option, net revenue growing 25%, a 72.6% adjusted EBITDA margin, $774M of net cash, a 6.6% free-cash-flow yield and a dividend just raised 19%. Guidance has been raised twice this year. The June selloff priced a threat — perpetual futures — that is crypto-native, under legal challenge by CME and, in management's framing, solves a different problem than a defined-risk option. Meanwhile Cboe is building its own new wrappers (event and KPI contracts) on top of an index nobody else can list. The average of 17 analyst targets is $315.64, 16% above the price.

Bear Case

The record numbers are a volume boom, and volume booms end. 0DTE is about 65% of SPX volume and retail is 55–60% of SPX — the most mobile flow in finance, now with a regulator willing to approve competing leveraged products. Volatility, the other driver, is outside Cboe's control, as June 16 showed. Consensus already expects EPS growth to slow from +32% in 2026 to +7.9% in 2027, which makes a 21x multiple look full rather than cheap. Management is simultaneously exiting businesses, cutting staff and launching new products. Three analysts rate it a Sell, and the 10-month moving average has turned down.

Top 3 Risks

01
Retail options flow migrates to new products or venues. The June 2 reaction (−7.6%) was the market's estimate of this risk. If perpetual futures or other leveraged wrappers pull even a slice of retail 0DTE activity, the fastest-growing, highest-margin line shrinks first.
02
A calm market. Transaction revenue scales with hedging demand. A sustained low-VIX regime — like the risk-on move on June 16 — would slow volumes regardless of market share. Consensus revenue growth for 2027 is only 3.7%.
03
Execution of the strategic reset. Divestitures, business closures and a reported ~20% workforce reduction, alongside new clearing, event-contract and extended-hours initiatives that need regulatory approval. Any stumble lands on a stock that has lost its momentum support.
Industry Commentary
Perpetual futures and the new-wrapper problem
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A perpetual future is a leveraged contract with no expiry date, born in crypto markets. On June 2, 2026, after the CFTC approved the first US-regulated bitcoin perpetual and issued a policy statement on perpetual listings, US exchange stocks sold off together: Cboe −7.6%, CME −4.3%, ICE −4.1%. The fear is not bitcoin; it is precedent — that the same wrapper could eventually carry equity-index exposure to retail traders who today use short-dated SPX options. CME Chairman and CEO Terry Duffy argues perpetuals are swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act, and CME sued the CFTC on June 18. Cboe's own counter is product design: options give "capped risk downside," and roughly 95% of 0DTE contracts are defined-risk. Both things can be true; the market will judge by volumes, not by arguments.

Volatility as a revenue input
ONGOING HEADWIND

Exchanges are one of the few businesses that earn more when markets are frightened. Cboe's low 0.41 beta reflects that: it tends to hold up when the index falls. The flip side came on June 16, 2026, when a tentative US–Iran agreement compressed the VIX and the stock fell 7.05% in a sector that was up 1.11%. Record volumes in 2026 were earned in a volatile year. The multiple you pay depends on how much of that you believe is structural (the 0DTE habit, international demand for SPX access) and how much is weather.

Data and clearing — the recurring layer
EMERGING TAILWIND

Across the exchange group, the pattern of the last decade has been the same: trading is the attraction, data and infrastructure are the annuity. Cboe's Data Vantage line — $177.8M in Q2 2026, growing 15% organically, with about half of new data sales coming from overseas clients per management — is the part of the story that does not care about the VIX. The bull framing: each new product (event contracts, KPI contracts, longer trading hours) generates data and connectivity revenue on top of transaction fees, and Cboe is pursuing securities clearing registration to keep more of the value chain. The bear framing: Data Vantage is about a quarter of net revenue; the stock will still trade on the 0DTE tape. Four consecutive quarters of Data Vantage growth at or above the "low teens" guide, alongside stable options market share, would be the evidence that the franchise is broadening rather than cresting.
