01 Company Overview

Coca-Cola Europacific Partners is the world's largest Coca-Cola bottler by revenue: it buys concentrate from The Coca-Cola Company, then makes, bottles, sells and delivers Coca-Cola, Fanta, Sprite, Monster and a long tail of waters, teas and coffees across 31 countries serving nearly 600 million consumers and over 4 million customer outlets — Western Europe, Australia, New Zealand, the Pacific islands, Indonesia and, since February 2024, the Philippines. FY2025 revenue was €20.90B, up 2.3% reported (+2.8% comparable, currency-neutral) on 4.0 billion unit cases, with comparable operating profit of €2.8B (+7.1%). The company is UK-incorporated, reports in euros, and carries its primary share listing on Nasdaq — so the $105.76 you see in the header is a US-dollar quote on a euro-earning business.

The debate here is not about growth — nobody buys a bottler for growth. It is about whether the machine keeps compounding at 7% operating profit on 3–4% revenue when the two inputs the bottler does not control — concentrate cost set by the franchisor through incidence pricing, and consumer demand set by weather, sugar taxes and European wallets — both have a bad year at once. The stock has doubled in five years on precisely that margin-expansion story. On the same day this snapshot was taken, JPMorgan re-initiated at Underweight with a $92 target, and the shares fell 3.1%. Sections 02, 04 and 08 lay out the volume × price/mix × margin arithmetic that both sides are arguing over.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EXCLUSIVE COKE FRANCHISE — 31 COUNTRIES 4M+ OUTLET DIRECT DISTRIBUTION COOLER FOOTPRINT (+80K IN H1) ROUTE-TO-MARKET DENSITY ZERO-SUGAR PORTFOLIO MONSTER DISTRIBUTION RIGHTS ANCHOR SHAREHOLDERS (55%)

The moat is a licence plus a truck fleet. The licence — exclusive, perpetual-in-practice rights to bottle the world's most valuable soft-drink brands in defined territories — cannot be replicated by anyone at any price, because The Coca-Cola Company does not grant overlapping franchises. The truck fleet is the part that is merely very hard: direct delivery to over 4 million outlets, a cooler in most of them, and the sales-force density to restock a corner shop in Manila and a supermarket in Munich on the same system. That combination is why the bottler earns a 13–14% operating margin on what is, physically, sugar-water logistics. What the moat does not protect is the split of the pie with the franchisor. Concentrate is priced by incidence — a percentage of the bottler's revenue per unit case rather than a fixed price per litre — so The Coca-Cola Company automatically shares in every euro of pricing CCEP takes. That aligns incentives on volume and mix, but it caps how much of any pricing windfall the bottler keeps, and it makes the franchisor's 17.8% equity stake a second lever of influence on top of the contract.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Sugar taxes and health regulation. The UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy has existed since 2018; France raised its sugar tax in March 2025, and the H1 2026 release directly attributes Original Taste declines in France to it. The company's defence is portfolio — Zero Sugar grew double digits in the same market — but each new levy resets the price ladder, and a tax that hits the flagship product is a tax on the brand's cultural centre of gravity, not just its sugar. Reformulation, smaller packs and zero variants have worked so far; the risk is a jurisdiction that taxes all sweeteners, or volume-based rather than sugar-based.
▸
Franchisor dependence. Roughly all of the revenue sits under brands CCEP does not own, on bottling agreements with a company that is also its second-largest shareholder. Incidence pricing means concentrate cost rises with CCEP's own pricing; the release itself notes cost of sales per unit case is "directly linked to revenue per UC through incidence pricing." A franchisor deciding to reset incidence rates, or to insist on marketing spend, changes the bottler's margin with no operational failure on the bottler's part.
▸
Weather and consumer discretion. Soft-drink volume is a function of summer temperature and out-of-home spending. H1 2026 benefited from "favourable weather in June" and a World Cup; the six extra selling days in H1 reverse to six fewer in Q4. Europe volume grew only 1.6% with that tailwind. A cold summer, a weak European consumer or a Middle East shock to energy prices (flagged by the CEO in Q1) can turn a 2% volume year into a flat one, and with 90% of FY26 commodities hedged the protection expires annually.
▸
Emerging-market dilution. The Philippines and Indonesia are the growth story, but they sell a case for roughly two-thirds of the European price and APS revenue per case fell 1.5% in H1 2026. If APS becomes a larger share of the mix faster than its margins catch up, group revenue per case and group margin both drift lower even as volume accelerates — the numbers would look like "growth" and "deleverage" at the same time. Indonesia in particular has been a decade-long turnaround for its successive owners.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items distort the year-over-year comparisons, both flagged by the company.

Calendar: six extra selling days in H1 2026. CCEP's H1 ran to 3 July 2026, giving it six more consumption days than H1 2025; those six days come out of Q4. H1 volume growth of +2.2% is quoted on a "days-adjusted" basis, but reported revenue (+4.4%) and reported operating profit (+6.9%) include the calendar benefit. Expect Q4 2026 to look mechanically soft, and expect that to be misread as a slowdown.

Suntory alcohol exit in Australia (June 2025). CCEP stopped distributing Suntory's alcoholic ready-to-drink brands in Australia, a ~0.5% headwind to full-year revenue. This is why APS reported revenue grew only 0.4% in H1 2026 despite 3.5% volume growth; Australia's underlying soft-drink volumes rose mid-single digits ex-alcohol.

Comparable vs reported. The company's "comparable" operating profit (€1,481M H1) strips ~€23M of restructuring and other items from reported (€1,458M); comparable EPS €2.20 vs reported €2.17. The gap is small — this is a company whose adjusted numbers are close to its statutory ones, which is worth noting in an era when that is rare. FY2024's reported net income dip to €1,418M (from €1,669M) means the FY2025 "+37% earnings growth" headline on stockanalysis overstates the run-rate; operating profit grew 7%.

Buyback: €593M of the €1B 2026 programme executed by 31 July; shares outstanding down 2.4% year over year to 443.18M. Net debt rose €461M in H1 as a result — the buyback is debt-funded at the margin during the seasonally weak cash half.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $52.65 to $105.76 on September 4, 2026, CCEP's US-listed shares returned +100.9% in price terms — a compound rate of about 15% a year before dividends, which added roughly another 2–3% a year. For a 0.48-beta staple that is a remarkable outcome, and the shape of the chart says how it happened: not a melt-up, but a staircase. The stock troughed at $42.62 in September 2022 (−25% from the window's start) as the European energy crisis and a strong dollar hammered every euro-earning consumer name, then climbed almost without interruption for three years as pricing recovered margins, the Philippines added volume and buybacks shrank the share count. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The machine, term by term. Start with volume: 4.0 billion unit cases in FY2025, growing +2.2% in H1 2026 — Europe +1.6%, APS +3.5%. Multiply by revenue per unit case: €5.22 for the group in FY2025, up only +0.4% in H1 2026 because Europe's +1.3% was almost cancelled by APS's −1.5% mix drag. Volume plus price/mix gives comparable revenue growth of +6.1% in H1 (with the calendar helping) and a full-year guide of 3–4%. Then the third term, margin: comparable operating margin of 13.8% in H1 (€1,481M ÷ €10,724M), Europe at 14.3%, APS at 12.4%, with operating profit guided to grow ~7% — roughly twice revenue. That gap is the operating leverage: cost of sales per unit case rising only 0.6% in H1 against revenue per case +0.4% sounds like nothing, but overheads spread across more cases, cooler investment lifts throughput, and the S/4HANA and AI programmes are real if unglamorous cost projects. Below the operating line, the ~26% tax rate and a share count shrinking ~2.4% a year turn 7% operating-profit growth into ~10% comparable EPS growth (H1: +10.6%). Add a 2.3% dividend and you have the bull case's arithmetic: 12–13% total return a year at a constant multiple.

The catch is the word "constant." At 20.8× trailing and 19.4× forward, CCEP trades at a premium to Coca-Cola HBC (19.4× / 17.2×) and a wide premium to Coca-Cola FEMSA (17.2×). Both are Coke-system bottlers with the same franchisor, the same incidence pricing and — in HBC's case — faster revenue growth (+9.1% TTM). CCEP's premium is a premium for Western European stability and for the balance-sheet discipline that keeps it A-rated while buying back €1B a year. JPMorgan's $92 target is, in effect, a statement that CCEP should trade at the peer multiple, not above it: $92 ÷ $5.08 = 18.1×. Nothing operational needs to go wrong for that to happen; the market simply has to stop paying a stability premium for a company whose home region grows volume at 1–2%.

Currency, twice over. Two separate FX effects sit between a US holder and this business. First, the company reports in euros and earns in sterling, Australian dollars, pesos and rupiah — the H1 release guides a ~40bp revenue and ~10bp operating-profit headwind from translation for FY2026. Second, the Nasdaq share is a USD quote of a euro-denominated equity: when the dollar weakens, the USD price rises with no change in the business, and vice versa. A meaningful part of the 2025 rally from $77 to $97 is the second effect. The euro figures in this report were converted at roughly 1.14 USD/EUR; a return to 2022's parity would cut every USD number here by about 12%.

The franchisor question. The Coca-Cola Company owns 17.8% of CCEP, sells it every drop of concentrate, and prices that concentrate as a share of CCEP's revenue per case. This is a partnership with a permanent senior partner. In good years it means the franchisor funds brand marketing that the bottler benefits from without paying for in full; in a dispute it means the bottler has limited leverage. Olive Partners at 37.5% is the counterweight — a long-term Spanish family holder whose interest is dividends and compounding, not a quick exit — and together the two anchors own 55%, which is why the free float is only ~197M shares and why the stock can move 3% on a single broker note.

Bull Case

A 13–14% margin bottler growing operating profit ~7% and EPS ~10% a year, paying out half in dividends and buying back 2% of its shares annually, with A-rated leverage and a franchisor as its second-largest owner. Zero Sugar and Monster are winning the mix battle inside the existing footprint; the Philippines and Indonesia add volume at improving margins with a new plant arriving in 2027; 80,000 new coolers a half-year is the kind of unglamorous capex that compounds. At 19.4× forward earnings for a 0.48-beta stream of euro cash flows, the shares remain a reasonable way to own defensive growth — and every target in the published range except JPMorgan's sits above the price.

Bear Case

Europe — 74% of revenue — grows volume 1–2% in a year with a World Cup and good weather, and revenue per case in the growth segment is falling. FY2026 operating-profit guidance of ~7% depends on a Q4 that loses six selling days and on 90% commodity hedges that reset next year. The company already trades at a premium to two peers that share its franchisor, and JPMorgan's $92 says that premium is undeserved. Net debt is rising to fund buybacks at 52-week highs, the current ratio is 0.89, and a euro sliding back toward parity would erase a year of USD-denominated gains for US holders without a single case fewer being sold.

Top 3 Risks

01
Multiple compression toward Coke-system peers. At 20.8× trailing vs 17–19× for HBC and FEMSA, roughly 10–15% of the share price is a stability premium. It can vanish on nothing more than a rotation out of staples or a second bearish initiation — JPMorgan's $92 target is that scenario written down.
02
A bad European summer plus a sugar-tax step-up. Volume in the core region is already only +1.6% with favourable June weather and a World Cup. A cold 2027 summer or another French/UK-style levy expansion would push Europe volume negative; with incidence pricing sharing any price response with the franchisor, the bottler cannot fully price its way out.
03
Emerging-market mix dilution outrunning margin catch-up. APS revenue per unit case fell 1.5% in H1 2026 while volume rose 3.5%. If the Philippines and Indonesia scale before their margins reach the ~10% target — and Indonesia has disappointed owners for a decade — group revenue per case and margin drift down together, and the "volume × price × margin" story loses two of its three terms at once.
  Industry Commentary
Sugar Taxes Are Now a Permanent Feature of the European Price Ladder
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The UK levy (2018) proved the template: tax by sugar content, and the industry reformulates rather than absorbs. France's March 2025 increase is the live case — CCEP's own H1 2026 release links it to a decline in Coca-Cola Original Taste, offset by double-digit growth in Zero Sugar. The bottler has so far won this trade because both products carry the same brand and similar price; the systemic risk is a jurisdiction that taxes volume or all sweeteners, which removes the substitution escape hatch. For the sector, every new levy is a one-off volume hit and a permanent narrowing of the pricing corridor, because the tax now occupies part of the shelf price the bottler used to own.
Energy, Packaging and the Hedge Cliff
ONGOING HEADWIND
Bottling is energy-intensive — blowing PET, chilling, and running a delivery fleet across 31 countries — and the 2022 European gas shock is why the stock printed $42.62 that September. Management's answer is a rolling hedge book: ~90% of FY2026 commodity exposure is covered, cost of sales per unit case rose only 0.6% in H1, and the full-year guide is ~1.5%. The catch is that hedges expire; each year the industry re-prices its inputs, and a Middle East disruption (flagged by the CEO in Q1 2026) hits diesel, resin and aluminium at once. Incidence pricing helps here — concentrate cost falls if revenue per case falls — but it also means the franchisor takes a cut of every price increase used to recover an energy spike.
Zero Sugar, Energy and Coolers: Mix Is the New Volume
EMERGING TAILWIND
Across the Coke system, growth has migrated from selling more litres to selling better litres. Zero-sugar variants grew 5.3% at CCEP in FY2025 while full-sugar fell 2.1%; Monster grows double digits in every region and carries a premium price per case; and 80,000 new coolers in a half-year expand the physical shelf without a single new store opening. Bull framing: this is a structural upgrade in revenue per case that survives sugar taxes and weather, and it is why operating profit can grow 7% on 3–4% revenue indefinitely. Bear framing: the mix upgrade in Europe is being offset one-for-one by geographic mix downgrade in APS (group revenue per case +0.4% in H1), the franchisor captures part of every premium through incidence, and at 20.8× earnings the market is already paying for a decade of it.
