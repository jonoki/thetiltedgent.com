01 Company Overview

Crown Castle is a landlord whose tenants are phone companies. It owns roughly 40,000 cell towers across the United States and rents vertical space on them to wireless carriers, which bolt their antennas on and sign long leases to stay there. Since May 1, 2026 that is the whole company: on that date it closed the sale of its fiber and small-cell businesses for $8.5B gross (~$8.4B net) — fiber to Zayo, small cells to EQT's Arium Networks — and CEO Chris Hillabrant declared it "the only U.S. focused, large publicly traded pure-play tower company." As a REIT, the number that matters is not net income but AFFO: FY2025 AFFO was $1.904B, or $4.36 per share, and the 2026 guide midpoint is $1.980B, or $4.60 per share.

The story of the last five years is a company that spent a decade betting that fiber and small cells would be the next tower, and then paid to unwind the bet. The stock closed October 2021 at $180.30 and December 2021 at $208.74; it closed October 26, 2026 at $87.47. Along the way it posted a $3.90B net loss for FY2024, announced the fiber/small-cell sale on March 13, 2025, and reset the quarterly dividend from $1.565 to $1.0625 (about −32%) with the June 13, 2025 ex-date. The sale proceeds went to paying down more than $7B of debt and a $1B share buyback. What is left is a simpler business with two hard problems: one customer (DISH) stopped paying and is now in Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and the three that remain — T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon — make up 93% of site rental revenue. October then changed the mood. The stock closed September at $66.65, the lowest month-end close in the five-year chart, and rose 31.2% to $87.47 by October 26, on two pieces of news about SpaceX, which is building a mobile network and may need space on towers like these (Section 06). No lease with SpaceX has been announced. Asked on the October 21 call about satellite companies, SpaceX among them, CEO Chris Hillabrant declined to give customer-specific detail and said “you can assume that we’re talking to all parties” (call transcript via StockAnalysis). The question for the stock is whether a tower landlord yielding 4.86% at roughly 19x this year’s AFFO guide is cheap, or already priced for a customer that has not signed.

A tower is a fixed cost with a variable number of tenants. Once it stands, each additional carrier antenna is almost pure margin — which is why tower economics are about colocation and lease amendments, not construction. Reported site rental revenue is falling (−5% YoY in Q3 2026; FY2025 was $4,049M, −5.1%) for two identifiable reasons: the tail of Sprint lease cancellations after the T-Mobile merger ($204M drag in FY2025, $5M in Q3 2026) and the DISH terminations ($61M drag in Q3 2026, $49M in Q2, $220M in the February 2026 outlook). Strip those out and the underlying business is growing: organic contribution to site rental billings was $35M in Q3 2026 excluding DISH and Sprint: 3.5% growth, or 3.8% with DISH also taken out of last year’s base (core leasing 1.8%, built-in annual rent increases 2.7%, lost leases −0.7%). The full-year 2026 outlook is unchanged at about 3.4%, or 3.6% on the second basis. The whole investment case is the gap between those two numbers — reported shrinking, underlying growing — and how long it takes for the drag to roll off.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

~40,000 U.S. towers in place Long-term master lease agreements Zoning & permitting barriers to new builds Near-zero marginal cost per tenant 97% fixed-rate debt

A tower moat is local and physical. A carrier that needs coverage in a given spot either rents on the tower that already stands there or tries to get a new one permitted — slowly, expensively, and often against neighbourhood opposition. Once an antenna is on, moving it means re-engineering the network around it, so leases renew. Crown Castle, American Tower and SBA Communications are the three large listed U.S. tower owners. The vulnerability is the mirror image: a landlord with three big tenants has three big negotiators, and when one of them merges (Sprint into T-Mobile) or walks away (DISH), the landlord has no one else to rent that space to at the same price.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Customer concentration is 93%. T-Mobile 42%, AT&T 28%, Verizon 23%. With the fiber and small-cell businesses gone, there is no second leg. $643M of annualised AT&T rent comes up for renewal in 2028 (company supplement, as of September 30, 2026) — a single negotiation worth about a sixth of site rental revenue (computed against the $3.875B FY2026 guide midpoint).
02
The fourth carrier is gone. EchoStar sold spectrum licences to AT&T and SpaceX in deals valued at more than $40B, and DISH stopped paying. Crown Castle terminated in January 2026 and claims more than $3.5B. DISH denies breach and filed for Chapter 11 on June 30, 2026. Losing a growth tenant removes the colocation upside the tower model depends on; how much Crown Castle recovers through the bankruptcy and the FCC-ordered trust is not in guidance. A new fourth carrier may yet appear in another form: SpaceX has agreed to buy spectrum and is reported to be in talks with Crown Castle over tower leases (Section 06), but no lease has been announced.
03
Leverage is still REIT-high. Net debt was $17,599M at September 30, 2026, or 6.5x Q3 adjusted EBITDA of $673M annualised (our calculation; the company’s Q3 supplement shows the same 6.5x). About 97% of the debt is fixed-rate, which protects near-term AFFO but means refinancing at higher rates is a headwind.
04
No diversification left. Unlike American Tower, whose reported results carry foreign-exchange and data-center effects, Crown Castle has no international towers and no data-center business. The pure-play simplicity that management sells is also a single-country, single-asset-class exposure to U.S. carrier capex cycles — and JPMorgan's September 2026 downgrade was explicitly about low tower activity.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the May 1, 2026 sale: fiber and small cells are discontinued operations, so trailing revenue, cash-flow and income figures mix two different companies until the TTM window rolls past mid-2027. Second, cost savings: AFFO/share rose 11% in Q3 2026 while site rental revenue fell 5%, so the gain is not leasing growth; the CFO attributed the latest guidance raise to ground lease buyouts, operating efficiencies and a customer agreement. Third, the $1B buyback and $7B+ of debt repayment change the per-share base; diluted shares were 426M in Q3 (437M a year earlier) and the 2026 outlook assumes about 430M, against 426.00M outstanding per StockAnalysis. Long-run per-share series (EPS, dividends) span both the pre-sale and post-sale company and the June 2025 dividend reset; there have been no stock splits.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 close of $180.30 to the October 26, 2026 settled close of $87.47, CCI lost 51.5% of its price, and 58.1% from the December 2021 month-end peak of $208.74 (price only; dividends are not included). Some of the fall is the rate cycle — a long-lease landlord is a bond with a roof on it, and it was repriced as such in 2022–2023 — and some is the company's own fiber bet, which ended in a sale for less than the market had once valued it and a one-third dividend cut.

The 6-period monthly RSI reads 55.9 — neutral, after 21.5 at the September month-end. The five-year extreme was 17.4 in September 2023, at the rate-cycle low of $92.03. The 10-month moving average sits at about $82.02 and the 3-month average at $76.75: the October rally has taken the price back above both, after a June–September stretch at the lows.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 19x this year’s AFFO guide for ~40,000 U.S. towers, a 4.86% dividend covered by AFFO at roughly 92%, 97% fixed-rate debt, and leverage of 6.5x net debt to EBITDA after $7B+ of paydown. The reported revenue decline is the Sprint and DISH churn rolling off; underneath, organic growth ex-DISH/Sprint ran 3.8% in Q3 2026. Once the DISH drag is fully in the base, reported and organic growth converge, and a simpler pure-play landlord with a smaller share count should earn a multiple closer to its peers. The DISH claim — more than $3.5B, now pursued in DISH’s bankruptcy and against a $2.4B FCC-ordered trust — is a free option, and a SpaceX lease, if one is signed, would add a tenant that guidance does not include.

Bear Case

A landlord with three tenants controlling 93% of rent, one of which (AT&T, $643M of annualised rent) renegotiates in 2028, has little pricing power over the people who pay it. The fourth carrier is gone, so the colocation growth that made tower economics special now depends on three carriers' capex budgets — and JPMorgan's downgrade said activity is low. Q3 AFFO per share rose 11% while site rental revenue fell 5%, so it is not leasing growth. Organic growth of about 3.4% guided for 2026 is modest, the dividend was cut once already, and the stock has made lower highs for five years. October’s 31% rally rests on a report of SpaceX talks, and no lease has been announced, so the price now carries hope that the guidance does not.

Top 3 Risks

01
Carrier concentration and the 2028 AT&T renewal. T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon are 93% of site rental revenue. A renewal on worse terms, or a network-sharing deal between two of them, reprices the core asset directly.
02
Low leasing activity. With DISH out and carriers in a slower spending phase, organic growth depends on amendments from three customers. The 2026 outlook assumes about 3.4% ex-DISH/Sprint; a miss would hit AFFO growth.
03
Rates and leverage. At about 6.5x net debt / EBITDA, a long-duration REIT is priced off the bond market. Higher long-term yields compress the multiple, and refinancing cheap fixed-rate debt at higher rates erodes AFFO per share.
Industry Commentary
The fourth carrier that wasn't — and one that might be
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The U.S. tower industry's growth story rested on a fourth national carrier building out a new network. EchoStar's sale of spectrum licences to AT&T and SpaceX, in deals valued at more than $40B, ended that. DISH stopped paying, denies breach — the same defence trade press reports it used against American Tower — and filed for Chapter 11 on June 30, 2026. For Crown Castle the cost is concrete: a $220M revenue hole in 2026 guidance and a smaller pool of future tenants. In October 2026 the idea returned in a new form: SpaceX agreed to buy Grain Management's 800 MHz spectrum (pending federal approval), and Bloomberg reported, as relayed by MarketScreener and TheFly, that it is in talks with Crown Castle over tower leases. No lease has been announced.

Churn from consolidation still rolling off
ONGOING HEADWIND

Sprint lease cancellations — the legacy of the T-Mobile/Sprint merger — took $204M off Crown Castle's FY2025 revenue and a further $5M in Q3 2026. It is a known, contracted drag, which is why management reports organic growth excluding it. The lesson for the industry is structural: every carrier merger is, eventually, a landlord's lost tenant.

The pure-play valuation argument — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: Crown Castle is now the only large U.S.-only pure-play tower company, with the fiber capital sink gone, debt cut by more than $7B, and AFFO guidance raised in May, July and October ($1.920B in February to $1.980B; per share $4.43 to $4.60). At ~19x AFFO and a 4.9% yield, the stock prices it as a bond, not as the owner of hard-to-replicate infrastructure. The bear framing: "pure-play" also means undiversified, and the market's response to a beat-and-raise in July was a 0.8% monthly gain. The October print moved the stock 0.2% the next session; the 9.1% jump two days later came from the SpaceX report. Investors want proof that organic leasing growth, 3.8% in Q3, can carry AFFO. A signed SpaceX lease and the 2028 AT&T renewal are the events that settle it.
