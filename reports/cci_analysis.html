01 Company Overview

Crown Castle is a landlord whose tenants are phone companies. It owns roughly 40,000 cell towers across the United States and rents vertical space on them to wireless carriers, which bolt their antennas on and sign long leases to stay there. Since May 1, 2026 that is the whole company: on that date it closed the sale of its fiber and small-cell businesses for $8.5B gross (~$8.4B net) — fiber to Zayo, small cells to EQT's Arium Networks — and CEO Chris Hillabrant declared it "the only U.S. focused, large publicly traded pure-play tower company." As a REIT, the number that matters is not net income but AFFO: FY2025 AFFO was $1.904B, or $4.36 per share, and the 2026 guide midpoint is $1.975B, or $4.59 per share.

The story of the last five years is a company that spent a decade betting that fiber and small cells would be the next tower, and then paid to unwind the bet. The stock closed September 2021 at $173.32 and December 2021 at $208.74; it closed September 21, 2026 at $73.22. Along the way it posted a $3.90B net loss for FY2024, announced the fiber/small-cell sale on March 13, 2025, and reset the quarterly dividend from $1.565 to $1.0625 (about −32%) with the June 13, 2025 ex-date. The sale proceeds went to paying down more than $7B of debt and a $1B share buyback. What is left is a simpler business with two hard problems: one customer (DISH) stopped paying and is in litigation, and the three that remain — T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon — make up 93% of site rental revenue. The question for the stock is whether a no-growth-this-year tower landlord yielding 5.80% at roughly 16x forward AFFO is cheap, or correctly priced for a business whose customer list just got shorter.

A tower is a fixed cost with a variable number of tenants. Once it stands, each additional carrier antenna is almost pure margin — which is why tower economics are about colocation and lease amendments, not construction. Reported site rental revenue is falling (−4% YoY in Q2 2026; FY2025 was $4,049M, −5.1%) for two identifiable reasons: the tail of Sprint lease cancellations after the T-Mobile merger ($204M drag in FY2025, $5M in Q2 2026) and the DISH terminations ($49M drag in Q2 2026, $220M in the 2026 outlook). Strip those out and the underlying business is growing: organic contribution to site rental billings was $38M or 4.2% in Q2 2026 excluding DISH and Sprint, and the full-year 2026 outlook assumes about 3.5% on the same basis. The whole investment case is the gap between those two numbers — reported shrinking, underlying growing — and how long it takes for the drag to roll off.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

~40,000 U.S. towers in place Long-term master lease agreements Zoning & permitting barriers to new builds Near-zero marginal cost per tenant 100% fixed-rate debt (3.7% avg)

A tower moat is local and physical. A carrier that needs coverage in a given spot either rents on the tower that already stands there or tries to get a new one permitted — slowly, expensively, and often against neighbourhood opposition. Once an antenna is on, moving it means re-engineering the network around it, so leases renew. Crown Castle, American Tower and SBA Communications are the three large listed U.S. tower owners. The vulnerability is the mirror image: a landlord with three big tenants has three big negotiators, and when one of them merges (Sprint into T-Mobile) or walks away (DISH), the landlord has no one else to rent that space to at the same price.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Customer concentration is 93%. T-Mobile 42%, AT&T 28%, Verizon 23%. With the fiber and small-cell businesses gone, there is no second leg. About $774M of annualised AT&T leases come up for renewal in 2028 — a single negotiation worth roughly a fifth of site rental revenue (computed against the $3.855B FY2026 guide midpoint).
02
The fourth carrier is gone. EchoStar sold spectrum licences to AT&T and SpaceX in deals valued at more than $40B, and DISH stopped paying. Crown Castle terminated in January 2026 and claims more than $3.5B. DISH denies breach. Losing a growth tenant removes the colocation upside the tower model depends on; the litigation outcome is binary and not in guidance.
03
Leverage is still REIT-high. Net debt was 6.3x annualised adjusted EBITDA after the paydown, inside a stated 6.0–6.5x target. Debt is all fixed-rate at a 3.7% weighted average, which protects near-term AFFO but means every refinancing above 3.7% is a headwind.
04
No diversification left. Unlike American Tower, whose reported results carry foreign-exchange and data-center effects, Crown Castle has no international towers and no data-center business. The pure-play simplicity that management sells is also a single-country, single-asset-class exposure to U.S. carrier capex cycles — and JPMorgan's September 2026 downgrade was explicitly about low tower activity.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the May 1, 2026 sale: fiber and small cells are discontinued operations, so trailing revenue, cash-flow and income figures mix two different companies until the TTM window rolls past mid-2027. Second, interest savings: the +11% AFFO/share in Q2 2026 came from lower interest expense and interest income on proceeds, not from leasing — a one-time step-up that will not repeat as growth. Third, the $1B buyback and $7B+ of debt repayment change the per-share base; diluted shares were 434M in Q2 and the 2026 outlook assumes 430M, against 425.46M currently outstanding per StockAnalysis. Long-run per-share series (EPS, dividends) span both the pre-sale and post-sale company and the June 2025 dividend reset; there have been no stock splits.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $173.32 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $73.22, CCI lost 57.8% of its price, and 64.9% from the December 2021 month-end peak of $208.74. Dividends soften that but do not rescue it: adding back roughly $26 per share paid over the window (an estimate from annual dividends per share, not a reinvested total return) leaves the holder down on the order of 40%. Some of the fall is the rate cycle — a long-lease landlord is a bond with a roof on it, and it was repriced as such in 2022–2023 — and some is the company's own fiber bet, which ended in a sale for less than the market had once valued it and a one-third dividend cut.

The 6-period monthly RSI reads 27.1 — oversold, and it has spent most of the last twelve months below 35. The five-year extreme was 17.4 in September 2023, at the rate-cycle low of $92.03. The 10-month moving average sits at about $82.82, well above the price, and the 3-month average at $75.22: the long-term trend is still down, and the June–September stretch has been a flat base at the lows rather than a turn.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 16x forward AFFO for ~40,000 U.S. towers, a 5.80% dividend covered by AFFO at roughly 93%, 100% fixed-rate debt at 3.7%, and leverage already inside the 6.0–6.5x target after $7B+ of paydown. The reported revenue decline is the Sprint and DISH churn rolling off; underneath, organic growth ex-DISH/Sprint ran 4.2% in Q2 2026. Once the DISH drag is fully in the base, reported and organic growth converge, and a simpler pure-play landlord with a smaller share count should earn a multiple closer to its peers. The DISH lawsuit — more than $3.5B claimed — is a free option. Every analyst target on the board, including the lone Sell at $80, sits above the current price.

Bear Case

A landlord with three tenants controlling 93% of rent, one of which (AT&T, $774M of annualised leases) renegotiates in 2028, has little pricing power over the people who pay it. The fourth carrier is gone, so the colocation growth that made tower economics special now depends on three carriers' capex budgets — and JPMorgan's downgrade said activity is low. The Q2 AFFO beat came from interest savings, not leasing, and the market ignored it. Organic growth of 3.5% guided for 2026 is barely above inflation, the dividend was cut once already, and the stock has made lower highs for five years. Cheap on AFFO is what a no-growth, rate-sensitive, concentrated landlord looks like.

Top 3 Risks

01
Carrier concentration and the 2028 AT&T renewal. T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon are 93% of site rental revenue. A renewal on worse terms, or a network-sharing deal between two of them, reprices the core asset directly.
02
Low leasing activity. With DISH out and carriers in a slower spending phase, organic growth depends on amendments from three customers. The 2026 outlook assumes 3.5% ex-DISH/Sprint; a miss would hit AFFO growth when interest savings are no longer there to cover it.
03
Rates and leverage. At 6.3x net debt / EBITDA, a long-duration REIT is priced off the bond market. Higher long-term yields compress the multiple, and refinancing debt currently fixed at 3.7% at higher rates erodes AFFO per share.
Industry Commentary
The fourth carrier that wasn't
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The U.S. tower industry's growth story rested on a fourth national carrier building out a new network. EchoStar's sale of spectrum licences to AT&T and SpaceX, in deals valued at more than $40B, ended that. DISH stopped paying and, in court, denies breach — the same defence trade press reports it used against American Tower. For Crown Castle the cost is concrete: a $220M revenue hole in 2026 guidance and a smaller pool of future tenants.

Churn from consolidation still rolling off
ONGOING HEADWIND

Sprint lease cancellations — the legacy of the T-Mobile/Sprint merger — took $204M off Crown Castle's FY2025 revenue and a further $5M in Q2 2026. It is a known, contracted drag, which is why management reports organic growth excluding it. The lesson for the industry is structural: every carrier merger is, eventually, a landlord's lost tenant.

The pure-play valuation argument — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: Crown Castle is now the only large U.S.-only pure-play tower company, with the fiber capital sink gone, debt cut by more than $7B, and AFFO guidance raised in May and again in July ($1.920B in February to $1.975B; per share $4.43 to $4.59). At ~16x AFFO and a 5.8% yield, the stock prices it as a bond, not as the owner of hard-to-replicate infrastructure. The bear framing: "pure-play" also means undiversified, and the market's response to a beat-and-raise in July was a 0.8% monthly gain. Investors want proof that organic leasing growth can carry AFFO once the one-time interest savings are in the base. The Q3 print on October 21 is the next data point; the 2028 AT&T renewal is the one that settles it.
