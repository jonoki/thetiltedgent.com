TWO STRUCTURAL NOTES BEFORE YOU READ ANY NUMBER

1. This is a dual-listed company (DLC), not a single corporation. "Carnival Corporation & plc" is two separately incorporated companies — Carnival Corporation (Panama, NYSE: CCL) and Carnival plc (England & Wales, NYSE ADR: CUK) — run under a single management team with a single set of combined financial statements. The financial statements used throughout this page are the combined DLC statements. The share count used here, 1,372 million (May 31, 2026 balance sheet, stockanalysis.com), is likewise the combined Carnival Corporation + Carnival plc count. The market cap shown above (~$34.0B) is that combined count multiplied by the CCL price of $24.76. That is the convention stockanalysis.com uses, and it is the honest way to size the enterprise — but be aware it values Carnival plc shares at the Carnival Corporation price, and CUK has historically traded at a discount to CCL. Any market cap you see for "Carnival" that is materially smaller may be counting only one half of the DLC.

2. Carnival's fiscal year ends November 30, not December 31. "FY2025" means the twelve months ended November 30, 2025 — it is not the calendar year 2025. The "TTM" (trailing twelve months) column throughout this page ends May 31, 2026, which is the end of fiscal Q2 2026. Fiscal Q3 2026 ended on August 31, 2026 and had not been reported as of this snapshot.
01 Company Overview

Carnival Corporation & plc is the world's largest cruise operator, running a multi-brand portfolio that includes Carnival Cruise Line, Princess Cruises, Holland America Line, Cunard, Seabourn, AIDA Cruises, Costa Cruises and P&O Cruises. Fiscal 2025 (ended November 30, 2025) revenue was a record $26.62B, up 6.40% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through May 31, 2026 is $27.31B, up 5.15%. The company ranks roughly #416 globally by market capitalization. But the number that actually explains this stock is not on the income statement: total debt peaked at $35.88B at the end of fiscal 2022 and stood at $24.89B on May 31, 2026 — and the share count roughly doubled along the way. This is a balance sheet story that happens to own 100-plus ships.

The cruise fare itself. Carnival sold 96.5 million ALBDs (available lower berth days — one berth, in a cabin counted at double occupancy, available for one day; it is the industry's unit of capacity, the equivalent of an airline's available seat mile) across fiscal 2025. In fiscal Q2 2026 the company operated 24.7 million ALBDs, up 2.1% YoY, at 104% occupancy (occupancy is passengers carried divided by ALBDs — because the denominator assumes exactly two people per cabin, any third or fourth berth sold pushes the figure above 100%; a reading of 104% means Carnival is filling cabins and selling the extra upper berths, not that it is overbooked). For scale on the recovery: in fiscal Q1 2019 the same metric was $167.86, so net yield is roughly 22% above pre-pandemic, on ~16% more capacity.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
LARGEST FLEET / SCALE NINE-BRAND PORTFOLIO $9.0B CUSTOMER DEPOSITS VALUE-PRICE POSITIONING SHIPYARD CAPACITY SCARCITY REPEAT-GUEST DATABASE PRIVATE DESTINATIONS

Carnival's moat is mostly arithmetic. It operates the industry's largest fleet across nine brands spanning North America, Europe and Australia, which gives it the ability to redeploy hulls between markets when one region turns hostile — the fiscal Q2 2026 commentary attributed the quarter's principal headwind to geopolitical disruption in Mediterranean deployments, a problem a single-region operator absorbs and a multi-region operator reroutes around. Capacity in this industry cannot be added quickly: a new large cruise ship is a multi-year, billion-dollar order at one of a handful of European yards, so supply growth is structurally capped and visible years ahead. That, combined with $9.0B of customer deposits — cash guests hand over months in advance, which funds working capital at zero interest and gives management an unusually long look at demand — is why cruise pricing has held up better than most consumer-discretionary categories. The positioning matters too: Carnival skews to a more value-oriented customer than Royal Caribbean. That shows in the financials as lower margin (11.2% net margin TTM vs 23.5% at RCL) and a lower net yield per ALBD, but it also means Carnival's guest is arguably less exposed to the "trade-down" risk — there is not much cheaper for them to trade down to when a week at sea, with food and lodging included, already undercuts most land-based vacations on a per-day basis. The counterpoint is that Carnival's customer has less discretionary cushion when the labour market softens.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
Balance sheet still carries the crisis. Total debt is $24.89B against $12.98B of shareholders' equity — a debt-to-equity ratio of 2.02 and a current ratio of 0.33. Net debt to EBITDA is 3.1x. Deleveraging has been fast, but the company has no slack if a demand shock arrives before the ladder is refinanced further.
B
Fuel is an uncapped input. Fuel cost per metric ton was $793 in fiscal Q2 2026 versus a $610 full-year fiscal 2025 average — roughly 30% higher YoY. Efficiency improved (28.2 metric tons per thousand ALBDs, better by 5.6%) but nowhere near enough to offset the price. Fuel is why fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS guidance of $2.22 sits slightly below fiscal 2025's $2.25 despite record yields.
C
Emissions regulation is a compounding capital claim. The EU Emissions Trading System now covers maritime, FuelEU Maritime imposes escalating greenhouse-gas intensity limits on fuel used in EU ports, and the IMO's decarbonisation trajectory tightens through 2030 and beyond. Carnival's Q2 2026 fuel disclosure explicitly quotes cost per metric ton "excluding allowances," which is itself the tell — allowances are now a separate, growing line. Compliance means shore-power retrofits, LNG-capable newbuilds and dual-fuel conversions, all of which compete with debt paydown for the same cash.
D
Event risk is asymmetric and non-diversifiable. A pandemic, a high-profile onboard outbreak, a maritime incident, or a regional conflict closing a deployment area hits revenue immediately while the cost base — ships, crew, insurance, interest — barely moves. 2020 is the proof: revenue fell from $20.83B in fiscal 2019 to $5.60B in fiscal 2020 and $1.91B in fiscal 2021, a 91% decline from peak, while the fleet still had to be maintained, crewed and financed.
  The Deleveraging Story — Read This Before the Multiples

In fiscal 2020 and 2021 Carnival's revenue went to essentially zero — $5.60B then $1.91B, against $20.83B in fiscal 2019 — while more than 100 ships continued to cost money every single day. The company survived by issuing an enormous amount of debt and equity at distressed terms. Everything reported since is the work of digging out of that hole. This is the whole point: the stock is down ~53% from 2019 while the company's total equity value is down only ~6%. The gap is not lost value — it is the second share issued next to every share that already existed.

Read that last row again, because it is the single most useful thing on this page. A retail investor who anchors on "CCL used to be $50 and it's $25, so it's half-recovered" has made an arithmetic error. The market capitalisation — what the whole company is worth — is within 6% of its 2019 level, on 31% more revenue, with 22% higher net yields and 16% more capacity. The enterprise has more than recovered. The per-share price has not, and it structurally cannot, because there are now two shares where there was one. Anyone waiting for $50 is implicitly waiting for a $71B market cap (1,372M × $52.27), which would be roughly double the 2019 valuation of a company that was, at the time, the industry leader in a good year. That is not impossible. It is simply a much bigger ask than the price chart makes it look. The flip side, and the bull's honest rejoinder: the buyback has now started — over $450M of repurchases under the current programme as of the Q2 2026 release — and the $0.15 quarterly dividend was reinstated in February 2026. Both are the mechanism by which the share count starts working for the holder rather than against.

One caveat stated plainly: the share count did not stop growing after the emergency raises. Shares outstanding went 1,136M (FY2021) → 1,259M (FY2022) → 1,264M (FY2023) → 1,308M (FY2024) → 1,312M (FY2025) → 1,372M (May 2026), and the FY2025 convertible redemption alone issued 69 million shares. Shares are still up 1.09% YoY per stockanalysis.com. The buyback is not yet large enough to shrink the count — it is, so far, only large enough to slow the growth.

Notable One-Off Items. Three items distort period-to-period comparability and should be adjusted for. (1) Debt extinguishment and refinancing charges. Carnival's GAAP and adjusted results diverge repeatedly because of losses on early debt extinguishment — a direct consequence of paying off expensive crisis-era paper ahead of schedule. In fiscal Q2 2026 GAAP net income was $537M and fell 5.0% YoY, while adjusted net income was $569M and rose 21.1%; the gap is largely refinancing cost. Treat GAAP declines in this company as frequently good news. (2) The FY2025 convertible note redemption. Settled with $500M of cash plus 69 million newly issued shares — a real, permanent dilution event embedded in an otherwise routine-looking deleveraging line. (3) Fuel. Not non-recurring, but the ~30% YoY increase in cost per metric ton in fiscal Q2 2026 is the single reason FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance ($2.22) sits below FY2025 actual ($2.25) despite record yields, record revenue and record customer deposits. Strip fuel out and the operating trajectory is unambiguously up.

In plain English: RSI measures how one-sided recent trading has been. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It says nothing about whether the business is good. Computed here on monthly closes, so each step is a month, not a day — this is a multi-year momentum reading, not a swing-trading signal.

The five-year trajectory: a round trip, not a recovery. The chart above is the whole argument. Carnival closed September 2021 at $25.01 and closed August 28, 2026 at $24.76 — a five-year price return of −1.00%, a CAGR of −0.20%. Five years, zero. What happened in between was violent in both directions: from that $25.01 the stock fell 71.9% to a monthly-close low of $7.03 in September 2022, when $35.9B of debt sat against a $10.5B equity value and the market was openly pricing a wipeout. It then compounded +252% off that trough to a five-year closing high of $31.89 in August 2025, retested $31.55 in February 2026, and has since given back 21.5% — a lower high, then a lower low, over the last six months.

Why the round trip is the point. The enterprise recovered and the share did not, because the recovery was paid for in shares. The count went from 693M (Q1 2019) to ~1,136M by FY2021 and is still drifting up (+1.09% YoY). Market capitalisation tells the flattering version — $19.56B at the end of 2020, $10.47B at the end of 2022, then +131.34% in 2023, +38.07% in 2024, +23.85% in 2025 to $41.41B, and ~$34.0B today — an enterprise that has quadrupled off the bottom and now sits within 6% of its 2019 value. The per-share chart tells the version an owner actually experienced: the equity value came back, but it came back divided across roughly 70% more shares, so a holder from September 2021 has earned nothing across a full operating recovery, a return to profitability, a record year, and an investment-grade upgrade. The 12-month moving average ($28.10) sits above the 6-month ($26.93) and the price ($24.76) sits below both — the textbook shape of a trend that has rolled over, not one that is basing. Trailing 52 weeks the stock is down 24.85%, against a 52-week range of $23.45 – $34.03: within about 6% of the low, roughly 27% below the high. Monthly RSI(14) at 48.9 is squarely neutral — this is not a washed-out tape, it is a stock that has simply stopped working.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The deleveraging thesis is working on schedule and is now externally validated: debt down $10.99B from the 2022 peak, net debt/EBITDA at 3.1x, and an S&P upgrade to BBB− that returns Carnival to investment grade for the first time since June 2020 — which lowers the cost of every future refinancing and reopens the buyer base for its paper. Meanwhile the operating business is at records on every metric management reports: twelve consecutive quarters of record net yields, $9.0B of customer deposits, 93% of 2026 booked, 2027 running ahead on both volume and price. At 11.36x trailing and 10.60x forward earnings with a 2.42% yield, a restarted buyback and the lowest target on the entire street ($28.70) sitting 16% above the current price, you are being paid to wait while interest expense — currently ~18% of EBITDA — converts into equity value one refinancing at a time.

Bear Case

The share count roughly doubled and is still growing (+1.09% YoY; 69 million shares issued in the FY2025 convertible redemption alone), so the per-share recovery will always lag the enterprise recovery — and the enterprise is already valued within 6% of its 2019 level, meaning most of the easy re-rating is behind you, not ahead. Fuel at $793/metric ton, up ~30% YoY, is enough on its own to push FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance ($2.22) below FY2025 actual ($2.25) despite record everything. The balance sheet still carries $24.89B of debt against $12.98B of equity at a 0.33 current ratio, BBB− is one notch from junk, and beta of 2.34 means any recession scare hits this equity roughly twice as hard as the index. The market gave Carnival a record quarter and an investment-grade upgrade on the same day in June — and the stock is 18% lower since.

Top 3 Risks

1
Consumer demand rolling over before the balance sheet is finished. Carnival needs roughly two more years of $3B+ free cash flow to get leverage comfortably inside investment grade. A recession that dents booking volumes would hit customer deposits first, revenue second, and the credit rating third — and at BBB− there is exactly one notch of cushion before the cost of debt reprices against the company again.
2
Fuel and emissions cost, compounding together. Fuel is already the reason earnings are guided flat in a record year. Layered on top are EU ETS allowances, FuelEU Maritime intensity limits and IMO decarbonisation targets, all of which convert into either higher operating cost or higher capex for shore power, LNG capability and dual-fuel retrofits. Both compete directly with debt paydown for the same free cash flow.
3
Continued dilution outrunning the buyback. More than $450M of repurchases has not yet been enough to shrink the share count — shares are still up 1.09% YoY. Until buybacks exceed issuance from equity compensation and convertible settlements, every dollar of enterprise value created is divided across a slowly growing denominator, and per-share metrics improve more slowly than the headline financials suggest.
  Industry Commentary
Fuel & Emissions Regulation
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Fuel cost per metric ton ran $793 in fiscal Q2 2026 against a $610 FY2025 full-year average — roughly 30% higher year over year. Carnival improved consumption to 28.2 metric tons per thousand ALBDs, better by 5.6%, and it was not close to enough. The company's own disclosure quotes cost "excluding allowances," which flags that emissions allowances are now a separate and growing charge under the EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime regimes. This is the reason a company setting revenue, yield and deposit records is guiding fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS ($2.22) marginally below fiscal 2025 actual ($2.25). For a fleet this large, every $100/metric ton move in bunker prices is a material earnings event, and the regulatory ratchet only moves one way.
Value Positioning vs Premium Peers
ONGOING HEADWIND
Carnival runs the industry's largest fleet aimed at a more value-oriented customer than Royal Caribbean, and the market prices that difference brutally. RCL earns a 23.54% net margin and 15.54% ROIC; CCL earns 11.24% and 11.73%. RCL trades at 17.23x trailing earnings; CCL at 11.36x. RCL's market cap is $72.74B on $18.68B of revenue; Carnival's is ~$34.0B on $27.31B. Some of that gap is deserved — RCL's newbuilds and private-destination strategy command genuine pricing power. Some of it is stale: CCL's debt-to-equity (2.02) is now below RCL's (2.25), and CCL generates $3.20B of TTM free cash flow while RCL's is negative. The question for the next two years is whether the multiple gap narrows on Carnival's own execution, or whether the value customer is simply worth less per berth in perpetuity.
Advance Bookings as the Real Signal
EMERGING TAILWIND
Bull framing: customer deposits of $9.0B are a record, up over $450M YoY; 93% of fiscal 2026 capacity is already booked; the booking curve is the "furthest out on record"; and 2027 volumes and prices are both running ahead. In an industry where guests pay months in advance, this is a genuine forward indicator, not a management adjective — and it is why S&P cited "robust advance bookings" by name in its investment-grade upgrade. Bear framing: a booking curve this extended is also a curve with nothing left to sell into strength. Being 93% booked for 2026 means the remaining upside for this fiscal year is 7% of capacity plus onboard spend; it also means any demand deterioration shows up not as slower bookings but as cancellations and discounting into a fixed cost base. Deposits are the best early-warning system in this sector — which cuts both ways, and the next reading comes September 28, 2026.
