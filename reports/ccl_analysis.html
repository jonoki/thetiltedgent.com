TWO STRUCTURAL NOTES BEFORE YOU READ ANY NUMBER

1. Since May 7, 2026 this is one company, not two. Until this year "Carnival Corporation & plc" was a dual-listed company: two separately incorporated parents, Carnival Corporation (Panama, NYSE: CCL) and Carnival plc (England & Wales, London-listed and traded in New York as CUK), run as one business. On May 7, 2026 the company completed the unification of that structure under a single company, Carnival Corporation Ltd.: Carnival Corporation moved its incorporation from Panama to Bermuda and took the new name, Carnival plc became a UK subsidiary, and Carnival plc's listings in London and New York were cancelled. Each Carnival plc share became one Carnival Corporation Ltd. common share, and the shares trade on the NYSE as CCL. So there is now one share price and one share count: 1,344.6 million shares outstanding on September 22, 2026 (cover of the fiscal Q3 10-Q). The market cap above, ~$33.7B, is that count multiplied by the October 1 close of $25.07 (our calculation).

2. Carnival's fiscal year ends November 30, not December 31. "FY2025" means the twelve months ended November 30, 2025. The "TTM" (trailing twelve months) figures on this page end August 31, 2026, the end of fiscal Q3 2026, which Carnival reported on September 29, 2026. Fiscal Q4 runs to November 30, 2026.

01 Company Overview

Carnival Corporation Ltd. describes itself as "the largest global cruise company and among the largest leisure travel companies." It runs eight cruise lines: AIDA Cruises, Carnival Cruise Line, Costa Cruises, Cunard, Holland America Line, P&O Cruises, Princess Cruises and Seabourn, with a fleet of more than 90 ships. Fiscal 2025 (ended November 30, 2025) revenue was a record $26.62B, up 6.40% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through August 31, 2026 is $27.59B, up 5.20%. But the number that actually explains this stock is not on the income statement. Total debt, including leases, peaked at $35.88B at the end of fiscal 2022 (on fiscal year-end figures) and stood at $25.16B on August 31, 2026, and the share count roughly doubled along the way. In 2026 that count has finally started to fall. This is a balance-sheet story that happens to own more than 90 ships.

In fiscal Q3 2026 Carnival operated 24.9 million ALBDs (available lower berth days: one berth in a cabin counted at double occupancy, available for one day; it is the industry's unit of capacity, like an airline's available seat mile), up from 24.6 million a year earlier, at 111.8% occupancy. Occupancy is passengers carried divided by ALBDs, and because the denominator assumes exactly two people per cabin, every third or fourth berth sold pushes it above 100%. The summer quarter, full of families, runs highest (fiscal Q2 was 104%). 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
LARGEST GLOBAL CRUISE COMPANY EIGHT-BRAND PORTFOLIO $7.6B CUSTOMER DEPOSITS VALUE-PRICE POSITIONING SHIPYARD CAPACITY SCARCITY REPEAT-GUEST DATABASE PRIVATE DESTINATIONS

Carnival's moat is mostly arithmetic. It is the largest global cruise company by its own description, with eight brands selling into the North American and European cruise markets. That spread lets it move ships between markets when one region turns hostile. In fiscal Q3 2026 the release cited elevated logistics costs from disruption caused by the Middle East conflict, the kind of problem a multi-region operator can route around. Capacity cannot be added quickly: a large new cruise ship is a multi-year, billion-dollar order at one of a handful of yards, so supply growth is capped and visible years ahead. Carnival is guiding its own 2027 capacity up only 0.5%. Add $7.6B of customer deposits, cash guests hand over months before they sail, which funds working capital at no interest and gives management a long look at demand. Private destinations add to the pull: Celebration Key on Grand Bahama welcomed almost 2.5 million guests in its first year, and the CEO expects about 3.5 million next year. The positioning matters too. Carnival skews to a more value-oriented customer than Royal Caribbean, which shows up as a lower margin (11.4% net margin TTM vs 23.5% at RCL). It also means Carnival's guest has less to trade down to when a week at sea, food and lodging included, already undercuts many land holidays. The counterpoint is that the same customer has less cushion when the job market softens.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
The balance sheet still carries the crisis. The company's own debt measure was $23.91B at August 31, 2026, against $14.19B of shareholders' equity. On stockanalysis's broader measure (leases included), debt-to-equity is 1.77 and the current ratio 0.27. Net debt to trailing adjusted EBITDA is about 3.1x by our calculation. Debt is coming down quickly, but BBB− leaves little room if a demand shock hits first.
B
Fuel is an uncapped input. Fuel cost per metric ton was $826 in fiscal Q3 2026 against $607 a year earlier, up about 36%. Consumption per thousand ALBDs improved from 28.0 to 26.9 metric tons, nowhere near enough to offset the price. The release put the net hit from fuel prices and currency at $0.10 a share ($131M) for the quarter, and guides fourth-quarter fuel at $896 a ton. Fuel expense rose $223M in the first nine months ($1,607M against $1,384M), about 16 cents a share, while fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS guidance of about $2.24 still sits a cent below fiscal 2025's $2.25 despite record yields.
C
Emissions rules are a growing claim on capital. The EU Emissions Trading System now covers shipping, FuelEU Maritime sets tightening greenhouse-gas limits on fuel burned on EU voyages, and the IMO's decarbonisation path tightens through 2030 and beyond. Carnival's full-year guidance already treats emission allowances as part of the fuel bill: $2.25B of fuel expense for fiscal 2026. Compliance means shore-power fittings, LNG-capable newbuilds and dual-fuel conversions, all competing with debt paydown and buybacks for the same cash.
D
Event risk is lopsided and can't be diversified away. A pandemic, a high-profile onboard outbreak, a maritime incident or a regional conflict that closes a cruising area hits revenue immediately, while the cost base of ships, crew, insurance and interest barely moves. 2020 is the proof: revenue fell from $20.83B in fiscal 2019 to $5.60B in fiscal 2020 and $1.91B in fiscal 2021, a 91% fall from peak, while the fleet still had to be maintained, crewed and financed.
  The Deleveraging Story — Read This Before the Multiples

In fiscal 2020 and 2021 Carnival's revenue all but vanished, to $5.60B and then $1.91B against $20.83B in fiscal 2019, while the fleet kept costing money every day. The company survived by issuing an enormous amount of debt and equity on distressed terms, and everything reported since is the work of digging out of that hole. This is the whole point: the stock is down ~52% from that level while the company's total equity value is down only ~7%. The gap is not lost value; it is the extra share issued for nearly every share that already existed.

Read that last row again, because it is the single most useful thing on this page. A retail investor who thinks "CCL used to be $50 and it's $25, so it's half-recovered" has made an arithmetic error. The market capitalisation, what the whole company is worth, is within 7% of its 2019 level on 32% more revenue. The company has more than recovered; the per-share price has not, and it structurally cannot, because there are now nearly two shares where there was one. Anyone waiting for $50 is implicitly waiting for a market cap of about $70B (1,344.6M × $52.27), almost double the 2019 valuation of a company that was then the industry leader in a good year. That is not impossible. It is simply a much bigger ask than the price chart makes it look. The bull's honest reply: the share count is now falling. Carnival has bought back about $1.2B of stock this fiscal year, 45 million shares by the CFO's count, and with the $0.15 quarterly dividend he expects nearly $2 billion returned to shareholders this fiscal year.

The count kept growing long after the emergency raises. On stockanalysis's fiscal year-end figures shares went 1,136M (FY2021) → 1,259M (FY2022) → 1,264M (FY2023) → 1,308M (FY2024) → 1,312M (FY2025). Then, on December 5, 2025, Carnival settled its convertible notes with $500M of cash and 69 million new shares. Since then buybacks have taken the count down to 1,352M on the August 31, 2026 balance sheet and 1,344.6M on September 22, and stockanalysis shows shares down 0.89% YoY. The buyback is now big enough to shrink the denominator, not just slow its growth.

Notable One-Off Items. Three items make periods hard to compare. (1) Debt extinguishment and refinancing charges. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The deleveraging thesis is working and is now externally validated: debt is down $10.72B from the fiscal 2022 peak (leases included), net debt/EBITDA is about 3.1x, S&P's BBB− makes two investment-grade ratings, and no secured debt remains. The business keeps setting records: all-time-high net yields, record third-quarter deposits of $7.6B, and 2027 booked at record occupancy and pricing with capacity up only 0.5%. That is a supply-constrained setup. For the first time since the crisis the share count is shrinking: about $1.2B of buybacks this fiscal year, 45 million shares, on top of a $0.60 annual dividend. At 11.04x trailing and 9.75x forward earnings with a 2.39% yield, and the lowest target on stockanalysis's list ($28.70) 14% above the price, you are paid to wait while interest expense keeps falling (−16.6% in nine months) and turns into equity value one refinancing at a time.

Bear Case

Even after the buyback the share count is nearly double 2019's, so the per-share recovery will keep lagging the company's, and the company is already valued within 7% of its 2019 level: most of the easy re-rating is behind you. Fuel at $826 a ton in Q3 (up ~36%) and $896 guided for Q4 has added $223M of fuel expense in nine months, and fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS (~$2.24) is guided a cent below fiscal 2025 despite record everything. Revenue growth has slowed to mid-single digits, with 3.5% in Q3. Debt is still $23.91B on the company's measure, BBB− is one notch from junk, the current ratio is 0.27, and a beta of 2.38 means any recession scare hits this stock about twice as hard as the index. Analysts cut more targets than they raised even after the beat, and the stock is 21% below its 2025 high.

Top 3 Risks

1
Consumer demand rolling over before the balance sheet is finished. Carnival needs more years of $3B-plus free cash flow to settle comfortably inside investment grade. A recession that dented bookings would hit customer deposits first, revenue second and the credit rating third. At BBB− there is exactly one notch of cushion before borrowing costs reprice against the company.
2
Fuel and emissions costs, compounding together. Higher fuel prices took $150M off adjusted net income against June guidance, by the release's count, and Q4's $896-a-ton guide is higher still. On top come EU ETS allowances, FuelEU Maritime limits and IMO decarbonisation targets, which turn into higher operating costs or higher capex for shore power, LNG capability and dual-fuel conversions. Both compete with debt paydown and buybacks for the same free cash flow.
3
Capital returns running ahead of the balance sheet. The CFO expects nearly $2 billion returned to shareholders this fiscal year in buybacks and dividends, while debt is still $23.91B and the rating sits on the lowest investment-grade rung. That is a good use of cash while the cycle holds. If demand turns, every dollar spent on stock is a dollar not spent cutting debt.
  Industry Commentary
Fuel & Emissions Regulation
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Fuel cost per metric ton ran $826 in fiscal Q3 2026 against $607 a year earlier, about 36% higher, and the company guides $896 for Q4 and $768 for the full year. Carnival cut consumption to 26.9 metric tons per thousand ALBDs from 28.0, and it was not close to enough. Full-year fuel expense is guided at $2.25B with emission allowances included. Fuel expense rose $223M in the first nine months, about 16 cents a share, and a company setting yield and deposit records guides fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS (~$2.24) a cent below fiscal 2025's actual ($2.25). For a fleet this size, every big move in bunker prices is a material earnings event, and the regulatory ratchet only turns one way.
Value Positioning vs Premium Peers
ONGOING HEADWIND
Carnival aims its fleet at a more value-oriented customer than Royal Caribbean does, and the market prices that difference harshly. RCL earns a 23.54% net margin and 15.54% ROIC; CCL earns 11.37% and 11.59%. RCL trades at 16.66x trailing earnings; CCL at 11.04x. RCL's market cap is $72.21B on $18.68B of revenue; Carnival's is ~$33.7B on $27.59B. Some of that gap is deserved: RCL's newbuilds and private-destination strategy command real pricing power. Some of it looks stale: CCL's debt-to-equity (1.77) is below RCL's (2.25), CCL generates $3.18B of TTM free cash flow while RCL's is negative, and Carnival now has its own private destination, Celebration Key, drawing millions of guests. The question for the next two years is whether the gap narrows on Carnival's own execution, or whether the value customer is simply worth less per berth for good.
Advance Bookings as the Real Signal
EMERGING TAILWIND
Bull framing: third-quarter customer deposits of $7.6B are a record, $0.5B above last year, despite flat capacity growth; 2027 is about half booked at record occupancy and pricing; and the company says 2028 is off to a good start. In an industry where guests pay months ahead, this is a real forward indicator, not a management adjective, and S&P pointed to forward bookings when it upgraded Carnival to investment grade. Bear framing: a booking curve this far out leaves little to sell into strength, and with 2027 capacity up only 0.5%, yield growth has to do all the work. If demand weakens, it shows up not as slower bookings but as cancellations and discounting against a fixed cost base. Deposits are the best early-warning system in this sector, and they cut both ways. The next reading comes with the fiscal Q4 report.
