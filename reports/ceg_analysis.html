01 Company Overview

Constellation Energy is the largest competitive power producer in the United States and, by its own description, "the nation's largest producer of clean, low-carbon energy," delivering roughly 10% of the country's clean energy. It is an independent power producer (IPP) — not a rate-regulated utility — meaning earnings are driven by wholesale power prices, hedging, and long-dated bilateral contracts rather than an allowed regulatory return. Following the January 7, 2026 close of the Calpine acquisition, the combined company operates roughly 55 GW of generation capacity across nuclear, natural gas, geothermal, wind, solar and hydro, and serves about 2.5 million retail and business customers. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $25.53B (+8.3% YoY); TTM revenue through Q2 2026 has jumped to $31.27B (+26.0% YoY), almost entirely because Calpine was consolidated for the first time. Constellation ranks #254 globally and #125 in the US by market capitalization.

The nuclear fleet is the earnings engine and the reason CEG trades at an IPP-plus multiple. In Q2 2026 the fleet ran a 93.0% capacity factor and produced roughly 40 terawatt-hours while executing six planned refueling outages that averaged 23 days — about 40% shorter than the ~38-day industry average, which is a direct margin lever because every outage day is lost zero-marginal-cost output. Capacity in GW is not separately disclosed in the sources reached; ~20 GW is derived arithmetically from Q2 output ÷ (91 days × 24h × 0.93) and should be treated as an estimate. The critical commercial development is that roughly 30% of clean baseload output is now under long-term contract, versus a fleet that was historically merchant and hedged only 1–3 years out. Nuclear output also carries a federal production tax credit (PTC) floor — an inflation-adjusted revenue backstop projected at $50.88/MWh by 2030 (raised from $49.88 by the 2025 IRS inflation adjustment, worth roughly +$0.30/share to base earnings) that caps downside if power prices fall.

This is the segment the equity is really being priced on. Constellation sells power and energy services to ~2.5 million retail and business customers, but the marginal value driver is the long-dated power purchase agreement (PPA) book with hyperscalers and large corporates. In Q2 2026 the company signed roughly 920 MW of nuclear PPAs with investment-grade counterparties at an average 18.5-year term, commencing 2029–2031 — 890 MW from existing capacity and 30 MW from a Dresden uprate. The one named counterparty was Walmart (176 MW, its first-ever nuclear PPA); management said all deals priced within the previously disclosed $20–$50/MWh range and described the forward pipeline as "robust and active." The anchor deal remains the 20-year Microsoft PPA signed in 2024 to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1 as the Crane Clean Energy Center (835 MW). Each incremental PPA converts merchant exposure into contracted, credit-backed cash flow — which is the mechanism by which an IPP earns a utility-like (or better) multiple.

Constellation's cheapest new megawatts do not come from greenfield build — they come from squeezing more output out of assets that already have interconnection rights. The company has said uprates across the existing nuclear fleet "could add up to 2,000 megawatts of new, clean and reliable baseload capacity," and the 30 MW Dresden uprate already sold into a PPA is the template. The Crane Clean Energy Center restart is the flagship: 835 MW of pressurized-water reactor capacity, targeted for 2027, supported by a $1B Department of Energy loan announced in late 2025. Progress markers as of mid-2026: the site is roughly 80% staffed with 500 full-time employees, steam generator and main generator inspections are complete or near complete, the NRC granted a fuel license amendment, and in June 2026 FERC approved a waiver transferring capacity interconnection rights from the retired Eddystone station to Crane — resolving a PJM queue issue that had raised the risk of slipping to 2031. Wind, solar and hydro round out the carbon-free portfolio but are not separately material to earnings.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Largest US Nuclear Fleet Irreplaceable Interconnection Rights Nuclear PTC Revenue Floor 18.5-Year Investment-Grade PPAs 23-Day Refueling Outages 55 GW Multi-Market Scale NRC Licence & Operator Barrier

Constellation's moat is physical and regulatory rather than technological. Nuclear plants in the United States are effectively non-replicable at current cost and permitting timelines: the licensing, siting, spent-fuel handling and skilled-operator requirements mean the existing fleet is a closed set, and Constellation owns the largest piece of it. That scarcity has become an economic moat only recently, because hyperscaler demand for firm, carbon-free, 24/7 power co-located with grid interconnection turned a commodity into a differentiated product. The second layer is operational: a 93.0% capacity factor and 23-day refueling outages versus a ~38-day industry average translate directly into incremental zero-fuel-cost megawatt-hours on assets whose fixed costs are already sunk. Third, the nuclear PTC creates an asymmetric payoff — an inflation-indexed floor projected at $50.88/MWh by 2030 truncates downside if wholesale prices collapse, while leaving Constellation fully exposed to upside above the strike. Fourth, the Calpine deal added dispatchable gas in ERCOT and California, letting Constellation sell shaped, firm products rather than raw nuclear output. Finally, the contract book itself is becoming a moat: 18.5-year average terms with investment-grade counterparties lock in cash flows that competitors with smaller carbon-free fleets simply cannot offer at the same scale.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Narrative-dependent valuation. At a 26.6× trailing P/E against an XLU utility-sector 21.6× and an IPP peer median near 28×, CEG carries a premium that is underwritten by expectations of continued data-center contracting rather than by current regulated earnings. The stock is already −33.6% from its 52-week high of $412.70 and −15.1% over the past year, which is a live demonstration of how quickly that premium can unwind when PPA announcements slow.
▸
Commodity and hedge volatility. As an IPP, reported GAAP results swing with mark-to-market on the hedge book. Q2 2026 GAAP EPS fell to $1.42 from $2.67 even as adjusted operating EPS rose to $2.55 from $1.91 — a 45% GAAP decline against a 34% adjusted increase in the same quarter. H1 2026 fair-value changes on derivatives reduced operating cash flow by $589M. Investors are effectively asked to trust a non-GAAP number.
▸
Leverage and integration risk from Calpine. Long-term debt rose to $19.11B from $7.25B at year-end 2025, total debt is $24.70B, and $11.11B of goodwill now sits on the balance sheet. If gas spark spreads in ERCOT or California compress, or if synergy delivery slips, that goodwill is impairment-exposed and the deal's accretion math weakens.
▸
Regulatory and interconnection dependency. The Crane restart required a FERC waiver in June 2026 to move capacity interconnection rights from Eddystone; PJM had flagged a scenario pushing the unit to 2031. Behind-the-meter and co-location arrangements for data centers remain an unsettled area of FERC and state policy, and any adverse ruling on cost allocation or grid-charge treatment would directly damage the PPA economics that support the multiple. The nuclear PTC itself is a statutory construct subject to political change.

Notable One-Off Items. Constellation's FY2026 reported figures are heavily distorted, and the distortions run in both directions.

1. Calpine purchase accounting. The January 7, 2026 close introduced $11,107M of goodwill on total consideration of $21,835M (50M shares valued at $17,603M plus $4,342M cash), with $12,551M of debt assumed. Total assets jumped to $98.25B from $57.25B and long-term debt to $19.11B from $7.25B. Every YoY comparison in FY2026 — revenue +26.0%, H1 net income $2,103M versus $957M — is contaminated by this and should not be read as organic performance.

2. Mark-to-market on hedges. H1 2026 net fair-value changes on derivatives reduced operating cash flows by $589M; current derivative assets and liabilities stood at $2,167M and $716M. This is the primary reason Q2 GAAP EPS of $1.42 diverged so sharply from adjusted operating EPS of $2.55 (prior-year quarter: $2.67 GAAP versus $1.91 adjusted — the sign of the gap literally flipped year over year).

3. Nuclear fuel, PTC pass-through and decommissioning trusts. Nuclear PTC pass-through reductions lowered net operating revenue by roughly $275M in H1 2026. Separately, net realized and unrealized gains on nuclear decommissioning trust funds contributed $419M, with the NDT balance at $20,583M versus $19,396M a year earlier — a mark-driven, non-operating item that flatters GAAP results in up markets.

4. Divestiture gains. The DOJ-mandated sales — 4.4 GW of PJM gas to LS Power for $5.0B and Brazos Valley (606 MW) for $860M — are one-time proceeds at implied values of ~$1,142/kW and ~$1,420/kW respectively. They fund buybacks but are not recurring earnings.

From the first monthly close after the spin — $44.48 in February 2022 — to $274.17 on August 20, 2026, CEG has returned +516% on price alone over roughly four and a half years, or about +49% compounded annually. That is an extraordinary outcome for what began life as a merchant power spinoff that Exelon shareholders were widely expected to sell. The path had three distinct phases. Phase one (Feb 2022 – Jan 2024) was a re-rating from post-spin neglect to a recognised carbon-free asset: $44 to $120, driven by strong 2022 power prices, Inflation Reduction Act nuclear credits and buybacks. Phase two (Feb 2024 – Oct 2025) was the AI-power melt-up: $120 to a monthly-close peak of $375.48 in October 2025, a 3.1× move in twenty months catalysed first by data-center demand forecasts, then by the September 2024 Microsoft/Three Mile Island PPA, then by the January 2025 Calpine announcement. Phase three (Nov 2025 – Aug 2026) has been multiple compression: down to $248.37 in June 2026 before recovering to $274.17, a peak-to-trough drawdown of −33.9% and a 52-week return of −15.1%. Nothing broke operationally — capacity factors improved, guidance was raised, PPAs were signed. What changed was the price investors would pay for the story.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Constellation owns an irreplaceable 55 GW fleet anchored by the largest US nuclear position, running at a 93.0% capacity factor with 23-day refueling outages against a ~38-day industry norm. Demand is structurally inflecting: US generation hit a record 4,430 TWh in 2025 (+2.8%) after two flat decades, and roughly 30% of clean baseload is now locked into 18.5-year investment-grade PPAs at $20–$50/MWh. Management raised FY2026 adjusted operating EPS guidance to $11.50–$12.50 and reiterated a >20% base-earnings growth algorithm through 2029, with the nuclear PTC providing a $50.88/MWh floor by 2030. At 22.6× forward earnings against a $349.96 consensus target, the stock is already 33.6% off its high with no analyst carrying a sell rating — and CEG has the strongest balance sheet in the group at 0.76× debt-to-equity versus a 4.83× peer median.

Bear Case

CEG trades at 22.6× forward earnings and a 1.47 PEG versus an IPP peer median of 11.5× and 0.49 — a premium underwritten entirely by expectations of continued hyperscaler contracting, not by regulated returns. That premium is already compressing: the stock is −15.1% over twelve months and −33.9% peak-to-trough despite beating estimates, and every major broker in late July cut targets while holding ratings. If PPA announcements slow, if FERC or state regulators restrict behind-the-meter and co-location structures, or if AI capex plateaus, the multiple can compress toward peers without any operational deterioration — roughly a 40–50% de-rating. Meanwhile the fundamentals are noisier than the adjusted numbers suggest: Q2 GAAP EPS fell to $1.42 from $2.67 on hedge marks, H1 derivative fair-value swings cut operating cash flow by $589M, TTM free cash flow is just $295M, long-term debt has risen to $19.11B from $7.25B, and $11.11B of Calpine goodwill sits exposed to ERCOT and California spark spreads. Crane slipping past 2027 would remove the most visible near-term catalyst.

  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Power Demand and Behind-the-Meter Deals
EMERGING TAILWIND

US electricity generation reached a record 4,430 TWh in 2025, up 2.8% from 4,310 TWh in 2024 — a genuine break from roughly two decades of flat consumption, and EIA expects further growth through 2026–2027 led by commercial-sector data-center load. For a generator with spare interconnected capacity this is the most favourable demand backdrop in a generation. Constellation has monetised it directly: ~920 MW of nuclear PPAs signed in Q2 2026 at an average 18.5-year term with deliveries starting 2029–2031, pricing inside a disclosed $20–$50/MWh band, and Walmart's 176 MW deal marking the first nuclear PPA by a major retailer. About 30% of clean baseload is now contracted. The open question is regulatory: co-location and behind-the-meter structures remain contested at FERC and in state proceedings, and Constellation itself needed a June 2026 FERC waiver to move capacity interconnection rights from Eddystone to Crane. The demand is real; the legal architecture for capturing it is still being written.

The Revaluation of Existing Nuclear
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Five years ago US nuclear plants were closing for economic reasons — Three Mile Island Unit 1 shut in 2019. Today Constellation is spending to restart it as the 835 MW Crane Clean Energy Center under a 20-year Microsoft PPA, with a $1B DOE loan, ~80% of staffing already in place and a 2027 target. That reversal is the industry story in one asset. The economics rest on three legs: hyperscaler willingness to pay a premium for firm carbon-free power; the nuclear production tax credit, whose strike price the 2025 IRS inflation adjustment lifted to a projected $50.88/MWh by 2030 (worth ~$0.30/share) and which functions as a revenue floor; and the fact that uprates on existing licensed sites — up to 2,000 MW across Constellation's fleet — are far cheaper and faster than new build. The vulnerability is that two of those three legs are policy-dependent. A change in the federal nuclear credit regime would not merely trim EPS; it would remove the downside protection that justifies paying a premium multiple for merchant nuclear at all.

Power Prices, Capacity Markets and the Bull/Bear Frame
ONGOING HEADWIND

Capacity markets have repriced violently — management cited a PJM EMAAC reference price of $289.67 against $125.71 prior, a 2.3× move. For an IPP with no regulated true-up, that flows straight to earnings in both directions. The bull framing: tight reserve margins plus record demand mean scarcity pricing persists, the PTC floors the downside at $50.88/MWh by 2030, Calpine's gas fleet lets Constellation sell shaped firm products into ERCOT and California, and a 0.76× debt-to-equity ratio versus a 4.83× peer median means CEG can fund growth when levered peers cannot. Management's guidance raise to $11.50–$12.50 and >20% base-earnings algorithm through 2029 rest on this. The bear framing, weighted equally: capacity prices that tripled can halve; the entire IPP complex de-rated together over the past year (CEG −15.1%, Talen −14.5%, NRG −19.9%, Vistra −27.8%) while QQQ rose 24.7%, showing this is a sentiment-driven cohort; CEG's 22.6× forward P/E and 1.47 PEG sit roughly double the peer median on 11.5× and 0.49; TTM free cash flow is $295M against a $97B market cap; and $11.11B of goodwill plus $24.70B of debt were added to fund a gas fleet whose value depends on the same spark spreads. If AI capex plateaus or PPA signings pause for two quarters, the de-rating path back toward peer multiples implies substantially more downside than the $290 low analyst target contemplates.
