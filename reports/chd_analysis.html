01 Company Overview

In 1846 two brothers-in-law, Austin Church and John Dwight, started the business that became Church & Dwight. A hundred and eighty years later the arm-and-hammer logo is still on the box, but the company around it has become something else: a collector of niche consumer brands — OxiClean, Batiste dry shampoo, Waterpik, Trojan, Zicam, TheraBreath mouthwash, Hero acne patches and, since 2025, Touchland hand sanitiser — most of them bought rather than built, as smaller brands in niches the largest household-products companies had not locked up. FY2025 net sales were $6.20B, up 1.6%, with EPS under standard accounting rules (GAAP) of $3.02 and adjusted EPS (which strips out one-off items) of $3.53. Through nine months of 2026, net sales were $4.60B (+0.9%) with adjusted EPS of $2.76 (+3.4%). At $111.31 the company is worth $26.27B.

The 2026 story is a turnaround inside a staple. In 2025 the machine sputtered: organic sales (which strip out acquisitions, exits and currency) grew only 0.7% for the year, management cut its outlook on May 1, 2025, and then it pruned — exiting Flawless, Spinbrush and Waterpik showerheads and selling the VitaFusion and L'il Critters vitamin brands at year-end. The stock slid from a $111.20 close in February 2025 to $83.85 in December 2025. Pruning makes reported growth look worse and underlying growth look better, and that is what 2026 has shown: organic sales up 5.0% in Q1, 5.8% in Q2 and 5.1% in Q3, against reported sales of +0.2%, +1.6% and +1.0%. The market noticed — the stock went back above $104 in February, gave about half of that back by March, then climbed after the Q3 print to $111.31 on November 9, 2026, a touch above the February 2025 month-end close of $111.20. The central debate is whether 34.1 times the last twelve months' earnings is a fair price for a household-goods company growing organic sales at about 5% with the dead weight removed, or whether buyers are paying up for three good quarters.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

ARM & HAMMER brand (logo since 1867) TheraBreath mouthwash share 26.8% (record) Cat litter share 24% Repeat buyer of small brands Low spending on plants and equipment (~2% of sales) 30 years of dividend growth

The moat is not scale — at $26.27B, Church & Dwight is about a fifteenth the size of Procter & Gamble ($390.27B). It is a repeatable way of buying brands: find a small brand that leads a niche (Zicam in 2020 for $530M, TheraBreath in 2021 for $580M, Hero in 2022 for $630M, Touchland in 2025 for about $700M upfront plus up to $180M tied to its 2025 sales), plug it into a distribution and marketing machine that already exists, and grow it. That machine throws off cash — $1.06B of trailing free cash flow on spending on plants and equipment of about 2% of sales — which funds the next purchase. The weakness of the model is equally clear: not every buy works. The 2025 exits of Flawless, Spinbrush, Waterpik showerheads and the vitamin brands were the bill for the ones that did not.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
You pay a fast-grower's price for a household-goods company. 34.1 times the last twelve months' GAAP earnings and a PEG of 3.79 price in the good version of the story. Consensus EPS growth is about 6.9% for 2026 and 7.4% for 2027 (StockAnalysis). At that growth rate, any organic slowdown hits the share price, not just the earnings.
02
Acquisition risk is the business model. Growth depends on buying brands well. The 2025 clean-up — $51.0M of exit-related impairment in H1 2025 and a one-time $45.6M after-tax charge on the vitamin sale in Q4 — shows what a miss costs. Touchland, the latest buy, has slowed: on the Q3 call the CEO said its U.S. growth has fallen short of the company's original expectations and that the business will be "close to flattish all in" in 2026, with growth expected in 2027.
03
Cost inflation. On the Q3 call the CFO put 2026 inflation at about $150M, $50M more than the original outlook (the increase was $30M on the Q2 call), a mix of commodities and transportation. Laundry and litter are commodity-heavy categories where a cost spike is hard to pass on quickly. On the Q3 call the CEO said inflation will be "quite large next year", which he expects to offset with another record year of productivity savings, product mix and spreading fixed costs over more sales.
04
Buybacks have been thin. After $900M of repurchases in 2025, the company bought back only $37.1M of stock in the first nine months of 2026, against $600.0M a year earlier. With the Q3 release the board approved a new authorization of up to $1B with no expiry, replacing a program from 2021; the CEO called it "more housekeeping than anything" on the call, and an authorization is not a purchase. Net debt was $1.76B at September 30 ($2,206.9M of borrowings less $446.9M of cash, from the release's balance sheet).

Notable One-Off Items

GAAP earnings here are noisy, and the noise comes from portfolio decisions. In 2025 the company exited Flawless, Spinbrush and Waterpik showerheads, booking $51.0M of business-exit impairment in H1 2025, and took a one-time $45.6M after-tax charge in Q4 on the sale of the VitaFusion and L'il Critters vitamin brands to Piping Rock, completed December 31, 2025 (the vitamin brands were under 5% of 2025 net sales). H1 2026 had no impairment, and Q3 2026 adjustments were small too: GAAP operating income was $291.4M against $297.8M adjusted, with Touchland restricted-stock expense among the items excluded. The company says the exits together meant about $400M of lower revenue and the related costs left behind, to absorb in 2026 planning. Q2 2026 adjustments were small: $4.5M of ERP (business software) project costs and $6.3M of Touchland restricted-stock expense. The swings in net margin across FY2022 and FY2024 in the table also reflect charges: a $411M impairment of Flawless intangible assets in Q4 2022, after a major retailer discontinued some products, and $357.1M of tradename and other asset impairments in 2024, mainly in the vitamin business (FY2022 Form 10-K and the results release of January 31, 2025). The CFO said on the Q3 call that tariff refunds were a net of about $15M in the third quarter and should be about $5M in the fourth; the release lists them among the drivers of Q3 gross margin.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 9, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $89.38 to the November 9, 2026 settled close of $111.31, CHD gained 24.5% on price, about 4.5% a year before dividends, which are not included. The path went in two big cycles: a 2022 fall to $71.44 as gross margin dropped to 41.9%, a steady climb to a $111.20 month-end close in February 2025, a ten-month slide to $83.85 through the 2025 growth scare and portfolio clean-up, and a recovery that carried past that February 2025 level after the Q3 2026 print.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 17.8 in December 2025 — the most oversold reading in the five-year series, lower than the 25.0 of the September 2022 low — and has since climbed to 71.5, just above the 70 line that conventionally marks "overbought". The 10-month moving average, at about $99.91, sits 10.2% below the current price: the stock is trading well above its long-term trend line.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The clean-up worked. With the weak brands gone, organic growth jumped from 0.7% in 2025 to 5.0%, 5.8% and 5.1% in the first three quarters of 2026, led by volume rather than price, and management lifted full-year organic guidance to about 5%. Adjusted gross margin is guided up about 100 bps, e-commerce is a quarter of consumer sales and growing 20%, TheraBreath reached a record 26.8% mouthwash share, and $1.06B of annual free cash flow, with debt at 1.66 times EBITDA, funds the next acquisition and a new $1B buyback. A stock that has moved about half as much as the market, with 30 years of dividend growth and organic growth of about 5%, has historically commanded a higher price relative to its earnings.

Bear Case

At 34.1 times the last twelve months' earnings and 29.5 times expected 2026 earnings for about 7% consensus EPS growth, the price already assumes the good quarters continue. Reported sales are growing only about 0.5% this year, nine-month adjusted EPS is up just 3.4%, the average analyst target ($110.28) is below the $111.31 close, and large cost inflation is expected next year. The model's growth depends on buying brands well, and 2025 showed the cost when it doesn't: $51.0M of exit-related impairment in H1 2025 and a $45.6M after-tax charge on the vitamin sale. Barclays' $89 Sell target sits 20% below the current price. The five-year record is about 4.5% a year on price alone — a high price without high returns, until this quarter.

Top 3 Risks

01
A high price for the growth. The stock trades at a PEG of 3.79. If organic growth slows toward the ~4.0% Q4 guide, a household-goods stock at 34 times past earnings has a long way to fall toward what similar companies sell for, without any change in the business.
02
Input-cost inflation. On the Q3 call the CFO put 2026 inflation about $50M above the original outlook (it was $30M above on the Q2 call), a mix of commodities and transportation, and the CEO said inflation will be "quite large next year". Tariff refunds helped Q3 but fall to about $5M in Q4. Laundry and cat litter are commodity-heavy.
03
Acquisition execution. Touchland cost about $700M upfront plus up to $180M tied to its 2025 sales, and the CEO now expects it to be "close to flattish all in" in 2026. The next deal will be judged against the 2025 write-offs, and the company now also has a $1B buyback authorization competing for the same cash.
Industry Commentary
Cost inflation returns to household products
ONGOING HEADWIND

Household-products makers buy resin, chemicals, paper and freight, and 2026 has brought those costs back into focus. Church & Dwight's CFO put the full-year hit at roughly $30M on the Q2 call, citing "raw materials, transportation costs and various premiums resulting from the conflict in the Middle East," and raised the increase to $50M on the Q3 call, a mix of commodities and transportation. So far it has offset the hit — Q3 adjusted gross margin rose 170 bps on volume, productivity, tariff refunds and mix. How long that lasts is the sector's main question; the CEO said on the Q3 call that inflation will be "quite large next year".

Where Church & Dwight sits in the group
SCALE

On the November 9, 2026 close, StockAnalysis lists Procter & Gamble at $390.27B, Colgate-Palmolive at $77.85B, Kimberly-Clark at $68.70B and Clorox at $12.79B, against Church & Dwight at $26.27B — about a fifteenth of P&G and twice Clorox. It is a mid-sized name in a group of giants, which is why its edge has to come from faster growth in the niches it buys.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

The bull framing: a staple growing organic sales at about 5% by volume, with expanding gross margin and a history of buying category leaders, deserves its higher price — the 2025 clean-up removed the drag, and reported growth will catch up to organic growth once a full year has passed since the exits. The bear framing: 34.1 times the last twelve months' earnings with a 1.11% dividend yield and a ~4.0% free-cash-flow yield leaves little room for a slower quarter, and the five-year price return of about 4.5% a year shows that paying up has not served shareholders well. The test is simple and dated: Q4 organic growth against the ~4.0% guide, gross margin against about 100 bps of expansion for the year, and what the January investor meeting says about the 2030 plans.
