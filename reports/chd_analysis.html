01 Company Overview

In 1846 two brothers-in-law, Austin Church and John Dwight, started the business that became Church & Dwight. A hundred and eighty years later the arm-and-hammer logo is still on the box, but the company around it has become something else: a collector of niche consumer brands — OxiClean, Batiste dry shampoo, Waterpik, Trojan, Zicam, TheraBreath mouthwash, Hero acne patches and, since 2025, Touchland hand sanitiser — most of them bought rather than built, as smaller brands in niches the largest household-products companies had not locked up. FY2025 net sales were $6.20B, up 1.6%, with GAAP EPS of $3.02 and adjusted EPS of $3.53. At $95.49 the company is worth $22.65B.

The 2026 story is a turnaround inside a staple. In 2025 the machine sputtered: organic sales grew only 0.7% for the year, management cut its outlook on May 1, 2025, and then it pruned — exiting Flawless, Spinbrush and Waterpik showerheads and selling the VitaFusion and L'il Critters vitamin brands at year-end. The stock slid from a $111.20 close in February 2025 to $83.85 in December 2025. Pruning makes reported growth look worse and underlying growth look better, and that is what 2026 has shown: organic sales up 5.0% in Q1 and 5.8% in Q2, against reported sales of +0.2% and +1.6%. The market noticed — January and February 2026 took the stock back above $104 — then gave almost half of it back. The central debate is whether a 30.7x trailing multiple is fair for a staple growing organic sales at 4–5% with the dead weight removed, or whether it is paying up for two good quarters.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

ARM & HAMMER brand (logo since 1867) #2 mouthwash — TheraBreath 25.3% Cat litter share 24.5% Bolt-on M&A platform Asset-light (capex ~2% of sales) 30 years of dividend growth

The moat is not scale — at $22.65B, Church & Dwight is a fifteenth the size of Procter & Gamble. It is a repeatable way of buying brands: find a small brand that leads a niche (Zicam in 2020 for $530M, TheraBreath in 2021 for $580M, Hero in 2022 for $630M, Touchland in 2025 for $700M upfront plus earn-out), plug it into a distribution and marketing machine that already exists, and grow it. That machine throws off cash — $1.12B of trailing free cash flow on capex of about 2% of sales — which funds the next purchase. The weakness of the model is equally clear: not every buy works. The 2025 exits of Flawless, Spinbrush, Waterpik showerheads and the vitamin brands were the bill for the ones that did not.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
You pay a growth multiple for a staple. 30.7x trailing GAAP earnings and a PEG of 3.37 price in the good version of the story. Consensus EPS growth is about 7% a year for 2026 and 2027. At that growth rate, any organic slowdown hits the multiple, not just the earnings.
02
Acquisition risk is the business model. Growth depends on buying brands well. The 2025 clean-up — $51.0M of exit-related impairment in H1 2025 and a one-time $45.6M after-tax charge on the vitamin sale in Q4 — shows what a miss costs. Touchland, the latest and largest buy, is guided to "high single digits" growth — a lower rate than the brand-acquisition thesis usually needs.
03
Cost inflation. Management flagged roughly $30M of 2026 cost headwinds from raw materials, freight and Middle East conflict premiums, and the Q2 guide given on May 1, 2026 ($0.88 adjusted EPS) was well below the $0.97 analysts expected. Laundry and litter are commodity-heavy categories where a cost spike is hard to pass on quickly.
04
Buybacks paused. After $900M of repurchases in 2025, there were none in H1 2026 and none discussed on the Q2 call. With net debt of $2.17B, per-share growth now has to come from the business itself.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $82.57 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $95.49, CHD gained 15.6% on price. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The clean-up worked. With the weak brands gone, organic growth jumped from 0.7% in 2025 to 5.0% and 5.8% in the first two quarters of 2026, led by volume rather than price, and management raised full-year organic guidance to 4–5%. Gross margin is guided up another 100–120 bps, e-commerce is a quarter of consumer sales and growing 22.7%, TheraBreath and cat litter are gaining share, and $1.12B of annual free cash flow with 1.74x debt/EBITDA funds the next acquisition. A 0.47-beta stock with 30 years of dividend growth, growing organically at 4–5%, has historically earned a premium multiple — and at $95.49 it is still 14% below its February 2025 high.

Bear Case

At 30.7x trailing and 25.3x forward earnings for about 7% consensus EPS growth, the price already assumes the good quarters continue. Reported sales are growing only 0–1% this year, adjusted EPS fell 5.3% in Q2, buybacks have stopped, and roughly $30M of Middle East-linked cost inflation is in the numbers. The model's growth depends on buying brands well, and 2025 showed the cost when it doesn't: $51.0M of exit-related impairment in H1 2025 and a $45.6M after-tax charge on the vitamin sale. Barclays' $86 Sell target sits below the current price. The five-year record is about 4% a year including dividends — compounder pricing without compounder returns.

Top 3 Risks

01
Multiple compression. The stock trades at a PEG of 3.37. If Q3 organic growth lands at the ~3% guide instead of repeating Q2's 5.8%, a staple at 30x trailing has a long way to fall toward peer multiples without any change in the business.
02
Input-cost inflation. About $30M of 2026 headwinds from raw materials, freight and Middle East conflict premiums; the May 1 Q2 guide already missed consensus on it. Laundry and cat litter are commodity-heavy, and promotions have been cut by 300 bps, leaving less room to pull back further.
03
Acquisition execution. Touchland cost $700M upfront plus an earn-out and is now guided to "high single digits" growth. The next deal, from a screened list of about 100, will be judged against the 2025 write-offs.
Industry Commentary
Cost inflation returns to household products
ONGOING HEADWIND

Household-products makers buy resin, chemicals, paper and freight, and 2026 has brought those costs back into focus. Church & Dwight's CFO put the full-year hit at roughly $30M, citing "raw materials, transportation costs and various premiums resulting from the conflict in the Middle East," and the company guided Q2 below consensus in May on the same pressure. So far it has offset the hit — Q2 gross margin rose 40 bps adjusted — through mix and productivity, while cutting laundry promotion by 300 bps and holding share. How long that lasts is the sector's main question.

Divergence inside the staples group
DISPERSION

Household products has not moved as one block. On the September 21, 2026 close, Clorox was worth $10.26B after its market value fell 33.1%, while Church & Dwight's was up 1.1% at $22.65B — per StockAnalysis's comparison figures. The money is moving toward the companies with growing volumes, and away from the ones without.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

The bull framing: a staple growing organic sales at 4–5% by volume, with expanding gross margin and a history of buying category leaders, deserves its premium — the 2025 clean-up removed the drag, and reported growth will catch up to organic growth as the exits lap in 2026. The bear framing: 30.7x trailing earnings with a 1.29% dividend yield and a ~4.9% free-cash-flow yield leaves little room for a slower quarter, and the five-year return of about 4% a year shows that the premium has not paid shareholders well. The test is simple and dated: organic growth on October 30 against the ~3% guide, and whether gross margin is still expanding.
