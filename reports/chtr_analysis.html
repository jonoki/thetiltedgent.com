01 Company Overview

Charter sells home internet, mobile, TV and phone service under the Spectrum brand. On August 20, 2026 it closed its $34.5B combination with Cox Communications, which takes its footprint to more than 70 million homes and businesses across 45 states. Within a year, the parent company will rename itself Cox Communications while still trading as Spectrum in every market. Before the deal, standalone Charter reported FY2025 revenue of $54.77B (−0.56%), and EPS kept climbing anyway: $24.47 in FY2021, $36.21 in FY2025. The reason is a shrinking share count, not a growing business.

The story of this stock fits in one number: −83%. At the end of September 2021 CHTR closed at $727.56. On September 21, 2026 it settled at $123.35. That is within four cents of the $123.31 close on July 24, 2026, the day Q2 results came out. The company is still very profitable: trailing Adjusted EBITDA is $22.3B and trailing free cash flow $4.7B. The market is not questioning today's profits. It is questioning whether the core product is shrinking. Internet customers have fallen every quarter shown in the latest release, and the loss widened to 172,000 in Q2 2026 from 116,000 a year earlier. Mobile lines grew 406,000 over the same quarter. The debate is simple to state: can a converged internet-plus-mobile bundle, now spread across the Cox footprint, stop the broadband losses before roughly $94B of debt becomes the main thing that matters to shareholders?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Wired network to 70M+ passings Scale after Cox (45 states) Internet + mobile bundle Franchise rights ($67.5B on balance sheet) ~45M WiFi access points Commercial fiber (Segra, Cox Business)

The moat is physical plant, the cable already running past the house. Charter carries $67.5B of franchise rights and $48.0B of net property on its balance sheet. Building that network again from scratch is not realistic for most competitors. The problem is that a network moat protects only against rivals who have to dig. Fiber overbuilders do dig, and fixed-wireless providers don't need to, because they reuse cell towers they already own. The drop in penetration from 55.7% to 53.4% in one year shows the moat shrinking in practice. Management's answer is to make the bundle the moat: a customer with internet and mobile from Spectrum faces a bigger switching decision than one with internet alone. Two-product penetration rising to 35.1% is the evidence that this is working. Internet net losses of 172K are the evidence that it isn't working fast enough.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The core product is shrinking in customers and price. Internet customers −1.7% YoY and residential revenue per customer −1.8% add up to a −3.2% internet revenue line. Neither number is improving, and Comcast warned in September that its own broadband losses would not improve in Q3.
02
Leverage leaves little room for error. Principal debt was $93.8B at June 30, 2026, about 4.2x trailing Adjusted EBITDA of $22.3B (our arithmetic: ($93.8B − $0.5B cash) ÷ $22.3B). About $12B of Cox debt and finance leases now sits at Charter subsidiaries on top of that. Interest coverage is 2.57x (StockAnalysis). With equity worth about $20B against more than $100B of debt, small changes in EBITDA cause large swings in equity value.
03
Integration during a CFO handover. Cox customers are moving to Spectrum pricing and packaging, Cox's customer service is being brought back to the US over 18 months, and the free mobile year delays revenue. All of this is being done while the CFO leaves on October 15 with only an interim replacement named. Wolfe Research's September 14 downgrade to Underperform ($118) cited debt and integration risk directly.
04
Losing index buyers. Removal from the NASDAQ-100, effective June 22, 2026, took a large price-insensitive buyer off the register. A short interest of 13.55% of float (StockAnalysis) shows how many investors are betting against the stock.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, debt repurchases: in Q2 2026 Charter bought back $1.2B face value of its own notes for $1.0B in cash. The resulting gain on extinguishment offset lower Adjusted EBITDA, which is why Q2 net income ($1,292M) was flat year over year while EBITDA fell 4.3%. Second, Cox transition expenses of $65M in Q2 ($89M year to date) reduced Adjusted EBITDA before the deal even closed. Third, the share count changes completely from Q3. The Liberty Broadband merger was a net reduction of about 4.7M shares, while Cox received about 33.6M exchangeable partnership units plus $6B of 6.875% convertible preferred units (convertible into 12.6M units) and about $4B in cash. Any trailing per-share number in this report should be read with that in mind.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 3.2x trailing (pre-Cox) earnings and 5.3x EV/EBITDA, the market is pricing Charter as if it were in terminal decline. Meanwhile it produces $22.3B of trailing Adjusted EBITDA and $4.7B of free cash flow, and it has just added Cox's network. Synergies have reportedly been raised above $1 billion (TIKR only; the August 20, 2026 closing release gives no synergy figure). Mobile is growing 15.5% a year and making the bundle stickier. Capex should fall once the network upgrade finishes in 2027. With equity at only about 17% of enterprise value, a small improvement in EBITDA would have a large effect on the share price. The median target of $150 implies +21.6%, and the most bullish target of $380 implies about 3x. Cox Enterprises, a long-term family owner, took most of its payment in Charter equity rather than cash.

Bear Case

The low multiple reflects real risk. Internet customers are falling faster each quarter (−116K → −172K), revenue per customer is falling, and a major competitor has said publicly that Q3 won't improve. Principal debt of $93.8B is about 4.2x EBITDA before adding about $12B of Cox debt, and interest coverage is only 2.57x. The company is integrating its largest acquisition while its CFO leaves. Short interest is 13.55% of float, 5 of 21 rated analysts say Sell, and Wolfe's $118 target sits below today's price. If EBITDA keeps falling at 3–4% a year, leverage rises even without new borrowing, and the equity could fall much further than the business itself.

Broadband subscriber losses keep getting worse. −116K, −119K, −120K and −172K over the quarters shown. Internet is 43% of revenue. If Q3 2026 is worse than Q2, as Comcast has suggested for its own business, the market will assume the losses are structural.
02

Industry Commentary
Cable broadband vs. fiber and fixed wireless
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For cable, broadband used to be a near-monopoly product with steady price increases. The numbers now show the opposite. Charter's penetration of homes passed fell from 55.7% to 53.4% in a year, and monthly residential revenue per customer is falling. The September 9 comments from Comcast's finance chief applied to the whole industry: fiber pricing he called "irrational," and no improvement in Q3 subscriber losses. Charter's CEO counters that fixed wireless looks cheap only because carriers bundle it with mobile, and that Spectrum's own bundle gives better overall value. The coming quarters will show whether bundling can hold on to customers against rivals who compete on price.

Consolidation: Charter + Cox
EXECUTION TEST

Merging two cable operators is a bet on scale: spreading programming, technology and product costs over more customers. The deal closed on August 20, 2026. Cox Enterprises took about 33.6M exchangeable partnership units (implied value about $5B), $6B of 6.875% convertible preferred units and about $4B in cash. About $12B of Cox debt stays at Charter subsidiaries. Management has committed publicly to US-based service staff and a $20 minimum starting wage in the combined company. The deal gives Charter scale. It does not answer the competitive question in Cox's markets, which face the same fiber and wireless pressure as Charter's.

The valuation debate — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

CHTR trades at 3.2x trailing (pre-Cox) earnings, 0.37x sales and 0.90x book value, with a 5-year median P/E of 10.9x (GuruFocus). The bull framing: this is a leveraged cash-flow business priced as if its cash flows are about to disappear. If internet losses stabilize, synergies arrive and capex falls after 2027, debt paydown and buybacks shift value back to shareholders. The bear framing: a low P/E on a business with 4x-plus leverage and falling EBITDA is simply what shrinking equity looks like. GuruFocus itself calls the stock a "possible value trap." Quarterly internet net adds will decide which view is right. Charter reports that number every quarter, and on current evidence it has not yet turned.
