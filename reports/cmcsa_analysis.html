01 Company Overview
Comcast is the largest cable broadband provider in the United States (Xfinity, Comcast Business), a fast-growing mobile virtual network operator (MVNO — it rents Verizon's wireless network and sells it under the Xfinity Mobile brand), and, through NBCUniversal and Sky, a global media company that owns NBC, Telemundo, Universal Pictures, the Universal theme parks and the Peacock streaming service. In FY2025 it reported $123.7B of revenue, flat YoY, $37.4B of adjusted EBITDA (−1.8%) and a record $19.2B of free cash flow. On Jan 2, 2026 it spun off its cable networks (USA, CNBC, MSNBC, E!, Syfy, Oxygen, Golf Channel) as Versant (NASDAQ: VSNT), and on Jun 29, 2026 it announced a far bigger move: a tax-free spin-off of all of NBCUniversal and Sky within roughly 12 months, leaving "Comcast" as a pure U.S. connectivity company.
The central debate is "cheap or broken?" At $26.48 the stock trades at 8.6x trailing and 7.6x forward earnings, yields 5.0% and has been retiring ~5% of its share count a year — a near-10% total shareholder yield on paper. Against that, the business the market actually trades on — domestic broadband — lost 708K subscribers in 2025 and another 167K in Q2 2026 to fibre overbuilders and fixed-wireless access (FWA — 5G home internet from T-Mobile, Verizon and AT&T), broadband revenue is now falling 5.5% a year, and the buyback was paused on Jun 29 while the company works through the split. This report leads with broadband net adds, then Peacock's turn to profit, Theme Parks after Epic Universe, and what the Versant and NBCU separations mean for the numbers.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

65M+ Homes & Businesses Passed Largest US Converged Network ~45% of Base on Gigabit-Plus 10.2M Wireless Lines (MVNO on Verizon) 57%-Margin Business Services Universal IP → Parks Flywheel $20B+ Annual FCF
Comcast's core advantage is a physical one: a hybrid fibre-coax network passing more than 65 million homes and businesses that already delivers multi-gig speeds to ~45% of its customers, financed decades ago and throwing off $20B+ of free cash flow a year ($20.4B TTM). Bundling Xfinity Mobile — bought wholesale from Verizon, sold at a discount to the big three — turns that network into a "convergence" product that lowers churn on both sides; Q2's record 448K line adds are the evidence. On the media side, owning Universal's film library, the NBC broadcast reach and the theme parks creates a self-reinforcing IP loop that only Disney can match.

Key Vulnerabilities

Broadband share loss to FWA and fibre: Comcast has lost domestic broadband subscribers every quarter for over two years — 708K in 2025, 167K in Q2 2026. Fixed-wireless from T-Mobile, Verizon and AT&T targets exactly the price-sensitive customers cable used to own, while fibre overbuilders attack the premium tier. Comcast's answer (five-year price locks, simplified plans, $2.3B/quarter of network capex, +19.9% YoY) protects ARPU at the cost of margin.

Video and linear-TV decay: $27.3B of FY2025 revenue is still video, shrinking (−280K video customers in Q2 alone) and low-margin after programming costs. Versant removed the worst of the cable-network exposure, but NBC and Sky remain tied to a linear advertising market in structural decline.

Execution risk on two spin-offs in 18 months: The NBCU/Sky separation means dis-synergies, a paused buyback, a leadership handover (Angelakis returning as CEO, Cavanagh leaving for NBCU) and a debt re-allocation across two new investment-grade balance sheets — all while the core business is shrinking. Split stories often produce a "sum-of-the-parts" pop, but the parts here both face secular headwinds.

Consumer-discretionary exposure: Parks (softening since June 2026), theatrical and advertising are all cyclical. A U.S. consumer slowdown hits the growth segments exactly when the stable segment is losing subscribers.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case — "Cheap"

At 7.6x forward earnings and a ~22% FCF yield, the market is treating Comcast as a run-off. But broadband losses are narrowing (−167K vs −201K), ARPU still grows ~4%, wireless is adding a record 450K lines a quarter into a base that is only 7% penetrated, Business Services compounds at 57% margins, and Peacock has flipped from a $2B annual drag to a profit. The dividend (4.98%) plus the historical buyback (4.74%) gave a 9.7% total shareholder yield; the spin of NBCU/Sky — a company with Universal, Peacock, the parks and $4B+ of EBITDA — is the catalyst that could finally close a conglomerate discount that has persisted for a decade.

Bear Case — "Broken"

The single-digit P/E is a value trap if broadband keeps shrinking: domestic broadband revenue fell 5.5% in Q2 2026 and Residential EBITDA fell 8%, and there is no sign FWA or fibre overbuilding is slowing. The 9.7% shareholder yield is a rear-view number — the buyback is paused, so today's real yield is the 5% dividend, on a payout that must now be split across two balance sheets. Parks are softening a year after Epic, World Cup ad dollars don't recur, Studios are lumpy, and management has just signalled a year of restructuring during which Comcast holds up to 19.9% of an asset it no longer controls. Goldman's $25 target sits below the price.

  Industry Commentary
Fixed Wireless and Fibre Are Ending Cable's Broadband Monopoly
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
For twenty years cable's hybrid fibre-coax plant was the only real high-speed option in most U.S. neighborhoods, and Comcast priced accordingly. That ended around 2022 when T-Mobile and Verizon began selling 5G fixed-wireless home internet at $50/month with no contract, and when AT&T, Frontier and a wave of private-equity-backed overbuilders started laying fibre past cable homes. The industry result is that U.S. cable operators have now posted broadband net losses for well over two years — Comcast −708K in 2025, −167K in Q2 2026, and Charter on the same trajectory. Comcast's response is to compete on price certainty (five-year locks, simplified tiers), bundle mobile to lower churn, and spend more capex ($2.3B in Q2, +19.9%) upgrading to DOCSIS 4.0 (the next cable standard, enabling multi-gig symmetrical speeds) — all of which protects subscribers at the expense of the 40%+ margins investors used to count on. The bull argument is that FWA is capacity-constrained and will saturate; the bear argument is that fibre isn't.
Big Media Is Unbundling Itself — Spins, Splits and Sales
ONGOING HEADWIND
Comcast's twin separations are part of an industry-wide retreat from the "own everything" model. Cable-network portfolios are being isolated (Versant; Warner Bros. Discovery's own split), studios are changing hands (Paramount Skydance), and Disney has consolidated Hulu. The logic is that declining linear assets drag on the multiple of growing ones, so separate them and let each find its natural owner. For Comcast, this means NBCUniversal/Sky will trade on Peacock's profitability, the parks and the studio slate — a Disney-like profile — while "RemainCo" Comcast becomes a pure telecom judged on broadband net adds and free cash flow. The headwind is that both halves inherit secular declines (linear TV; cable broadband), and the transition year brings a paused buyback, one-time separation costs and the market's tendency to sell the parent until the pieces are visible.
Streaming Finally Makes Money — and Sports Is Why
EMERGING TAILWIND
What is happening: After a decade of subsidized growth, the streaming industry has pivoted to profitability — price rises, ad tiers, password-sharing crackdowns and, above all, live sports that viewers won't cancel around. Peacock's first profitable quarter ($189M in Q2 2026, on the NBA playoffs and the World Cup) and its August price increase are textbook examples. NBC's new NBA package, Premier League, Big Ten and the 2028 Olympics give NBCU one of the deepest sports calendars in the business.

Bull implication: If Peacock scales toward $1B+ of annual EBITDA on 48M subscribers, the spun-off NBCU gets valued as a growth media company rather than a linear-TV run-off, and Comcast shareholders own it outright.

Bear implication: Sports rights inflate faster than subscription revenue, the $440M World Cup bump won't repeat until 2030, and the August price hike will be tested by churn the moment the NBA season ends. A profitable streamer at 48M subs is still a distant #4 behind Netflix, Disney and Amazon.
