01 Company Overview

Chipotle Mexican Grill operates a single-brand, entirely company-owned fast-casual restaurant system built around a customizable Mexican-inspired menu — burritos, bowls, tacos, quesadillas and salads — assembled to order on a visible service line. At the end of Q3 2026 the company operated 4,267 company-owned restaurants plus 20 partner-operated international locations. It generated FY2025 revenue of $11.93B, up 5.4% year over year — a sharp slowdown from the 14–15% growth posted in each of FY2022, FY2023 and FY2024 — and growth has picked up since: Q3 2026 revenue rose 9.6%, and trailing-twelve-month revenue reached $12.71B.

That $12.71B of TTM revenue is about 7.0% of the $181.56B combined revenue of the US-listed restaurant companies tracked by StockAnalysis. The defining issue for the shares right now is not growth in units — Chipotle is still opening 350+ restaurants a year — but the transaction line, the number of orders. Comparable restaurant sales fell −1.7% in FY2025 on transactions of −2.9%, with Q4 2025 transactions down −3.2%. Transactions have grown in every quarter of 2026 so far: +0.6% in Q1, +1.0% in Q2 and +0.8% in Q3, when total comps rose 2.1%. The recovery held, but it slowed: CFO Adam Rymer said industry headwinds from mid-July slowed the quarter and that October softened in line with the broader industry, while CEO Scott Boatwright, asked about comparable sales, said September finished up 3.8% with very little promotional activity. Whether traffic keeps growing while margins shrink is the entire debate.

Chipotle's moat is unusual in restaurants because it is structural rather than contractual. Almost every scaled peer — McDonald's, Yum, Restaurant Brands — has franchised away the restaurant P&L in exchange for a high-margin royalty. Chipotle owns all 4,267 of its company-owned restaurants outright. That decision is the single most important fact about the shares, and it cuts both ways. On the upside, it means Chipotle keeps the entire 23.3% restaurant-level operating margin (Q3 2026) rather than a 4–5% royalty; it can push a new equipment package, a new menu item, or a pricing decision across the whole system in weeks without negotiating with franchisees; and it can fund 350+ new restaurants a year entirely from operating cash flow ($2.35B TTM operating cash flow against $838M of capex, leaving $1.51B of FCF) while still cutting its diluted share count 5.4% in a year (Q3 2026 against Q3 2025). Layer on genuine unit economics — a $3.1M average unit volume — plus a brand built on ingredient sourcing claims that competitors find expensive to copy, and you have a business that earns 20.0% ROIC and 48.5% ROE. On the downside, owning every restaurant's profit and loss means owning every cent of wage and beef inflation with nowhere to hide.

Value perception after years of price increases. Between 2021 and 2024 Chipotle took repeated menu price increases; average check rose while the brand's price gap to McDonald's, Taco Bell and regional Mexican operators widened. The bill arrived in 2025: FY2025 transactions −2.9% with average check still +1.2% — the textbook signature of a chain trading traffic for price. Management reversed course for 2026, holding menu pricing to 1–2% against 3–4% cost inflation through most of the year; the CFO said on the Q3 call that pricing rises to about 3% in Q4, for roughly 2% over the full year. That fixes perception slowly and squeezes margin immediately.

Concentration risk — one brand, one country, one cuisine. There is no second banner to cushion a Chipotle-specific problem, no franchise royalty floor, and ~98% of revenue is North American. A food-safety event is the acute version of this risk and Chipotle has lived through it before. In August 2026 it pulled jalapeños from one Mexican grower out of the restaurants that had received them, during a suspected Salmonella outbreak; it said the CDC and FDA saw no ongoing risk from its restaurants (company statement, Aug 5, 2026). Sweetgreen, whose same-store sales fell 6.2% in the quarter to June 28, estimated that cyclospora outbreak news cut its July comparable sales by a further 6 points, and lowered its full-year outlook (Nation's Restaurant News, Aug 6, 2026) — a reminder of how fast a fresh-produce-heavy fast-casual chain can be marked down.

Labor cost exposure with no franchisee buffer. Labor ran 25.7% of revenue in Q3 2026 (up from 25.2% a year earlier), after 25.0% in Q2 and 25.7% in Q1. The 10-Q puts the Q3 increase down to lower sales per restaurant, wage inflation and bonuses, and extra labor to support promotions. With 130,301 employees on Chipotle's own payroll, every state minimum-wage move, scheduling regulation or benefits rule lands directly on Chipotle's income statement rather than a franchisee's. Peers with 90%+ franchised systems simply do not carry this line.

Commodity and tariff pass-through. Q3 2026 food, beverage and packaging costs fell to 29.5% of revenue from 30.0%: menu price increases, tariff relief and a favourable mix of limited-time offers outweighed beef and freight inflation and higher protein usage (Q3 2026 10-Q). Chipotle's menu is unusually exposed to a handful of volatile inputs — beef, avocado, chicken — and to imported produce, where tariff policy on Mexican and Latin American farm imports is a real variable that management can neither hedge away nor fully price through in a year when pricing is held to about 2%.

Notable One-Off Items

Chipotle's reported numbers are unusually clean, but a few non-recurring items are worth isolating. FY2025 carried $34.8M of stock-based retention grants tied to the CEO transition — a real cash-equivalent cost but explicitly one-time — plus $4.4M of legal-proceedings expense (down sharply from a $21.4M charge in FY2024, so the YoY comparison flatters 2025 by roughly $17M) and $2.5M of restaurant impairment. In Q3 2026 the adjustments were small — $4.3M of restaurant asset impairment and $1.5M of stock-based compensation — so adjusted diluted EPS matched GAAP at $0.27; reported G&A was $185.2M, up from $146.7M a year earlier.

The larger distortions are not accounting items but deliberate choices in how the restaurants are run. The 2026 margin decline — restaurant-level margin from 24.5% to 23.3% in Q3, from 27.4% to 25.2% in Q2, and 2.5 points lower in Q1 — is driven by (i) management holding menu pricing to about 2% for the year against 3–4% input inflation, (ii) beef and freight inflation, and (iii) extra labor, including staff to support promotions. None of these are "one-offs" in the accounting sense, but all are management-controlled and reversible, which is exactly why bulls treat the current EPS base as understated and bears treat it as the new normal.

On a split-adjusted basis CMG closed October 2021 at $35.58 and closed at $31.89 on Oct 30, 2026 — a price return of −10.4% over five years, with no dividend to soften it, against +64.6% for SPY (an S&P 500 index fund) and +78.8% for QQQ (a Nasdaq-100 index fund) over the same dates, also price only. That line hides two violent halves. From the June 2022 monthly low of $26.15, the stock more than doubled to a monthly-close peak of $63.19 in April 2024 — a period when comps ran high-single-digit, restaurant-level margin expanded toward 27%, and the market paid 60x+ earnings for a chain funding rapid unit growth from its own cash. From that peak the shares have fallen −49.5% (April 2024 month-end close to the Oct 30, 2026 close), for two reasons: the transaction comp turned negative, and the market now pays a restaurant-stock multiple rather than a growth-stock one. Over one year the change is a milder −2.0%, because the starting point, the Oct 30, 2025 close of $32.53, was already near the 2025 low ($31.69 at the October 2025 month-end).

Where it sits technically. At $31.89 the shares are below both the 50-day ($33.07) and 200-day ($34.06) moving averages, by 3.6% and 6.4%. The stock is 23.0% below its 52-week high of $41.42 (intraday, Jan 21, 2026) and 16.8% above the 52-week low of $27.31 (intraday, Oct 23, 2026, when it closed at $27.52). The 14-day RSI on daily closes is 56.6, neutral after the post-print bounce. Beta is 0.96, which understates how far this stock has actually fallen: the swings have come from its own earnings news rather than from the market.

Relative performance. Against the S&P 500 the five-year gap is stark: −10.4% against +64.6% for SPY, a 75-percentage-point shortfall. Within its own industry the picture is more nuanced: the listed restaurant sector trades at a 25.99x weighted-average P/E versus Chipotle's 29.8x, so CMG still carries a premium to its peer group. Among the fast-casual growth chains, CAVA ($6.86B market cap) is still growing revenue +26.8%, while Sweetgreen ($1.32B) is shrinking on a −6.2% comp. Chipotle sits between: too slow to be valued like CAVA, too profitable and too well-capitalised to be priced like Sweetgreen.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The transaction line has turned: −2.9% in FY2025 became +0.6% in Q1 2026, +1.0% in Q2 and +0.8% in Q3, and Boatwright, asked about comparable sales, said September finished up 3.8%. Crucially, the 2026 margin squeeze is chosen — pricing held to about 2% for the year against 3–4% inflation — so restaurant-level margin should recover once value perception is repaired and pricing normalises: the CFO called a mid-24% margin the baseline for 2027, against 23.3% in Q3. Consensus expects EPS to rise 21.6% to $1.33 in FY2027 (StockAnalysis). Meanwhile the growth engine is untouched: 350–370 new restaurants in 2026 and 340–360 in the US and Canada in 2027 plus 15–25 elsewhere, ~80% with Chipotlanes, ~40% year-two returns on European units, 4,267 restaurants against a ~7,000 North American target, and a high-efficiency equipment package rolling to ~2,000 sites that was delivering 2–4 points of comp outperformance without discounting. All of it is funded from $1.51B of free cash flow while the diluted share count fell 5.4% in a year — with Q3 repurchases done at $34.05 versus $42.54 in FY2025. At 29.8x trailing and 24.0x FY2027 earnings with 20.0% ROIC, you are paying a modest premium to a 26.0x industry for much better returns on capital and a live recovery.

Bear Case

A 29.8x trailing multiple and a 1.83 PEG on a company whose consensus FY2026 EPS is falling 6.3% is a growth premium that may not be finished shrinking. Three quarters of +0.6%, +1.0% and +0.8% transactions after a year of −2.9% is a low bar, the third weaker than the second, and the average check (+1.3% in Q3) still contributes more than traffic — the same mix that got the brand into trouble. Restaurant-level margin has fallen from 26.7% (FY24) → 25.4% (FY25) → 23.3% (Q3'26), with just under 22% guided for Q4, and sales per restaurant were flat at ~$3.1M in Q2 versus $3,186K in Q4 2024: each restaurant is earning less while the restaurant count grows. Owning 100% of the restaurants means owning 100% of the labor line (25.7% of revenue in Q3, up from 25.2%) and 100% of beef and freight inflation, with no franchise royalty floor and no second brand. October softened in line with the industry, the CFO said, and TD Cowen cut to Hold on Oct 21. Analysts offer little cushion: every target change after the print was a cut, and the lowest target, $32, sits at the price. If transactions turn negative again, the multiple has room to fall — the industry average P/E is 26.0x and McDonald's trades at 21.0x with a 3.0% dividend.

Top 3 Risks
01
The traffic recovery fades. Everything in the bull case rests on three quarters of modestly positive traffic, and the third (+0.8%) was weaker than the second; the CFO said October softened. If Q4 2026 shows transactions turning negative again, the stock loses both its earnings recovery and its premium multiple at once — and with a 29.8x trailing P/E and every post-print target change a cut, there is little support on the way down.
02
Margin does not recover on schedule. Restaurant-level margin was 23.3% in Q3 2026, down 1.2 points from a year earlier, and the CFO guided Q4 to just under 22%, about 1.5 points lower than last year; pricing is only about 2% for the year while beef and freight inflate and labor runs 25.7% of revenue. Because Chipotle franchises nothing, there is no royalty stream to absorb a bad cost year — a beef spike, a wage mandate, or a tariff on imported produce lands entirely on Chipotle's own income statement.
03
Single-brand, single-cuisine, single-market concentration. ~98% of revenue is North American, from one banner, with a fresh-produce-heavy supply chain. A food-safety event — the acute risk Chipotle has already lived through once, and the one that cut Sweetgreen's July sales — would hit the entire company at once; the August 2026 jalapeño withdrawal during a suspected Salmonella outbreak was a small reminder. The 20 partner-operated international restaurants are strategically important but far too small to diversify anything today.
  Industry Commentary
Fast-Casual Traffic & the Low-Income Consumer
ONGOING HEADWIND
The traffic problem is industry-wide, not Chipotle-specific, and it is concentrated at the lower end of the income distribution. McDonald's — the value benchmark — lost traffic in Q2 2026 even with an under-$3 menu and a $4 breakfast meal, and CEO Chris Kempczinski blamed execution rather than the strategy (Reuters, Aug 11, 2026). Sweetgreen's same-store sales fell 6.2% in its quarter to June 28, before cyclospora headlines cut its July comps by an estimated 6 points more. Taco Bell was running −2% same-store sales quarter-to-date at Yum's July 30 results, after a cyclospora outbreak (Reuters). Against that backdrop, Chipotle's +2.1% comp on +0.8% transactions in Q3 2026 stands out — but management itself blamed industry-wide weakness from mid-July for slowing the quarter and said October softened in line with the broader industry, the gains follow FY2025 transactions of −2.9%, and they came only after the company deliberately stopped taking price. The read-through: when the low-income consumer trades down, a $12 burrito bowl is exactly the kind of discretionary occasion that gets cut, and no amount of throughput improvement fully offsets it.
Food Commodity & Labor Cost Inflation
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The squeeze is arithmetic. In Q3 2026 food, beverage and packaging costs actually eased to 29.5% of revenue from 30.0%, as price increases, tariff relief and the promotional mix outweighed beef and freight inflation. The pressure moved elsewhere: labor rose to 25.7% of revenue from 25.2%, occupancy to 5.4% from 5.3%, and other operating costs to 16.0% from 15.0%. Net result: restaurant-level margin of 23.3% versus 24.5%, 1.2 points lower, with just under 22% guided for Q4. Against roughly 3–4% cost inflation the company is taking only about 2% of menu price this year by choice. Because Chipotle owns every restaurant and franchises none, all of this lands directly on earnings — the flip side of the 25%+ restaurant margin it earned in good years. Tariffs on imported produce sit on top of this as a policy variable no one can hedge.
Why the Earnings Multiple Falls When Growth Slows
DEFINES THE BULL/BEAR SPLIT
This is where the two cases collide. Fast-growing restaurant stocks are valued on how long same-store sales keep growing, not on how many restaurants they open — and when comps slow, the market quickly starts paying what it pays for mature chains. CMG has already travelled most of that distance: from 60x+ earnings at the April 2024 peak of $63.19 to 29.8x trailing / 24.0x FY2027 earnings today, a −49.5% fall in the share price. The bull reading: most of that fall is done, the multiple now sits only modestly above the 26.0x industry average while ROIC is 20.0%, the earnings base is held down by a deliberate pricing choice, and management bought back stock at $34.05 in Q3 versus $42.54 last year. The bear reading: the floor is not 26x, it is McDonald's at 21.0x with a 2.99% dividend, or Yum at 19.6x — and nothing in a 1.83 PEG on falling FY2026 EPS says the fall has finished. The Q4 report, with the first 2027 comp guidance, decides it.
