01 Company Overview

Chipotle Mexican Grill operates a single-brand, entirely company-owned fast-casual restaurant system built around a customizable Mexican-inspired menu — burritos, bowls, tacos, quesadillas and salads — assembled to order on a visible service line. At the end of Q2 2026 the company operated 4,186 company-owned restaurants plus 15 partner-operated international locations, and generated FY2025 revenue of $11.93B, up 5.4% year over year — a sharp deceleration from the 14–15% growth posted in each of FY2022, FY2023 and FY2024. Trailing-twelve-month revenue has since re-accelerated to $12.42B (+7.3%).

The company ranks roughly #594 globally by market capitalisation at $43.86B, and its $12.42B of TTM revenue represents about 6.9% of the aggregate revenue of the 54 US-listed restaurant companies tracked by StockAnalysis ($181.18B). The defining issue for the equity right now is not growth in units — Chipotle is still opening 350+ restaurants a year — but the transaction line. Comparable restaurant sales fell −1.7% in FY2025 on transactions of −2.9%, with Q4 2025 transactions down −3.2%. Transactions turned positive again in Q1 2026 (+0.6%) and accelerated to +1.0% in Q2 2026, with total comps of +2.2%. That inflection — and whether it holds — is the entire debate.

Chipotle's moat is unusual in restaurants because it is structural rather than contractual. Almost every scaled peer — McDonald's, Yum, Restaurant Brands — has franchised away the restaurant P&L in exchange for a high-margin royalty. Chipotle owns all 4,186 of its US restaurants outright. That decision is the single most important fact about the equity, and it cuts both ways. On the upside, it means Chipotle captures the entire 25.2% restaurant-level operating margin rather than a 4–5% royalty; it can push a new equipment package, a new menu item, or a pricing decision across the whole system in weeks without negotiating with franchisees; and it can fund 350+ new restaurants a year entirely from operating cash flow ($2.33B TTM operating cash flow against $759M of capex, leaving $1.57B of FCF) while still retiring ~3.9% of shares a year. Layer on genuine unit economics — a $3.1M average unit volume is roughly double a typical fast-casual box — plus a brand built on ingredient sourcing claims that competitors find expensive to replicate, and you have a business that earns 21.0% ROIC and 49.6% ROE. On the downside, owning the P&L means owning every basis point of wage and beef inflation with nowhere to hide.

Value perception after years of price increases. Between 2021 and 2024 Chipotle took repeated menu price increases; average check rose while the brand's price gap to McDonald's, Taco Bell and regional Mexican operators widened. The bill arrived in 2025: FY2025 transactions −2.9% with average check still +1.2% — the textbook signature of a chain trading traffic for price. Management has now reversed course, capping FY2026 pricing at 1–2% against 3–4% cost inflation. That fixes perception slowly and compresses margin immediately.

Concentration risk — one brand, one country, one cuisine. There is no second banner to cushion a Chipotle-specific problem, no franchise royalty floor, and ~98% of revenue is North American. A food-safety event is the acute version of this risk and Chipotle has lived through it before; the Sweetgreen cyclospora episode driving a −6.2% comp and a sixth straight decline is a live reminder of how fast fresh-produce-heavy fast-casual can be de-rated.

Labor cost exposure with no franchisee buffer. Labor ran 25.0% of revenue in Q2 2026 (up from 24.7%) and 25.7% in Q1 2026 (up 70bps). With 130,301 employees on Chipotle's own payroll, every state minimum-wage move, scheduling regulation or benefits mandate lands directly on Chipotle's income statement rather than a franchisee's. Peers with 90%+ franchised systems simply do not carry this line.

Commodity and tariff pass-through. Q2 2026 food, beverage and packaging costs rose to 29.7% of revenue from 28.9%, driven by beef and freight inflation plus higher protein and produce usage, partially offset by lower avocado and dairy costs. Chipotle's menu is unusually exposed to a handful of volatile inputs — beef, avocado, chicken — and to imported produce, where tariff policy on Mexican and Latin American agricultural imports is a genuine variable that management can neither hedge away nor price through in a year when pricing is capped at 1–2%.

Notable One-Off Items

Chipotle's reported numbers are unusually clean, but a few non-recurring items are worth isolating. FY2025 carried $34.8M of stock-based retention grants tied to the CEO transition — a real cash-equivalent cost but explicitly one-time — plus $4.4M of legal-proceedings expense (down sharply from a $21.4M charge in FY2024, so the YoY comparison flatters 2025 by roughly $17M) and $2.5M of restaurant impairment. In Q2 2026, reported G&A of $190.5M versus adjusted G&A of $176.2M implies roughly $14.3M of add-backs, and adjusted diluted EPS of $0.33 versus GAAP $0.32 reflects the same.

The larger distortions are not accounting items but deliberate P&L choices. The 2026 margin decline — restaurant-level margin from 27.4% to 25.2% in Q2, and 250bps lower in Q1 — is driven by (i) management capping menu pricing at 1–2% against 3–4% input inflation, (ii) beef and freight inflation, offset by lower avocado and dairy, and (iii) incremental labor investment. None of these are "one-offs" in the accounting sense, but all are management-controlled and reversible, which is exactly why bulls treat the current EPS base as understated and bears treat it as the new normal.

The five-year round trip. On a split-adjusted basis CMG closed September 2021 at $36.35 and sits at $34.66 today — a total return of roughly −4.6% over five years, with no dividend to soften it, against an S&P 500 that compounded meaningfully positive over the same stretch. That flat line conceals two violent halves. From the June 2022 monthly low of $26.15, the stock more than doubled to a monthly-close peak of $63.19 in April 2024 — a period when comps ran high-single-digit, restaurant-level margin expanded toward 27%, and the market paid 60x+ for a self-funding unit-growth compounder. From that peak the shares have fallen −45.1%, with the entire decline attributable to two things: the transaction comp turning negative, and the multiple compressing from growth-stock to restaurant-stock territory. The 52-week change alone is −20.1%.

Where it sits technically. At $34.66 the shares are above both the 50-day ($33.47) and 200-day ($34.47) moving averages — a marginal but real improvement after twelve months below them. The stock is 21.2% below its 52-week high of $43.99 and 23.6% above the 52-week low of $28.04. Beta is 0.94, which understates how sharply this name has actually drawn down: the volatility has been idiosyncratic (earnings-driven) rather than market-driven.

Relative performance. Against the S&P 500 the five-year gap is stark — a roughly flat-to-negative absolute return versus a strongly positive index means several thousand basis points of underperformance. Within its own industry the picture is more nuanced: the listed restaurant sector trades at a 28.23x weighted-average P/E versus Chipotle's 32.12x, so CMG has de-rated toward its peer group without yet reaching it. Against the fast-casual growth cohort, CAVA still commands a 127.8x P/E on +26.8% revenue growth and a +9.0% Q2 comp with +5.3% traffic, while Sweetgreen has collapsed to a $693M market cap on a −6.2% comp. Chipotle sits between: too slow to be re-rated as CAVA, too profitable and too well-capitalised to be priced like Sweetgreen.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The transaction line has inflected: −2.9% in FY2025 became +0.6% in Q1 2026 and +1.0% in Q2 2026, and management raised full-year comp guidance from "about flat" to low-single-digit growth. Crucially, the 2026 margin compression is chosen — pricing capped at 1–2% against 3–4% inflation — so restaurant-level margin should recover mechanically once value perception is repaired and pricing normalises, making the $1.15 FY2026 consensus EPS an artificially depressed base. Meanwhile the growth engine is untouched: 350–370 new restaurants in 2026, ~80% with Chipotlanes, ~40% year-two ROI on European units, 4,186 restaurants against a ~7,000 North American target, and a high-efficiency equipment package rolling to ~2,000 sites that is delivering 200–400bps of comp outperformance without discounting. All of it is funded from $1.57B of free cash flow while retiring 3.9% of shares a year — with Q2 repurchases done at $32.55 versus $42.54 in FY2025. At 32.1x trailing and 27.4x forward with 21.0% ROIC, you are paying a modest premium to a 28.2x industry for materially better returns on capital and a live recovery.

Bear Case

A 32.1x trailing multiple and a 2.00 PEG on a company whose consensus FY2026 EPS is declining 1.7% is the definition of a growth premium that has not finished compressing. Two quarters of +0.6% and +1.0% transactions after a year of −2.9% is a low bar, and the average check contribution (+1.2% in Q2) still exceeds transactions — the same mix that got the brand into trouble. Restaurant-level margin has fallen from 26.7% (FY24) → 25.4% (FY25) → 25.2% (Q2'26) with AUV flat at ~$3.1M versus $3,186K two years ago, so unit-level earnings power is eroding while the unit count grows — a combination that historically ends in weaker incremental returns and eventually slower openings. Owning 100% of the estate means owning 100% of the labor line (25.0% of revenue and rising) and 100% of beef and freight inflation, with no franchise royalty floor and no second brand. And the sell-side offers no cushion: 0 sell ratings, lowest target above spot. If Q3 transactions flatten, the de-rating has considerable room left — the industry average P/E is 28.2x and McDonald's trades at 21.7x with a 2.8% dividend.

Top 3 Risks
01
The transaction inflection fails to hold. Everything in the bull case rests on two quarters of modestly positive traffic. If Q3 2026 (Oct 28) shows transactions flattening or turning negative again, the stock loses both its earnings recovery and its growth multiple simultaneously — and with a 32.1x trailing P/E, 0 sell ratings and a low target of $35.00, positioning offers no protection on the way down.
02
Margin does not recover on schedule. Restaurant-level margin is down 220bps YoY to 25.2% and management has capped pricing at 1–2% while food/beverage/packaging runs 29.7% of revenue on beef and freight inflation, and labor 25.0% and rising. Because Chipotle franchises nothing, there is no royalty stream to absorb a bad cost year — a beef spike, a wage mandate, or a tariff on imported produce lands entirely on Chipotle's own income statement.
03
Single-brand, single-cuisine, single-market concentration. ~98% of revenue is North American, from one banner, with a fresh-produce-heavy supply chain. A food-safety event — the acute risk Chipotle has already lived through once, and the one currently crushing Sweetgreen's comps — would hit the entire enterprise at once. The 15 partner-operated international restaurants are strategically important but far too small to diversify anything today.
  Industry Commentary
Fast-Casual Traffic & the Low-Income Consumer
ONGOING HEADWIND
The traffic problem is industry-wide, not Chipotle-specific, and it is concentrated at the lower end of the income distribution. McDonald's — the value benchmark — posted its weakest US growth in over a year in Q2 2026 after a value menu that "failed to draw enough diners," prompting leadership changes. Sweetgreen is on its sixth straight negative comp at −6.2%. Taco Bell was running −2% same-store sales quarter-to-date after a food-safety incident. Against that backdrop, Chipotle's +2.2% comp on +1.0% transactions is genuinely differentiated — but it follows FY2025 transactions of −2.9% and comes only after the company deliberately stopped taking price. The read-through: when the low-income consumer trades down, a $12 burrito bowl is exactly the kind of discretionary occasion that gets cut, and no amount of throughput improvement fully offsets it.
Food Commodity & Labor Cost Inflation
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The squeeze is arithmetic. Q2 2026 food, beverage and packaging costs were 29.7% of revenue versus 28.9% a year ago on beef and freight inflation plus higher protein and produce usage, partly offset by lower avocado and dairy. Labor was 25.0% of revenue versus 24.7% (25.7% in Q1). Occupancy rose to 5.2% from 5.0%, other operating costs to 14.9% from 14.0%. Net result: restaurant-level margin of 25.2% versus 27.4%, a 220bps compression. Against roughly 3–4% cost inflation the company is taking only 1–2% of menu price by choice. Because Chipotle owns every restaurant and franchises none, this is a pure, unbuffered pass-through to earnings — the structural counterpart to the 25%+ restaurant margin it enjoys in good years. Tariffs on imported produce sit on top of this as an unhedged policy variable.
Multiple Compression When Unit-Growth Stories Decelerate
DEFINES THE BULL/BEAR SPLIT
This is where the two cases collide. Growth restaurant equities are valued on the durability of the comp, not the unit count — and when comps decelerate the market re-rates them toward mature-restaurant multiples with brutal speed. CMG has already travelled most of that distance: from 60x+ at the April 2024 peak of $63.19 to 32.1x trailing / 27.4x forward today, a −45.1% drawdown. The bull reading: the de-rating is largely done, the multiple now sits only modestly above the 28.2x industry average while ROIC (21.0%) is roughly double the sector's, the earnings base is artificially suppressed by a deliberate pricing choice, and management is buying back stock at $32.55 versus $42.54 last year. The bear reading: the floor is not 28x, it is McDonald's at 21.7x with a 2.78% dividend, or Yum at 18.4x — and there is nothing in a 2.00 PEG on declining FY2026 EPS that says the compression has finished. The Oct 28 transaction comp adjudicates it.
