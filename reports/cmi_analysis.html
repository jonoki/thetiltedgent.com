01 Company Overview

Cummins designs and builds engines, powertrain components, aftertreatment systems, generator sets and, increasingly, the electrolysers and electric drivetrains that are supposed to replace them. It reports through five segments — Engine, Distribution, Components, Power Systems and Accelera — and its products end up under the hood of the heaviest (Class 8) trucks, inside RAM heavy-duty pickups, behind data-centre walls as standby power, and in mining and marine applications. FY2025 revenue was $33.67B, down 1.3% from $34.10B in FY2024; the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $35.83B, up 6.7%, and the most recent quarter was $9.44B, up 13.5% YoY, a shade below the second quarter's record $9.46B. At $61.69B the company ranks #198 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation.

The debate this page is built around is that Cummins is now two companies wearing one ticker. The legacy company — Engine and Components — is a North American truck business whose next year depends on a federal emissions rule and on whether a pre-buy (fleets buying trucks early to avoid costlier new engines) shows up; on the third-quarter call management said what it has seen this year has been limited. The growth company is Power Systems, where data-centre standby generator sets have pushed EBITDA margin to 26.3% in the first nine months of 2026 and orders for its larger generator sets out to the second half of 2028. And bolted underneath is Accelera, which lost money every quarter of the period examined here. The stock has paid for the bad news: it closed at $713.21 in June 2026 and sits 36.9% below that today, even though the third quarter brought record EBITDA of $1.79B (18.9% of sales). Most of that fall came before the results: the shares closed at $471.84 the night before. On November 5 Cummins cut its 2026 revenue growth forecast to about 10% from 10–13%, blaming weak pickup-truck demand, and they fell a further 4.93%. What follows is an attempt to separate which of those three businesses the $450.07 price is actually discounting.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
INSTALLED-BASE AFTERMARKET EMISSIONS CERTIFICATION IP SELLS TO RIVAL TRUCK MAKERS SCARCE LARGE-ENGINE CAPACITY OWNED DISTRIBUTION NETWORK HYPERSCALER CONTRACTS 107-YEAR BRAND IN UPTIME

The real moat is the installed base plus the certification barrier, and the two reinforce each other. Cummins is one of very few engine makers that sells to truck makers it does not own — Paccar, Daimler and others fit Cummins engines alongside their own — which means its volume is not hostage to one brand's market share. Every engine sold seeds decades of parts and service revenue through the company-owned Distribution segment, which is why Distribution is the largest segment by gross sales at $9.752B in the first nine months and why its nine-month EBITDA margin of 13.9% sat above Engine's 12.2%. On top of that sits the certification barrier: getting an engine family through EPA and CARB (the California Air Resources Board, whose standards several states adopt) is a multi-year, capital-intensive process, and the model-year 2027 transition is exactly the kind of event that punishes smaller competitors more than it punishes Cummins. The newest and least appreciated piece of the moat is physical: capacity to build the largest generator sets is scarce, orders for them run into the second half of 2028, and a customer who wants gigawatts of standby power in 2027 cannot simply conjure a second supplier.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Regulatory dependence cuts both ways. Tighter emissions rules raise what Cummins sells per truck and its competitive advantage — but they also determine the shape of the demand curve. The EPA has confirmed the model-year 2027 start date stands while simultaneously proposing to change the surrounding requirements (warranty periods, the mileage over which engines must stay compliant, nonconformance penalties, which are fees that let makers keep selling engines that miss the new limit, and DEF derates, the engine slowdowns when a truck's diesel exhaust fluid system faults). Cummins responded with a deliberately phased launch. Both the extra-equipment story and the pre-buy story now depend on a rule that has been reopened but not finalised.
02
The pre-buy looks limited. The historical pattern — fleets buying ahead of an emissions deadline to avoid new, costlier, less proven engines — is the reason many models carried a strong 2026 for Engine and Components. Fleet Maintenance reported on August 25, 2026 that the 2027 pre-buy "has not materialized to the level many predicted", blaming a weak freight market, poor fleet profitability and regulatory uncertainty. On the Q3 call Rumsey said what Cummins has seen this year has been "pretty limited pre-buy in the U.S. truck market", and James Hopkins, the vice president who oversees investor relations, said "industry expectations are for truck production in North America to be flattish in 2027 versus 2026". A limited pre-buy means less demand pulled forward into 2026 and a smaller drop in 2027 — but also no 2027 surge.
03
Concentration in a single demand theme. Power Systems is carrying the margin story, and within it a growing share of sales is data-centre standby power tied to hyperscaler capital spending. That is one customer set, one capital cycle, and one thesis. A slowdown in AI-driven data-centre construction would hit the segment while pickup-truck demand, the reason for the November guidance cut, stays weak.
04
Prior regulatory settlement and its consent decree. Cummins announced on December 22, 2023 an agreement in principle to settle regulatory proceedings over its emissions certification and compliance process for pick-up truck applications — the RAM 2500/3500 engine matter. Cummins' filings say the settlement agreements became final and effective in April 2024, after a $2.0B charge in the fourth quarter of 2023 (Q1 2024 Form 10-Q).

Notable One-Off Items. Four distortions matter when reading the earnings history. RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the market has been buying much harder than selling and the move is described as "overbought"; below 30 the reverse, "oversold". It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a stock can stay overbought for a long time in a long rally.

Over the nearly five years from the December 2021 close of $218.14 to the November 6, 2026 close of $450.07, CMI returned +106.3% on price alone, before the dividend — which has been raised for 17 consecutive years and now runs at $8.80 annualised. That headline number hides two completely different regimes. From December 2021 through late 2023 the stock went almost nowhere: it closed December 2021 at $218.14 and December 2023 at $239.57, a 24-month gain of 9.8%, with a trough of $189.19 in April 2022. Everything happened afterwards. From the December 2023 close to the June 2026 peak monthly close of $713.21, the stock rose 197.7%; since then it has given back more than a third.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Power Systems is a capacity-constrained business with contractual hyperscaler commitments and orders for larger generator sets into the second half of 2028, growing 17.5% over nine months with EBITDA margin expanding to 26.3% from 23.1% — and management said third-quarter order intake beat its expectations while capacity steps up next year. Underneath it sits a Distribution segment with $9.8B of sales in nine months, largely repeat parts and service, and 18.40% ROIC with $3.46B of trailing free cash flow. Buy it at 15.49 times FY2026 consensus earnings, 37% below the June peak, after a record-EBITDA quarter, and you are paying the price of an ordinary industrial company for a business whose fastest-growing, highest-margin segment has orders for larger generator sets running into the second half of 2028.

Bear Case

Management has just cut its 2026 revenue growth forecast to about 10% and lowered Engine's margin guidance, with pickup-truck engine shipments down 16% in the quarter. It describes this year's pre-buy as limited and cites industry expectations for flattish North American truck production in 2027, so the truck half of the company has no obvious catalyst. Accelera lost $402M of EBITDA in nine months on $349M of revenue ($203M without the fuel cell sale charge), and a stop-use recommendation on its HyLYZER electrolysers is outside guidance. The 18.78 trailing P/E is being paid on the strength of one demand theme — data-centre standby power — whose customers are hyperscalers with the ability to slow their capital plans at will. A 19.7% fall in two months, through a record-EBITDA quarter, is the market already asking this question.

Top 3 Risks

01
Data-centre capital spending concentration. Power Systems is 19% of gross segment sales but roughly a third of the EBITDA of the four profitable segments, and more and more of its growth is hyperscaler standby power. Any deceleration in AI-driven data-centre construction removes the segment that is currently offsetting weakness in pickup trucks.
02
A flat 2027 truck year with no pre-buy cushion. The EPA kept the 2027 start date but has proposed rewriting its warranty, compliance-mileage and derate provisions; Cummins has phased its own launch across three quarters of 2027. Management describes pre-buying this year as limited and cites industry expectations for flattish North American truck production in 2027, so a slipped ramp would hit Engine and Components with weak volume and elevated launch cost together.
03
Accelera as an open-ended cash drain. The segment lost $402M of EBITDA in the first nine months against $349M of revenue (including a $199M charge on the fuel cell sale). Until Cummins puts a date on breakeven, this is a recurring charge against the free cash flow that funds the dividend and buyback.
  Industry Commentary
Data-centre power is a physical shortage, not a demand forecast
EMERGING TAILWIND

The distinguishing feature of this cycle is that the constraint sits on the supply side. Cummins' larger generator sets are limited by what it can build, not by what customers want. On the Q3 call Rumsey said the global supply chain "remains a key pacing item as we bring additional capacity online"; orders for larger generator sets extend into the second half of 2028, and Cummins has contractual commitments from some of its big hyperscaler customers. Power Systems has the highest margin and, over nine months, the strongest growth; its margin was 25.4% in Q3 versus 22.9%, and 26.3% across nine months versus 23.1%. A supplier short of capacity can hold its prices in a way that one short of customers cannot. The counterpoint is that capacity investment — the May 21, 2026 large-engine programme, inside the unchanged $1.35–1.45B of 2026 capital spending — arrives in a market where the constraint may have eased.

EPA 2027: reopened, not repealed — and the pre-buy is missing
ONGOING HEADWIND

The near-term truck story hinges on a rule whose deadline is fixed and whose contents are not. The 0.035 g/bhp-hr NOx standard for model year 2027 stands; EPA rejected the industry's request to delay it, and in July 2026 proposed keeping the emissions warranty at 100,000 miles rather than raising it to 450,000 miles, pushing back the longer compliance mileage, allowing nonconformance penalties and removing DEF derates. For Components that means the extra equipment per truck may be smaller than originally modelled. For Engine it means a launch executed into regulatory ambiguity — hence the phased X15/X10 ramp. Meanwhile the classic pre-buy is not materialising: Fleet Maintenance in August blamed a weak freight market and poor fleet profitability, and on the Q3 call Rumsey said what Cummins has seen this year has been "pretty limited pre-buy in the U.S. truck market" while Hopkins said industry expectations are for flattish North American truck production in 2027. Management is maintaining its 240,000–250,000-unit 2026 North American heavy-duty forecast. The pickup business is the live problem: engine shipments of 34 thousand in Q3 were down 16%, and that is what cut the revenue guide.

What the $450.07 price is actually paying for
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Strip the company down and there are three businesses that would each earn a different valuation inside one 18.78 trailing P/E. Distribution ($9.752B over nine months, 13.9% EBITDA, +6.9%) deserves a steady price-to-earnings ratio in the mid-teens. Power Systems ($6.500B, 26.3% EBITDA, +17.5%, orders for larger generator sets into the second half of 2028) deserves considerably more. Engine and Components ($17.16B combined, EBITDA up 1.1% on sales up 7.4%) deserve a lower ratio, because their earnings rise and fall with the truck cycle. And Accelera deserves a negative number: −$402M of nine-month EBITDA on $349M of revenue (−$203M without the fuel cell sale charge) means shareholders are paying to hold this long-term bet, not getting it free. Bull implication: if you believe generator sets stay in short supply and the truck market is merely flat, the 15.49 forward P/E on 22.2% consensus EPS growth is the entry point, and the $662.35 average target is defensible. Bear implication: if Power Systems' growth normalises toward the company average while pickups stay weak and Accelera keeps burning, then you are paying 18.78× for a diversified industrial with a 9.26% net margin, and the correct comparison is the $462 low target — 2.7% above today — not the $880 high.
