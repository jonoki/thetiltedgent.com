01 Company Overview

In 1886 two men set out to light downtown Jackson, Michigan with electric arc lamps. The company they started is still headquartered in Jackson, and through its utility, Consumers Energy, it now serves 1.9 million electric and 1.8 million gas customers in Michigan. CMS Energy reported FY2025 revenue of $8.54B (+13.6%) and diluted EPS of $3.53; on a trailing-twelve-month basis revenue is $8.81B and EPS $3.33.

A regulated utility is a simple machine to describe and a hard one to price. The state regulator, the Michigan Public Service Commission, lets the company earn an allowed return on equity on the capital it has invested in poles, wires, pipes and plants — the rate base. Grow the rate base, keep the allowed ROE, and earnings grow with it. CMS plans to take rate base from $28.4B in 2025 to $46.8B in 2030, a 10.5% compound rate, funded by a $24B five-year utility investment plan. On July 28, 2026 it went further toward pure-play status: it is exiting non-utility renewables development at its NorthStar Clean Energy arm, so that, in CEO Garrick Rochow's words on the Q2 call, "nearly 100% of our earnings and future growth will be rate base driven within the utility."

The market has not rewarded the simplification. The stock closed at $78.07 in February 2026 and at $64.70 on September 21, 2026 — down 17% — touching a fresh 52-week intraday low of $64.42 in the same session. Nothing in guidance broke: 2026 adjusted EPS guidance of $3.83–$3.90 was reaffirmed and 2027 guidance of $4.08–$4.17 introduced. The question for this report is what the price is now paying for — and what could make that 10.5% rate base growth fail to reach the shareholder.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchised service territory Regulated return on rate base 3.7M customer connections Forward-looking rate filings A-rated utility bonds 18 years of dividend growth

A utility has no competitors in the usual sense: nobody runs a second set of wires to a Michigan house. The moat is the franchise plus the regulatory compact — the company builds what the state needs and the state lets it earn a fair return. The weak point is that the compact is renegotiated in every rate case. The house can set the table limit; the regulator sets the rake. Consumers Energy's senior secured bonds are rated A/A1/A+; the CMS parent's unsecured debt is BBB/Baa2/BBB — the gap is the price of the holding-company layer.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The regulator decides the payout. A 9.90% allowed ROE on 66% of the ask is a solid Michigan outcome, but the consolidated ROE actually earned over the last twelve months was 9.16% (StockAnalysis) — below the allowed rate. Any drift down in allowed ROE or grant ratios flows straight through a model where 100% of growth is rate-base driven.
02
Negative free cash flow is the model, and it needs funding. TTM free cash flow is −$1.91B against $19.30B of debt and a 1.86 debt-to-equity ratio. The plan calls for $3.75B of new equity over five years. Every dollar of that sold at $64.70 rather than $78 dilutes more.
03
Storms and weather hit the quarter. H1 2026 took an 8-cent hit from weather and a 19-cent hit from storm costs and reliability spending; Q2 adjusted EPS fell to $0.37 from $0.71. Management expects 25 cents of relief in H2 — a promise to track, not a result yet.
04
Affordability caps the ask. Rate base growing 10.5% a year while bills rise ~3% only works if fuel and power costs fall, efficiency programs deliver, and new load — data centers above all — spreads fixed costs. If the load does not arrive, the bill math strains and regulators push back.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $59.73 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $64.70, CMS gained 8.3% on price. Dividends paid over the window add roughly $10 a share (our estimate from annual dividends per share, not reinvested), taking the total return to roughly +25% over five years — a bond-like result from a stock that behaves like a bond. The shape follows interest rates more than the business: a 2022 fall, a 2023 trough, a 2024–early 2026 climb to the $78.07 high in February 2026, and a slide since June.

The 6-period monthly RSI now reads 29.3, just inside oversold and level with the October 2022 (29.3) and September 2023 (29.2) readings — the two most oversold points in the five-year series, both of which were followed by recoveries. The 10-month moving average sits at roughly $72.76, about 12% above the price: the trend is down and the stock has not tested it from below yet.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 16.7x 2026 consensus earnings for a Michigan monopoly that plans 10.5% annual rate base growth, guides 6–8% EPS growth "toward the high end," pays a 3.52% dividend it has raised for 18 straight years, and is removing its one non-utility distraction. The March electric order at 9.90% ROE shows the regulator is still granting the core of each ask. Every analyst target — even the lowest, $68 — sits above the price, the monthly RSI is at levels that marked the last two troughs, and a single GW of data-center load would add $2–5B of rate base the plan does not yet count.

Bear Case

A utility is a bond with a growth kicker, and the kicker costs money. Free cash flow is −$1.91B, debt is $19.30B, and the plan needs $3.75B of new equity — sold at lower prices every month the stock slides. Earned ROE (9.16%) already trails the allowed rate. Q2 adjusted EPS halved on weather and storms, and the full-year number now leans on an H2 recovery. The analyst community is 8 Holds to 7 Buys and cutting targets. If bond yields stay high, the multiple has no reason to expand, and 6–8% growth priced at 16.7x is not obviously cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory outcomes. With nearly all earnings rate-base driven after the NorthStar exit, the October 16 gas order and the pending $456M electric case set the return on the entire plan. A lower ROE or grant ratio is a direct hit.
02
Financing cost and dilution. $19.30B of debt, negative free cash flow and a $3.75B five-year equity program make CMS sensitive to both interest rates and its own share price.
03
Load that doesn't show up. Bill growth of ~3% against 10.5% rate base growth relies partly on new large-load customers. The one signed data-center deal is still pending local zoning; if the pipeline stalls, affordability pressure rises and regulators push back.
Industry Commentary
Utilities as rate-sensitive income stocks
ONGOING HEADWIND

Coverage of the September slide points to interest-rate sensitivity and sector sentiment rather than anything company-specific: one summary of the Morgan Stanley cut describes the stock as down about 7% over one month and 11% over three, and names "higher financing costs and regulatory decisions in Michigan" as the ongoing risks. CMS's own history agrees: the two worst stretches in the five-year chart, 2022 and 2023, were rate-driven.

Large load as the new growth lever
EMERGING TAILWIND

CMS's pitch is that new industrial and data-center demand lets rate base grow faster than bills: spread a larger fixed-cost base over more kilowatt-hours and each customer pays less per unit. It has contracted roughly 135 MW of manufacturing and industrial load year-to-date and cites a ~9 GW pipeline. The bull framing: every GW converted is billions of rate base and a cheaper bill, a rare case where shareholders and ratepayers want the same thing. The bear framing: pipeline is not contract, and a utility that builds ahead of load that never arrives leaves the stranded cost with someone — usually a regulator deciding who.
