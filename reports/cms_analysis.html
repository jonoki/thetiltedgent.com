01 Company Overview

In 1886 two men set out to light downtown Jackson, Michigan with electric arc lamps. The company they started is still headquartered in Jackson, and through its utility, Consumers Energy, it now serves 1.9 million electric and 1.8 million gas customers in Michigan. CMS Energy reported FY2025 revenue of $8.54B (+13.6%) and diluted EPS of $3.53; on a trailing-twelve-month basis (the four quarters through September 30, 2026) revenue is $8.98B and EPS is $2.80, held down by NorthStar write-downs.

A regulated utility is a simple machine to describe and a hard one to price. The state regulator, the Michigan Public Service Commission, lets the company earn an allowed return on equity on the capital it has invested in poles, wires, pipes and plants — the rate base. Grow the rate base, keep the allowed ROE, and earnings grow with it. CMS plans to take rate base from $28.4B in 2025 to $46.8B in 2030, a 10.5% compound rate, funded by a $24B five-year utility investment plan. On July 28, 2026 it went further toward pure-play status: it is exiting non-utility renewables development at its NorthStar Clean Energy arm, so that almost all of its earnings and growth come from the regulated utility. At the Q3 report on October 27 it had agreed or closed sales covering more than 35% of NorthStar's assets, with most of the rest in advanced talks (Utility Dive, October 28, 2026), and it booked $456M of asset impairment and restructuring charges in the first nine months, before a $117M tax benefit — $1.08 per share of the gap between Q3 reported and adjusted earnings.

The stock closed at $78.07 in February 2026 and at $70.76 on November 2, 2026 — down 9.4%. In between it fell to a 52-week intraday low of $62.29 on September 29, the day after a $62.60 close, and from that September 28 close it rose 13.0% to the November 2 close. Guidance did not change: 2026 adjusted EPS guidance of $3.83–$3.90 and 2027 guidance of $4.08–$4.17 were reaffirmed with the Q3 results. Adjusted EPS was $2.95 for the first nine months, so the 2026 range needs $0.88–$0.95 in the fourth quarter. The question for this report is what the price is now paying for — and what could make that 10.5% rate base growth fail to reach the shareholder.

The two numbers that actually matter

For a regulated utility, judge it on how much rate base it can add and how much of its asks the regulator grants. Everything else is a rounding error on those two.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchised service territory Regulated return on rate base 3.7M customer connections Forward-looking rate filings A-rated utility bonds 18 years of dividend growth

A utility has no competitors in the usual sense: nobody runs a second set of wires to a Michigan house. The moat is the franchise plus the regulatory compact — the company builds what the state needs and the state lets it earn a fair return. The weak point is that the compact is renegotiated in every rate case. The house can set the table limit; the regulator sets the rake. Consumers Energy's first mortgage bonds carry ratings of A1/A/A+ from Moody's, S&P and Fitch (July 30, 2026 term sheet, sec.gov); the $1B of junior subordinated notes the CMS parent priced on October 29 carry Baa3/BBB−/BB+ — the gap reflects both the holding-company layer and those notes' low rank among its debts.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The regulator decides the payout. A 9.90% allowed ROE on 66% of the ask (electric, March) and 9.80% on 60% (gas, October) are solid Michigan outcomes, but the consolidated ROE actually earned over the last twelve months was 5.31% (StockAnalysis), pulled down by the NorthStar charges and not a like-for-like comparison with the allowed rate. Any drift down in allowed ROE or grant ratios flows straight through a model where nearly all growth is rate-base driven.
02
Negative free cash flow is the model, and it needs funding. TTM free cash flow is −$1.74B against $20.21B of debt and a 1.96 debt-to-equity ratio. The plan calls for $3.75B of new equity over five years. Every dollar of that sold at $70.76 rather than $78 dilutes more.
03
The year leans on the fourth quarter. Second-quarter adjusted EPS fell to $0.37 from $0.71; Q3 adjusted EPS then came in at $1.44 against $0.93 a year earlier. With $2.95 booked for nine months, the 2026 range of $3.83–$3.90 needs $0.88–$0.95 in Q4.
04
Affordability caps the ask. Rate base growing 10.5% a year while bills rise ~3% only works if fuel and power costs fall, efficiency programs deliver, and new load — data centers above all — spreads fixed costs. If the load does not arrive, the bill math strains and regulators push back.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 18.2x 2026 consensus earnings for a Michigan monopoly that plans 10.5% annual rate base growth, guides 6–8% EPS growth "toward the high end," pays a 3.22% dividend it has raised for 18 straight years, and is removing its one non-utility distraction. Q3 adjusted EPS of $1.44 beat the year-earlier $0.93 and guidance was reaffirmed. The regulator is still granting the core of each ask (66% electric in March, 60% gas in October, at 9.90% and 9.80% ROE). The average analyst target of $73.85 sits 4.4% above the price, and each added GW of large load could mean $2–5B of capital investment; the up-to-1 GW data center is not among the signed contracts (Utility Dive).

Bear Case

A utility is a bond with a growth kicker, and the kicker costs money. Free cash flow is −$1.74B, debt is $20.21B, and the plan needs $3.75B of new equity. Earned ROE (5.31%, depressed by NorthStar charges) trails the allowed rate, and the gas order gave 60% of the ask. The full-year number needs $0.88–$0.95 in Q4. The analyst community is 9 Holds to 7 Buys and most October target changes were cuts. If bond yields stay high, the multiple has no reason to expand, and 6–8% growth priced at 18.2x is not obviously cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory outcomes. With nearly all earnings rate-base driven after the NorthStar exit, the pending $443M electric case (order expected by early April 2027) sets the return on the entire plan after a gas order that granted about 60% of the ask. A lower ROE or grant ratio is a direct hit.
02
Financing cost and dilution. $20.21B of debt, negative free cash flow and a $3.75B five-year equity program make CMS sensitive to both interest rates and its own share price.
03
Load that doesn't show up. Bill growth of ~3% against 10.5% rate base growth relies partly on new large-load customers. The up-to-1 GW data center is still moving through local zoning; if the pipeline stalls, affordability pressure rises and regulators push back.
Industry Commentary
Utilities as rate-sensitive income stocks
ONGOING HEADWIND

The stock closed at $62.60 on September 28 and has since risen 13.0% to $70.76 with guidance unchanged. CMS's own history shows how rate-driven it can be: the two worst stretches in the five-year chart, 2022 and 2023, came as bond yields rose, and its beta to the market is only 0.31. Michigan regulatory decisions (a 9.80% allowed return on gas in October) and financing costs remain the ongoing risks.

Large load as the new growth lever
EMERGING TAILWIND

CMS's pitch is that new industrial and data-center demand lets rate base grow faster than bills: spread a larger fixed-cost base over more kilowatt-hours and each customer pays less per unit. Consumers has energized about 450 MW of new large load against 745 MW of signed contracts in its five-year plan, and CMS projects 2% to 3% annual load growth through 2030 (Utility Dive). The bull framing: every GW converted is billions of rate base and a cheaper bill, a rare case where shareholders and ratepayers want the same thing. The bear framing: a prospect is not a contract, and a utility that builds ahead of load that never arrives leaves the stranded cost with someone — usually a regulator deciding who.
