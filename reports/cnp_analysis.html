01 Company Overview

On July 8, 2024, Hurricane Beryl came ashore as a Category 1 storm and left 2.2 million CenterPoint customers without power — the largest outage in the company's history. Two years later the same wires company is telling investors that data centers and other large loads in its Houston territory have asked to connect 17 gigawatts through ERCOT's "Batch Zero" process, of which about 14 GW is expected to be eligible — against a Houston Electric system peak of 21 GW today. One company, two stories: a utility that had to rebuild its standing with customers and regulators, and a utility whose large-load queue equals two-thirds of its current peak.

CenterPoint is a regulated utility holding company serving over 7 million metered customers across Texas, Indiana, Minnesota and Ohio: electric transmission and distribution in Houston, a vertically integrated electric utility in southern Indiana, and gas distribution in several states. The math of a utility is short. Profit ≈ rate base × equity share of the capital structure × allowed ROE. Grow the rate base, keep the allowed return, and earnings follow. Houston Electric's most recent rate case settlement, approved in March 2025, raised its allowed ROE to 9.65% from 9.4%; management now guides Houston Electric's rate base to compound at more than 18% a year for three years and group non-GAAP EPS to grow at the mid-to-high end of 7–9% a year through 2028.

The stock has noticed — then changed its mind. A September 2021 month-end close of $24.60 became $44.04 by June 2026, a five-year high. It has since slid to $37.99, within 2.1% of its 52-week low, while management reaffirmed guidance and raised the ten-year capital plan to $66.7B. The debate is not whether the growth exists. It is who pays for it: a utility spending $6.8B of capital this year against $2.58B of operating cash flow has to fund the gap with debt, asset sales and eventually equity, and every dollar of that funding is priced off interest rates the company does not control.

Houston Electric is a pure transmission and distribution utility inside ERCOT: it owns the poles and wires but does not generate or sell the power, so its earnings depend on how much it invests and what the Texas commission lets it earn, not on commodity prices. Houston Electric earned $202M in Q2 2026, up 43% year over year (per the Energy and Policy Institute's reading of company filings). 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service franchise 9.65% allowed ROE (Houston Electric) ~85% tracker recovery ERCOT large-load queue (~14 GW) 7M+ metered customers $66.7B 10-year capital plan

A utility's moat is legal, not competitive. Nobody else can string wires to a Houston data center. Within its territory CenterPoint is the only provider, and the commission sets its return. That protects the business from competition. It does not protect it from the regulator. The moat is only as wide as the political tolerance for customer bills, and in Texas that tolerance was tested by Beryl. The settlement that followed went the company's way on ROE (9.65%) while cutting revenue by $46.6M and removing $2.4M of executive expense from rates. That is the trade a utility makes: return on capital in exchange for a visible concession on bills.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The capex is bigger than the cash. TTM operating cash flow was $2.58B against $5.27B of capex, for −$2.70B of free cash flow, and 2026 capex is guided to $6.8B. Debt is $24.64B against $453M of cash, and debt-to-equity is 2.10. Management says the current plan needs no new equity through 2027, but it has flagged about $3B of equity issuance over 2028–2035. The growth is real, and it will be paid for partly through dilution.
02
Political and storm risk in Houston. Beryl's 2.2 million outages brought public backlash and closer regulatory scrutiny. The company later won approval to recover about $425M of costs from the May 2024 windstorm and derecho, adding about $1 a month to bills. Each storm-cost recovery uses up regulatory goodwill that the next rate case will need.
03
Load that has not arrived yet. The 14 GW Batch Zero figure is eligible demand, not connected demand. Data-center projects get delayed, resized and cancelled. The ~$900M of customer cash and security already posted is meaningful protection, but the 18%+ rate base CAGR assumes much of this load shows up on schedule.
04
Allowed ROE is a ceiling. Consolidated ROE is 9.82% and Houston Electric's allowed ROE is 9.65%. Earning above the authorised level invites a rate review; earning below it through regulatory lag is common. The upside from here comes from a bigger rate base, not a higher return.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 19.9x consensus 2026 earnings for a utility whose largest subsidiary is guided to compound rate base at more than 18% a year, earns on a 9.65% allowed ROE, and recovers about 85% of its capital through trackers. The load behind it is concrete: ~14 GW of eligible large-load demand against a 21 GW system peak, with ~$900M of customer cash already posted. The Ohio sale at ~1.9x rate base funds part of the build without dilution, and management has kept the no-new-equity-through-2027 commitment while raising the plan to $66.7B. Every published analyst target is above $37.99, with a $45.41 average. If 7–9% EPS growth compounds as guided, the September sell-off was a chance to buy that growth near a 52-week low.

Bear Case

The growth is real, and it is expensive. Free cash flow is −$2.70B, debt is $24.64B, FFO/debt of 13.4% leaves a narrow cushion over a downgrade, and about $3B of equity is already planned for 2028–2035. The 14 GW is eligible load, not connected load. The Houston customer base still remembers Beryl, so every future rate case and storm-cost recovery gets tested politically. Consolidated ROE of 9.82% is already at the allowed return, so there is little left to gain on returns. At 22.6x trailing GAAP earnings with a 2.42% yield, the stock is priced for execution. The analyst sequence since July — targets trimmed, ratings mostly Hold — suggests the market is waiting for proof.

Top 3 Risks

01
Financing the capital plan. $6.8B of 2026 capex against $2.58B of TTM operating cash flow. If FFO/debt slips, the choice is between a rating downgrade and earlier equity, and both lower the per-share value of the growth. The Ohio sale closing on time in Q4 2026 is the near-term checkpoint.
02
Regulatory and political treatment. The 9.65% allowed ROE was won in a post-Beryl settlement that also cut revenue. Another major storm, a bill spike, or tougher outcomes in the Minnesota and Indiana gas cases planned before year-end would put pressure on returns across the portfolio.
03
Load conversion. The >18% Houston Electric rate base CAGR depends on large-load customers connecting on schedule, with 3 GW targeted in 2027 and nearly all 14 GW by end-2030. Delays or cancellations would leave the utility with capex and financing already committed, ahead of the revenue.
Industry Commentary
Texas large-load growth and the wires utility
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Houston Electric is a T&D utility inside ERCOT, so it does not bet on power prices. It earns on the wires that connect new load. ERCOT's Batch Zero process has CenterPoint handling 17 GW of submitted large-load projects, ~14 GW of them expected to be eligible, against a 21 GW system peak today. At Q4 2025 management had spoken of 7.5 GW of data-center load by the end of 2028; the Q2 2026 queue is almost twice that. Management's argument to regulators is that fixed network costs spread over much more demand will lower residential and commercial delivery charges by more than $5B over a decade. That argument is what makes the growth politically viable. If it holds, the load growth also eases the tension between higher investment and customer bills.

Affordability and storm resilience after Beryl
ONGOING HEADWIND

Beryl turned CenterPoint into a political story in Houston. The 2024 rate case, initially filed asking for a 10.4% ROE and later withdrawn and settled, ended at 9.65% with a $46.6M revenue cut and $2.4M of executive expense removed from rates. Since then, storm-cost recovery of about $425M for the May 2024 events added about $1 a month back onto bills. The pattern is familiar across the industry: regulators grant returns and recover storm costs, and in exchange they want visible bill discipline. Houston Electric's 43% jump in Q2 2026 profit, reported by a utility watchdog, shows how that tension plays out in public.

Capital intensity and the cost of money
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A $66.7B ten-year plan on a $25.02B market cap means CenterPoint will spend more than two and a half times its current equity value over the decade. The regulated return is fixed in each rate case, but the cost of the debt and equity that fund it moves with markets. Between rate cases, higher borrowing costs cut directly into earned returns: interest expense cost $0.05 a share in Q4 2025 and $0.01 in Q2 2026 year over year. The company's answers are capital recycling (Ohio at ~1.9x rate base), trackers that shorten regulatory lag, and equity deferred to 2028 and later. Whether a 9.65% allowed return leaves a wide enough margin over the utility's cost of capital is what the stock is pricing.
