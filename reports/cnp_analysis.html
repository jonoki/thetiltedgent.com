01 Company Overview

On July 8, 2024, Hurricane Beryl came ashore as a Category 1 storm and left 2.2 million CenterPoint customers without power — the largest outage in the company's history. Two years later the same wires company is telling investors that data centers and other large loads in its Houston territory have asked to connect about 14 gigawatts through "Batch Zero", a group review of large new connection requests by ERCOT, the Texas grid operator, of which ERCOT deemed about 10 GW base load — against a Houston Electric system peak of 21 GW today. One company, two stories: a utility that had to rebuild its standing with customers and regulators, and a utility whose large-load queue equals two-thirds of its current peak.

CenterPoint is a regulated utility holding company serving nearly 7 million metered customers across Texas, Indiana and Minnesota (it sold its Ohio gas business on October 1, 2026): electric transmission and distribution in Houston, a vertically integrated electric utility in southern Indiana, and gas distribution in several states. The math of a utility is short. Profit ≈ rate base × equity share of the capital structure × allowed ROE. Grow the rate base, keep the allowed return, and earnings follow. Houston Electric's most recent rate case settlement, approved in March 2025, raised its allowed ROE to 9.65% from 9.4%; management now guides the group's rate base to compound at more than 14% a year through 2030 and non-GAAP EPS (earnings excluding items management treats as one-off) to grow at the mid-to-high end of 7–9% a year through 2030.

The stock has noticed — then changed its mind, and changed it again. An October 2021 month-end close of $26.04 became $44.04 by June 2026, a five-year high. It slid to a $36.80 September close, within 1.2% of its 52-week low, then rebounded 15.3% in October to $42.44 as management raised 2026 guidance to $1.90–$1.92, started 2027 at $2.07–$2.09 and raised the ten-year capital plan to $70.7B. The debate is not whether the growth exists. It is who pays for it: a utility that spent $4.17B of capital in the first nine months of 2026 against $2.05B of operating cash flow has to fund the gap with debt, asset sales and equity, and every dollar of that funding is priced off interest rates the company does not control.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service franchise 9.65% allowed ROE (Houston Electric) 80%+ tracker recovery ERCOT large-load queue (~14 GW submitted) ~7M metered customers $70.7B 10-year capital plan

A utility's moat is legal, not competitive. Nobody else can string wires to a Houston data center. Within its territory CenterPoint is the only provider, and the commission sets its return. That protects the business from competition. It does not protect it from the regulator. The moat is only as wide as the political tolerance for customer bills, and in Texas that tolerance was tested by Beryl. The settlement that followed went the company's way on ROE (9.65%) while cutting revenue by $46.6M a year. That is the trade a utility makes: return on capital in exchange for a visible concession on bills.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The capex is bigger than the cash. TTM operating cash flow was $2.83B against $5.65B of capex, for about −$2.8B of free cash flow; 2026 capex was guided to $6.8B; the CFO now expects it modestly below that on project timing, and the Q3 presentation shows about $6.5B. Debt is $24.90B against $132M of cash at September 30, and debt-to-equity is 2.12. The CFO raised the remaining ten-year equity need by $1.6B to $4.5B, with about $4B to be issued over 2027–2030 including $500M–$700M of common equity in 2027 (Q3 call); the 10-Q shows about 9 million new shares ($165M net) issued in the first nine months of 2026, and the Q3 presentation counts about $1.1B of equity already issued or priced, including forward sales that settle by February 2027. The growth is real, and it will be paid for partly through dilution.
02
Political and storm risk in Houston. Beryl's 2.2 million outages brought public backlash and closer regulatory scrutiny. In October 2025 the Texas commission approved recovery of about $1.2B of repair costs from Beryl and two other storms, which the company said would add about $2 a month to the average bill (Community Impact). Each storm-cost recovery uses up regulatory goodwill that the next rate case will need.
03
Load that has not arrived yet. The 14 GW Batch Zero figure is submitted demand, not connected demand, and about 2 GW of studied load dropped out in the state audit. Data-center projects get delayed, resized and cancelled. The security deposits that base-load projects must post, equal to the cost of their interconnection, are meaningful protection, but the 14%+ rate base growth through 2030 assumes much of this load shows up on schedule.
04
Allowed ROE is a ceiling. Consolidated ROE is 10.14% and Houston Electric's allowed ROE is 9.65%. Earning above the authorised level invites a rate review; earning below it through regulatory lag is common. The upside from here comes from a bigger rate base, not a higher return.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 22.2x consensus 2026 earnings for a utility whose enterprise rate base is guided to compound at more than 14% a year through 2030, whose largest subsidiary earns on a 9.65% allowed ROE, and which recovers well over 80% of its capital through trackers. The load behind it is concrete: about 14 GW submitted to Batch Zero, about 10 GW of it deemed base load by ERCOT, against a 21 GW system peak. The Ohio sale at ~1.9x rate base closed on October 1 and funds part of the build, the capital plan rose to $70.7B, and management raised 2026 guidance to $1.90–$1.92 and started 2027 at $2.07–$2.09. The $44.24 average analyst target is 4.2% above $42.44. If 7–9% EPS growth compounds as guided, October's rebound from the September low was a first step, not a finish.

Bear Case

The growth is real, and it is expensive. Free cash flow is about −$2.8B, debt is $24.90B, and the CFO now puts the remaining ten-year equity need at $4.5B, up $1.6B, including $500M–$700M of common equity in 2027. FFO/debt of 13.7% still leaves a narrow cushion over a downgrade. The 14 GW is submitted load, not connected load, and about 2 GW of studied load dropped out in the state audit. The Houston customer base still remembers Beryl, so every future rate case and storm-cost recovery gets tested politically. Consolidated ROE of 10.14% is already above the Houston allowed return, so there is little left to gain on returns. At 24.4x trailing GAAP earnings with a 2.26% yield, the stock is priced for execution. Ratings split 10 buy and 11 hold, and after the print Hold-rated Mizuho cut its target from $45 to $43.

Top 3 Risks

01
Financing the capital plan. $6.8B of 2026 capex guidance (management now expects modestly less) against $2.83B of TTM operating cash flow. If FFO/debt slips, the choice is between a rating downgrade and earlier equity, and both lower the per-share value of the growth. Industry Commentary
Texas large-load growth and the wires utility
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Houston Electric is a wires-only (transmission and distribution) utility inside ERCOT, so it does not bet on power prices. It earns on the wires that connect new load. ERCOT's Batch Zero process has CenterPoint handling about 14 GW of submitted large-load projects, about 10 GW of them deemed base load, against a 21 GW system peak today. Management's argument to regulators is that fixed network costs spread over much more demand will bring about $5B of savings for Texas residential and commercial customers over the next decade (Q3 release). That argument is what makes the growth politically viable. If it holds, the load growth also eases the tension between higher investment and customer bills.

Affordability and storm resilience after Beryl
ONGOING HEADWIND

Beryl turned CenterPoint into a political story in Houston. The 2024 rate case, initially filed asking for a 10.4% ROE and later withdrawn and settled, ended at 9.65% with a $46.6M annual revenue cut. Since then, recovery of about $1.2B of Beryl and other storm costs, approved in October 2025, added about $2 a month to the average bill. The pattern is familiar across the industry: regulators grant returns and recover storm costs, and in exchange they want visible bill discipline.

Capital intensity and the cost of money
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A $70.7B ten-year plan on a $28.13B market cap means CenterPoint will spend about two and a half times its current equity value over the decade. The regulated return is fixed in each rate case, but the cost of the debt and equity that fund it moves with markets. Between rate cases, higher borrowing costs cut directly into earned returns: higher interest expense cost $0.01 a share in Q2 2026 and higher financing expenses $0.04 in Q3 2026, each against a year earlier. The company's answers are capital recycling (Ohio at ~1.9x rate base, closed October 1), trackers that shorten regulatory lag, and about $4B of equity spread over 2027–2030. Whether a 9.65% allowed return leaves a wide enough margin over the utility's cost of capital is what the stock is pricing.
