01 Company Overview

Coherent designs and manufactures optical transceivers, lasers, photonic components and engineered materials. In the quarter ended September 30, 2026, the first of its fiscal 2027, revenue was a record $2,370.3M, up 49.9% YoY, or 56% once the businesses it has sold are taken out of the comparison. Fiscal 2026 (year ended June 30, 2026) revenue was $7,118M, up 22.5%. The company ranks #272 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $37.98B, employs 51,478 people, and is the merged II-VI / Coherent entity: II-VI bought Coherent Inc. in 2022 and took its name.

The framing that matters: this is an AI-infrastructure optics company wearing a diversified-industrial disguise. Coherent reports two segments, and in the September quarter Datacenter & Communications was 82% of revenue and grew 79.1%, while Industrial was 18% of revenue and fell 14.8% as reported (flat once sold businesses are excluded, management said). Nearly all of the growth comes from 800G and 1.6T optical transceivers, the modules that carry traffic between GPUs inside and across AI clusters, and from the indium phosphide (InP — the compound semiconductor from which the laser chips inside those transceivers are cut) supply chain that Coherent owns end-to-end. The central debate on this page is whether that ownership is a lasting margin advantage or a temporary shortage premium that Chinese module makers and the big cloud companies will compete away. It now costs more to find out: Coherent spent $776M on plants and equipment in the September quarter alone, more than two-thirds of all of fiscal 2026, and borrowed to help pay for it. Then the competitive map moved. On November 2, Marvell agreed to buy Lumentum, Coherent's closest rival, and Coherent's pitch to customers is now that it is the independent supplier.

The September quarter did $1,951.7M, up 79.1% YoY; datacenter revenue on its own rose 85% YoY and 17% from the June quarter, management said. Management expects 1.6T to keep ramping quickly and 800G to keep growing through calendar 2027. The margin argument sits one level below the module: Coherent makes its own indium phosphide laser chips rather than buying them, which is where the scarce value in an AI transceiver actually is. Coherent doubled internal InP output capacity year over year in the September quarter, one quarter ahead of plan, and says it is on track to more than double it again by the end of calendar 2027. In August it said a six-inch InP wafer yields about four times the output of a three-inch wafer at roughly half the cost; in November it said six-inch yields are running above its three-inch lines, with production in Zurich due to begin in the first half of calendar 2027. Visibility is unusually long for a components business. Asked about demand on the November 4 call, CEO Jim Anderson said "calendar 2027, is basically booked out, and the customers are booking out calendar 2028 now." Customers typically pay part of such deals up front to help fund the capacity, and money received in advance rose to $333M at September 30 from $63M in June (10-Q).

The core advantage is one level below the product Coherent sells. Most optical-module vendors buy their laser chips and assemble modules; the assembly step is competitive, low-margin and increasingly Chinese. Coherent grows its own indium phosphide and cuts its own lasers, which means when transceiver demand spikes the bottleneck, laser supply, is inside its own factories rather than in a supplier's queue. Anderson named indium phosphide as a constraint over the past year and a half on the November call. It shows in the numbers: by June gross margin had expanded in eight of the past nine quarters, by more than 660 basis points (6.6 percentage points) in total, CFO Sherri Luther said in August, and it reached 40.6% non-GAAP in the September quarter, up 1.84 points YoY. Management now targets a first quarter above 45% before the end of calendar 2028, three points above its previous goal of 42%, and credited better pricing as a large contributor. The six-inch wafer transition adds to it, at four times the output per wafer for roughly half the cost. The moat is being reinforced with cash: $775.5M of capital spending in the September quarter, against $104M a year earlier, with management guiding it higher again in the December quarter. In August management cited roughly an 18-month payback on datacenter investment. Around that sit customer commitments that are rare in components: calendar 2027 basically booked out, bookings reaching into calendar 2028, long-term agreements with customers paying part of the cost up front, and in March a $2B equity investment and multibillion-dollar purchase commitment from NVIDIA.

Key Vulnerabilities

●
Customer concentration among a handful of buyers. Datacenter & Communications is 82% of revenue and sells to a short list of cloud companies and networking equipment makers. A single new agreement is worth $20B over five years; NVIDIA is both a customer and a roughly 4% shareholder. A book that is "basically booked out" is booked by a small number of counterparties, each of whom has the scale to buy from a second supplier, renegotiate, or design the pluggable module out entirely. Concentration cuts both ways, and right now it is cutting favourably.
●
Chinese module makers are the real share threat. Zhongji Innolight's September-quarter revenue rose 171.8% to CN¥27.77B. Innolight and Eoptolink compete directly in 800G and 1.6T transceivers sold to any buyer and have historically won on price and speed of qualification. Coherent's defence is the laser chip, not the module; if InP supply loosens across the industry, which is exactly what everyone's capacity expansion is meant to achieve, module prices should fall and the premium with them. This is a structural argument, not a quantified one.
●
Co-packaged optics could cut out the pluggable, and the rivals are consolidating. CPO moves the optics onto the switch package, which is Broadcom's home turf, and Marvell's purchase of Lumentum would put a laser maker inside a switch and interconnect chip company. Coherent is playing both sides: PhotonLink covers co-packaged and near-packaged optics with named revenue targets, and Anderson argued that customers want an independent optical supplier with no competitive conflict. A shift of value from module assembly toward switch silicon is still not obviously good for a module vendor, however good its lasers are.
●
Debt is rising again. Total debt rose to $4,000.6M from $3,222.2M in one quarter, while cash and short-term investments fell to $1,752.9M from $1,987.0M (a further $583.4M of restricted cash brings the company's quoted cash figure to $2.34B). The increase came from new credit facilities taken out by foreign subsidiaries over the summer. Management's debt leverage ratio, debt measured against a year of earnings, is 1.1x, up from 0.7x in June. ROE of 9.27% and ROIC of 7.86% are the residue of a large merger goodwill and asset base that the datacenter business is only now beginning to earn a return on.
●
There is no second engine. Industrial is 18% of revenue and, pro forma, flat. If the AI transceiver cycle pauses, what is left is a much smaller industrial business that has stopped growing.

Notable One-Off Items

The cash flow is the one-off, and it may not be one. Over the last four quarters Coherent earned $961.7M attributable to shareholders and produced −$59.1M of operating cash flow. The gap is mostly working capital: inventory rose to $3,341.4M from $2,581.0M in one quarter, and receivables to $1,604.2M from $1,343.3M, built ahead of shipments that are already booked. Subtract $1,774.5M of capital spending and free cash flow is −$1,834M, negative in each of the four quarters and worst in the latest (−$868M, on $775.5M of capital spending). The quarter's gap was covered by selling down short-term investments, which fell to $200.0M from $825.0M, and by about $773M drawn on foreign credit facilities (the Term A and B loans and the senior notes did not change). A shareholder should be clear-eyed about what this means: the accounting profit is real, and the company spent nearly two dollars for every dollar of it. That is defensible if the payback claims hold and the upfront customer payments and bookings are honoured. It is not defensible if 2028 demand disappoints, because the capacity will already be built and paid for.

Divestitures completed in FY2026. The aerospace-and-defence business and the Munich tools business were both sold during the fiscal year. Their absence is why reported September-quarter growth of 49.9% becomes 56% on a pro-forma basis, and why Industrial's reported 14.8% fall is flat pro forma.

GAAP versus non-GAAP. September-quarter GAAP EPS was $1.90 against non-GAAP $2.09, a gap of 10%, far narrower than fiscal 2026's 36% ($4.12 against $5.61). The add-backs are mostly share-based pay ($73.2M) and amortisation of acquired intangibles ($69.5M), less a $121.1M tax adjustment; a $13.9M foreign-exchange gain flattered GAAP. At 40.0× GAAP and about 29.5× non-GAAP, the choice of earnings base moves the P/E by about a quarter.

Prior-year context. After preferred dividends, the amount left for common shareholders was −$80.6M in FY2025 (net earnings before those dividends were +$49.4M), −$279.5M in FY2024 and −$403.7M in FY2023. The trailing P/E therefore has no meaningful multi-year history to anchor to: fiscal 2026 was the first year in four with a profit for common shareholders.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 close of $62.53 to the November 6, 2026 close of $192.81, COHR returned +208.3%, a compound annual rate of about 25.6%, with no dividend, against +61.4% for SPY and +66.6% for QQQ on price over the same dates. The path was anything but straight. The stock fell 52.7% from November 2021 to its October 2023 monthly close of $29.60 as the merger loaded on debt and goodwill and fiscal 2023 ended in a loss for common shareholders. From that low to the June 2026 monthly close of $394.47 it rose more than thirteen-fold in thirty-two months, essentially all of it after the AI transceiver cycle began. It has since given back 51.1% from that peak, and the stock is now 56.2% below the $440 intraday high of June 3, even as the 52-week price change is still +21.0%.

Relative performance. The peer comparison is the informative one. On July 28, 2026, Coherent fell 10.3%, Lumentum 8.4%, Applied Optoelectronics 9.9% and the iShares Semiconductor ETF 4.8% close to close, so Coherent fell about twice as far as the ETF. November 5 was different: the whole chip sector sold off, with Coherent down 13.0%, Lumentum down 18.7% and the semiconductor ETF down 12.2%; 24/7 Wall St. pointed to Barron's asking whether the Marvell deal could fall apart as a further weight on Lumentum. Over the past year COHR's +21.0% still beats SPY (+9.7%) and QQQ (+7.3%). In market value Lumentum sits at $48.84B against Coherent's $37.98B, on less than half the trailing revenue. Whether that is Lumentum being expensive or Coherent's industrial side being a drag on its value is the best way to frame the Coherent-versus-Lumentum question, and with Marvell paying in stock, Lumentum's price now also moves with Marvell's.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the segment split, because it is the whole argument. In the September quarter, Datacenter & Communications did $1,951.7M and grew 79.1%; Industrial did $418.6M and fell 14.8%, flat pro forma. The consolidated 49.9% growth rate is a weighted blend of a very fast-growing optics company and a smaller, stalled industrial one, and valuing the blend at a single P/E is how this stock gets mispriced in both directions. The honest question is what an investor is paying for the 82% of revenue that is AI optics, and what the other 18% is worth alongside it.

The vertical-integration claim, and what actually supports it. The bull case is not "Coherent sells transceivers." Everyone sells transceivers. It is that Coherent grows its own indium phosphide and cuts its own laser chips, so the scarce input in an AI transceiver sits inside its own factories. The August checkpoint was met: internal output capacity doubled year over year in the September quarter, one quarter early. The plan from here is to more than double again by the end of calendar 2027, with six-inch wafers giving about 4× the output of three-inch at about half the cost and six-inch production in Zurich in the first half of calendar 2027. The result shows up where it should, in gross margin: 40.6% non-GAAP in the September quarter, and the long-term target raised to above 45%. This is a manufacturing programme with quarterly checkpoints, and so far it is hitting them.

What it costs. Over the last four quarters Coherent spent $1,774.5M of capital and produced −$59.1M of operating cash flow, for free cash flow of −$1,834M. The September quarter alone burned $868M, and capital spending is guided higher again. Management's defence is the demand book: calendar 2027 basically booked out, calendar 2028 being booked now, the $20B agreement and customers helping to fund capacity up front. In August it also cited an 18-month payback and take-or-pay minimums, where customers pay even if they take less, on some long-term agreements. If those contracts are what they are described as, the cash burn is the correct decision and free cash flow should swing hard once capital spending falls. If demand normalises first, Coherent will own a great deal of indium phosphide capacity at exactly the moment the industry no longer needs it, and the shortage premium in the gross margin goes with it.

The balance sheet has stopped improving. Total debt of $4,000.6M against $1,752.9M of cash and short-term investments, debt-to-equity of 0.34, and a debt leverage ratio of 1.1x, up from 0.7x in June. That is still a far better position than the merger-era balance sheet, and NVIDIA's $2B in March helped. But the company is now borrowing to build, and a downturn would push the ratio up further without a single dollar of new debt, because earnings would fall. Meanwhile ROIC of 7.86% and ROE of 9.27% say the merged asset base still earns modest returns.

Valuation, and which earnings base. On trailing GAAP EPS of $4.82, COHR trades at 40.0×. On trailing non-GAAP EPS of about $6.54, at 29.5×. On fiscal 2027 consensus EPS of $10.41, at 18.5×, and consensus fiscal 2027 revenue of $11.15B implies 56.7% growth, which is a bet that the booked order book converts cleanly. The PEG of 0.30 is the bulls' best number, and it is only that low because expected growth is that large. On free cash flow there is no multiple at all, because free cash flow is negative. The defensible read: this is much cheaper than it was in September on every earnings measure, because the price fell while the earnings estimates rose. It is only cheap against a future that management says is booked. That is a more concrete foundation than most AI-adjacent stocks offer, and it is still a forecast.

Bull Case

Coherent owns the scarce input, indium phosphide laser chips, in the fastest-growing part of AI infrastructure, and doubled that capacity a quarter ahead of schedule while moving to six-inch wafers at roughly half the unit cost. Datacenter & Communications grew 79.1% to 82% of quarterly revenue, the gross-margin target is now above 45%, calendar 2027 is basically booked out and a new $20B agreement runs for five years. Consensus has revenue reaching $11.15B and EPS $10.41 in fiscal 2027, an 18.5× forward P/E on a 0.30 PEG, after the stock has halved from its June monthly peak. If Marvell absorbs Lumentum, customers who want a supplier with no competing chip business have fewer places to go.

Bear Case

The company burned $1.83B of free cash flow over the last four quarters, $1.77B of capital spending against −$59.1M of operating cash flow, and is guiding capital spending higher still, into a demand book from a handful of very large customers. It added $778M of debt in one quarter to help pay for it, and its leverage ratio rose to 1.1x from 0.7x. ROIC is 7.86%. Chinese rivals are growing faster: Innolight's revenue rose 171.8% in the same quarter. Broadcom's co-packaged optics and a Marvell-Lumentum combination could move value onto the switch package. With a beta of 2.23, the stock lost 33% in July, 27% in October and 13% in a day after a beat, which is a fair description of what an investor is actually holding.

Top 3 Risks

01
The capital spending bet is unhedged. $776M in one quarter and rising, against a customer list short enough to fit on one line. Upfront payments and booked orders provide real protection, but a large customer that wants to renegotiate generally can. If AI transceiver demand plateaus in calendar 2027, Coherent has already spent the money.
02
The shortage premium undoes itself. Coherent's gross margin expansion depends in large part on tight indium phosphide supply. Coherent is doubling its InP output, then more than doubling it again, and competitors are adding capacity too. The programme that creates the margin today is the programme that could erode it in 2028, unless demand grows faster than the industry's combined capacity additions, which is the assumption inside a 45% margin target.
03
It trades on someone else's cash flow statement. July 28, 2026: −10.3% in a day, which 24/7 Wall St. tied to Alphabet's capital spending; October −26.9%; November 5 −13.0% after a beat. Beta 2.23. With no dividend, negative free cash flow and 82% of quarterly revenue tied to datacenter buildout, there is nothing in this security to cushion a sentiment reversal in AI spending, and the stock is 56.2% below a high set five months ago.
  Industry Commentary
Indium Phosphide Is the Sector's Real Bottleneck
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every 800G and 1.6T optical module needs laser chips, and those chips come from indium phosphide wafers that take years of process development to yield well. This, not module assembly, which is competitive and increasingly Chinese, is where the AI optics supply chain is actually tight, and it is the reason a components company can post 40% gross margins. Coherent doubled its internal output capacity year over year in the September quarter, a quarter ahead of plan, with six-inch wafers delivering four times the output at half the cost of three-inch. The constraint is real today. The industry's collective response to it is the largest medium-term risk to the margins it currently supports.
Big Cloud Spending Is Now the Whole Cycle
ONGOING HEADWIND
On July 28, 2026, Alphabet's quarterly capital spending of $44.92B and 2026 plan of $195–205B coincided with falls in optical stocks: Coherent −10.3%, Applied Optoelectronics −9.9%, Lumentum −8.4%, against −4.8% for the iShares Semiconductor ETF. The group fell together again on November 5, with Coherent −13.0% and Lumentum −18.7%, and this time the ETF fell 12.2%. Two things follow. First, the demand is genuinely enormous and genuinely funded. Second, it is concentrated in a handful of buyers whose own investors are beginning to ask whether the spending converts into returns. Coherent's order book is only as good as those budgets, and the market now resets the prices of optical stocks on any large customer's cash flow statement, regardless of what the component makers themselves report.
Co-Packaged Optics: New Market, Substitution, and Now Consolidation
EMERGING TAILWIND
CPO moves the optical engine from a pluggable module onto the switch package itself, cutting power per bit at the cost of serviceability. Coherent has PhotonLink revenue passing $100M in the March 2027 quarter and $250M a quarter before the end of calendar 2027, with shipments for links inside a single AI system beginning in the second half of calendar 2027. Bull framing: whoever supplies the ultra-high-power continuous-wave lasers that CPO requires captures value regardless of packaging format, and on the November call Anderson said Coherent sees only one competitor in that laser. Bear framing: CPO's centre of gravity is switch silicon, which is Broadcom's, and a world where the module disappears into the chip package is a world with fewer independent module vendors. Marvell's $115B agreement to buy Lumentum shows chip companies intend to own the optics themselves. Coherent is positioned for both outcomes, but only one of them justifies today's price.
