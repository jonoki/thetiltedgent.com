01 Company Overview

Coherent designs and manufactures optical transceivers, lasers, photonic components and engineered materials. Fiscal 2026 (year ended June 30, 2026) revenue was $7,118M, up 22.5% from $5,810M, and the June quarter alone did $2,046M, up 33.7% YoY. The company ranks roughly #465 globally by market capitalisation at $55.20B, employs 51,478 people, and is the merged II-VI / Coherent entity — II-VI bought Coherent Inc. in 2022 and took its name.

The framing that matters: this is an AI-infrastructure optics company wearing a diversified-industrial disguise. Coherent now reports two segments, and in the June 2026 quarter Datacenter & Communications was 79% of revenue and grew 58.6%, while Industrial was 21% of revenue and shrank 15.8%. Nearly all of the growth, and nearly all of the margin expansion, comes from 800G and 1.6T optical transceivers — the modules that carry traffic between GPUs inside and across AI clusters — and from the indium phosphide (InP — the compound semiconductor from which the laser chips inside those transceivers are cut) supply chain that Coherent owns end-to-end. The central debate on this page is whether that vertical integration is a durable structural margin advantage, or a temporary scarcity rent that Chinese module makers and the hyperscalers' own supply-chain engineering will compete away before Coherent finishes spending $1.1B a year to expand it.

The core advantage is one level below the product Coherent sells. Most optical-module vendors buy their laser chips and assemble modules; the assembly step is competitive, low-margin and increasingly Chinese. Coherent grows its own indium phosphide and cuts its own lasers, which means when transceiver demand spikes the binding constraint — laser supply — is inside its own factories rather than in a supplier's queue. That is what turns a components business into a scarcity business, and it is visible in the numbers: gross margin expanded in eight of the past nine quarters, +660 basis points cumulatively, reaching 40.2% non-GAAP in the June quarter (+215bps YoY) against a stated target of above 42%. The six-inch wafer transition compounds it — four times the die per wafer at roughly half the cost — and the moat is being reinforced with cash: $1,102.9M of capital expenditure in fiscal 2026, including $556M in the June quarter alone, against $131M in the year-ago quarter. Management claims roughly an 18-month payback on datacenter investment. Around that sits demand-side lock-in that is rare in components: fiscal 2027 effectively sold out, orders booked into calendar 2028, and long-term agreements to the end of the decade with minimum take-or-pay volumes.

Key Vulnerabilities

●
Customer concentration among a handful of buyers. The entire Datacenter & Communications segment sells into a short list of hyperscalers and networking OEMs. Coherent did not disclose customer-concentration percentages in the release fetched, and no verified figure is stated here — but the structure is not in doubt: a business whose fiscal year is "fully booked" is fully booked by a small number of counterparties, each of whom has the scale to dual-source, to renegotiate, or to design the pluggable module out entirely. Concentration cuts both ways, and right now it is cutting favourably.
●
Chinese module makers are the real share threat. Innolight and Eoptolink compete directly in merchant 800G and 1.6T transceivers and have historically won on price and speed of qualification. Coherent's defence is the laser chip, not the module; if InP supply loosens industry-wide — which is precisely what everyone's capacity expansion is designed to achieve — the module layer commoditises and the premium compresses. No verified share or revenue figures for either competitor were obtained, so this is a structural argument, not a quantified one.
●
Co-packaged optics could disintermediate the pluggable. CPO moves the optics onto the switch package, which is Broadcom's home turf. Coherent is playing both sides — it guides first CPO revenue in the December 2026 quarter and cites a >$15B incremental opportunity — but a transition that shifts value from module assembly toward switch silicon is not obviously good for a module vendor, however good its lasers are.
●
The balance sheet still carries the merger. Total debt of $3,222.2M against $1,987.0M of cash and short-term investments. Leverage has fallen hard — to roughly 0.6–0.7x net from about 2x — but that improvement was driven by EBITDA growth as much as by repayment, which means it is levered to the AI cycle in both directions. ROE of 7.98% and ROIC of 6.74% are the residue of a large merger goodwill and asset base that the datacenter business is only now beginning to earn a return on.
●
Half the company is cyclical and shrinking. Industrial fell 15.8% in the June quarter. It is 26% of revenue and provides essentially none of the growth. If the AI transceiver cycle pauses, there is no second engine — there is a slower-growing industrial business in a manufacturing downturn.

Notable One-Off Items

The cash flow is the one-off — and it may not be one. Fiscal 2026 turned $769.9M of net income into $79.5M of operating cash flow. That $690M gap is a working-capital build: inventory and receivables staged for a fiscal 2027 that is already sold out. Subtract $1,102.9M of capital expenditure — up from a run rate near $500M — and free cash flow is −$1,023M, negative in every one of the four quarters, and worst in the June quarter (−$486M on $556M of capex). A shareholder should be clear-eyed about what this means: the accounting profit is real, and the company consumed a billion dollars of cash producing it. That is defensible if the 18-month payback claim holds and the take-or-pay contracts are honoured. It is not defensible if 2027 demand disappoints, because the capacity will already be built and paid for.

Divestitures completed in FY2026. The aerospace-and-defence business and the Munich tools business were both sold during the fiscal year — verified from the August 12, 2026 release. Their absence is why reported Q4 growth of 34% becomes 42% on a pro-forma basis, and it flatters the mix shift toward datacenter while depressing reported Industrial revenue. Any other announced-but-unclosed disposals were not verified from the sources fetched and are not listed.

GAAP versus non-GAAP. FY2026 GAAP EPS $4.12 against non-GAAP $5.61 — a 36% gap, driven by merger-related intangible amortisation, share-based compensation and restructuring from the site rationalisation. The gap is narrowing as the II-VI/Coherent intangibles run off, but at 68× GAAP and 50× non-GAAP the choice of denominator moves the valuation by a third.

Prior-year context. FY2025 and FY2024 were GAAP losses (−$80.6M and −$279.5M). FY2023 lost $403.7M on the merger. The trailing multiple therefore has no meaningful multi-year history to anchor to — this is the first clean year.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the segment split, because it is the whole argument. In the June 2026 quarter, Datacenter & Communications did $1,615.0M and grew 58.6%; Industrial did $430.5M and shrank 15.8%. Two businesses, opposite signs, in one reporting entity. The consolidated 33.7% growth rate that appears in the headline is the arithmetic average of a hypergrowth optics company and a shrinking industrial cyclical, and valuing the blend at a single multiple is how this stock gets mispriced in both directions. On a sum-of-the-parts basis, the honest question is what an investor is paying for the 74% of revenue that is AI optics, and what the other 26% is worth — or costs — sitting alongside it.

The vertical-integration claim, and what actually supports it. The bull case is not "Coherent sells transceivers." Everyone sells transceivers. It is that Coherent grows its own indium phosphide and cuts its own laser die, so the scarce input in an AI transceiver sits inside its own fabs. The supporting evidence is specific and dated: InP production up ~80% YoY in the June quarter; internal output on track to double by the end of the September quarter, one quarter early; a plan to more than double again by the end of calendar 2027; six-inch wafers delivering 4× the output of three-inch at ~50% of the cost; a third six-inch site in Zurich in H1 calendar 2027. And the result shows up where it should — in gross margin, which has expanded in eight of nine quarters for a cumulative +660bps, hitting 40.2% non-GAAP in Q4 against a >42% target. This is not a narrative; it is a manufacturing programme with quarterly checkpoints.

What it costs. Fiscal 2026 spent $1,102.9M of capex and generated $79.5M of operating cash flow, for free cash flow of −$1,023M. The June quarter alone burned $486M. Capex is guided higher again in the September quarter. Management's defence is an 18-month payback on datacenter investment and a demand book that is fully sold for fiscal 2027, extends into calendar 2028, and includes long-term agreements to the end of the decade with minimum take-or-pay volumes. If those contracts are what they are described as, the cash burn is the correct decision and free cash flow inflects violently once the capex cycle rolls over. If demand normalises first, Coherent will own a great deal of indium phosphide capacity at exactly the moment the industry no longer needs it — and the whole scarcity premium in the gross margin goes with it.

The balance sheet is no longer the problem it was. Total debt of $3,222.2M against $1,987.0M of cash and short-term investments, debt-to-equity of 0.32, and net leverage of roughly 0.6–0.7x, down from about 2x. That is a genuine improvement on the merger-era balance sheet. But read how it was achieved: leverage is a ratio, and the denominator — EBITDA — grew far faster than the numerator shrank. Coherent deleveraged mostly by earning more, not by paying down much. A cyclical downturn would push that ratio back up without a single dollar of new borrowing. Meanwhile ROIC of 6.74% and ROE of 7.98% say the merged asset base still does not earn its cost of capital — the datacenter business is carrying an industrial company's balance sheet.

Valuation — and which earnings base. On trailing GAAP EPS of $4.12, COHR trades at 68.4×. On non-GAAP EPS of $5.61, at 50.2×. On FY2027 consensus EPS of $9.42, at 29.9× — and consensus FY2027 revenue of $10.62B implies 49% growth on top of fiscal 2026's 22.5%, which is not a conservative estimate; it is a bet that the sold-out order book converts cleanly. The PEG of 0.57 is the bulls' single best number, and it is only 0.57 because the growth denominator is that large. On free cash flow there is no multiple, because free cash flow is negative. The defensible read: at 50× non-GAAP trailing earnings with negative free cash flow, this is not cheap on anything you can currently measure — it is cheap only against a fiscal 2027 that management says is already booked. That is a more concrete foundation than most AI-adjacent stocks offer, and it is still a forecast.

Bull Case

Coherent owns the scarce input — indium phosphide laser die — in the fastest-growing part of AI infrastructure, and is doubling that capacity a quarter ahead of schedule while cutting unit cost roughly in half via six-inch wafers. Datacenter & Communications grew 58.6% to 79% of quarterly revenue, gross margin has expanded in eight of nine quarters toward a >42% target, fiscal 2027 is effectively sold out with take-or-pay agreements running to the end of the decade, and consensus has revenue reaching $10.62B and EPS $9.42 next year — a 29.9× forward multiple on a 0.57 PEG. Leverage is down to ~0.6x, the industrial drag is being actively divested, and CPO adds a company-cited $15B+ of incremental market from December.

Bear Case

The company burned $1.02B of free cash flow in its "best ever" year — $1.10B of capex against $79.5M of operating cash flow — and is guiding capex higher still, into a demand book from a handful of hyperscalers that can be renegotiated faster than a fab can be repurposed. ROIC is 6.74%, below any plausible cost of capital. A quarter of revenue is a shrinking industrial cyclical. Chinese module makers compete on the assembly layer that Coherent's own capacity expansion is helping to de-bottleneck, and Broadcom's co-packaged optics could move value onto the switch package entirely. With a beta of 2.10, the stock fell 33% in July on Alphabet's capex disclosure alone, without a single company-specific data point — which is a fair description of what an investor is actually holding.

Top 3 Risks

01
The capex bet is unhedged. $1.1B spent in FY2026 and rising, against an 18-month payback assumption and a customer list short enough to fit on one line. Take-or-pay minimums provide real protection, but they were not quantified in the sources fetched, and a hyperscaler that wants to renegotiate generally can. If AI transceiver demand plateaus in calendar 2027, Coherent has already spent the money.
02
The scarcity premium is self-liquidating. Coherent's gross margin expansion is substantially a function of tight indium phosphide supply. Coherent is doubling its InP output, then more than doubling it again, and every competitor is doing the same. The programme that creates the margin today is the programme that erodes it in 2028 — unless demand grows faster than the industry's combined capacity additions, which is precisely the assumption embedded in a 68× trailing multiple.
03
It trades on someone else's cash flow statement. July 28, 2026: −11% in a day, −33% for the month, on Alphabet's capex disclosure. Beta 2.10, short interest rising 10.9% month over month. With no dividend, negative free cash flow and 79% of quarterly revenue tied to hyperscale AI buildout, there is nothing in this security to cushion a sentiment reversal in AI capital spending — and the stock is still 36% below a peak set eleven weeks ago.
  Industry Commentary
Indium Phosphide Is the Sector's Real Bottleneck
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every 800G and 1.6T optical module needs laser die, and those die come from indium phosphide wafers that take years of process development to yield well. This — not module assembly, which is competitive and increasingly Chinese — is where the AI optics supply chain is actually tight, and it is the reason a components company can post 40% gross margins. Coherent grew InP production about 80% year over year in the June quarter and is doubling internal output a quarter ahead of plan, with six-inch wafers delivering four times the die at half the cost of three-inch. The constraint is real today. The industry's collective response to it is the single largest medium-term risk to the margins it currently supports.
Hyperscale Capex Is Now the Whole Cycle
ONGOING HEADWIND
On July 28, 2026, Alphabet reported $44.92B of quarterly capital expenditure, negative free cash flow of −$5.86B and a 2026 capex plan of $195–205B. Optical component stocks fell in unison — Coherent −11%, Applied Optoelectronics −10%, Lumentum −9%, against −4% for the semiconductor index. Two things follow. First, the demand is genuinely enormous and genuinely funded. Second, it is concentrated in a handful of buyers whose own investors are beginning to ask whether the spending converts into returns. Coherent's fiscal 2027 order book is only as good as those budgets, and the market now reprices the entire optical complex on any hyperscaler's cash flow statement, regardless of what the component makers themselves report.
Co-Packaged Optics: New Market or Substitution?
EMERGING TAILWIND
CPO moves the optical engine from a pluggable module onto the switch package itself, cutting power per bit at the cost of serviceability. Coherent guides first CPO revenue to the December 2026 quarter, scale-up applications to 2H calendar 2027, and cites more than $15B of incremental addressable market across CPO, near-packaged and integrated optics — a company estimate, not independently verified. Bull framing: whoever supplies the ultra-high-power continuous-wave lasers that CPO requires captures value regardless of packaging format, and Coherent has a Texas facility already shipping production wafers for it — the laser franchise survives the transition intact. Bear framing: CPO's centre of gravity is switch silicon, which is Broadcom's, and a world where the module disappears into the ASIC package is a world with fewer independent module vendors. Coherent is positioned for both outcomes; only one of them is worth 68× earnings.
