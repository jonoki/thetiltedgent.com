01 Company Overview

Cooper is two companies wearing one ticker. CooperVision makes soft contact lenses, including torics for astigmatism and multifocals. CooperSurgical sells fertility products and services, cryostorage, and office and surgical devices for women's health. The fiscal year ends October 31. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through July 2026 was $4.24B, up 4.73%, with a 65.9% gross margin. At the September 21, 2026 close of $54.12 the equity is worth about $10.29B (our calculation: $54.12 × 190.16M shares).

The story of the last twelve months is a table that thought it was holding a draw. On December 4, 2025, after the close and alongside fourth-quarter results, and under pressure from activists JANA Partners and Browning West, the board opened a formal strategic review; MarketScreener reported an 8% gain in the shares on the news. The market's working assumption was that something would be sold or split. On September 9, 2026 the river came: the board kept CooperSurgical, fiscal 2026 revenue guidance was cut to $4.229–$4.252B from $4.285–$4.321B, and adjusted EPS guidance fell to $4.51–$4.55 from $4.58–$4.66. The stock fell 6.22% on September 9 — before the 16:17 ET release — and another 14.67% on September 10, a two-day move from $67.69 to $54.17. Nine days later JANA published a letter demanding an external CEO search. The central question is now simple to state: is the CooperVision slowdown a one-off U.S. distributor destocking, as management says, or the first sign that the lens franchise has stopped taking share?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Repeat-purchase lens habit Practitioner-fitted prescriptions Toric & multifocal specialty mix Fertility lab consumables footprint 65.9% gross margin

Contact lenses are one of healthcare's better subscription businesses without a subscription. A wearer is fitted by a practitioner, gets a prescription for a specific brand and parameters, and reorders the same lens for years. Switching needs a new fitting. Specialty lenses (torics, multifocals) are harder to fit, carry better pricing, and made up just over half of CooperVision's Q3 revenue. That habit is what a 65.9% gross margin rests on. The weakness is that the habit protects the wearer, not the shipment. Revenue is recognised when distributors buy, and when distributors decide to hold less stock, reported growth can go to zero while nobody's eyes change.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Credibility is now a line item. Revenue guidance has been lowered twice in a row. JANA's letter cites "negative surprises, expectation mismanagement and poor capital allocation." The market now discounts whatever management says the next number will be, and that is the cost of the channel-inventory explanation even if it proves correct.
02
Buybacks at the wrong price. In Q3 FY2026 Cooper repurchased 4.9M shares at an average $69.16, about 28% above the $54.12 close, weeks before the guidance cut. JANA names repurchases ahead of disappointing announcements explicitly. The authorisation has since been raised from $2B to $3B, with about $1.5B remaining.
03
CooperVision regional softness beyond the U.S. The distributor story explains the Americas (−2% organic). It does not explain Asia Pacific at −5% organic. EMEA at +5% is holding the division flat.
04
Recall litigation tail. The Q2 FY2026 charge of $271.6M (net of $52.5M expected insurance recoveries) resolved claims tied to the December 2023 embryo culture media recall. The amount is reserved, but a fertility franchise carrying recall history is harder to sell at a premium. The board's decision to keep CooperSurgical may partly reflect that.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

On a split-adjusted basis, COO went from a September 2021 month-end close of $103.33 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $54.12, a 47.6% price decline over five years. The company paid no dividend to offset it. The September 21 close is the lowest point in the 61-point series. The path had two halves. Through September 2024 the stock was range-bound, with a 2022 low of $65.97 (September 2022) and a five-year month-end high of $110.34 in September 2024. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 11.9x current-year adjusted earnings and a ~6.6% trailing free-cash-flow yield for a business with a 65.9% gross margin, record quarterly FCF of $273.0M and a reaffirmed target of more than $2.2B of FCF over FY2026–2028. If management is right that the CooperVision cut is purely distributor destocking against mid-single-digit patient demand, the revenue shortfall is a timing gap that reverses. The $1.5B remaining buyback then retires shares at $54 instead of $69. There are also two activists on the register, and one of them is now asking for a new CEO and a sale of the fertility assets. Even the lowest analyst target, $58, is above the price.

Bear Case

Revenue guidance has been cut twice in a row, the deal premium is gone, and the explanation for the miss is the one that is hardest to verify from outside. Asia Pacific CooperVision at −5% organic is not a U.S. distributor problem. ROIC of 5.03% says the capital in the business is not earning its cost. Management bought $339.1M of stock at an average $69.16 weeks before a 20% drop, which is the capital-allocation record the next buyback will be judged against. The trailing P/E of 18.7 rests on a tax benefit and a litigation charge, not a clean earnings base. And a board that just refused to sell CooperSurgical is now being asked by an activist to do exactly that.

Top 3 Risks

01
CooperVision growth does not come back. The division went from +4% organic in Q2 to flat in Q3, with guidance of 1–2% organic for the year. If Q4 does not show the U.S. channel normalising, the market will treat the slowdown as share loss, not stocking.
02
Governance fight distracts the business. JANA wants a new CEO and chair; Browning West already has a cooperation agreement. A drawn-out public fight can freeze decisions just as the lens business needs execution.
03
CooperSurgical's value stays trapped. The board said the offers it received were not good enough. If fertility growth (+5% organic) slows, or recall-litigation history keeps weighing on buyer interest, the sum-of-the-parts case weakens with no transaction to crystallise it.
Industry Commentary
Channel inventory vs. patient demand in contact lenses
KEY DEBATE

Contact-lens companies sell to distributors and practitioners, not directly to the wearer. Reported revenue can therefore diverge from what patients actually buy for a quarter or two. Management says U.S. consumption grew mid-single digits while shipments fell because distributor inventory was pulled down deliberately (Q3 call, per the Motley Fool transcript). If that is right, it is a one-off: once distributors reach their new target stock level, shipments track consumption again. The market's 20% two-day reaction says investors did not give that explanation full credit, partly because EMEA (+5%) and Asia Pacific (−5%) moved in opposite directions in the same quarter.

Activism and the conglomerate discount
POTENTIAL TAILWIND

The activists' thesis is that a lens franchise and a fertility/women's-health device business are worth more apart than together. JANA earlier floated combining CooperVision with Bausch + Lomb, and Browning West pushed for alternatives for CooperSurgical (per Investing.com, November 2025). The strategic review tested that thesis with real bidders and the board declined. The bull framing: the review showed there is buyer interest, and JANA now argues the fertility and device assets would earn premium valuations under different ownership. The bear framing: the offers were apparently not high enough, and a process that has already run once rarely produces a better price the second time.

Quality of earnings in a noisy year
READ THE ADJUSTMENTS

FY2026 GAAP earnings contain a $271.6M litigation charge and a $307.2M tax benefit, two items the size of a normal quarter's net income. Neither says anything about how many lenses Cooper sold. The numbers that do are organic growth (2–3% guided for the year), non-GAAP gross margin (67% in Q3, down 60bps) and free cash flow ($678M TTM). On those three, Cooper is a slower-growing but still very profitable business. The dispute is over what multiple slower growth deserves.
