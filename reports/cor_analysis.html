01 Company Overview

Start with the margin, not the revenue, or you will misread this company entirely. Cencora's TTM gross margin is 3.96% — 396 basis points, and its operating margin is 1.35%. Net margin is 0.79%. Against that, TTM revenue of $332.77B (+5.1%) is one of the largest revenue lines in American business — but it buys a market capitalisation of only $63.15B, or 0.19× sales. Cencora is the second-largest of the three companies that move essentially every prescription drug in the United States, roughly #436 globally by market value, and it does about $6.5M of revenue per employee — a number that tells you this is a logistics utility, not a manufacturer.

The central debate on this page is therefore not "will revenue grow." Revenue growth is the least informative number Cencora publishes. A distributor earns a small negotiated spread plus fee-for-service compensation on branded drugs, and a materially better margin on generics. So the earnings engine is generic mix, generic sourcing economics and manufacturer list-price increases — not volume. The clearest demonstration sits inside the June 2026 quarter: GLP-1 sales rose about $2.3B year over year, more than half of the entire $4.09B increase in quarterly revenue, and management explicitly describes those products as carrying lower gross profit margins. Revenue went up; the economics of that revenue went sideways. Everything else — customer concentration, the Walgreens relationship, opioid liabilities, the move into specialty and physician practice management — is a question of whether Cencora can add margin dollars faster than the mix erodes them.

HOW A DISTRIBUTOR ACTUALLY MAKES MONEY

Cencora does not buy drugs low and sell them high in any meaningful sense. On a branded drug it takes title, warehouses it, and delivers it, earning a negotiated discount off the manufacturer's list price plus explicit fee-for-service compensation — distribution service agreements that pay a fixed percentage of list for handling, data and inventory services. Crucially, a portion of that compensation is tied to the drug's price, so when manufacturers raise list prices, distributor gross profit rises on the same physical volume. This is the single most under-appreciated driver of the sector's earnings, and it is entirely outside Cencora's control.

On a generic drug the economics invert. There is no list price to index to, but there are dozens of interchangeable manufacturers competing for shelf space, so the distributor's scale purchasing power translates into a real buying advantage and a materially wider percentage margin. Generics are a small fraction of revenue dollars and a large fraction of gross profit dollars. The offsetting force is generic deflation — prices in the category grind downward year after year, shrinking the base the margin is earned on.

Put those together and you get the rule that governs this stock: generic mix and manufacturer price increases drive earnings; revenue growth barely does. A quarter in which volumes surge on low-margin branded specialty products — which is exactly what GLP-1 growth is — will show excellent revenue and mediocre profit. A quarter with strong generic launches and healthy branded price inflation will show unremarkable revenue and excellent profit. The June 2026 quarter delivered 4.9% U.S. revenue growth and 15.9% U.S. operating income growth, and that spread, not the 4.9%, is the number to underwrite.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
THREE-PLAYER OLIGOPOLY SCALE PURCHASING NEGATIVE WORKING CAPITAL GENERIC SOURCING JV COLD-CHAIN & SPECIALTY DEA/STATE LICENSING SWITCHING COSTS

The moat is structural, not clever. McKesson, Cencora and Cardinal Health together account for over 90% of U.S. pharmaceutical distribution revenue — roughly $903B between them in fiscal 2025. Nobody enters this market: it requires a national network of temperature-controlled distribution centres, DEA and 50-state licensing, controlled-substance monitoring systems, and the willingness to run a business at a 1% operating margin. The economics only work at scale, and scale here is a purchasing weapon — the larger buyer sources generics cheaper, and generics are where the margin lives. Cencora also participates in a global generic purchasing arrangement with Walgreens Boots Alliance, which enlarges the buying pool beyond its own volumes.

The second, less-discussed advantage is the balance sheet mechanics. Distributors collect from customers faster than they pay manufacturers, which means the business runs on negative working capital — growth funds itself. That is how a company with just $3.24B of stockholders' equity (June 30, 2026) supports a $332.77B revenue base and generates $4.06B of TTM free cash flow. It is also why the ROE reads at an absurd 96.87% and the debt-to-equity at 4.47 — the equity base has been shrunk by decades of buybacks, so both ratios are artefacts of capital structure rather than statements about profitability or risk. ROIC of 20.95% is the honest number, and it is genuinely good.

Three companies, one business model, and a striking amount of dispersion in a year. Cardinal Health has re-rated hardest, McKesson has compounded steadily, and Cencora has gone backwards in market value while raising guidance twice. The bull argument for COR is essentially a relative-value argument: same oligopoly, same structural economics, better return on invested capital than the sector's reputation implies, and the lowest forward multiple. The bear argument is that the discount is deserved because Cencora carries the most concentrated customer book of the three — Walgreens at roughly a quarter of revenue — and the market is pricing 2029 rather than 2026. Note also that McKesson disclosed a cybersecurity incident on August 25, 2026, a reminder that the sector's operational risk is not limited to drug pricing.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer concentration is extreme, and it is the single most important fact about this company. In the most recent figures verified here (fiscal 2023 Form 10-K), Walgreens Boots Alliance was approximately 26% of revenue, Express Scripts approximately 14%, and the top ten customers approximately 66%. Two customers were therefore about 40% of the top line. The U.S. distribution agreement with Walgreens runs to 2029 and the Boots UK arrangement to 2031. Losing or repricing either relationship would not dent this business — it would reshape it. Comparable percentages for fiscal 2025 could not be verified from the sources fetched and are not estimated here.
▸
Walgreens' own upheaval flows straight through. WBA has spent years shrinking its store base, cutting its dividend and restructuring, and it reduced its Cencora stake from roughly 30% to below 5% by mid-2025. The strategic alignment that once made this the most stable customer relationship in the industry is gone; what remains is a commercial contract with an expiry date and a counterparty under financial pressure. A weakened Walgreens negotiates harder, and closes stores that used to order.
▸
Earnings depend on prices Cencora does not control. Branded fee-for-service compensation is partly indexed to manufacturer list-price increases; generic margins erode with ongoing generic deflation. Management named roughly $2.4B of manufacturer list-price reductions as a revenue headwind in the June 2026 quarter alone. A policy environment that suppresses U.S. drug list prices — the explicit goal of most current reform proposals — compresses distributor gross profit as a direct mechanical consequence, not as a second-order effect.
▸
Disintermediation risk in exactly the fastest-growing category. GLP-1 products are driving the majority of Cencora's revenue growth while carrying below-average gross margins. Manufacturers of those same drugs have been building direct-to-patient channels. If the highest-volume, lowest-margin category is also the one most likely to move outside the traditional three-step channel, the revenue line and the profit line could decouple in the unhelpful direction.
▸
Opioid liabilities are still real cash. The three distributors collectively committed to approximately $21B over 18 years under the 2022 national settlement, and those payments continue to run through operating cash flow. Cencora's specific annual instalment was not verified from the sources fetched and is not stated here. The direction of travel has recently been favourable — the nine months to June 30, 2026 included a $102.0M reduction of the opioid liability on dismissal of litigation — but the liability is a multi-year cash drag on a business whose entire equity base is $3.24B.

Notable One-Off Items

There are several, they are large relative to a $2.62B net income line, and they run in both directions. Anyone valuing this stock on the trailing $13.46 GAAP EPS is valuing a number that will not repeat.

+$1.1B gain on remeasurement of an equity-method investment (quarter ended March 31, 2026). When Cencora acquired the remaining stake in OneOncology in February 2026, accounting rules required it to mark its existing minority holding to fair value and book the difference as a gain. This is why GAAP EPS was $8.40 in that single quarter against $3.68 a year earlier. It is entirely non-cash and entirely non-recurring.

−$723.9M goodwill impairment on PharmaLex (quarter ended September 30, 2025), which produced a GAAP net loss of $339.7M (−$1.75 per share) in a quarter with $944.6M of operating income. This followed a $418.0M charge in the prior-year quarter on the same asset. That is roughly $1.14B written off a services acquisition across two years.

−$249.5M impairment on the U.S. Consulting Services business, divested in April 2026. Together with PharmaLex, this is the accounting record of a strategy that has been abandoned.

+$102.0M reduction of opioid liability in the nine months to June 30, 2026, on dismissal of opioid litigation. Favourable, but it is a legal-reserve release, not operating profit.

Net effect: GAAP diluted EPS for the nine months to June 2026 was $15.21, up 56.8%. Adjusted diluted EPS over the same period was $13.31, up 9.5%. The adjusted number is the one that describes the business. The 9.5% is the real growth rate.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $122.02 to the September 4, 2026 close of $330.95, COR returned +171.2% in price alone — a compound annual rate of about 22.1% before dividends. For a company with a 0.79% net margin and a beta of 0.57, that is a remarkable outcome, and it happened without a single dramatic re-rating story. The stock simply compounded: earnings grew, the share count shrank through relentless buybacks, and the multiple drifted from the low teens toward the high teens as the market accepted that GLP-1 volumes and specialty distribution had structurally lifted the growth rate. Note also the ticker: this history is AmerisourceBergen (ABC) through August 2023 and Cencora (COR) thereafter — the rename in August 2023 sits at roughly $176 on this chart and had no discernible effect on the price. Any screen that starts the series at the COR ticker will show you a three-year history and miss the first 44% of the move.

The path was not a straight line. The stock ran from $122.02 to a monthly-close high of $372.14 in February 2026, then fell 27.6% to $269.36 in May 2026, then recovered 22.9% into the current price. The February-to-May drawdown is the interesting passage: it coincided with the OneOncology buy-in closing and with drug-pricing policy noise, and it is the only period in five years when the market appeared to seriously reprice the durability of distributor margins. It did not last.

Relative performance. A precise five-year total-return comparison against the S&P 500 (the benchmark for this NYSE listing) or the NASDAQ-100 could not be verified from the sources fetched and is therefore not stated. What can be said from verified data: over the trailing twelve months COR's price is +12.13%, and over calendar 2026 to date its market capitalisation is −10.4% — against +23.2% for McKesson and +59.9% for Cardinal Health on the same measure and the same date. Cencora is the laggard of the oligopoly in 2026, which is precisely why it now carries the lowest forward multiple of the three (17.13× against 19.51× and 19.67×). The five-year record says this is the best-compounding of the three; the twelve-month record says it is currently the least loved.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A three-company oligopoly with >90% share of a non-cyclical, volume-growing market, generating 20.95% ROIC and $4.06B of free cash flow on almost no invested capital, trading at 17.13× forward earnings — a 13% discount to both McKesson and Cardinal Health. The mix is improving where it counts: the MSO segment earns 4.29% operating margins against 1.16% in distribution, and both platforms are running ahead of plan. Segment operating income grew 15.9% (U.S.) and 20.8% (International) in the June quarter on 5% revenue growth, which is the definition of operating leverage in a spread business. Opioid liabilities are being released rather than added to, buybacks are absorbing $1B a quarter at prices near the lows, and the stock is the 2026 laggard of a sector that has worked.

Bear Case

You are buying 79 basis points of net margin with 40% of revenue concentrated in two customers, one of which — Walgreens, ~26% of revenue as last verified — has been restructuring for years and has sold down its equity stake to below 5%, with the distribution contract expiring in 2029. Earnings depend on manufacturer list-price increases and generic sourcing spreads, neither of which Cencora sets, in a policy environment explicitly aimed at lowering U.S. drug prices; management already flagged $2.4B of list-price reductions in one quarter. The growth engine, GLP-1, is the lowest-margin revenue in the book and the category most exposed to manufacturer-direct channels. Equity is $3.24B against $14.46B of debt and multi-year opioid instalments. And the trailing $13.46 EPS everyone quotes contains a $1.1B non-cash gain that will not repeat.

Top 3 Risks

01
Walgreens contract renewal and Walgreens' own condition. Roughly a quarter of revenue sits with a customer in structural decline, under a contract expiring in 2029. Any renegotiation on worse terms compresses gross profit on a base where a 10 basis point move is worth ~$333M — about 13% of net income.
02
Drug-pricing policy compressing the spread. Distributor gross profit is mechanically linked to branded list-price inflation and to generic sourcing economics. Reform aimed at lowering U.S. list prices reduces the fee base directly, and there is no volume response that offsets it — you cannot sell more units to compensate for a smaller percentage of a smaller number.
03
Channel disintermediation in GLP-1 and specialty. The categories driving growth are the ones manufacturers have the strongest incentive and capability to route directly to patients and providers. Cencora's answer is to own the provider (the MSO strategy), which is sound — but it is early, small, and now carries policy risk of its own through physician reimbursement.
  Industry Commentary
The Oligopoly Is the Industry
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
McKesson, Cencora and Cardinal Health booked roughly $903B of revenue between them in fiscal 2025 and control over 90% of U.S. pharmaceutical distribution revenue. This is not a market with competitive dynamics in the usual sense — it is a regulated-utility structure in private hands. The consequence cuts both ways: no new entrant can threaten the position, and no participant can raise price, because the customers on the other side of the table (WBA, Express Scripts, CVS, OptumRx) are themselves an oligopoly of comparable concentration. Margins sit at 1% because two cartels are negotiating with each other and neither can win. That equilibrium has held for two decades and is the deepest reason to underwrite these businesses — and the reason none of them will ever earn a software multiple.
GLP-1: Enormous Revenue, Negligible Margin
ONGOING HEADWIND
The GLP-1 class is the clearest illustration of the distributor model that has ever appeared in these numbers. In the June 2026 quarter GLP-1 sales rose about $2.3B year over year — more than half of Cencora's entire $4.09B revenue increase — and the company states plainly that these products carry lower gross profit margins. So the headline revenue number accelerates while the earnings quality of that revenue declines. Any investor watching the top line here is watching the wrong variable. The same dynamic is playing out at McKesson (+8.8% revenue) and Cardinal (+14.2%), both of which are growing revenue faster than Cencora and neither of which is converting it at a meaningfully better rate.
Vertical Integration Into the Provider
EMERGING TAILWIND
All three distributors have reached the same conclusion: if you cannot widen the spread, buy the customer. Cencora owns OneOncology and Retina Consultants of America; McKesson has the US Oncology Network and bought Precision Medicine Group for $2.25B in 2026. The prize is the buy-and-bill channel — as CEO Bob Mauch put it, "Part B will always be good." Bull framing: the MSO segment already earns 4.29% operating margins versus 1.16% in U.S. distribution, and every point of mix shift toward it is worth several times the equivalent revenue in distribution; if this scales, the consolidated gross margin genuinely re-rates rather than merely re-classifies. Bear framing: Cencora's last diversification away from pure distribution — PharmaLex and U.S. Consulting Services — produced roughly $1.4B of impairments across three years and ended in divestiture. Owning physician practices exchanges customer-concentration risk for reimbursement-policy risk, and the accounting benefit (consolidating high-margin service revenue) arrives years before the strategic benefit can be verified.
