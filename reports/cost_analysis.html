Section 01

Company Overview

Costco operates roughly 930 membership-only warehouse clubs across 15 countries, selling a deliberately narrow assortment — about 3,800 SKUs versus 30,000+ at a typical supermarket — at gross margins capped near 11% on merchandise. Fiscal 2025 (ended August 31, 2025) revenue was $275.24B, up 8.2% YoY, and the trailing twelve months through May 2026 reached $293.59B, up 9.2%. It is the third-largest retailer on earth by revenue behind Walmart and Amazon, the largest warehouse club operator globally with roughly 62% of the U.S. club channel, and the #2 gasoline retailer in the United States by volume.

The economics are unusual and worth stating plainly: Costco makes almost no money selling merchandise. In FY2025, membership fee income of $5.32B was roughly 51% of operating income of $10.38B. Merchandise is effectively sold at cost-plus-a-sliver to drive renewals; the profit line is a subscription business wearing a retailer's clothes. Renewal rates run about 92.3% in the U.S. and Canada and ~90% worldwide, and the September 2024 fee increase — the first in seven years, to $65 Gold Star and $130 Executive — is still flowing through deferred revenue into reported fee income, which is up to $5.78B on a trailing basis.

Section 03

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

Scale Purchasing Power Membership Lock-In Brand Trust / Price Credibility Kirkland Signature Private Label Structurally Low Cost Base Irreplaceable Real Estate Employee Retention & Culture

Moat description. Costco's advantage is a self-reinforcing cost loop that is easy to describe and extremely hard to copy. It carries roughly 3,800 SKUs versus 30,000–40,000 at a supermarket, which concentrates enormous volume behind single items and gives it supplier leverage no conventional grocer has. It caps merchandise markups at ~14% on branded goods and ~15% on Kirkland by internal policy — a self-imposed ceiling that makes price undercutting structurally impossible for a competitor earning a normal retail margin. It runs SG&A near 9% of sales, roughly half of Target's, because warehouses are unmerchandised concrete boxes with pallets on the floor and no advertising budget. The savings are handed back as lower prices, which drives volume, which deepens supplier leverage, which lowers cost again. The membership fee sits on top of that loop and converts it into profit: members who pay $65–$130 up front are psychologically committed to shopping the store to "earn back" the fee, which is why renewal runs above 92% and why the average member visits more than twice a month. Layer on 40+ years of accumulated owned real estate in high-density suburban corridors and an employee base paid well above retail scale (average U.S. hourly wage north of $30, turnover under half the industry average), and the result is a business where the durable advantage is operational rather than technological — nothing here can be disrupted by a better algorithm.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Valuation is the dominant risk, not the business

At 47.9x trailing and 43.5x forward earnings against high-single-digit revenue growth and low-double-digit EPS growth, COST carries a PEG of 4.05 — a multiple typically reserved for high-margin software. The stock has already demonstrated what compression looks like: from the February 2025 all-time high near $1,078 it fell to $844 by late 2025 without any deterioration in the underlying business. Owning COST means underwriting the multiple as much as the company.

2. Sam's Club has stopped being a passive competitor

Walmart has invested aggressively in Sam's Club — remodels, Scan & Go checkout-free shopping, club openings after a decade of closures, and membership growth running comparable to or ahead of Costco's. Walmart's scale ($725B in TTM revenue versus Costco's $294B) means it can subsidise club pricing from a far larger P&L. Costco's club-channel share lead is real but is being contested for the first time in roughly fifteen years.

3. Tariffs and import cost inflation land squarely on non-foods

Roughly a third of Costco's U.S. sales are imported goods, concentrated in the fastest-growing and highest-margin non-foods category. Costco's stated posture is to absorb or re-source rather than pass through, which protects the price-trust brand but compresses merchandise gross margin. Management has flagged tariff mitigation — re-routing sourcing, pulling forward inventory, shifting to domestic suppliers — as an ongoing 2026 cost.

4. The membership fee lever is now spent for several years

The September 2024 increase provided a clean, high-margin earnings tailwind that has been flowing through deferred revenue for roughly two years. That benefit largely anniversaries during FY2027. Costco's historical cadence implies the next increase is unlikely before ~2029–2030, meaning membership income growth reverts to member-count growth alone — mid single digits — right as the market is capitalising it at 48x.

Notable One-Off Items

• September 2024 membership fee increase. Not a one-off in the accounting sense but a distortion worth isolating: because fee revenue is recognised ratably over the twelve-month term, the increase has been flowing into reported membership income across FY2025 and FY2026 and largely anniversaries out during FY2027. Membership income up 10.3% in FY2025 flatters the growth rate relative to underlying member-count growth of ~6%.
• $15.00 per share special dividend, declared December 2023, paid January 12, 2024 — roughly $6.7B of cash out the door. This distorts FY2024 payout-ratio and cash-balance comparisons badly (the FY2024 payout ratio screens at 123%). Costco has now paid five specials since 2012; the market periodically prices in another, and the current $18.9B cash balance keeps that speculation alive.
• Gasoline price deflation. Warehouse Ancillary revenue grew only 2.4% in FY2025 despite rising fuel volumes, purely because pump prices fell. This mechanically drags reported total revenue and comparable sales; management reports comps "excluding gas and FX" for exactly this reason, and that adjusted figure has been running several hundred basis points higher than the headline.
• Tariff mitigation costs. Ongoing through FY2026 — re-sourcing, inventory pull-forwards and absorbed duty on imported non-foods. Not disclosed as a discrete charge, but management has repeatedly cited it as a gross-margin headwind.
• No material write-downs, litigation charges, impairments or acquisition accounting. Costco does not acquire, does not restructure, and its reported GAAP earnings require essentially no adjustment. Among mega-caps this is genuinely unusual and is part of why the market pays up for reported earnings quality.

What the Five Years Actually Show

COST closed at roughly $452 in August 2021 and sits at $954.08 today — a price return of about +111%, or roughly 16.1% annualised, before the ~$25 per share of ordinary and special dividends paid over the period. That comfortably beats the S&P 500 over the same window and demolishes the consumer staples sector, which returned low single digits annually. But the shape matters more than the total: essentially all of that return was earned between October 2022 and February 2025. The stock has now spent eighteen months going sideways — the 50-day and 200-day moving averages are within fifty cents of each other at $957.37 and $957.90, and the 52-week price change is −0.16%. Earnings have grown roughly 25% over that period, which means the entire eighteen months has been multiple compression from about 60x to 48x, absorbed by fundamentals rather than expressed in the price. That is what a great business de-rating gently looks like.

Relative Performance

Over the full five years COST's ~16% annualised price return is roughly double the S&P 500's and multiples of the Consumer Staples Select Sector index, which has compounded in the low single digits — Costco has been, by a wide margin, the best-performing large-cap staple in the world. Against its direct comparison set the record is more mixed recently: Walmart's stock has materially outperformed COST over the past twenty-four months as its own re-rating (39x earnings for a 3.1%-margin retailer, on the strength of advertising, marketplace and membership income) played out later. Against the NASDAQ-100, COST has lagged badly since early 2025 — the index has been carried by artificial-intelligence capital spending while Costco has been de-rating. The trailing twelve-month picture is stark: COST −0.16% against a strongly positive index. Beta of 0.87 and a monthly RSI of ~49 describe a stock that is neither stretched nor washed out, sitting on flat moving averages and waiting for the September earnings print.

Section 08

Summary & Key Risks

BULL CASE

Costco is arguably the highest-quality retail business ever built: 38.4% ROIC with a net-cash balance sheet, a 92%+ renewing subscription generating half of operating profit, and a cost advantage that strengthens with every incremental dollar of volume. Revenue is compounding at 9% with a visible decade-long runway of ~30 new warehouses a year skewing international, where penetration is a fraction of the U.S. The stock has already absorbed eighteen months of multiple compression — from ~60x to 48x — while earnings grew 25%, so time is now working in the shareholder's favour rather than against it, with a possible sixth special dividend as free optionality.

BEAR CASE

You are paying 47.9x trailing and 43.5x forward earnings — a PEG of 4.05 — for a business that earns three cents on the sales dollar and grows earnings at low double digits. There is no scenario in which the operating results are good enough to justify that multiple expanding further, and every scenario in which a normal 30–35x multiple, still a premium to the market, implies a share price near $700. The one clean earnings lever — the membership fee — was pulled in September 2024 and will not be available again until roughly 2029. Meanwhile Sam's Club is competing seriously for the first time in fifteen years, and tariffs sit directly on the fastest-growing, highest-margin category.

Top 3 Risks

  1. Multiple compressionFar and away the dominant risk. A de-rate from 48x to a still-premium 35x forward earnings is a ~27% drawdown with zero deterioration in the business — and the market demonstrated exactly this between February 2025 and December 2025, taking the stock from $1,078 to $844 on rising earnings. Duration risk in a staples wrapper.
  2. Gross margin compression from tariffs and input costsAt a 3.01% net margin, a 40 basis point hit to merchandise gross margin is roughly a 13% hit to net income. Costco's cultural commitment to not passing costs to members is a brand asset and an earnings liability simultaneously; imported non-foods and beef inflation are the specific pressure points.
  3. Membership growth decelerationThe entire premium rests on the subscription line. U.S. and Canadian household penetration is already high, renewal rates cannot meaningfully improve from 92%+, and the next fee increase is years away. If paid-household growth slips from mid-single digits toward low single digits, the "it's really a subscription business" argument that supports 48x loses its force quickly.

Industry Commentary

Valuation vs. Structural Growth Ceiling

Critical constraint

Warehouse-club retail is a physically constrained growth model. Every incremental dollar of revenue requires a 150,000 square-foot building, a parking lot, and 200 employees; there is no software-style operating leverage waiting to be unlocked. Costco converts that constraint into extraordinary returns — 38.4% ROIC, 29.2% ROE, a net-cash balance sheet — but it cannot convert it into acceleration. Revenue growth has run between 5% and 9% in four of the last five years; EPS growth between 11% and 15%. Against that, the market is paying 47.9x trailing and 43.5x forward, a PEG of 4.05, and a premium to the NASDAQ-100's ~34x. The eighteen-month sideways period since February 2025 is precisely the market resolving this tension by holding the price flat while earnings grow into it. Another eighteen months of the same would bring the forward multiple to roughly 35x — which is likely the actual bull case for a patient holder.

Tariffs, Sourcing and the Cost of Keeping the Promise

Ongoing headwind

Roughly a third of Costco's U.S. sales are imported goods, and the exposure is concentrated in non-foods — the 25.7% of revenue growing fastest (+11.3% in FY2025) and carrying the best merchandise margin. Costco's response has been characteristic: re-route sourcing, pull inventory forward, negotiate with suppliers, and absorb what remains rather than raise member prices. That is the right long-run decision for a business whose entire moat is price credibility, and Costco's scale means it absorbs duty more cheaply than any regional competitor, which historically converts cost shocks into share gains. But the near-term arithmetic is unforgiving at a 3.01% net margin: roughly every 40 basis points of merchandise gross margin is 13% of net income. Watch merchandise gross margin ex-gas in the September quarter — it is the single cleanest read on how much of the tariff bill Costco is eating.

The Value-Seeking Consumer — and the Bull/Bear Frame

Emerging tailwind

The dominant consumer behaviour of this cycle is trade-down: households across income brackets consolidating trips, buying private label, and paying for memberships that pay for themselves. Costco is the purest expression of that trend — 92.3% U.S./Canada renewal rates, Kirkland Signature at roughly a third of sales, and share gains in fresh food and general merchandise from both grocers and department stores. Higher-income households, which are Costco's core demographic and the most resilient spending cohort, have been the strongest source of new member growth. The bull framing: this is a business that gains share when consumers are anxious and gains share when they are confident, growing 9% with a decade of warehouse openings ahead and a subscription line that renews at 92% — and the stock has already given back eighteen months of multiple. The bear framing: all of that is well understood and fully priced. At 43.5x forward earnings, an investor is not buying quality that the market has overlooked; they are buying quality the market has arguably over-celebrated, in a business whose one discretionary earnings lever — the membership fee — was pulled in 2024 and reloads no earlier than 2029. Both statements are true. The disagreement between the $740 bear target and the $1,315 bull target is entirely about which one gets priced.
