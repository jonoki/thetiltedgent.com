01 Company Overview

For most of its public life this company was called FLEETCOR and sold fuel cards to trucking fleets: a plastic card that lets a driver fill up anywhere while the fleet owner sees every gallon. On March 25, 2024 it became Corpay and started trading as CPAY on the NYSE — a name chosen for a portfolio that had drifted toward corporate payments. Same company, same share count history, new name. The S&P 500 membership carried over: FLEETCOR joined the index on June 20, 2018.

Today it is a toll booth on business spending. Corpay takes a small cut when fleets buy fuel and pay tolls, when companies wire money across borders or pay suppliers by virtual card, and when airlines and railroads house crews in hotels. Revenue over the last twelve months (TTM) is $5.23B, up 21.2%, on an 80.8% gross margin and a 43.9% operating margin. Q3 2026 revenue rose 18% to $1,381.9M (11% organic) and adjusted EPS rose 32% to $7.54; management raised its full-year forecast to $27.90–$28.10 adjusted EPS, from $27.15–$27.55. The debate is not whether the machine makes money. It is how much to pay for a company whose fuel-card business a federal court found charged hidden and unauthorised fees — the FTC matter Corpay agreed to settle for $100M on September 18, 2026, which still awaits final FTC approval — and whose growth increasingly comes from acquisitions in cross-border currency payments (FX) while it sells the businesses that no longer fit.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Closed-loop fuel-card networks Embedded in fleet & AP workflows 92% retention 80.8% gross margin Cross-border FX scale Serial acquirer / integrator

The moat is inertia, charged in tiny percentages. A fleet manager who has issued cards to 200 drivers, set fuel-type and location controls and wired the data into accounting does not switch to save a fraction of a cent per gallon. A finance team that pays foreign suppliers through one platform does not rebuild that plumbing for a slightly tighter FX spread. Small cuts of large, sticky flows produce the 44% operating margin. The weakness in that model is that fees nobody notices are fees nobody agreed to — which is exactly what the FTC litigated.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Fee practices on the record. The FTC says a federal district court found in 2023 that FleetCor charged hidden or unauthorised fees and misrepresented fuel-card savings, and that an appeals court upheld the judgment in 2026. Corpay agreed to pay $100M without admitting wrongdoing, subject to final FTC approval after public comment. The cash is small next to $1.33B of free cash flow; the question is whether fee practices elsewhere in the portfolio attract the same scrutiny.
02
Macro does part of the work. Management attributed roughly $30M of Q2's revenue upside to macro conditions — fuel prices and FX — and only about $15M to the underlying business. On Q3 Ron Clarke again named "a slightly better macro in the quarter" alongside the spend-management beat, and the fourth-quarter raise was attributed in part to favorable macro conditions. Revenue that rises with diesel and currency volatility falls with them too.
03
Leverage and acquisition dependence. $11.23B of debt, including the $2.30B securitization facility (borrowing backed by what customers owe it), against $3.07B of cash; debt-to-equity of 3.36 and a 2.72x leverage ratio at quarter-end. Corporate Payments' 41% reported growth against 18% organic shows how much of the story is bought. Buying growth works until an integration stumbles or rates stay high.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 16.3x FY2026 consensus adjusted EPS (14.3x on FY2027) you buy a business guiding to 31% adjusted EPS growth this year, with 11% organic revenue growth in the latest quarter, 92.2% retention and a 57% adjusted EBITDA margin. Corporate Payments grew 18% organically and is $8.5M a quarter short of being the largest segment. Management has sold two non-core businesses for about $1.2B and has more on the market, and it bought back 5.25M shares in the first nine months of 2026. Ron Clarke is outlining 9–11% organic growth again for 2027. The FTC case, a legal cloud since 2019, now has a $100M price tag, about 7.5% of one year's free cash flow. Every target change in the ratings table after the print was a raise.

Bear Case

A federal court found the fuel-card business charged customers fees they did not agree to, and an appeals court upheld it. Margins built on fees customers don't notice deserve a lower price, not a higher one. Reported growth of 18% is 11% organic, about a point of Corporate Payments' 18% was one-time, and management again cited a better macro in explaining the quarter. Leverage rose to 2.72x in a quarter with $747M of buybacks. On GAAP earnings the stock trades at 25.0x, with 3.36x debt-to-equity. It has gained 63.7% in a year, sits at a monthly RSI of 77.7, and with 13 of 16 analysts already at Buy the average target implies only 8.1% more.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory and fee-practice risk. The FTC settlement awaits final approval, and the underlying finding was about fee disclosure — the same revenue mechanism that runs across the company.
02
Macro sensitivity. Fuel prices, FX volatility and interest rates (via float income) all feed revenue; float took about two points off Corporate Payments' organic growth in Q3 (18% against 20% without it).
03
Leverage and M&A execution. $11.23B of debt, a 2.72x leverage ratio at quarter-end and a strategy that buys growth and sells what doesn't fit. Each integration and divestiture is a chance to miss.
Industry Commentary
From fuel cards to B2B payments
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

The rename tells you where management thinks the growth is. Business-to-business payments — supplier invoices, cross-border transfers — are still heavily paper and bank-wire; every flow moved onto a card or platform gives the processor a fee or an FX spread. Corpay's Corporate Payments segment grew 18% organically in Q3 2026 while Vehicle grew 8% and Lodging 3%. The mix shift is what the market is being asked to pay for.

Fee transparency under scrutiny
ONGOING HEADWIND

The FTC's case against FleetCor ran from 2019 to a 2023 district-court finding, a 2026 appeals decision and a $100M settlement. For a business whose economics depend on small, unnoticed charges on large flows, disclosure rules are a direct input to revenue, not a compliance footnote.
