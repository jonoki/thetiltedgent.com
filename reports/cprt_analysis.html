01 Company Overview

Copart runs the world's largest online auction marketplace for salvage and damaged vehicles — the cars that insurance carriers declare a total loss after a collision, flood, hail event or theft. Roughly 80% of its volume comes from insurance companies, and Copart earns most of its money as an agent: it stores, photographs, titles and sells the vehicle for a fee rather than buying it. Fiscal 2026 (ended July 31, 2026, reported September 10) brought revenue of $4.666 billion, up just 0.4%, with net income down 4.4% to $1.484 billion and diluted EPS of $1.55. At the September 21, 2026 close the market capitalization is $26.8 billion (our calculation: $28.91 × 925.81M shares).

The fourth quarter sharpened the story rather than changing it. Revenue still edged up (+2.4%) on pricing, but U.S. insurance units fell 7.5% — worse than the −4.2% of the prior quarter — gross profit fell 5.5%, and EPS of $0.35 missed consensus. The same evening Copart announced its first large acquisition in years: ACV, the dealer-to-dealer digital wholesale marketplace, for $10.50 a share in cash, about $1.9 billion. Copart remains one of the highest-quality franchises in the market — ROIC of 28.4%, a 35.4% operating margin, $4.49 billion of cash and held-to-maturity securities against $88 million of finance-lease debt — and yet the stock has lost 14.3% since the September 4 edition and trades at 18.7x trailing earnings. The central debate of this report: is the de-rating a volume air-pocket in a structurally advantaged duopoly, or is the market repricing a business whose insurance funnel is shrinking faster each quarter — and which has now chosen to spend over 40% of its cash pile buying growth outside that funnel?

02 Core Businesses

Copart reports two geographic segments — United States and International — and within each splits revenue between service revenue (agent fees: seller fees, buyer fees, storage, transport, title processing) and purchased vehicle sales (cars Copart buys on principal and resells, where the entire vehicle price runs through the top line at a much thinner margin). That second distinction matters far more to the earnings model than the geography does, and it is the single most misread line in the P&L.

This is the franchise. Insurance carriers consign totalled vehicles; Copart tows them to one of its owned yards, holds them through the claims and title process, photographs and lists them, and sells them on VB3, its proprietary virtual-bidding platform, to a global buyer base of dismantlers, rebuilders, exporters and licensed dealers. Copart takes a fee from both the seller and the buyer plus ancillary charges for storage, transport and title work, so it captures value without carrying vehicle inventory risk. In Q4 FY2026 U.S. segment operating income was $312 million on $930 million of U.S. revenue, a 33.6% operating margin (38.9% in Q3; 37.3% for the full year). The pressure point is volume, not price, and it got worse: U.S. insurance units fell 7.5% year over year in the quarter, from −4.2% in Q3, and management attributed the decline to industry-wide falls in claims frequency. Price is still the offset — global revenue per unit rose 5.4% and global ASPs 3.5% — but it no longer covers the unit loss at the gross-profit line. Non-insurance U.S. volume was roughly flat (+0.2%), with dealer units up 5.8%.

Revenue Mix
On principal transactions Copart buys the vehicle outright and books the full resale price as revenue, so a $6,000 car adds $6,000 of revenue and perhaps a few hundred dollars of gross profit — versus an agent transaction where a $6,000 car might add $600 of fee revenue at close to a 50% margin. The result is that purchased-vehicle revenue mechanically depresses reported gross margin without depressing gross profit dollars. The mix shift is real but small: for the full fiscal year 2026, vehicle sales were $696.7 million of $4,666 million total revenue, or 14.9%. Where the effect genuinely bites is internationally: purchased vehicles were 32.3% of FY2026 international revenue versus 11.0% of U.S. revenue, and international operating margin was 27.2% against the U.S. segment's 37.3% for the year — a gap that is substantially a principal-versus-agent artifact rather than an efficiency gap. As international grows faster than the U.S., consolidated margin faces a slow structural drag even when nothing operationally deteriorates. On the Q4 call CEO Jay Adair said international vehicle purchasing will keep expanding (per Repairer Driven News).

In Q4 FY2026 international revenue rose 11.7% (our calculation from the segment tables: $222.1M vs $198.8M), international insurance units rose 11.2% (per MarketBeat's call summary) and international operating income rose 3.0% — against a U.S. segment whose operating income fell 12.7%. ACV is the new piece. On September 10, 2026 Copart agreed to buy the digital dealer-to-dealer wholesale marketplace for $10.50 per share in cash (about $1.9 billion of equity value), a 45% premium to ACV's unaffected August 10 close, funded from cash on hand; the tender offer launched in mid-September and is scheduled to expire September 30, with closing expected by calendar year-end subject to a majority tender and Hart-Scott-Rodino clearance. Per call summaries, ACV transacted about $10 billion of gross merchandise value in 2025 across more than 22,000 active buyers, and the brands will run separately with shared buyer liquidity. It is Copart's biggest bet on the whole-car (non-salvage) market — the part of the funnel not governed by insurance claims. Purple Wave, acquired in 2022, is Copart's online auction platform for heavy construction, agricultural and transportation equipment — a direct assault on Ritchie Bros.' historical home turf, and the clearest evidence that Copart's playbook (owned yards, virtual bidding, national buyer liquidity) travels beyond cars. On the Q3 FY2026 call (May 2026) management said gross transaction value grew more than 25% over the trailing twelve months, driven by territorial expansion and deeper enterprise accounts, and that the team has grown 2.5–3x since acquisition. Title Express is the title-processing service that turns Copart's single most annoying operational bottleneck into a product: obtaining a clean salvage title across fifty different state DMV regimes is slow, and management stated the platform is processing at "6, 7, 8x" industry throughput levels, with expanded account penetration lifting service revenue. It is a retention weapon as much as a revenue line — a carrier that routes titles through Copart is materially stickier. BluCar is the whole-car commercial channel (fleet, finance, dealer trade-ins) that grew over 4% in Q3 FY2026 and is the main hedge against insurance-unit softness. None of these is separately reported, so their contribution must be inferred rather than measured.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Owned land, not leased Two-sided auction liquidity Effective duopoly with IAA Zoning & permitting barriers Global buyer base (11 countries) Title & regulatory expertise Catastrophe surge capacity Fortress balance sheet ($4.49B cash & securities, no borrowings)

The core advantage is dirt. Copart owns most of the acreage under its yards rather than leasing it — a strategy that looks like capital inefficiency in a spreadsheet and is in fact the moat. A salvage yard needs hundreds of contiguous acres within reasonable towing distance of a metro area, with permits to store thousands of wrecked and often leaking vehicles. That land is nearly impossible to assemble today, and the permitting is harder still. It also converts Copart's biggest operational risk into an advantage: when a hurricane produces 100,000 flooded vehicles in a week, the operator with spare owned acreage takes the volume and the operator renting by the month cannot. Layered on top is classic marketplace network effect — carriers consign where the bidding is deepest, and international buyers bid where the inventory is broadest — and a regulatory competence (fifty state title regimes, environmental handling, export documentation) that is dull, unglamorous and genuinely hard to replicate. The result is a business earning a 28.4% ROIC on an asset-heavy footprint, which should be nearly impossible.

Key Vulnerabilities
01
Copart does not control its own volume. Insurance carriers decide how many cars enter the funnel, and the decline is accelerating: U.S. insurance units fell 4.2% in Q3 FY2026 and 7.5% in Q4, which management put down to industry-wide declines in claims frequency. When revenue is fee-per-unit and units shrink, price must do all the work. In Q4 it no longer did: revenue per unit rose 5.4% and revenue still grew 2.4%, but gross profit fell 5.5% and U.S. operating income fell 12.7%.
02
Total-loss frequency is a ratchet that can slip. Total-loss frequency — the share of damaged vehicles that carriers write off rather than repair — reached 23.3% in the second quarter of 2026, the highest second-quarter level on record and up from 22.4% a year earlier, per management on the Q4 call. It rose because repair costs (parts, labour, ADAS sensor recalibration) outran used-vehicle values, so writing the car off got cheaper than fixing it. That is the single biggest driver of Copart's unit growth. But it is a ratio, and it can compress: if used-car prices soften, the salvage value of the wreck falls and the repair-versus-total-loss maths swings back toward repair. A soft used-vehicle pricing backdrop therefore hits Copart twice — lower ASPs on what it sells, and less favourable arithmetic for the decision that creates its inventory in the first place.
03
A duopoly is only comfortable while both parties behave. IAA under RB Global is a better-capitalised competitor than IAA standing alone was, with an owner that has every incentive to fight for national carrier contracts. Salvage supply agreements are periodically re-bid, and a single large carrier defection moves volume in step changes rather than increments. Copart's pricing power is real but untested against a determined share-taker.
04
Mix and currency are quietly flattering the growth rate. International carries a much heavier purchased-vehicle load (32.3% of FY2026 segment revenue) at a 27.2% operating margin versus 37.3% in the U.S. The faster international grows, the more consolidated margin drifts down for reasons that have nothing to do with operations — and in Q3 roughly six points of international growth were currency translation.
05
The first big acquisition in years. ACV costs about $1.9 billion in cash — over 40% of the $4.49 billion of cash and securities at July 31 — for a dealer-to-dealer marketplace in a different, more competitive part of the used-car market. Management expects it to be roughly breakeven to earnings at first and accretive from fiscal 2028. Until then it is capital that is not going into buybacks, and an integration test for a company whose record is organic.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean buyers have dominated to an unusual degree ("overbought"); below 30 means sellers have ("oversold"). It says nothing about whether a company is cheap — only about the balance of recent momentum.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying an irreplaceable asset base — thousands of owned, permitted acres inside a genuine duopoly — at 18.7x trailing earnings, with 28% ROIC, a 35% operating margin, $4.49 billion of cash and securities and no borrowings. The volume decline is cyclical, not structural: claims frequency moves with premium affordability and will normalise, while total-loss frequency (23.3% in the second quarter of 2026, a record for that quarter) is still ratcheting up as cars become "computers on wheels" that are costly to repair. The parts of the business management controls keep growing — international revenue +11.7% in Q4, international insurance units +11.2%. And ACV is a sensible use of idle cash: it buys Copart a scaled digital position in dealer wholesale, the whole-car market outside the insurance funnel, at a price management says is neutral to EPS in year one and accretive from fiscal 2028. Even after the deal, over $2 billion of cash remains.

Bear Case

The funnel is shrinking faster, not slower. U.S. insurance units fell 4.2% in Q3 and 7.5% in Q4, and this time pricing did not rescue the gross-profit line: revenue +2.4%, gross profit −5.5%, operating income −10.6%, net income −17.4%, EPS $0.35 against a $0.38–0.39 consensus. Management gave no guidance. Buybacks — the lever that manufactured EPS growth through flat net income — slowed to a trickle in Q4 and will now compete with a $1.9 billion acquisition for cash. ACV is a dealer-to-dealer marketplace in a far more contested market than salvage, bought at a 45% premium, and Copart has little record of large-scale integration. At 17.7x forward on a consensus that has already been cut, the stock is cheap only if FY2027 EPS growth of 5% actually arrives; Barclays' $25 target is now just 13.5% away.

Top 3 Risks
01
Insurance claims volume keeps falling. U.S. insurance units went from −4.2% in Q3 to −7.5% in Q4, and management attributed the decline to industry-wide falls in claims frequency — collision claim frequency fell 3.4% year over year, per the Q4 call as reported by Repairer Driven News. That is an insurance-market problem, not a Copart execution problem, and it could persist. Copart has no lever to pull on it.
02
ACV execution and capital cost. About $1.9 billion in cash for a business management expects to be only neutral to EPS in its first full year. If the dealer-wholesale market or ACV's growth disappoints, Copart will have swapped buyback capacity for a lower-return asset — and the tender still needs a majority of ACV shares and antitrust clearance.
03
Total-loss frequency reverses. Total-loss frequency is the master variable — it is why Copart's unit count grew for a decade even as vehicles got safer. It rose because repair costs outran salvage values. If used-vehicle prices stay soft, that arithmetic swings back toward repair, and Copart loses the secular tailwind that has offset every cyclical headwind.
Industry Commentary
Personal auto insurance affordability is throttling the funnel
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Copart's supply of inventory is entirely downstream of insurance claims, and claims are downstream of how many people carry how much coverage and how often they crash. In Q3 management blamed consumers reducing coverage in response to higher premiums; in Q4 it pointed to industry-wide declines in claims frequency, with collision claim frequency down 3.4% year over year while average collision severity rose 8.8% to over $6,300 (as reported by Repairer Driven News from the Q4 call). Fewer claims, each more expensive: good for the total-loss ratio, bad for the unit count. U.S. insurance units fell 7.5% in Q4 while non-insurance U.S. volume held roughly flat (+0.2%) and dealer units grew 5.8%. The constraint is not Copart's execution; it is the size of the pool it fishes in — which is exactly the problem ACV is meant to work around.
Soft used-vehicle pricing cuts both ways — and the second cut is the dangerous one
ONGOING HEADWIND
The obvious effect of weak used-car values is lower auction proceeds and therefore lower fee revenue per unit. Copart has so far defied it — global ASPs rose 3.5% in Q4 (5.5% for the fiscal year) and revenue per unit 5.4%, evidence of genuine buyer demand and of the global bidder base bidding up scarce inventory. The subtler and more important effect runs through total-loss frequency. A carrier totals a car when the repair estimate exceeds the vehicle's actual cash value less its salvage value. If salvage values fall, the total-loss threshold rises and marginal vehicles get repaired instead of written off. So far repair inflation keeps winning: total-loss frequency was 23.3% in the second quarter of 2026, up from 22.4% a year earlier and a record for that quarter, and management expects the trend to continue. A sustained soft used-car market is the one thing that could stall the ratchet.
The bull-bear frame: a cyclical air pocket, or a structural plateau?
EMERGING TAILWIND
Everything reduces to one question, and the Q4 print moved the evidence toward the bears without settling it. For the bulls: international revenue grew 11.7% in the same quarter U.S. insurance units fell 7.5% — the model works where the funnel is growing — and total-loss frequency is still setting records. ACV gives Copart a scaled digital marketplace in dealer wholesale, and the balance sheet can pay for it in cash and still hold over $2 billion. For the bears: a fee-per-unit business with units falling faster each quarter is a business whose growth is capped by pricing, and in Q4 pricing stopped covering the gap at the gross-profit line. Management gave no guidance, the buyback slowed, and the new strategy is an acquisition in a more competitive market. The next print — the first with ACV in view — will say more than any valuation debate can.
