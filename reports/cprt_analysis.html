01 Company Overview

Copart runs the world's largest online auction marketplace for salvage and damaged vehicles — the cars that insurance carriers declare a total loss after a collision, flood, hail event or theft. Roughly 80% of its volume comes from insurance companies, and Copart earns most of its money as an agent: it stores, photographs, titles and sells the vehicle for a fee rather than buying it. Fiscal 2025 (ended July 31, 2025) revenue was $4.647 billion, up 9.7% year over year; the trailing-twelve-month figure through the April 2026 quarter is $4.64 billion, up just 1.0%. The company is the 824th most valuable listed company in the world at a $31.2 billion market capitalization — down from a $56.3 billion peak reached in 2024.

That gap is the whole story. Copart is still one of the highest-quality industrial franchises in the market — ROIC of 30.0%, a 37.8% operating margin, $4.20 billion of cash against $93 million of total debt — and yet the stock has lost 29.9% over the past 52 weeks and roughly 57% from its split-adjusted 2021 high. The central debate of this report: is the de-rating a temporary volume air-pocket in an otherwise structurally advantaged duopoly, or is the market repricing a business whose two great secular tailwinds — rising total-loss frequency and rising used-vehicle values — have finally stalled at the same time, leaving a 21x multiple attached to flat earnings?

02 Core Businesses

Copart reports two geographic segments — United States and International — and within each splits revenue between service revenue (agent fees: seller fees, buyer fees, storage, transport, title processing) and purchased vehicle sales (cars Copart buys on principal and resells, where the entire vehicle price runs through the top line at a much thinner margin). That second distinction matters far more to the earnings model than the geography does, and it is the single most misread line in the P&L.

This is the franchise. Insurance carriers consign totalled vehicles; Copart tows them to one of its owned yards, holds them through the claims and title process, photographs and lists them, and sells them on VB3, its proprietary virtual-bidding platform, to a global buyer base of dismantlers, rebuilders, exporters and licensed dealers. Copart takes a fee from both the seller and the buyer plus ancillary charges for storage, transport and title work, so it captures value without carrying vehicle inventory risk — U.S. gross margin ran 48.3% in Q3 FY2026, and U.S. segment operating income was $390 million on $1,002 million of U.S. revenue, a 38.9% operating margin. The pressure point is volume, not price: U.S. insurance units fell 4.2% year over year in the quarter (just over 3% excluding catastrophe volume), which management attributed to softer claims activity as consumers cut coverage in response to higher premiums. Price offset it — U.S. insurance ASPs rose 4.1% to a seasonally adjusted all-time high. Non-insurance lines are the offset that is working: Dealer Services grew about 1%, BluCar (whole-car commercial) grew more than 4%, and fleet/finance volumes grew at double-digit rates.

Revenue Mix
On principal transactions Copart buys the vehicle outright and books the full resale price as revenue, so a $6,000 car adds $6,000 of revenue and perhaps a few hundred dollars of gross profit — versus an agent transaction where a $6,000 car might add $600 of fee revenue at close to a 50% margin. The result is that purchased-vehicle revenue mechanically depresses reported gross margin without depressing gross profit dollars. The mix shift is real but smaller than commonly assumed: for the first nine months of FY2026, vehicle sales revenue was $514 million of $3,514 million total, or 14.6%, versus roughly 14.4% in the prior-year nine months (service revenue $3,000M, −0.4%; vehicle sales $514M, +0.9%). Twenty basis points of mix is not what de-rated this stock. Where the effect genuinely bites is internationally: purchased vehicles are 31.5% of international revenue versus 10.7% of U.S. revenue, and international operating margin is 31.5% against the U.S. segment's 38.9% — a ~740 basis point gap that is substantially a principal-versus-agent artifact rather than an efficiency gap. As international grows faster than the U.S., consolidated margin faces a slow structural drag even when nothing operationally deteriorates.

Purple Wave, acquired in 2022, is Copart's online auction platform for heavy construction, agricultural and transportation equipment — a direct assault on Ritchie Bros.' historical home turf, and the clearest evidence that Copart's playbook (owned yards, virtual bidding, national buyer liquidity) travels beyond cars. Title Express is the title-processing service that turns Copart's single most annoying operational bottleneck into a product: obtaining a clean salvage title across fifty different state DMV regimes is slow, and management stated the platform is processing at "6, 7, 8x" industry throughput levels, with expanded account penetration lifting service revenue. It is a retention weapon as much as a revenue line — a carrier that routes titles through Copart is materially stickier. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Owned land, not leased Two-sided auction liquidity Effective duopoly with IAA Zoning & permitting barriers Global buyer base (11 countries) Title & regulatory expertise Catastrophe surge capacity Fortress balance sheet ($4.2B cash, $93M debt)

The core advantage is dirt. Copart owns most of the acreage under its yards rather than leasing it — a strategy that looks like capital inefficiency in a spreadsheet and is in fact the moat. A salvage yard needs hundreds of contiguous acres within reasonable towing distance of a metro area, with permits to store thousands of wrecked and often leaking vehicles. That land is nearly impossible to assemble today, and the permitting is harder still. It also converts Copart's biggest operational risk into an advantage: when a hurricane produces 100,000 flooded vehicles in a week, the operator with spare owned acreage takes the volume and the operator renting by the month cannot. Layered on top is classic marketplace network effect — carriers consign where the bidding is deepest, and international buyers bid where the inventory is broadest — and a regulatory competence (fifty state title regimes, environmental handling, export documentation) that is dull, unglamorous and genuinely hard to replicate. The result is a business earning a 30.0% ROIC on an asset-heavy footprint, which should be nearly impossible.

Key Vulnerabilities
01
Copart does not control its own volume. Insurance carriers decide how many cars enter the funnel, and in Q3 FY2026 they sent 4.2% fewer U.S. insurance units. Management's own explanation — consumers dropping or reducing coverage because premiums rose — is a demand shock Copart cannot fix with better execution. Global insurance units fell 2.7%. When your revenue is fee-per-unit and units shrink, price increases must do all the work, and ASP growth of 4.1% only just covered a 4.2% volume decline.
02
Total-loss frequency is a ratchet that can slip. Total-loss frequency — the share of damaged vehicles that carriers write off rather than repair — reached 23.6% and has climbed almost five percentage points over four years. It rose because repair costs (parts, labour, ADAS sensor recalibration) outran used-vehicle values, so writing the car off got cheaper than fixing it. That is the single biggest driver of Copart's unit growth. But it is a ratio, and it can compress: if used-car prices soften, the salvage value of the wreck falls and the repair-versus-total-loss maths swings back toward repair. A soft used-vehicle pricing backdrop therefore hits Copart twice — lower ASPs on what it sells, and less favourable arithmetic for the decision that creates its inventory in the first place.
03
A duopoly is only comfortable while both parties behave. IAA under RB Global is a better-capitalised competitor than IAA standing alone was, with an owner that has every incentive to fight for national carrier contracts. Salvage supply agreements are periodically re-bid, and a single large carrier defection moves volume in step changes rather than increments. Copart's pricing power is real but untested against a determined share-taker.
04
Mix and currency are quietly flattering the growth rate. International revenue grew 14.1% headline but only 7.9% in constant currency, and international carries a much heavier purchased-vehicle load (31.5% of segment revenue) at a 31.5% operating margin versus 38.9% in the U.S. The faster international grows, the more consolidated margin drifts down for reasons that have nothing to do with operations — and the more reported growth depends on the dollar.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying an irreplaceable asset base — thousands of owned, permitted acres inside a genuine duopoly — at 20.9x trailing earnings, a 37% discount to the index it belongs to, with 30% ROIC, a 47.5% gross margin, $4.2 billion of net cash and zero effective debt. The volume decline is cyclical, not structural: insurance claims frequency moves with premium affordability and will normalise, while total-loss frequency at 23.6% is still on a decade-long upward ratchet driven by repair costs that keep rising. Meanwhile the parts of the business management does control are compounding fast — international units +5.9% and gross profit +21.9%, Purple Wave gross transaction value +25%, Title Express processing at multiples of industry throughput. With $1.6 billion of stock retired in nine months and $4.2 billion still idle, the buyback alone can carry EPS growth through a flat-revenue year.

Bear Case

The two engines that built this company have both stalled at once. Unit volumes are shrinking — global −2.4%, U.S. insurance −4.2% — and a fee-per-unit business cannot grow if units decline; ASP increases of 4.1% only just papered over the gap, and a soft used-car pricing backdrop threatens that offset too. Worse, weak used-vehicle values undermine total-loss frequency itself: lower salvage recoveries make repair the cheaper option again, which would reverse the ratchet that has driven a decade of unit growth. Consensus already models FY2026 EPS down 0.5%, so 20.7x forward is not cheap against zero growth — that is a PEG of 1.92. Net income actually fell 1.0% last quarter; the reported EPS gain was manufactured by buybacks. Barclays' $25 target implies another 26% downside, and at that point the stock still is not obviously mispriced.

Top 3 Risks
01
Total-loss frequency reverses. The 23.6% total-loss rate is the master variable — it is why Copart's unit count grew for a decade even as vehicles got safer and crashes got rarer. It rose because repair costs outran salvage values. If used-vehicle prices stay soft, that arithmetic swings back toward repair, and Copart loses the one secular tailwind that has offset every cyclical headwind since 2015.
02
Insurance claims volume stays depressed. Management attributed the 4.2% U.S. insurance unit decline to consumers reducing coverage as premiums rose. That is a structural affordability problem in personal auto insurance, not a weather-driven blip, and it could persist for years. Copart has no lever to pull on it.
03
The multiple has further to fall if growth does not return. At 20.7x forward earnings on a business consensus expects to shrink EPS this year, CPRT is priced for eventual reacceleration. The FY2027 consensus of +5.9% EPS growth is the load-bearing assumption. Guidance on September 10 that fails to support it would leave the stock re-rating toward the Barclays $25 case — roughly 15x — which is where genuinely no-growth industrials trade.
Industry Commentary
Personal auto insurance affordability is throttling the funnel
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Copart's supply of inventory is entirely downstream of insurance claims, and claims are downstream of how many people carry how much coverage. Management's stated explanation for the 4.2% decline in U.S. insurance units is that consumers reduced coverage in response to higher premiums — dropping comprehensive and collision, raising deductibles, or going uninsured. Fewer covered vehicles means fewer claims; higher deductibles mean more small damage never gets reported at all. This is the mechanism converting an insurance-industry pricing cycle into a Copart volume problem, and it is why global units fell 2.7% on the insurance side while every non-insurance line Copart controls — Dealer Services +1%, BluCar +4%, fleet/finance double-digit, international non-insurance +11.2% — actually grew. The constraint is not Copart's execution; it is the size of the pool it fishes in.
Soft used-vehicle pricing cuts both ways — and the second cut is the dangerous one
ONGOING HEADWIND
The obvious effect of weak used-car values is lower auction proceeds and therefore lower fee revenue per unit. Copart has so far defied it — U.S. insurance ASPs rose 4.1% to a seasonally adjusted record and global ASPs rose 4.6%, evidence of genuine buyer-demand strength and of the global bidder base bidding up scarce inventory. The subtler and more important effect runs through total-loss frequency. A carrier totals a car when the repair estimate exceeds the vehicle's actual cash value less its salvage value. If salvage values fall, the total-loss threshold rises and marginal vehicles get repaired instead of written off. Total-loss frequency has climbed almost five percentage points in four years to 23.6% precisely because repair inflation outpaced this effect; a sustained soft used-car market is the one thing that could stall the ratchet. Watch that 23.6% figure on September 10 more closely than the revenue line.
The bull-bear frame: a cyclical air pocket, or a structural plateau?
EMERGING TAILWIND
Everything reduces to one question, and the evidence genuinely cuts both ways. For the bulls: international units grew 5.9% and international gross profit grew 21.9% in the same quarter U.S. insurance units fell 4.2% — the model works fine where the funnel is growing, and Europe's total-loss frequency has a decade of convergence ahead of it. Purple Wave's 25%+ gross transaction value growth proves the platform extends beyond cars. Gross margin expanded 71 basis points and free cash flow grew 12% during a volume decline, which is what operating leverage on owned assets looks like. At 20.9x with $4.2 billion of cash and a running buyback, you are being paid to wait. For the bears: a fee-per-unit business with declining units is a business whose revenue growth is capped by pricing, and pricing depends on used-vehicle values that are soft. Consensus already expects FY2026 EPS to decline. Net income fell 1.0% last quarter; only the buyback made EPS positive. The 20.7x forward multiple embeds a recovery in a variable — insurance claims frequency — that management has explicitly said it cannot control. The September 10 print, and specifically the unit-volume and total-loss-frequency disclosures within it, will settle more of this argument than any valuation debate can.
