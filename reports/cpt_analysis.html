01 Company Overview

Camden is a Houston landlord. It builds, buys and runs apartment communities, and at June 30, 2026 owned 176 communities with 59,676 apartment homes, a count that still included the California portfolio it sold a month later. It is a REIT, so GAAP earnings are the wrong yardstick. The number that matters is FFO, and more precisely what is left after the roofs and carpets are paid for, AFFO. Over the trailing twelve months Camden produced $6.85 of Core FFO and $5.84 of Core AFFO per share, and it pays a $4.24 annualised dividend. Core FFO was $6.85 in 2024 and $6.88 in 2025, and management guides $6.75 for 2026. Three years, one number.

The story of 2026 is one big trade. In July Camden closed the sale of 11 California communities (3,620 homes) for about $1.625 billion. Executive Chairman Ric Campo laid out the plan on the Q2 call: "sell the California properties for $1.625 billion, acquire $1 billion of newer properties in our existing markets and spend the remainder to buy back Camden's shares." The arithmetic behind it is simple. CEO Alex Jessett put the sale at "a trailing 12-month FFO yield of 5.6% and an AFFO yield of 5.2%." At the September 21 close of $98.82, Camden's own stock trades at a 6.9% Core FFO yield. Selling buildings at 5.6% and buying your own at 6.9% is accretive on paper. The catch is the rest of the portfolio. Camden's Sun Belt markets are still absorbing a wave of new supply: same-property NOI fell 1.4% in Q2 and new leases signed 3.3% below the prior tenant. The stock is down 10.2% since December 31 and 13.7% since June 30. The central debate: is Camden a cheap landlord at the bottom of a supply cycle, doing the right thing with its capital, or a flat-FFO REIT whose rents have not turned yet?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in Sun Belt metros In-house development Investment-grade unsecured funding 95.7% occupancy 33 years as a public REIT Capital-recycling discipline

Apartments have thin moats. A tenant can move across the street, and anyone with land and a construction loan can build a competitor, which is exactly what happened across the Sun Belt. Camden's edge is cost of capital and operating scale: most of its debt is unsecured ($4.53B of the $4.85B total at June 30), it runs its own properties across dense metro clusters, and it can build when building pays and buy when buying is cheaper. The last point is the one to watch in 2026. When private buyers pay a 5.6% FFO yield for your older buildings and the public market prices your whole company at 6.9%, a management team that sells the one and buys the other is using the only moat a landlord really has: choosing where to put the capital.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
New-lease pricing is still negative. New leases signed 3.3% below the prior rent in Q2 2026, after −5.5% in Q1 and −5.3% in Q4 2025. Renewals at +2.8% are carrying the blend. Until new leases turn positive, same-property revenue has no room to grow.
02
Expenses are outrunning revenue. Q2 same-property expenses rose 2.4% against flat revenue; sequentially they rose 4.3%. Full-year guidance is expenses +2.50% against revenue +0.50%. That spread is why NOI is shrinking.
03
Leverage is higher than it looks after the sale. Net debt to annualised adjusted EBITDAre was 5.0x for the six months to June 30, and 5.3x on the second quarter alone (4.2x a year earlier). About $0.9B of sale proceeds went to debt, but Camden also took a $350M unsecured term loan after the quarter, and liquidity at June 30 was $287.4M against $140.1M of development still to fund.
04
Revenue-management litigation. Camden agreed a $53.0M settlement fund in the class action over revenue-management software, which has preliminary court approval. The company says it has no impact on 2026 Core FFO or Core AFFO guidance, but it sits in NAREIT FFO and it limits the rent-setting tools the whole sector leaned on.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 14.4x trailing Core FFO and 16.9x Core AFFO for a 4.29% dividend that takes only about 73% of AFFO. Management just sold its oldest, lowest-yielding buildings at a 5.6% FFO yield, paid down about $0.9B of debt, bought newer Sun Belt communities and is buying back stock at a 6.9% Core FFO yield. That is exactly what a landlord should do when private and public markets disagree about value. The leading indicators are turning: new-lease pricing improved from −5.5% to −3.3% in one quarter, occupancy rose to 95.7%, and management expects blended rent growth to go positive in the second half. Even the Street's most bearish target, Scotiabank's $102, is above the price.

Bear Case

Core FFO has been flat for three years ($6.85, $6.88, a guided $6.75) and is going down this year. Same-property NOI is still shrinking, expenses are growing five times faster than revenue on full-year guidance, and new leases still sign below the previous rent. NAREIT FFO guidance fell from $6.61 to $6.12 in five months. The California sale is dilutive until every dollar is redeployed, leverage is 5.0x, and analysts cut targets into the September slide. A landlord trading at a cheap multiple on flat-to-falling FFO is not a bargain until the FFO starts to grow.

Top 3 Risks

01
The rent recovery arrives late. New-lease pricing at −3.3% is the leading indicator. If Q3 does not show the positive blended growth management talked about, same-property revenue guidance (already trimmed once) goes lower and the Core FFO midpoint loses its cushion.
02
Redeployment risk on $1.6B. The California trade only works if the acquisitions and buybacks earn more than the 5.6% FFO yield given up. Acquisition yields were not disclosed on the pages fetched, and buybacks at an average $104.08 are already underwater at $98.82.
03
Expense growth against flat revenue. Guidance of +2.50% expenses against +0.50% revenue means NOI shrinks even if rents stabilise. Insurance, taxes and payroll in the Sun Belt do not reset when rents do.
Industry Commentary
The Sun Belt supply wave
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The same migration that made Sun Belt apartments the best trade of 2021 pulled a wave of new construction into those markets, and new supply takes new-lease pricing first. Camden's numbers show the damage and the healing at once: new leases at −5.3%, −5.5%, then −3.3% over the last three reported quarters, renewals steady near +2.8%, occupancy back up to 95.7%. On the Q2 call Jessett said Austin "is showing the highest improved momentum amongst all major U.S. markets", a market-wide figure rather than Camden's own, and that Camden's signed new leases there went from −11% in March to −3% in July (Motley Fool transcript). Market-level supply and completion data were not sourced for this report. The honest read is that the rate of decline is improving, and the level is still negative.

Revenue-management litigation
ONGOING HEADWIND

Apartment landlords across the sector have faced class actions over the use of shared revenue-management software to set rents. Camden's piece is a $53.0 million settlement fund with preliminary court approval, which the company says does not affect 2026 Core FFO or Core AFFO guidance. The cash cost is manageable; the lasting cost is that the pricing tools the industry used to push rents are now constrained, just as operators need pricing power to come back.

Private vs public pricing — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The California sale is a live data point on what private buyers will pay for apartments: a 5.6% trailing FFO yield on 19-year-old buildings. The public market prices all of Camden at a 6.9% Core FFO yield. The bull framing: that gap is the opportunity, and management is arbitraging it by selling assets and buying shares. If the market closed the gap, the stock would rerate toward the analysts' $116.65 average. The bear framing: California buildings in supply-constrained coastal markets deserve a lower yield than a Sun Belt portfolio still fighting new supply, so the gap is not mispricing. It is the right price for Camden's growth. The way to settle it is not the multiple. It is two numbers Camden reports every quarter: new-lease pricing and same-property NOI growth. Both need to turn positive together.
