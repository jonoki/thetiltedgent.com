01 Company Overview

Camden is a Houston landlord. It builds, buys and runs apartment communities, and at September 30, 2026 owned 168 operating communities with 56,995 apartment homes, plus four more under construction. It is a REIT, so GAAP earnings are the wrong yardstick. The number that matters is FFO, and more precisely what is left after the roofs and carpets are paid for, AFFO. Over the trailing twelve months Camden produced $6.86 of Core FFO and $5.83 of Core AFFO per share, and it pays a $4.24 annualised dividend. Core FFO was $6.85 in 2024 and $6.88 in 2025, and management guides $6.75 for 2026. Three years, one number.

The story of 2026 is one big trade, and the third quarter showed it finished. On July 29 Camden closed the sale of 11 California communities (3,620 homes) for about $1.6 billion, a gain of about $1.0 billion that lifted Q3 GAAP EPS to $8.71. About $0.9 billion of the proceeds repaid the revolving credit line and commercial paper, and Camden has bought 4.54 million of its own shares this year at an average of $104.22. At the November 13 close of $99.15, the stock trades at a 6.9% Core FFO yield (the year's Core FFO divided by the price). On the Q2 call management had put the California sale at a 5.6% trailing FFO yield, so selling buildings at 5.6% and buying back its own shares at 6.9% adds to FFO per share on paper. The catch is the rest of the portfolio. Same-property NOI (rent and other income less operating costs, for buildings owned in both periods) fell 0.2% in Q3 and 0.7% for the nine months, and new leases signed 2.0% below the prior tenant, better than 3.3% below in Q2 but still negative. Camden cut its full-year same-property revenue forecast midpoint to 0.00% from 0.50%. The stock is down 9.9% since December 31 and 13.4% since June 30. The central debate has not moved: is Camden a cheap landlord at the bottom of a supply cycle, using its capital well, or a flat-FFO REIT whose rents have not turned yet? On the Q3 call management said the 1.0% rise in rents across new leases and renewals combined was roughly in line with its expectations.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in Sun Belt metros In-house development Investment-grade unsecured funding 95.6% occupancy 33 years as a public REIT Capital-recycling discipline

Apartments have thin moats. A tenant can move across the street, and anyone with land and a construction loan can build a competitor, which is exactly what happened across the Sun Belt. Camden's edge is cost of capital and operating scale: about 93% of its debt is unsecured ($3.92B of $4.23B at September 30, our arithmetic from the balance sheet), it runs its own properties across dense metro clusters, and it can build when building pays and buy when buying is cheaper. The last point is the one to watch. When private buyers pay a 5.6% FFO yield for your older buildings and the public market prices your whole company at 6.9%, a management team that sells the one and buys the other is using the only moat a landlord really has: choosing where to put the capital. On the Q3 call management said it would rather buy its own shares at a clear discount than buy buildings.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
New-lease pricing is still negative. New leases signed 2.0% below the prior rent in Q3 2026, after −3.3% in Q2, −5.5% in Q1 and −5.3% in Q4 2025. Renewals at +3.6% are carrying the blend to +1.0%. Until new leases turn positive, same-property revenue has little room to grow, and Camden just cut its full-year revenue midpoint to 0.00%.
02
Costs have not been cut as fast as rent has stalled. Nine-month same-property revenue was flat (0.0%) while expenses rose 1.2%, so NOI is down 0.7%. Full-year guidance is expenses +1.75% against revenue 0.00%, and NOI −1.00% at the midpoint. Insurance, taxes and payroll in the Sun Belt do not reset when rents do.
03
Leverage has come down, but a loan is due next year. Net debt to annualised adjusted EBITDAre (operating profit before depreciation, a measure of how many years of cash profit it would take to repay debt) was 4.0x in Q3 and 4.6x for the nine months. Total debt fell to $4.23B from $4.85B at June 30, and liquidity was about $1.8B (about $0.6B of it in cash and in tax-deferred property-exchange accounts, known as 1031 accounts). The $500 million of notes due November 2026 were repaid at par in October; a $350M unsecured term loan matures in July 2027.
04
Legal costs and land write-downs. The release shows legal costs and settlements of $50.6M in the first nine months of 2026 and an $86.4M impairment on land held for development in Q3; both sit in GAAP and NAREIT FFO. Camden reported a $53.0M fund to settle the class action over rent-setting software with the Q2 results. The wider risk is that pricing tools the whole sector leaned on are now constrained.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 14.5x trailing Core FFO and 17.0x Core AFFO for a 4.28% dividend that takes only about 73% of AFFO. Management sold its oldest, lowest-yielding buildings at a 5.6% FFO yield, repaid about $0.9B of revolver and commercial paper, bought newer Sun Belt communities and is buying back stock at a 6.9% Core FFO yield. That is what a landlord should do when private and public markets disagree about value. The leading indicators are turning: new-lease pricing improved from −5.5% in Q1 to −3.3% in Q2 and −2.0% in Q3, blended lease pricing turned positive at +1.0%, and net debt to EBITDAre fell to 4.0x. Management says it has no further acquisitions planned in 2026, which leaves cash for the buyback.

Bear Case

Core FFO has been flat for three years ($6.85, $6.88, a guided $6.75) and is going down this year. Same-property NOI is still shrinking, and Camden has just cut its full-year revenue midpoint to 0.00%. New leases still sign below the previous rent, and the Q4 Core FFO guide of $1.63–$1.67 is below Q3's $1.71. NAREIT FFO guidance fell from $6.61 in February to $6.15. The California sale shrinks FFO per share until every dollar is put back to work, and the stock fell 4.1% after the print. A landlord trading at a cheap multiple on flat-to-falling FFO is not a bargain until the FFO starts to grow.

Expense growth against flat revenue. Guidance of +1.75% expenses against 0.00% revenue means NOI shrinks even if rents stabilise. Insurance, taxes and payroll in the Sun Belt do not reset when rents do.
Industry Commentary
The Sun Belt supply wave
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The same migration that made Sun Belt apartments the best trade of 2021 pulled a wave of new construction into those markets, and new supply takes new-lease pricing first. Camden's numbers show the damage and the healing at once: new leases at −5.3%, −5.5%, −3.3% and then −2.0% over the last four reported quarters, renewals up to +3.6%, occupancy at 95.6%. On the Q3 call management said apartment completions in Sun Belt markets have fallen to a seven-year low (call transcript as published by Earnings Whispers). The honest read is that the rate of decline is improving, and the level is still negative.

Revenue-management litigation
ONGOING HEADWIND

Apartment landlords across the sector have faced class actions over the use of shared revenue-management software to set rents. Camden reported a $53.0 million settlement fund with preliminary court approval with its Q2 results. The Q3 release shows $50.6 million of legal costs and settlements in the first nine months. The cash cost is manageable; the lasting cost is that the pricing tools the industry used to push rents are now constrained, just as operators need pricing power to come back.

Private vs public pricing — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The California sale is a live data point on what private buyers will pay for apartments: a 5.6% trailing FFO yield (per the Q2 call) on 19-year-old buildings. The public market prices all of Camden at a 6.9% Core FFO yield. The bull framing: that gap is the opportunity, and management is taking advantage of it by selling buildings and buying back shares. If the market closed the gap, the stock would move toward the analysts' $109.56 average. The bear framing: California buildings in supply-constrained coastal markets deserve a lower yield than a Sun Belt portfolio still fighting new supply, so the gap is not mispricing. It is the right price for Camden's growth. The way to settle it is not the multiple. It is two numbers Camden reports every quarter: new-lease pricing and same-property NOI growth. Both need to turn positive together.
