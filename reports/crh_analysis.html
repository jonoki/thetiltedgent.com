01 Company Overview

CRH is the largest building-materials business in North America and one of the largest in the world: aggregates, cement, asphalt, readymix concrete, precast products, and — increasingly — the paving and construction services that install them. FY2025 revenue was $37.45B, up 5.3% on FY2024's $35.57B, with Adjusted EBITDA of $7.68B across the three reported segments. The trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $38.63B, up 6.3%. At $62.71B of market capitalisation the company ranks #410 globally. All figures on this page are US GAAP and US dollars — CRH moved its primary listing to the NYSE in September 2023 and now reports on that basis; comparisons that reach back before 2023 cross an IFRS-to-US-GAAP boundary, and the company flags this itself.

The central debate is not whether the business is good. It is why the market pays 16.6× trailing earnings for it while paying 30.9× for Vulcan and 33.4× for Martin Marietta — three companies selling crushed stone out of the same kind of hole in the same states. The bull answer is that the discount is a legacy of the London listing and will close as index money and US coverage catch up. The bear answer is that CRH is not a pure-play: roughly a third of revenue is International, a fifth is a manufactured-products business whose adjusted EBITDA just fell 8%, and the growth has been bought — $4.1B of M&A in 2025 alone, with organic revenue in the flagship Americas Materials segment actually negative. Section 02 and Section 04 take that apart.

Revenue Mix

Start here, because this is the whole company. Aggregates — crushed stone, sand and gravel — are the base material under every road, foundation and concrete pour. They are also, uniquely among industrial commodities, economically unshippable: haulage cost per tonne rises fast enough with distance that a quarry's market is a radius, not a country. And you cannot open a new one where it matters. Permitting a quarry near a growing city takes years and frequently fails on zoning and community opposition. So the asset that already sits inside the radius is a permitted, non-replicable local monopoly-ish position, and the pricing power that follows is structural rather than cyclical.

The numbers say exactly that. FY2025 aggregates: volumes +4%, average price +4%. Q2 2026: volumes +2%, price +5%. Price is outrunning volume — the franchise is not being carried by a construction boom, it is being carried by the ability to charge more per tonne in a flat-to-modest volume environment. Segment revenue rose 10% and adjusted EBITDA 12% in Q2 2026, margin expanding.

Then comes the vertical integration, which is the second half of the story: CRH does not just sell the rock, it owns the asphalt plants, the readymix plants and — critically — the paving and construction services that consume them. That means it bids the job, wins the job, and supplies the job from its own quarry. FY2025 product-level detail: asphalt volumes +4% with price flat, readymix volumes +3%, price +2%, cement volumes +1%, price +1%. Aggregates is the only line where both volume and price ran at 4%. The margin lives in the hole in the ground; the downstream is how you make sure someone buys what comes out of it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
PERMITTED RESERVES HAULAGE RADIUS LOCAL PRICING POWER VERTICAL INTEGRATION MATERIALS + SERVICES BID M&A INTEGRATION MACHINE SCALE ACROSS 4,000 SITES

The moat is geology plus paperwork. Crushed stone is worth a few tens of dollars a tonne at the quarry gate and the cost of trucking it rises steeply with distance, so the economic market for any given quarry is a circle perhaps 25 to 50 miles wide. Inside that circle, price is set by whoever holds a permitted reserve — and permits near growing metropolitan areas are close to unobtainable, because the people who would object to a new quarry are exactly the people the quarry would serve. The result is that CRH's existing pits become more valuable as cities grow toward them, which is the opposite of how most industrial assets age. This is why aggregates pricing has been able to run +4% in FY2025 and +5% in Q2 2026 against volume growth of only +4% and +2% — the price is not demand-driven, it is scarcity-driven.

The second layer is vertical integration. CRH turns its own rock into asphalt and readymix and then, through its paving and construction services businesses, bids the highway job that lays it. That converts a commodity sale into a contracted service revenue stream and captures margin at two steps instead of one. It also gives visibility: management can see the bidding pipeline before the tonnes move. COO Randy Lake's characterisation on the Q2 2026 call was that "the quantum that we're bidding as well as what we've secured in terms of volumes, they're both up year-over-year" — a statement about forward work, not backward revenue.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Growth is bought, not grown. The single most uncomfortable disclosure on this page: FY2025 Americas Materials revenue growth of $856M decomposes into −$73M organic and +$961M from acquisitions. CRH spent $4.1B across 38 acquisitions in 2025 and a further $1.4B across 17 acquisitions in H1 2026. A serial acquirer whose organic line is flat is buying its earnings growth, and the market prices bought growth at a lower multiple than earned growth — deservedly.
▸
Public funding concentration. Infrastructure demand is a political variable. Management points to roughly 40% of the $1.2 trillion IIJA (Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act — the 2021 US federal infrastructure funding package) still unspent, which is genuine visibility, but it is also a countdown clock: the programme has a horizon, reauthorisation is a legislative event, and state DOT (Department of Transportation) budgets are set annually by legislatures with their own fiscal pressures. A funding air-pocket after the IIJA runs off is the sector's known unknown.
▸
Residential is already in a downturn. Americas Building Solutions saw adjusted EBITDA fall 8% in Q2 2026 on weak residential demand, and management does not expect recovery before late 2027 at the earliest. That is not a forecast of a coming risk — it is a description of a live one, with an admitted duration of more than a year.
▸
Leverage and buyback suspension. Net debt rose from $10.5B to $14.2B during 2025 (about 1.8× adjusted EBITDA), and to fund the $8.5B all-cash Arcosa acquisition CRH has paused the share buyback. The repurchase programme was a meaningful part of the total-return case — $1,181M of buybacks in 2025 alone — and it has been traded for balance-sheet capacity. If the deal underdelivers, shareholders have paid twice.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The stack, in the right order. Begin with the tonne. CRH sells aggregates at prices that rose 4% in FY2025 and 5% in Q2 2026 against volume growth of 4% and 2% — price ahead of volume, in a flat market, which is the signature of scarcity rather than demand. That is the permitted-reserve argument in one line, and it is the reason Americas Materials carries a 23.5% adjusted EBITDA margin on $17.03B of FY2025 revenue, the best of the three segments. Vertical integration then extends the capture: CRH's own asphalt and readymix plants consume its own aggregates, and its paving and services businesses install the result. FY2025 downstream pricing was weaker than aggregates pricing across the board — asphalt price flat on +4% volume, readymix +2% on +3%, cement +1% on +1% — which tells you the downstream is a volume and share business that exists to guarantee the pull-through on the upstream, not a margin engine in its own right.

Then the uncomfortable part: how much of this is organic? FY2025 Americas Materials revenue grew $856M. The disclosed decomposition is −$73M organic and +$961M from acquisitions. Group-wide, CRH spent $4.1B across 38 acquisitions in 2025 (largest: Eco Material Technologies at $2.1B) and $1.4B across 17 acquisitions in H1 2026. Net debt went from $10.5B to $14.2B over 2025, about 1.8× adjusted EBITDA. Nothing here is reckless — 1.8× is a comfortable level and bolt-on aggregates deals in a fragmented industry are the textbook use of a strong balance sheet — but it changes what you are underwriting. You are not buying a company whose quarries are throwing off more tonnes each year. You are buying a company whose management is competent at buying other people's quarries, and whose ROIC of 9.77% is the running scoreboard for whether that is creating value.

The funding backdrop. Management's stated visibility rests on roughly 40% of the $1.2 trillion IIJA still being unspent, on state DOT programmes, and on a new demand source that did not exist in the last cycle: data centres. CRH says it is active on 200 data-centre projects, 85% of them within 25 miles of a CRH facility — which is the haulage-radius moat pointed at a growth market rather than a mature one. On forward work, COO Randy Lake's Q2 2026 framing was that "the quantum that we're bidding as well as what we've secured in terms of volumes, they're both up year-over-year." That is a statement of confidence into 2027, but note what it is not: it is not a dollar backlog figure, and CRH does not publish one on the sources fetched. The visibility is management's, and you take it on their word.

Capital returns, and what was traded away. In 2025 CRH returned $2.18B to shareholders — $996M of dividends and $1,181M of buybacks — against $2.71B of capex. The quarterly dividend was raised 5% to $0.39, a 1.66% yield on a 23.5% payout ratio, which is conservatively covered and has room. The buyback is a different story: it has been paused to fund Arcosa, after cumulative returns of roughly $10B since 2018. Year-to-date 2026 returns are $1.2B combined. So the shareholder is being asked to accept a suspended repurchase in exchange for 35 million tons of annual aggregates capacity in Dallas and Phoenix and $175M of promised year-three synergies. That may well be the right trade — aggregates reserves in fast-growing Sun Belt metros are exactly the asset that cannot be recreated — but it is a trade, and the stock's 12% slide from the June announcement into July says the market wants proof.

Residential: the weak end. Americas Building Solutions is the segment with residential exposure and it is contracting — Q2 2026 revenue −2%, adjusted EBITDA −8%, hurt by divestitures, weak residential and haulage cost inflation. The FY2025 outlook language was that repair-and-remodel is resilient while new-build stays subdued; the Q2 2026 call sharpened that to no residential recovery expected before late 2027 at the earliest. At 19.0% of revenue, this is not existential. It is, however, roughly a fifth of the company operating in a downturn management has already told you will last more than another year.

Valuation — the gap that defines this stock. At $94.26 on $5.67 of trailing EPS, CRH trades at 16.6×. Vulcan trades at 30.9× and Martin Marietta at 33.4×. On enterprise value the gap narrows but does not close: CRH's EV of $79.50B against 10.28× EV/EBITDA is a mid-cycle industrial multiple, not a franchise multiple. Three explanations compete. (i) Mix — 35.5% of revenue is International at a 16.6% EBITDA margin, and 19.0% is a residential-exposed products business currently shrinking; only 45.5% is the pure aggregates-led franchise the peers are 100% of. (ii) Growth quality — organic revenue in that flagship segment was negative in FY2025, and the market discounts acquired growth. (iii) Domicile and listing history — an Irish-incorporated company that has been NYSE-primary for only three years, still carrying a London line under review for delisting, with a tax rate stepping from 21.6% to ~24%. The bull case is that (iii) is a fading artefact and (i) is improving fast, since International's margin expanded 200bp in a single year. The bear case is that (ii) is the real reason and it does not fade. At 16.6× on guidance of $5.60–$6.05 EPS, you are not paying for the resolution — which is the most interesting thing about the setup.

Bull Case

A structurally advantaged aggregates franchise — permitted reserves inside the haulage radius of growing cities, pricing up 5% on volumes up 2% — trading at 16.6× earnings against 31–33× for the two US pure-plays. International margin expanded 200bp in FY2025 and another 70bp in Q2 2026, closing the mix discount from the inside. Roughly 40% of $1.2 trillion of IIJA money is still unspent, bid volumes are up year-over-year, and 200 data-centre projects sit within 25 miles of CRH plants. Arcosa adds 35 million tons in Dallas and Phoenix with $175M of targeted synergies. Every one of 23 analysts carries a target above the price, average $138.32.

Bear Case

Organic revenue in the flagship Americas Materials segment was negative $73M in FY2025 — all the growth was the $961M of acquisitions, funded by net debt rising from $10.5B to $14.2B. ROIC is 9.77% and an $8.5B all-cash deal has to clear it. The buyback is suspended. A fifth of the company is in a residential downturn management says lasts until late 2027. The IIJA is a finite programme with a reauthorisation cliff behind it, the 2026 tax rate steps up ~240bp, and the stock is 4.5% off its 52-week low and down 15.77% over a year while every analyst target sits 11–75% higher. The market is voting against the sell side.

Top 3 Risks

01
The acquisition machine stalls or misprices. With organic growth around zero in the core segment, M&A is the growth algorithm. $5.5B deployed across 55 deals in 18 months, an $8.5B all-cash acquisition pending, ROIC below 10% and the buyback switched off to pay for it. If Arcosa's $175M of synergies slip or the multiple paid proves rich, there is no organic engine underneath to cover it.
02
Infrastructure funding air-pocket. The visible pipeline rests on the unspent 40% of the IIJA plus annually-appropriated state DOT budgets. Both have horizons. Highway reauthorisation is a legislative event, not a contractual one, and a gap between programmes would hit exactly the segment carrying the best margin — with no offset available from residential, which is already down.
03
The discount is structural, not temporary. The entire bull case rests on 16.6× converging toward the 31–33× the pure-plays get. But if the market is discounting mix (35.5% International at 16.6% margins), acquired growth, and a cyclical products business rather than merely a stale listing history, then the gap never closes and the return is limited to earnings growth of roughly 10% a year plus a 1.66% yield — a fine outcome, but not the 47% the consensus target implies.
  Industry Commentary
Permitting Scarcity Is the Whole Industry's Moat
EMERGING TAILWIND
Aggregates is the rare commodity where the constraint is regulatory rather than geological — there is plenty of rock, but almost no way to get permission to dig a new pit near a city. The consequence shows up directly in price: CRH's aggregates pricing rose 4% in FY2025 and 5% in Q2 2026 while volumes rose only 4% and 2%. Downstream products, where anyone can build a plant, priced far worse in the same year — asphalt flat, cement +1%, readymix +2%. The spread between those two numbers is the moat, quantified. It is also why every operator in the sector is consolidating: buying permitted reserves is the only way to add them.
Residential Is Down and Told You So
ONGOING HEADWIND
The building-materials complex has split in two. Public infrastructure and industrial/data-centre work is funded and busy; new residential construction is not. CRH's own Americas Building Solutions segment — the residential-exposed one — saw adjusted EBITDA fall 8% in Q2 2026, and management's guidance is that recovery does not arrive before late 2027 at the earliest. The sector-wide evidence is in the share prices: CRH −15.77% over twelve months, Vulcan 21% below its 52-week high, Martin Marietta 28% below its own. Three companies with different mixes, all repriced together, which points at a demand-expectation reset rather than company-specific stumbles.
Consolidation Has Replaced Organic Growth
STRUCTURAL SHIFT
The industry's growth is now bought. CRH spent $4.1B on 38 deals in 2025 and $1.4B on 17 in H1 2026, and has agreed to $8.5B for Arcosa; Martin Marietta's 14.8% TTM revenue growth is itself acquisition-assisted. Bull framing: a fragmented industry of family-owned quarries is being rolled up by three or four buyers with cost of capital advantages, and each acquired pit inherits the acquirer's pricing discipline — this is value creation, and CRH at 16.6× is the cheapest way to own it. Bear framing: when organic revenue in your best segment is −$73M and reported growth is +$961M of purchases, the multiple should be lower, because you are capitalising deal-making skill rather than an asset's own compounding. Which framing is right will be settled by CRH's ROIC — 9.77% today — over the three years after Arcosa closes.
