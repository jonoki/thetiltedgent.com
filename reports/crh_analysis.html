01 Company Overview

CRH is the largest building-materials business in North America and one of the largest in the world: aggregates (crushed stone, sand and gravel), cement, asphalt, readymix concrete, precast products, and — increasingly — the paving and construction services that install them. FY2025 revenue was $37.45B, up 5.3% on FY2024's $35.57B, with Adjusted EBITDA of $7.68B across the three reported segments. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through September 2026 is $38.96B (FY2025 plus the first nine months of 2026, less the first nine months of 2025). On October 29 CRH reported third-quarter revenue of $11.40B, up 3%, and cut its 2026 guidance: adjusted EBITDA to $7.8–8.0B from $8.1–8.5B. All figures on this page are US GAAP and US dollars unless marked adjusted — CRH moved its primary listing to the NYSE in September 2023 and now reports on that basis; comparisons that reach back before 2023 cross an IFRS-to-US-GAAP boundary, and the company flags this itself.

The central debate is not whether the business is good. It is why the market pays 17.1× trailing earnings for it while paying 33.3× for Vulcan (Martin Marietta is at 28.8× its FY2025 earnings) — three companies selling crushed stone out of the same kind of hole in the same states. The bull answer is that the discount is a legacy of the old London listing and will close as index money and US coverage catch up. The bear answer is that CRH is not a pure-play (a company in that one business only): roughly a third of revenue is International, a fifth is a manufactured-products business whose adjusted EBITDA fell 18% in the third quarter, and the growth has been bought — $4.1B of M&A in 2025 alone, with organic revenue in the flagship Americas Materials segment actually negative. The October print added a new strain: CEO Jim Mintern told the call that diesel and other costs jumped from July and are hard to recover late in the year (MarketBeat's call summary), so profit guidance came down while volumes held. Offsetting that, the board authorised a new $6B buyback and the $8.5B Arcosa acquisition closed ahead of schedule. Section 02 and Section 04 take it apart.

Revenue Mix

Start here, because this is the whole company. Aggregates — crushed stone, sand and gravel — are the base material under every road, foundation and concrete pour. They are also, uniquely among industrial commodities, economically unshippable: haulage cost per tonne rises fast enough with distance that a quarry's market is a radius, not a country. And you cannot open a new one where it matters. Permitting a quarry near a growing city takes years and frequently fails on zoning and community opposition. So the asset that already sits inside the radius is a permitted, non-replicable local monopoly-ish position, and the pricing power that follows is structural rather than cyclical.

The numbers say exactly that. FY2025 aggregates: volumes +4%, average price +4%. Q3 2026: volumes +2%, price +5%. Price is outrunning volume — the franchise is not being carried by a construction boom, it is being carried by the ability to charge more per tonne in a flat-to-modest volume environment. Q3 segment revenue rose 6% (acquisitions +$373M, divestitures −$83M, organic +$65M) to $5,985M, but adjusted EBITDA rose only 1% to $1,575M and the margin slipped from 27.6% to 26.3%: cement price fell 2% and asphalt volumes fell 2%, and fuel and haulage costs rose.

Then comes the vertical integration, which is the second half of the story: CRH does not just sell the rock, it owns the asphalt plants, the readymix plants and — critically — the paving and construction services that consume them. That means it bids the job, wins the job, and supplies the job from its own quarry. FY2025 product-level detail: asphalt volumes +4% with price flat, readymix volumes +3%, price +2%, cement volumes +1%, price +1%. In Q3 2026 asphalt price rose 7% and readymix price 3%. The margin lives in the hole in the ground; the downstream is how you make sure someone buys what comes out of it.

Organic vs bought: FY2025 segment growth of $856M breaks down as organic revenue −$73M and acquisitions +$961M. The flagship segment did not grow organically in 2025. It grew because CRH bought companies.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
PERMITTED RESERVES HAULAGE RADIUS LOCAL PRICING POWER VERTICAL INTEGRATION MATERIALS + SERVICES BID M&A INTEGRATION MACHINE SCALE ACROSS 4,000 SITES

The moat is geology plus paperwork. Crushed stone is worth a few tens of dollars a tonne at the quarry gate and the cost of trucking it rises steeply with distance, so the economic market for any given quarry is a circle perhaps 25 to 50 miles wide. Inside that circle, price is set by whoever holds a permitted reserve — and permits near growing metropolitan areas are close to unobtainable, because the people who would object to a new quarry are exactly the people the quarry would serve. The result is that CRH's existing pits become more valuable as cities grow toward them, which is the opposite of how most industrial assets age. This is why aggregates pricing has been able to run +4% in FY2025 and +5% in Q3 2026 against volume growth of only +4% and +2% — the price is not demand-driven, it is scarcity-driven. For 2027 the call summary has COO Randy Lake expecting high-single-digit aggregates price increases.

The second layer is vertical integration. CRH turns its own rock into asphalt and readymix and then, through its paving and construction services businesses, bids the highway job that lays it. That converts a commodity sale into a contracted service revenue stream and captures margin at two steps instead of one. It also gives visibility: management can see the bidding pipeline before the tonnes move. On the Q3 call Lake said infrastructure bidding and backlogs are in line with last year (as summarised by MarketBeat) — a statement about forward work, not backward revenue.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Growth is bought, not grown. The single most uncomfortable disclosure on this page: FY2025 Americas Materials revenue growth of $856M decomposes into −$73M organic and +$961M from acquisitions. CRH spent $4.1B across 38 acquisitions in 2025 and a further 27 acquisitions for about $1.9B in the first nine months of 2026. The same pattern held in Q3: Americas Materials revenue rose $373M from acquisitions against $65M organic. A serial acquirer whose organic line is flat is buying its earnings growth, and the market prices bought growth at a lower multiple than earned growth — deservedly.
▸
Public funding concentration. Infrastructure demand is a political variable. Management points to roughly 40% of the $1.2 trillion IIJA (Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act — the 2021 US federal infrastructure funding package) still unspent, which is genuine visibility, but it is also a countdown clock: the programme has a horizon, reauthorisation is a legislative event, and state DOT (Department of Transportation) budgets are set annually by legislatures with their own fiscal pressures. A funding air-pocket after the IIJA runs off is the sector's known unknown.
▸
Residential is already in a downturn. Americas Building Solutions saw adjusted EBITDA fall 8% in Q2 2026 and 18% in Q3 2026, and the Q3 release says management expects residential construction to stay weak in the near term. That is not a forecast of a coming risk — it is a description of a live one.
▸
Leverage and cost inflation. Net debt was $15.04B at September 30, 2026 (the Q3 release: $19.62B of debt and $0.63B of finance leases, less $5.11B of cash and $0.11B of net derivative assets), up from $14.15B at December 31, 2025 — and that is before the $8.5B Arcosa purchase completed on October 23, financed with a $2.5B term loan, a $3.3B bridge facility and cash on hand. The release does not state a leverage ratio and says CRH intends to keep a strong investment-grade credit rating. On the cost side, the call summary has Mintern saying diesel was up about 40% from its July low and that such inflation is difficult to recover late in the year; the guidance cut is the result. Against that, the board authorised a new $6B buyback to the end of 2030.

Notable One-Off Items. Four things distort the reported numbers. (1) Seasonality is extreme. Q1 2026 produced a net loss of $183M on $7,370M of revenue and Q1 2025 a $101M loss — Northern-hemisphere paving does not happen in February. (3) A changed definition. From Q3 2026 CRH's adjusted EBITDA also excludes substantial acquisition and integration costs and transformation costs ($4M and $49M in the quarter), so it is not strictly comparable with earlier quarters. The Q3 effective tax rate was about 28.7% and 2026 guidance assumes about 27% (previous edition: about 24%). The accounting basis also changed once: CRH reported under IFRS through 2022 and US GAAP from 2023, and FY2022's $3,902M net income sits on the far side of that line and is not cleanly comparable.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 close of $48.68 to the October 30, 2026 close of $90.81, CRH returned +86.5% in price terms over the five-year window — a compound annual rate of about 13.5% before dividends (November 30, 2021 to October 30, 2026), which is a strong outcome for a heavy-materials cyclical. But the shape matters more than the total. The stock fell 33.8% from November 2021 to the September 2022 monthly close of $32.23 as rising interest rates hit everything tied to the economic cycle, then rose 287% from that low to the December 2025 close of $124.80 — the highest monthly close in the series — as the NYSE listing, S&P 500 inclusion (December 22, 2025) and the US-infrastructure story all landed. It has since given back 27.2%. The September 2026 month-end close of $82.80 was the lowest since June 2024; the stock then touched a 52-week low of $78.62 on October 14 and has recovered 15.5% from it, to $90.81, after the third-quarter print. Over the twelve months from the October 31, 2025 close of $119.10 it is down 23.8%.

Relative performance. In the last two months CRH has lagged its two US peers: from the September 4 edition's closes to October 30, Vulcan rose 7.1% ($262.63 to $281.25), Martin Marietta rose 5.0% ($514.77 to $540.41) and CRH fell 3.7% ($94.26 to $90.81). 08 Summary & Key Risks

The stack, in the right order. Begin with the tonne. CRH sells aggregates at prices that rose 4% in FY2025 and 5% in Q3 2026 against volume growth of 4% and 2% — price ahead of volume, in a flat market, which is the signature of scarcity rather than demand. That is the permitted-reserve argument in one line, and it is the reason Americas Materials carried a 23.5% adjusted EBITDA margin on $17.03B of FY2025 revenue, the best of the three segments. Vertical integration then extends the capture: CRH's own asphalt and readymix plants consume its own aggregates, and its paving and services businesses install the result. FY2025 downstream pricing was weaker than aggregates pricing across the board — asphalt price flat on +4% volume, readymix +2% on +3%, cement +1% on +1% — which tells you the downstream businesses exist to make sure the quarries' output gets sold, not to earn big margins of their own.

Then the uncomfortable part: how much of this is organic? FY2025 Americas Materials revenue grew $856M. The disclosed decomposition is −$73M organic and +$961M from acquisitions. Group-wide, CRH spent $4.1B across 38 acquisitions in 2025 (largest: Eco Material Technologies at $2.1B) and about $1.9B across 27 acquisitions in the first nine months of 2026, before the $8.5B Arcosa deal. Net debt was $14.15B at the end of 2025 and $15.04B at September 30, 2026. Nothing here is reckless — small add-on aggregates deals in an industry of many small owners are the textbook use of a strong balance sheet — but it changes what you are betting on. You are not buying a company whose quarries are throwing off more tonnes each year. You are buying a company whose management is competent at buying other people's quarries, and whose ROIC of 9.36% is the running scoreboard for whether that is creating value.

The funding backdrop. Management's stated visibility rests on roughly 40% of the $1.2 trillion IIJA still to be deployed, on state DOT (Department of Transportation) programmes, and on demand sources that did not exist in the last cycle: data centres, grid build-out and factory construction. The Q3 release says management expects public infrastructure and reindustrialisation to support demand and calls the 2027 outlook positive across key end-markets; on the call, as summarised by MarketBeat, Lake said bidding and backlogs are in line with last year. That is a statement of confidence into 2027, but note what it is not: it is not a dollar backlog figure. The visibility is management's, and you take it on their word.

Capital returns, and what changed in October. In 2025 CRH returned $2.18B to shareholders — $996M of dividends and $1,181M of buybacks — against $2.71B of capex. In the first nine months of 2026 it paid $781M of dividends and repurchased $693M of stock (6.2M shares); in Q3 alone it bought back 0.8M shares for $86M (10-Q). The quarterly dividend was raised 5% to $0.39, a 1.72% yield on a 27.5% payout ratio. On October 29 the board authorised a new buyback of up to $6B running to the end of 2030, which CFO Aylwyn Bryan said on the call fits a $40B capital-allocation framework through 2030 (MarketBeat summary). So the shareholder is now being offered both: the Arcosa platform — whose engineered-structures revenue management expects to grow at double digits (MarketBeat call summary) — and a bigger repurchase programme. Whether the balance sheet carries both is the question the Feb 2027 results will start to answer.

Residential: the weak end. Americas Building Solutions is the segment with residential exposure and it is contracting — Q3 2026 revenue −3%, adjusted EBITDA −18% (after −2% and −8% in Q2), hurt by divestitures, soft residential demand, cost inflation and a year-earlier land-sale gain. The Q3 release says residential construction is expected to stay weak in the near term; on the call, as summarised by MarketBeat, Mintern attributed the weakness to affordability. At 19.0% of FY2025 revenue, this is not existential. It is, however, roughly a fifth of the company operating in a downturn.

Valuation — the gap that defines this stock. At $90.81 on $5.31 of trailing EPS, CRH trades at 17.1×. Vulcan trades at 33.3× and Martin Marietta at 28.8× its FY2025 earnings. On enterprise value (market cap plus net debt) the gap narrows but does not close: CRH's EV of about $76.9B is roughly 9.9× EBITDA (our calculation: $60.41B market cap plus $16.47B of net debt including leases, divided by the trailing EBITDA implied by stockanalysis.com's 10.41× at its own price), a middling industrial multiple, not a franchise multiple. Three explanations compete. (i) Mix — 35.5% of FY2025 revenue is International at a lower margin, and 19.0% is a residential-exposed products business currently shrinking; only 45.5% is the pure aggregates-led franchise the peers are 100% of. (ii) Growth quality — organic revenue in that flagship segment was negative in FY2025, and the market discounts acquired growth. (iii) Domicile — an Irish-incorporated company that has been NYSE-primary for only three years, with a tax rate stepping from about 22% in 2025 (income tax $1,041M on pre-tax income of $4,805M, FY2025 release) to about 27% in the 2026 guidance. The bull case is that (iii) is a fading artefact and (i) is improving fast. The bear case is that (ii) is the real reason and it does not fade. At 17.1× on a guidance range of $5.37–$5.59 adjusted EPS, you are not paying for the resolution — which is the most interesting thing about the setup, although this quarter's cut shows that the earnings number itself is not safe.

Bull Case

A structurally advantaged aggregates franchise — permitted reserves inside the haulage radius of growing cities, pricing up 5% on volumes up 2% — trading at 17.1× earnings against 29–33× for the two US pure-plays. International margin expanded 2 points in FY2025 and another 1.1 points in Q3 2026. Roughly 40% of $1.2 trillion of IIJA money is still to be deployed, bidding and backlogs are in line with last year, and the closed Arcosa deal adds a grid-and-data-centre structures business and a $175M synergy target. A new $6B buyback runs to 2030. Every published target is above the price (the lowest is $95), and the average is about $120.

Bear Case

Organic revenue in the flagship Americas Materials segment was negative $73M in FY2025 — all the growth was the $961M of acquisitions, funded by rising net debt, now $15.04B before the $8.5B Arcosa cheque. ROIC is 9.36% and that deal has to clear it. 2026 guidance was cut in October — adjusted EBITDA $7.8–8.0B from $8.1–8.5B, diluted EPS $4.63–$4.85 from $5.60–$6.05 — because diesel and haulage costs outran pricing. A fifth of the company is in a residential downturn. The IIJA is a finite programme with a reauthorisation cliff behind it, the 2026 tax rate steps up from about 22% to about 27%, and the stock is down 23.8% over twelve months while every analyst target sits 5–76% higher.

Top 3 Risks

01
The acquisition machine stalls or misprices. With organic growth around zero in the core segment, M&A is the growth algorithm. About $6.0B deployed across 65 deals in 2025 and the first nine months of 2026, an $8.5B all-cash acquisition just closed, a $6B buyback authorised on top, and ROIC below 10%. If Arcosa's $175M of synergies slip or the multiple paid proves rich, there is no organic engine underneath to cover it.
02
Infrastructure funding air-pocket, and costs. The visible pipeline rests on the unspent 40% of the IIJA plus annually-appropriated state DOT budgets. Both have horizons. Highway reauthorisation is a legislative event, not a contractual one. In the meantime, the October cut shows the second exposure: fuel, energy and haulage costs that cannot be passed through quickly.
03
The discount is structural, not temporary. The entire bull case rests on 17.1× converging toward the 29–33× the pure-plays get. But if the market is discounting mix (35.5% International), acquired growth, and a cyclical products business rather than a stale listing history, then the gap never closes and the return is limited to earnings growth of roughly 10% a year plus a 1.72% yield — a fine outcome, but not the 32% the consensus target implies.
  Industry Commentary
Permitting Scarcity Is the Whole Industry's Moat
EMERGING TAILWIND
Aggregates is the rare commodity where the constraint is regulatory rather than geological — there is plenty of rock, but almost no way to get permission to dig a new pit near a city. The consequence shows up directly in price: CRH's aggregates pricing rose 4% in FY2025 and 5% in Q3 2026 while volumes rose only 4% and 2%. Downstream products, where anyone can build a plant, priced far worse in FY2025 — asphalt flat, cement +1%, readymix +2%. The spread between those two numbers is the moat, quantified. It is also why every operator in the sector is consolidating: buying permitted reserves is the only way to add them.
Residential Is Down and Told You So
ONGOING HEADWIND
The building-materials industry has split in two. Public infrastructure and industrial/data-centre work is funded and busy; new residential construction is not. CRH's own Americas Building Solutions segment — the residential-exposed one — saw adjusted EBITDA fall 18% in Q3 2026, and the Q3 release expects residential to stay weak in the near term. The share prices show the market's mood: over the two months to October 30, Vulcan rose 7.1% and Martin Marietta 5.0% while CRH fell 3.7%, and all three rose about 5% on the day of CRH's print.
Consolidation Has Replaced Organic Growth
STRUCTURAL SHIFT
The industry's growth is now bought. CRH spent $4.1B on 38 deals in 2025 and about $1.9B on 27 in the first nine months of 2026, and has closed the $8.5B Arcosa deal; Martin Marietta's 14.8% TTM revenue growth is itself acquisition-assisted. Bull framing: a fragmented industry of family-owned quarries is being rolled up by three or four buyers with cost-of-capital advantages, and each acquired pit inherits the acquirer's pricing discipline — this is value creation, and CRH at 17.1× is the cheapest way to own it. Bear framing: when organic revenue in your best segment is −$73M and reported growth is +$961M of purchases, the multiple should be lower, because you are paying for management's skill at buying companies rather than for quarries that grow on their own. Which framing is right will be settled by CRH's ROIC — 9.36% today — over the three years after Arcosa closes.
