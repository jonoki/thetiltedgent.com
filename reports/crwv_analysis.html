01 Company Overview

CoreWeave rents Nvidia GPU compute at industrial scale to AI labs, hyperscalers and enterprises — a "neocloud," meaning a cloud built for one workload (AI training and inference) rather than the general-purpose clouds of Amazon, Microsoft and Google. FY2025 revenue was $5.13B, up 167.9% from $1.92B in FY2024, and revenue for the twelve months to September 2026 was $9.89B, up 130%. At September 30, 2026 it had more than 1.7 gigawatts of active power and more than 4.6 GW under contract. It is worth $29.55B and joined the Nasdaq-100 in June 2026, fifteen months after its March 2025 listing.

The debate on this page is a race between two clocks. On one side is a $132.5B revenue backlog — contracts already signed, mostly with a handful of counterparties — that is roughly 13 times trailing revenue. On the other is about $46.9B of balance-sheet debt (about $66.2B including lease obligations) borrowed to buy the GPUs that serve those contracts, with interest expense consuming 23 cents of every revenue dollar in the third quarter. The contracts are long; the hardware ages fast; the money is expensive, and in October the bond market said so, pushing yields on CoreWeave's unsecured notes to about 15%. Whether the backlog outlives the depreciation schedule and the interest bill is the whole investment case, and Sections 04 and 08 work through it with the company's own numbers.

Nvidia sits on all three sides of CoreWeave's table. It sells CoreWeave the GPUs (it is the dominant supplier; CoreWeave was the first cloud to validate the Vera Rubin NVL72 platform in Q2 2026 and brought multi-rack Vera Rubin systems online in Q3). And it has agreed to buy any unsold capacity up to $6.3B through April 2032, a backstop that converts idle GPUs into revenue and makes the lenders comfortable. Bulls read this as the world's most important AI company underwriting its favourite distribution channel. Bears read it as vendor financing by another name: the supplier funds the buyer's purchases and guarantees the buyer's revenue so that the buyer keeps buying. Both readings are true simultaneously; which dominates depends on whether end demand from labs and enterprises stays ahead of supply.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

NVIDIA PRIORITY ALLOCATION 4.6 GW CONTRACTED POWER $132B TAKE-OR-PAY BACKLOG CONTRACT-BACKED DEBT (DDTL) TIME-TO-DEPLOY SPEED MLPERF PERFORMANCE RECORDS SOFTWARE STACK (W&B, SUNK)

The moat is access and speed, not technology. Anyone can, in principle, buy Nvidia GPUs and rent them out. Almost nobody can get them first, get the power to run them, and get the money to pay for them — simultaneously, at gigawatt scale, on a timeline a frontier lab will sign for. CoreWeave has all three: an allocation relationship with Nvidia that includes an equity stake and a revenue backstop; more than 4.6 GW of contracted power when grid connection is the binding constraint on the whole industry; and a financing machine — the delayed-draw term loan (DDTL) structure, where lenders advance money against specific signed customer contracts — that let it borrow $27.0B in the first nine months of 2026 (after issuance costs; it repaid $7.6B in the same period). Adjusted EBITDA margin of 62% in Q3 says the operating business, before financing and depreciation, is a good one. What it is not is a moat against its own customers: Microsoft, Meta and OpenAI are all building or buying their own capacity, and the moat lasts exactly as long as they cannot build fast enough.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
GPU depreciation versus contract length. The central technical debate. A GPU cluster is depreciated over a set useful life, and the bear argument is that Nvidia's annual product cadence (Hopper → Blackwell → Vera Rubin) makes hardware economically obsolete faster than the accounting schedule, so that reported "adjusted" profits overstate true earnings. The counter-argument is that a five-year take-or-pay contract signed against a specific cluster makes the depreciation schedule irrelevant: the customer has agreed to pay for that hardware regardless of what ships next. That defence holds for the first contract on each cluster. It does not answer what a four-year-old GPU rents for when the contract rolls off — and with D&A of $1,889M in Q3 2026 alone, 52% of revenue, small changes to that answer swing the whole model. Management's answer on the Q3 call was that prices for its older Ampere and Hopper chips rose 30–50% from the second quarter, above their original contract prices; the test comes as more than 130,000 GPUs leave contract in 2027 and about 170,000 in 2028.
▸
Customer concentration and counterparty quality. One customer was 67% of FY2025 revenue; in Q3 2026 the two largest were 30% and 24%. The diversification into OpenAI and Meta helps the concentration ratio, but OpenAI is a private company whose own spending commitments across the industry dwarf its revenue, and the contracts in the backlog are only as good as the payer. The 10-K lists it among its risk factors: "A substantial portion of our revenue is driven by a limited number of our customers."
▸
Financing cost and refinancing risk. Its unsecured notes due 2031 and 2032 carry 9.75% and 9.625% coupons, and in October the market priced them to yield about 15% (CNBC). Net interest expense was $846M in Q3 2026 — 23.1% of revenue, up from $310M a year earlier, and is guided to $1.05–1.15B in Q4. $9.2B of recourse debt is current (due within twelve months) against $4.1B of cash. A current ratio of 0.44 and an Altman Z-score of 0.2 (a bankruptcy-risk score; under 1.8 is the conventional danger zone) both sit in territory that normally signals distress; here they reflect a business that is designed to be refinanced continuously. On the Q3 call CEO Michael Intrator said: "Looking ahead, we will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields, nor do we need to." That design works until credit markets close.
▸
Power and execution. Just over 1.7 GW of the more than 4.6 GW of contracted power is active. Every quarter of delay in energising a site is a quarter of revenue the backlog promised and a quarter of interest paid on hardware that is not yet earning. The FY2026 active-power target is "more than 1.85 gigawatts"; the 2030 objective, in Intrator's 2025 shareholder letter, is "8 plus gigawatts of active power."

Notable One-Off Items

Almost nothing in these numbers is "one-off" in the sense of being non-recurring. The distortions are structural, and they matter more than any single item.

The GAAP-to-adjusted bridge. Q3 2026 GAAP operating income was $207M, the first operating profit since Q3 2025; adjusted operating income was $379M; adjusted EBITDA was $2,256M. The gap between the first two is stock-based compensation of $160M plus $12M of amortisation of acquired intangibles. The gap between the second and third is mostly $1,889M of depreciation and amortisation — the GPUs wearing out. Adjusted EBITDA margin was 62%; adjusted operating margin was 10%, against 16% a year earlier; GAAP net margin was −19%. Which of those numbers you think is "the margin" is the entire disagreement about this stock.

Tax on a loss. Q3 carried an $83M income-tax provision on a pre-tax loss of $626M (a $127M tax benefit in Q3 2025), which is why the net loss of $709M is larger than the pre-tax loss.

Cash capex versus total capex. The cash-flow statement shows $9,441M of cash capex in Q3 2026 and $23,558M for the nine months. On top of that, the 10-Q lists $11,833M of liabilities for equipment additions not yet paid in cash, including equipment financed by its makers (OEM financing) and unpaid capitalised interest. That spending never passes through the cash-flow statement as capex. Both are real; only one appears in free cash flow. Use the higher figure when judging the pace of investment and the lower when judging cash burn.

Net loss. The FY2025 net loss was $(1,167)M ($2.81 a diluted share) in the company's release and 10-K, against $(863)M in FY2024. The trailing-twelve-month loss of $2.53B on this page is the sum of the last four quarters: $452M, $740M, $626M and $709M.

Equity and equity-linked issuance. $2,982M of net private-placement proceeds in 2026 (Nvidia's $2B at $87.20 in January among them); $3.7B of 2.875% convertible notes due 2033 priced in September, converting at about $97.85 a share, with an option for buyers to take $500M more (the Q3 release puts the total raised at about $4.2B); and an at-the-market program, set up on September 17, to sell up to 35M Class A shares through banks over time. Shares outstanding grew 45.7% year over year (stockanalysis.com); Q3 weighted-average shares were 559M against 498M a year earlier.

Core Scientific. The $9B all-stock acquisition of Core Scientific — which would have brought owned data-center power in-house — was terminated on October 30, 2025 after CORZ shareholders voted it down. The companies continue as landlord and tenant under their existing hosting agreements. The practical consequence is that CoreWeave leases more of its power than it would have owned, which shows up in the lease-inclusive debt figure.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and on only 21 monthly points it is a rough one.

Trajectory since listing. From the $40 IPO price to the November 13, 2026 close of $53.13, CRWV is up +32.8% in about nineteen and a half months; from the first month-end close of $37.08 (March 31, 2025) it is up +43.3%, against +34.9% for SPY and +45.0% for QQQ over the same dates (exchange-traded funds that track the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100; price returns from Yahoo Finance closes, dividends excluded). That headline hides a market that has changed its mind about the company roughly every quarter. The stock quadrupled from the March 2025 close to $163.06 at the end of June 2025, then fell 56% to $71.61 by December 2025 as the lockup expired, the Core Scientific deal collapsed and the debt load became the story. It rebounded 56% to $111.60 by April 2026, fell to an intraday low of $60.55 on July 29, 2026, and jumped to $107.73 after the Q2 results in August. Then the bond market turned: the stock lost 37% in October 2026 as yields on its unsecured notes rose toward 15%, and touched a 52-week intraday low of $40.58 on November 5. The Q3 results added 24.7% in one session; two down days followed. Over twelve months CRWV fell 32.2% (from $78.34 on November 13, 2025) while SPY rose 12.3% and QQQ 11.8%, and trailing revenue rose 130%. From the June 2025 month-end peak it is down 67%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with what is signed. Revenue backlog at September 30, 2026 was $132.5B, of which $132.3B is RPO (remaining performance obligations — the accounting term for contracted revenue not yet delivered). That is up 138% from a year earlier, from $104.2B at June 30 and from $66.8B at the end of 2025. Against trailing revenue of $9.89B, the backlog is 13.4 years of current revenue; against the FY2026 guide midpoint of $13.15B it is about ten. The 10-Q puts 36% of the RPO in the next 24 months, 38% in months 25 to 48 and 26% in months 49 to 86. It is also, by construction, revenue that has not been earned yet, from counterparties who can be counted on one hand.

Now the debt. At September 30, 2026 the balance sheet carried $41.6B of recourse debt ($9.2B current) and $5.3B of non-recourse debt — about $46.9B — plus lease obligations (mostly $18.7B of operating leases) that take stockanalysis.com's total to $66.2B. Cash was $4.1B, restricted cash $2.3B. Net cash is −$62.0B, or −$111.47 per share on a $53.13 stock: about 72% of the $91.5B enterprise value is debt. The unsecured notes due 2031 and 2032 carry 9.75% and 9.625% coupons; the DDTL facilities (delayed-draw term loans, where lenders advance against specific signed customer contracts and the GPUs that serve them) are the cheaper, secured layer, and management said on the Q3 call that the weighted average cost of its debt was 7.9% at the end of the quarter, 2.5 percentage points lower than a year earlier. New borrowing came to $27.0B in the first nine months of 2026 (after issuance costs), against $7.6B of repayments; in Q3 alone the company closed a $2.6B DDTL facility and sold about $4.2B of convertible notes.

What the debt costs. Net interest expense was $846M in Q3 2026 — 23.1% of revenue — up from $310M (22.7%) a year earlier, and below the $860–940M guide. For the first nine months it was $2,022M on $8,314M of revenue, 24.3%; FY2025 was $1,229M on $5,131M: 24.0%. Q4 guidance of $1.05–1.15B is about 23% of the revenue the full-year guide implies. The ratio has stopped rising but is not yet falling, because each new gigawatt is financed before it earns. For context, adjusted operating income — the company's own preferred profit measure, after depreciation but before interest — was $379M in the quarter the interest bill was $846M. Interest was 2.2 times the operating profit that is supposed to pay it, down from five times in Q2.

Capex versus cash generation. Operating cash flow is genuinely large: $5,638M in the first nine months of 2026 and $7.20B TTM, helped by customer prepayments. Cash capex was $23,558M in the nine months ($27.62B TTM), with $11.8B more of equipment bought on credit and not yet paid. FY2026 capex is guided to $37–39B — nearly three times guided revenue, and more than five times TTM operating cash flow. Free cash flow is −$20.42B TTM. Every dollar of that gap is bridged by new debt, new shares or customer prepayments: management expects about $9B of prepayments and deposits in the next three months, and CFO Nitin Agrawal said on the call: "we expect to raise less than $5 billion in incremental parent-level capital through 2027." The bull argument is that this is the correct behaviour for a company with $132B of signed demand and a cost of capital below its contracted return; the bear argument is that a business that cannot fund its own growth depends on the credit window staying open, and October showed how fast it can narrow.

The depreciation debate, stated fairly. D&A was $1,889M in Q3 2026 — 52% of revenue — and $4.43B for the nine months. If GPUs are economically useful for as long as the accounting schedule assumes, adjusted EBITDA of $2.26B a quarter is a reasonable proxy for the cash the fleet throws off, and it covered the quarter's interest about 2.7 times. If Nvidia's annual cadence shortens the economic life below the book life, then depreciation is understated, adjusted operating income of $379M is overstated, and GAAP — which still shows a $709M quarterly loss — is the more honest number. Management's evidence on the Q3 call: prices for older Ampere and Hopper GPUs rose 30–50% from Q2, above their original contract prices, and contracts on long-dated deals pay back the hardware in about one to two years at current pricing. The test is the renewal cycle, which starts in earnest when more than 130,000 GPUs come off contract in 2027.

The Nvidia triangle. Nvidia supplies the GPUs, owned 10.66% of the Class A shares at the April 2026 proxy after adding $2B in January 2026 at $87.20, and has committed to buy up to $6.3B of unsold capacity through April 2032. The backstop is why lenders will fund GPUs against contracts: in the worst case, the supplier rents them back. That is either the most credible guarantee in the industry or the definition of circular demand, and reasonable analysts hold each view. Note the price: at the November 13, 2026 close the stock was 39% below what Nvidia paid in January.

Valuation — on what base? There is no P/E, trailing or forward, because there are no earnings: TTM EPS is −$4.70, and the consensus on stockanalysis.com is −$3.89 for 2026 and −$1.75 for 2027. The multiples that exist, at the $53.13 close: 3.0× trailing sales, 2.3× 2026 consensus sales of $13.05B, 9.3× EV/sales, 17.6× EV/EBITDA, 7.3× book. On the year-end run-rate target of $20–21B, the enterprise value is about 4.5× forward run-rate revenue. The only framework that makes the stock cheap is the backlog: $91.5B of enterprise value against $132.5B of contracted revenue spread over up to about seven years, at a 62% adjusted EBITDA margin, is a business that "pays for itself" if every contract is honoured, every site energises on time, and the hardware is worth something at the end. The only framework that makes it expensive is GAAP: a company that lost $2.5B over the last twelve months, pays nearly a quarter of revenue in interest, has a 0.44 current ratio and 46% more shares than a year ago. Both frameworks use the same numbers. The seven-fold spread in analyst price targets is what it looks like when a market cannot agree which one applies.

Bull Case

$132.5B of signed, take-or-pay revenue at 62% adjusted EBITDA margins, up 138% in a year, with the world's most important AI supplier as a shareholder and buyer of last resort. Q3 showed the operating model turning: adjusted operating margin reached 10%, a quarter ahead of plan in the CEO's words, interest fell to 2.2 times adjusted operating profit from five times, and older Hopper and Ampere chips re-priced up, not down. About $9B of customer prepayments is due in the next three months, and management says it needs less than $5B of new parent-level capital through 2027. At 2.3× 2026 sales and about 4.5× the year-end run-rate, with an average target of $115, the stock prices in a funding accident that has not happened.

Bear Case

Interest is 23% of revenue and guided to $1.05–1.15B in Q4; in October the unsecured notes traded to yields near 15%, and S&P rates the company B+ (CNBC). Free cash flow is −$20.4B TTM and FY2026 capex of $37–39B is nearly three times revenue. The current ratio is 0.44 with $10.6B of debt due within a year, and the Altman Z-score is 0.2. Two customers were 54% of Q3 revenue; the diversification is into a private company with its own funding needs. D&A is 52% of revenue and the fleet has never been through a renewal cycle. Shares outstanding rose 46% in a year, an at-the-market program can add 35M more, 22.6% of the float is short, and the stock fell 32% in twelve months while revenue more than doubled — because the market has worked out that doubling revenue also doubles the debt.

Top 3 Risks

01
Refinancing and cost of capital. With about $46.9B of debt, $10.6B of it current, unsecured notes with 9.6–9.75% coupons that the market priced to yield near 15% in October, and a $37–39B capex programme funded almost entirely externally, CoreWeave's viability is a function of credit-market access. A rise in the extra yield investors demand on junk bonds, or a single loan that banks fail to place with investors, would force a choice between slowing the build (breaking the backlog delivery schedule) and diluting at whatever price the market offers.
02
Counterparty and concentration risk. The backlog is only worth its face value if Microsoft, OpenAI and Meta pay on schedule for five years. Microsoft is building its own capacity; OpenAI is a private company with commitments across the industry far larger than its revenue; Meta can cancel projects when the return case changes. A renegotiation by any one of them would hit both revenue and the collateral value backing the DDTL facilities at once.
03
Hardware obsolescence outrunning depreciation. Vera Rubin ships while Hopper-era clusters are still on the books. If renewal pricing on older GPUs comes in far below original contract rates, the residual-value assumption embedded in adjusted EBITDA breaks, the true cost of the fleet is higher than reported, and the equity — already only $7.27 of book value per share against $111 of net debt per share — absorbs the difference first.
  Industry Commentary
Power Is the Bottleneck, Not Chips
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
CoreWeave has more than 4.6 GW under contract and just over 1.7 GW live. The gap is grid interconnection, transformers, cooling and permits — none of which Nvidia can ship. This is why the company tried to buy Core Scientific outright for $9B in 2025 (owned power instead of leased) and why the deal's failure in October 2025 matters more than a normal broken merger: it left CoreWeave a tenant in a landlord's market. The whole neocloud sector — Nebius at $34.0B, IREN at $7.7B at the November 13 close — is now valued on contracted megawatts as much as on revenue, and the entrant with power wins the contract regardless of software.
The Circular-Financing Question Has Not Gone Away
ONGOING HEADWIND
Nvidia invests in CoreWeave, CoreWeave buys Nvidia GPUs with borrowed money, Nvidia guarantees to rent back what CoreWeave cannot sell, and Microsoft, CoreWeave's largest customer in 2025, is itself a major investor in OpenAI, another of its customers. Every link in that chain is a legitimate commercial arrangement; the concern is that the chain as a whole manufactures demand signals that would not exist independently. The November 2025 drawdown (−45% in a month) was this concern being priced, and October 2026 (−37%, with Nebius down 33% and IREN 38% in the month to October 21 per CNBC) was the same concern arriving through the bond market. It will resurface every time an AI-lab funding round wobbles, and CoreWeave, the link that borrows to buy the chips, will be priced on it each time.
Inference Demand and Contract Duration Are Lengthening
EMERGING TAILWIND
The shift from training runs to always-on inference favours committed, multi-year capacity over spot GPU rental — and 26% of CoreWeave's contracted revenue now falls more than four years out (10-Q, September 30, 2026), with weighted duration near five years at the 10-K. Longer contracts are the single best defence against the depreciation critique, because they push the residual-value question further out and make the DDTL collateral sturdier. Bull framing: if contracts keep lengthening and inference keeps growing, the fleet is paid for before it ages and the 17.6× EV/EBITDA of the November 13 close falls fast as EBITDA grows. Bear framing: longer contracts with the same four counterparties deepen concentration rather than curing it, and a five-year commitment from a customer who cannot afford it is a longer default, not a safer one.
