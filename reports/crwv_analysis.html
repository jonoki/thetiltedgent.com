01 Company Overview

CoreWeave rents Nvidia GPU compute at industrial scale to AI labs, hyperscalers and enterprises — a "neocloud," meaning a cloud built for one workload (AI training and inference) rather than the general-purpose clouds of Amazon, Microsoft and Google. FY2025 revenue was $5.13B, up 167.9% from $1.92B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $7.59B, up 115.3%. It runs 51 data centers with 1.5 gigawatts of active power and 4.2 GW under contract, ranks roughly #517 globally by market cap at $49.29B, and was added to the Nasdaq-100 in 2026. It is eighteen months old as a public company.

The debate on this page is a race between two clocks. On one side is a $104.2B revenue backlog — contracts already signed, mostly with a handful of counterparties — that is roughly 14 times trailing revenue. On the other is ~$35B of balance-sheet debt (about $51.6B including lease obligations) borrowed at high-single-digit rates to buy the GPUs that serve those contracts, with interest expense now consuming a quarter of every revenue dollar. The contracts are long; the hardware ages fast; the money is expensive. Whether the backlog outlives the depreciation schedule and the coupon is the whole investment case, and Sections 04 and 08 work through it with the company's own numbers.

Nvidia sits on all three sides of CoreWeave's table. It sells CoreWeave the GPUs (it is the dominant supplier, and CoreWeave was the first cloud to validate the Vera Rubin NVL72 platform in Q2 2026). Bulls read this as the world's most important AI company underwriting its favourite distribution channel. Bears read it as vendor financing by another name: the supplier funds the buyer's purchases and guarantees the buyer's revenue so that the buyer keeps buying. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

NVIDIA PRIORITY ALLOCATION 4.2 GW CONTRACTED POWER $104B TAKE-OR-PAY BACKLOG CONTRACT-BACKED DEBT (DDTL) TIME-TO-DEPLOY SPEED MLPERF PERFORMANCE RECORDS SOFTWARE STACK (W&B, SUNK)

The moat is access and speed, not technology. Anyone can, in principle, buy Nvidia GPUs and rent them out. Almost nobody can get them first, get the power to run them, and get the money to pay for them — simultaneously, at gigawatt scale, on a timeline a frontier lab will sign for. CoreWeave has all three: an allocation relationship with Nvidia that includes an equity stake and a revenue backstop; 4.2 GW of contracted power across 51 sites when grid interconnection is the binding constraint on the whole industry; and a financing machine — the delayed-draw term loan (DDTL) structure, where lenders advance money against specific signed customer contracts — that let it raise roughly $16.7B of net debt proceeds in the first half of 2026 alone. Adjusted EBITDA margin of 59% says the operating business, before financing and depreciation, is a good one. What it is not is a moat against its own customers: Microsoft, Meta and OpenAI are all building or buying their own capacity, and the moat lasts exactly as long as they cannot build fast enough.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
GPU depreciation versus contract length. The central technical debate. A GPU cluster is depreciated over a set useful life — the company's specific assumption was not visible on the pages fetched for this report, so no number is asserted here — and the bear argument is that Nvidia's annual product cadence (Hopper → Blackwell → Vera Rubin) makes hardware economically obsolete faster than the accounting schedule, so that reported "adjusted" profits overstate true earnings. The counter-argument is that a five-year take-or-pay contract signed against a specific cluster makes the depreciation schedule irrelevant: the customer has agreed to pay for that hardware regardless of what ships next. That defence holds for the first contract on each cluster. It does not answer what a four-year-old GPU rents for when the contract rolls off — and with D&A of $1,393M in Q2 2026 alone, 54% of revenue, small changes to that answer swing the whole model.
▸
Customer concentration and counterparty quality. One customer was 67% of FY2025 revenue. The diversification into OpenAI and Meta helps the concentration ratio, but OpenAI is a private company whose own spending commitments across the industry dwarf its revenue, and the contracts in the backlog are only as good as the payer. The company's 10-K names "heavy customer concentration" as a principal risk in its own words.
▸
Financing cost and refinancing risk. Senior notes carry 9.0–9.25% coupons; net interest expense was $640M in Q2 2026 — 24.9% of revenue, up from $267M a year earlier, and guided to $860–940M in Q3. $6.2B of recourse debt is current (due within twelve months) against $5.5B of cash. A current ratio of 0.46 and an Altman Z-score of 0.29 both sit in territory that normally signals distress; here they reflect a business that is designed to be refinanced continuously. That design works until credit markets close.
▸
Power and execution. Only 1.5 GW of 4.2 GW contracted power is active. Every quarter of delay in energising a site is a quarter of revenue the backlog promised and a quarter of interest paid on hardware that is not yet earning. The FY2026 active-power target is "more than 1.85 GW"; the 2030 ambition is 8 GW.

Notable One-Off Items

Almost nothing in these numbers is "one-off" in the sense of being non-recurring. The distortions are structural, and they matter more than any single item.

The GAAP-to-adjusted bridge. Q2 2026 GAAP operating loss was $(49)M; adjusted operating income was $128M; adjusted EBITDA was $1,510M. The gap between the first two is largely stock-based compensation of $165M plus acquisition costs. The gap between the second and third is $1,393M of depreciation and amortisation — the GPUs wearing out. Adjusted EBITDA margin was 59%; adjusted operating margin was 5%; GAAP net margin was −24%. Which of those three numbers you think is "the margin" is the entire disagreement about this stock.

Cash capex versus total capex. The earnings release reports $6,422M of cash capex in Q2 2026; the investor slides cite $9.4B for the quarter and $16.1B for the half. The difference is equipment acquired through financing arrangements (OEM and vendor financing, leases) that never passes through the cash-flow statement as capex. Both are real spending; only one appears in free cash flow. Use the higher figure when judging the pace of investment and the lower when judging cash burn.

Net-loss definitions. The FY2025 net loss is reported as $(1,167)M in the company's release and $(1,196)M on stockanalysis.com; FY2024 is shown as $(863)M in the 10-K summary and $(937)M on stockanalysis.com. The differences appear to reflect net loss versus net loss attributable to common stockholders (preferred-stock adjustments before the IPO). This page uses the stockanalysis.com series for consistency with the TTM figures.

Equity issuance. $2,982M of net private-placement proceeds in 1H 2026 (Nvidia's $2B at $87.20 in January; Jane Street's $1B) plus 2025 converts of ~$2.6B. Weighted shares grew 75.6% year over year. Per-share metrics are being diluted faster than almost any large-cap in the index.

Core Scientific. The $9B all-stock acquisition of Core Scientific — which would have brought owned data-center power in-house — was terminated on October 30, 2025 after CORZ shareholders voted it down, largely because CRWV's stock had fallen and the fixed exchange ratio no longer looked attractive to them. No break fee or write-off of note appears in the figures; the companies continue as landlord and tenant under their existing hosting agreements. The practical consequence is that CoreWeave leases more of its power than it would have owned, which shows up in the lease-inclusive debt figure.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with what is signed. Revenue backlog at June 30, 2026 was $104.2B, of which $103.7B is RPO (remaining performance obligations — the accounting term for contracted revenue not yet delivered) and $0.5B is other estimated future revenue under committed contracts. That is up from $30.1B a year earlier and $66.8B at the end of 2025, and management says a further $25B-plus was signed in the first weeks of Q3. Against trailing revenue of $7.59B, the backlog is 13.7 years of current revenue; against the FY2026 guide midpoint of $12.8B it is roughly eight years. The 10-K's weighted-average contract duration is about five years, and contracts of four years or longer grew from 10% to 21% of the mix in twelve months. This is the strongest revenue visibility of any company its size in the index. It is also, by construction, revenue that has not been earned yet, from counterparties who can be counted on one hand.

Now the debt. At June 30, 2026 the balance sheet carried $31.4B of recourse debt ($6.2B current) and $3.7B of non-recourse debt — about $35.1B — plus lease obligations that take stockanalysis.com's total to $51.6B. Cash was $5.5B, restricted cash $1.4B. Net cash is −$46.0B, or −$83.46 per share on an $89.36 stock: the enterprise value of $95.3B is almost half debt. The senior notes carry 9.0–9.25% coupons; the DDTL facilities (delayed-draw term loans, where lenders advance against specific signed customer contracts and the GPUs that serve them) are the cheaper, secured layer. First-half 2026 net debt proceeds were $16.7B, including a $3.1B DDTL described as the first publicly syndicated HPC-infrastructure-backed facility and more than $10B of unsecured notes and converts including a debut Eurobond.

What the debt costs. Net interest expense was $640M in Q2 2026 — 24.9% of revenue — up from $267M (22.0% of revenue) a year earlier. FY2025 interest was $1,229M on $5,131M of revenue: 24.0%. Q3 guidance of $860–940M against revenue of $3.45–3.6B is 25.5% at the midpoints. The ratio is not falling as the company scales; it is drifting up, because each incremental gigawatt is financed before it earns. Annualising Q2 interest gives roughly $2.6B on an average balance in the low-$30Bs — an implied blended cost in the high single digits, consistent with the 9%-area notes offset by cheaper secured paper. For context, adjusted operating income — the company's own preferred profit measure, after depreciation but before interest — was $128M in the same quarter the interest bill was $640M. Interest is five times the operating profit that is supposed to service it.

Capex versus cash generation. Operating cash flow is genuinely large: $3,663M in the first half of 2026 and $6.91B TTM, helped by customer prepayments. Cash capex was $14,117M in the first half ($20.57B TTM), and total capex including financed equipment was $16.1B for the half. FY2026 capex is guided to $35–39B — nearly three times guided revenue, and five to six times TTM operating cash flow. Free cash flow is −$13.66B TTM. Every dollar of that gap is bridged by new debt or new shares. The bull argument is that this is the correct behaviour for a company with $104B of signed demand and a cost of capital below its contracted return; the bear argument is that a business that cannot fund its own growth is entirely dependent on the credit window staying open, and the credit window is not something management controls.

The depreciation debate, stated fairly. D&A was $1,393M in Q2 2026 — 54% of revenue — and $2.54B for the half. If GPUs are economically useful for as long as the accounting schedule assumes, adjusted EBITDA of $1.51B a quarter is a reasonable proxy for the cash the fleet throws off, and the debt is covered roughly 2.4× by EBITDA on a quarterly basis. If Nvidia's annual cadence shortens the economic life below the book life, then depreciation is understated, adjusted operating income of $128M is overstated, and GAAP — which already shows a $626M quarterly loss — is the more honest number. The company's exact useful-life assumption was not visible on the pages fetched for this report, so none is claimed. What can be said is that the contracts, at about five years weighted average, are long enough to cover the first life of each cluster; the open question is the residual value of a cluster at renewal, and the market has not seen a CoreWeave renewal cycle yet.

The Nvidia triangle. Nvidia supplies the GPUs, owns 10.66% of the Class A shares after adding $2B in January 2026 at $87.20, and has committed to buy up to $6.3B of unsold capacity through April 2032. The backstop is why lenders will fund GPUs against contracts: in the worst case, the supplier rents them back. That is either the most credible put option in the industry or the definition of circular demand, and reasonable analysts hold each view. Note the price: Nvidia's last purchase was within 2.5% of today's close. The most informed buyer in the ecosystem paid roughly what the stock trades for now.

Valuation — on what base? There is no P/E, trailing or forward, because there are no earnings: TTM EPS is −$3.70 and 2026 consensus is −$3.93. The multiples that exist: 6.5× trailing sales, 2.5× 2026 consensus sales of $12.90B, 12.6× EV/sales, 25.2× EV/EBITDA, 9.8× book. On the FY2026 exit-ARR target of $18.5–19.5B, the enterprise value is about 5× forward run-rate revenue. The only framework that makes the stock cheap is the backlog: $95B of enterprise value against $104B-plus of contracted revenue over roughly five years, at a 59% adjusted EBITDA margin, is a business that "pays for itself" if every contract is honoured, every site energises on time, and the hardware is worth something at the end. The only framework that makes it expensive is GAAP: a company losing $1.9B a year, paying a quarter of revenue in interest, with a 0.46 current ratio and shares outstanding up 76%. Both frameworks use the same numbers. The 8× spread in analyst price targets is what it looks like when a market cannot agree which one applies.

Bull Case

$104B of signed, take-or-pay revenue at 59% adjusted EBITDA margins, growing 246% a year, with the world's most important AI supplier as a 10.7% shareholder and buyer of last resort. Operating cash flow is already $6.9B TTM and adjusted operating margin is guided to the low teens by Q4 2026 as 1.5 GW becomes 1.85 GW-plus. The customer roster (Microsoft, OpenAI, Meta, Nvidia) is the best in the industry; the financing stack is contract-backed and has been refinanced repeatedly at scale. At 2.5× 2026 sales and roughly 5× exit ARR, with a $144 average target, the stock discounts a funding accident that has not happened.

Bear Case

Interest expense is 25% of revenue and rising; adjusted operating income is one-fifth of the interest bill; free cash flow is −$13.7B and FY2026 capex of $35–39B is three times revenue. The current ratio is 0.46 with $6.2B of recourse debt due within a year, and the Altman Z-score is 0.29. One customer was 67% of 2025 revenue; the diversification is into a private company with its own funding needs. D&A is already 54% of revenue and the fleet has never been through a renewal cycle. Shares outstanding rose 76% in a year, 18% of the float is short, and the stock has round-tripped to flat over twelve months while revenue doubled — because the market has already worked out that doubling revenue doubles the debt.

Top 3 Risks

01
Refinancing and cost of capital. With ~$35B of debt, $6.2B current, 9%-area unsecured coupons and a $35–39B capex programme funded almost entirely externally, CoreWeave's viability is a function of credit-market access. A widening in high-yield spreads or a single failed syndication would force a choice between slowing the build (breaking the backlog delivery schedule) and diluting at whatever price the market offers.
02
Counterparty and concentration risk. The backlog is only worth its face value if Microsoft, OpenAI and Meta pay on schedule for five years. Microsoft is building its own capacity; OpenAI is a private company with commitments across the industry far larger than its revenue; Meta can cancel projects when the return case changes. A renegotiation by any one of them would hit both revenue and the collateral value backing the DDTL facilities at once.
03
Hardware obsolescence outrunning depreciation. Vera Rubin ships while Hopper-era clusters are still on the books. If renewal pricing on older GPUs comes in far below original contract rates, the residual-value assumption embedded in adjusted EBITDA breaks, the true cost of the fleet is higher than reported, and the equity — already only $9.12 of book value per share against $83 of net debt per share — absorbs the difference first.
  Industry Commentary
Power Is the Bottleneck, Not Chips
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
CoreWeave has 4.2 GW under contract and 1.5 GW live. The gap is grid interconnection, transformers, cooling and permits — none of which Nvidia can ship. This is why the company tried to buy Core Scientific outright for $9B in 2025 (owned power instead of leased) and why the deal's failure in October 2025 matters more than a normal broken merger: it left CoreWeave a tenant in a landlord's market. The whole neocloud sector — Nebius at $62B, IREN at $17.6B — is now valued on contracted megawatts as much as on revenue, and the entrant with power wins the contract regardless of software.
The Circular-Financing Question Has Not Gone Away
ONGOING HEADWIND
Nvidia invests in CoreWeave, CoreWeave buys Nvidia GPUs with borrowed money, Nvidia guarantees to rent back what CoreWeave cannot sell, and Microsoft rents CoreWeave capacity to serve OpenAI, in which Microsoft is itself a major investor. Every link in that chain is a legitimate commercial arrangement; the concern is that the chain as a whole manufactures demand signals that would not exist independently. The November 2025 drawdown (−46% in a month) was this concern being priced. It will resurface every time an AI-lab funding round wobbles, and CoreWeave, as the most levered public node in the chain, will move most.
Inference Demand and Contract Duration Are Lengthening
EMERGING TAILWIND
The shift from training runs to always-on inference favours committed, multi-year capacity over spot GPU rental — and CoreWeave's mix of 48-month-plus contracts doubled from 10% to 21% of backlog in a year, with weighted duration near five years. Longer contracts are the single best defence against the depreciation critique, because they push the residual-value question further out and make the DDTL collateral sturdier. Bull framing: if contracts keep lengthening and inference keeps growing, the fleet is paid for before it ages and today's 25× EV/EBITDA compresses fast. Bear framing: longer contracts with the same four counterparties deepen concentration rather than curing it, and a five-year commitment from a customer who cannot afford it is a longer default, not a safer one.
