01 Company Overview

CoStar Group owns the data and the storefronts of real estate. On the commercial side it sells CoStar, the subscription database of property inventory, leases, sales comps and ownership that brokers, lenders and landlords treat as the industry's system of record, and runs LoopNet, the commercial listings marketplace. On the residential side it runs Apartments.com, Homes.com and, since its 2025 takeover, Australia's Domain. FY2025 revenue was $3.25B, up 18.7%, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $3.56B, up 22.0%. The gross margin is 78.7%. The operating margin is 2.2%.

The gap between those two margins explains the stock. In FY2021 CoStar turned 22.2% of revenue into operating profit. By FY2025 that was −1.7%. Revenue kept growing; the profit went into building Homes.com, a national home-listing portal set up to take on the incumbents with heavy marketing and a large sales force. Third Point, the activist fund, later put CoStar's residential spend at roughly $5 billion over five years (as reported by RealEstateNews). The market went along with that bet for a long time. It stopped in 2026. The stock closed 2025 at $67.24. In late January Third Point wrote to the board demanding a pullback from Homes.com. On February 24 the Q1 guide for adjusted EBITDA of $95–115M arrived alongside a full-year guide that, per IndexBox, came in below analyst estimates. Then the Q2 release on July 28 cut the full-year revenue guide. The settled close on September 21, 2026 was $29.04, 56.8% lower than where the year started. The question now is whether the Homes.com spend was a cost that is now being cut, or a writedown of the franchise that is still to come. Q2 2026 gave the first real evidence either way: the residential segment turned adjusted-EBITDA positive for the first time, at $12M.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Proprietary commercial property database Everyday workflow for CRE professionals Subscription revenue model Two-sided marketplaces (LoopNet, Apartments.com) 78.7% gross margin Net cash balance sheet

The commercial moat is accumulated data. A rival would have to rebuild building-level research across the country before a broker would switch, and a broker who leaves loses the comps used to price every deal. That moat has held. The Commercial segment grew and earned $172M of adjusted EBITDA in a single quarter. What got hurt was the residential business. In for-sale homes, CoStar is the challenger, and a challenger pays for traffic and agents. The 2026 sell-off was a verdict on the price of that attempt.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Homes.com is still a cost centre. CoStar has said it does not expect Homes.com to reach positive adjusted EBITDA until 2030 (per RealEstateNews). Residential as a whole reached $12M of adjusted EBITDA in Q2, which is thin against $444M of revenue. If spending cuts start to erode traffic, the company has spent the money without getting the market position it was paying for.
02
Bookings slowed as the cuts landed. Net new bookings of $69M were down 26% year over year (Q2 call), and the full-year revenue guide was cut to $3.715–3.755B from $3.78–3.82B (the February and April guide). A leaner sales force sells less. The cost savings show up straight away, while the lost bookings reach revenue later.
03
Big-platform competition. The stock fell about 4% when Google expanded its Local Services Ads for home listings to all 50 states on June 11, 2026 (Yahoo Finance), and an FTC order announced August 24, 2026 requires Redfin to restart its rental advertising business within six months of the order being finalised (FTC), which would put a second competitor back into Apartments.com's market.
04
The activists have left. Third Point attacked the board in January, D.E. Shaw followed in February, and in April Third Point sold its entire stake and dropped any proxy fight (per RealEstateNews). The pressure that forced the Homes.com retreat has left the register, so holding management to the cuts falls to the board and its new Capital Allocation Committee.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The stock is priced as if residential will keep losing money, and the evidence is moving the other way. Residential swung from −$76M to +$12M of adjusted EBITDA in a year. Company-wide adjusted EBITDA doubled to $184M in Q2, and the full-year EBITDA guide of $780–820M was raised, not cut. At $29.04 the enterprise value is about 14.6x the midpoint of that guide (our calculation). For that price you get a growing commercial data franchise, net cash, and $587M of buybacks in six months. The CEO bought $2.49M of stock at $29.89 in August. If the cost cuts hold and residential margins keep widening, the market is pricing a failed bet that is in fact being wound down.

Bear Case

The EBITDA improvement came from spending less, and spending less shows up in bookings. Net new bookings fell about 26% year over year, the revenue guide was cut in July, and the Q3 revenue guide implies growth slowing to about 13%. By the company's own account Homes.com is not expected to make money until 2030. Google is moving further into home search and Redfin is being pushed back into rentals. The trailing P/E is 161 on GAAP earnings of $0.18. The activist who forced the retreat has sold out. A business that spent roughly $5 billion (Third Point's figure) to reach a challenger position may simply be worth less than the market once thought.

Top 3 Risks

01
Cutting spend means cutting growth. A leaner Homes.com sales force and a Ten-X restructuring are already showing up in the ~26% year-over-year drop in net new bookings. If revenue growth slows faster than costs fall, margin expansion stalls.
02
Platform competition in residential. Google's June 2026 expansion into home-search ads and Redfin's required return to rental advertising both land in the markets where CoStar is spending to win share.
03
Execution on a back-loaded guide. The $780–820M adjusted EBITDA guide likely needs a Q4 of around $300M (see Section 06). A miss would undercut the one number investors are currently giving CoStar credit for.
Industry Commentary
The challenger's bill in residential portals
ONGOING HEADWIND

Home-listing portals are a scale business. Agents advertise where buyers are, and buyers go where the listings are. A challenger has to pay for both sides before either pays it back. CoStar tried to do this with Homes.com, funded by a commercial data business with gross margins near 80%. Its operating margin fell from 22.2% to −1.7% between FY2021 and FY2025 while gross margin barely moved. The cost of the push was in marketing and sales, not in the product. The 2026 reset (a 35% cut to Homes.com spending, a 2030 profitability target and residential turning EBITDA-positive) is what it looks like when a challenger stops spending to win and settles for defending what it has.

AI as a distribution channel
EMERGING TAILWIND

Portals worry that AI assistants will answer "find me an apartment" without sending anyone to a portal. CoStar's position is that its listings become the data those assistants draw on. Apartments.com's own AI tools logged over half a million sessions averaging 20 minutes in Q2. In early September 2026, StockAnalysis's news feed carried a headline reading "OpenAI Features Apartments.com in GPT-6 Astra Apartment-Hunting Demo." Whether that turns into paid leads or just gives away traffic is unresolved.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

At $29.04 the market pays about 14.6x the midpoint of 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance (our calculation). The bull framing: ~15x is a normal price for a subscription data business, so the market is now valuing the commercial franchise alone and giving residential no credit, or even negative credit, just as residential turns profitable. The bear framing: the forward EBITDA is flattered by cost cuts that are already slowing bookings, and a lower multiple on a lower growth rate is a reasonable price, not a bargain. The next two quarters of net new bookings should show which reading is right.
