01 Company Overview

CSL began in 1916 as Commonwealth Serum Laboratories, owned by the Australian government; it was privatised and listed on the ASX in 1994. Through the purchases of ZLB Bioplasma (2000) and Aventis Behring (2004) it rose to what its Chair calls global leadership in plasma therapies — medicines refined from donated human blood plasma, chiefly immunoglobulin (Ig), albumin and clotting factors. It agreed in 2014 to buy the Novartis flu-vaccine business (now CSL Seqirus) and in December 2021 to buy Swiss kidney-and-iron specialist Vifor Pharma for US$11.7B. In the year to June 30, 2026 (FY26) CSL booked US$15.8B of revenue (down 1% at constant currency) and an underlying NPATA of US$3.1B (−2%) — but a statutory loss of US$2.6B after US$7.1B of write-downs and US$0.8B of restructuring costs.

CSL's own May 2026 review looked back on a decade of value creation followed by erosion. The last fourteen months were the erosion: a restructuring and a planned spin-off of Seqirus in August 2025, a profit-guidance cut at the October 2025 AGM, the chief executive's exit in February 2026, a second guidance cut and US$5B of extra impairments flagged in May. From its July 2024 month-end of A$309.72 the share price fell 68.8% to A$96.61 at the end of May 2026. It has since recovered 81.5% to A$175.35, about half of it from August 18, 2026 onward — the day of FY26 results, a guide to about 5% underlying profit growth in FY27 and a new A$1.1B buy-back, when the stock rose 17.3%. Interim CEO Gordon Naylor's own summary: "FY26 has been a year of reset. We have taken decisive action and created a clear path to return to sustainable growth."

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Own plasma collection network US$6.2B Ig franchise US therapies from US plasma Patented Ig-yield process (Horizon 2) Cell-based & adjuvanted flu vaccines HEMGENIX gene therapy

Plasma medicine is a hard business to enter. Supply depends on a network of donor centres, regulated manufacturing that takes years to build and approve, and the yield squeezed from every litre — CSL's Horizon 2 is a patented process that makes significantly more Ig from the same plasma. Demand is steady: the 90-day review called Ig demand balanced with supply and growing at mid-to-high single digits, with most eligible patients still undiagnosed. That structure has also helped in Washington: in April 2026 CSL said most of its US product sales should escape the new pharmaceutical tariffs, because its US plasma therapies are made entirely from US-sourced plasma. Interim CEO Naylor, May 2026: "…the industry is stable and growing and the company has evident strengths in plasma collections and influenza vaccines." The moat protects volumes; FY26 showed it does not protect margins from policy, pricing and its own cost base.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Capital allocation has destroyed value. CSL's own May 2026 review listed the problems: invested capital grew faster than earnings, infrastructure was overbuilt, the Vifor acquisition underperformed and several Phase 3 trials failed. FY26 impairments of US$7.1B are the bill.
02
US policy sets the price. Medicare Part D changes cut into Ig revenue; on September 1, 2026 CSL agreed to give Medicaid prices comparable with other developed countries and to price new launches the same way for all US payers. CSL expects no material FY27 impact.
03
Yield upgrade delayed. Horizon 2 needs clinical trials after talks with the FDA and EMA; CSL expects them to start in mid-2027 and has not given a timetable for approval. The US$1.5B Kankakee, Illinois expansion is being built in parallel.
04
Leadership in transition. An interim CEO, a CEO search, a new Chief Commercial Officer since July 1, 2026 and chair succession planning — all at once, mid-turnaround.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The reset is done and the bad news is in the accounts: US$7.1B of write-downs taken, Vifor's FY27 decline guided at ~25%, the US pricing and tariff questions settled by agreement. What remains is a plasma franchise whose end-demand grows at mid-to-high single digits, with most eligible patients still undiagnosed, plus a flu business gaining share. Cost savings scale from US$176M to up to ~US$550M a year by FY28, Horizon 2 offers more Ig per litre later in the decade, and US$3.5B of operating cash flow funds an unchanged dividend and an A$1.1B buy-back. On FY27 consensus the shares trade at about 20 times earnings, against a market on 18.7.

Bear Case

FY27 guidance asks for flat revenue and ~5% profit growth — modest, and from a company that cut guidance twice in FY26. Behring's gross margin fell, Ig sales were flat, albumin lost 17% in China, and Horizon 2 now needs clinical trials before it can lift yields. Vifor will shrink by a quarter. The chief executive has not been named, the chair is planning his succession, and the 81.5% rebound since May leaves the shares a little under the average analyst target. At about 20 times forward earnings CSL is no longer priced as a broken company.

Top 3 Risks

01
Execution of the turnaround. FY27 depends on Behring returning to growth and on the transformation savings arriving on schedule, under a CEO still to be appointed. CSL missed its own original FY26 guidance by a wide margin.
02
US pricing and reimbursement. Medicare Part D changes already cut Ig revenue; the September 2026 Medicaid pricing agreement and launch-pricing commitment limit upside on new drugs. CSL lists policy changes in the United States first among the factors that could move its guidance.
03
Plasma cost and yield. Margins rest on the cost of each litre collected and the medicine extracted from it. Cost per litre is falling, but Horizon 2's yield gains are delayed until after trials that start in mid-2027.
Industry Commentary
Plasma: steady demand, supply-side economics
TAILWIND

Ig is a market where demand is not the problem. CSL describes it as balanced, growing at mid-to-high single digits, with only about 35% of eligible patients diagnosed across the seven major markets. The economics are decided on the supply side: what donors are paid, how much plasma each centre collects, and how much medicine each litre yields. CSL is closing underperforming centres, moving part of its US fleet to newer collection machines, and says cost per litre keeps falling. Horizon 2 is the big lever, but needs clinical evidence first. On the results call the CFO set a goal of recovering Behring's lost 70bps of gross margin.

Washington writes the price list
MIXED

FY26 showed how much CSL's numbers depend on US policy: the Medicare Part D redesign hit Ig, falling US flu vaccination rates hit Seqirus, and the Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs announced in April 2026 threatened imports. The outcome so far is less damaging than feared. Plasma-derived therapies were recognised as a special case under the tariff proclamation, and CSL's September 1 agreements traded Medicaid and launch-price concessions for more certainty, with no material FY27 impact expected. The CFO said on the call that US vaccination rates are still falling, but more slowly.

The Vifor lesson
KEY DEBATE

CSL agreed to pay US$11.7B for Vifor to add kidney and iron medicines to a plasma company. Less than five years on, iron faces generics, VELPHORO's US add-on payment is ending, TAVNEOS has lost its EU licence, and US$4.9B of Vifor's value has been written off. Management's response is to put capital back into the core: reinvest in plasma, keep leverage at 1.5–2.0x, and return surplus cash. The debate is whether that focus restores CSL's old growth rate or simply makes it a well-run, slower-growing plasma company — and which of the two the price now assumes.
