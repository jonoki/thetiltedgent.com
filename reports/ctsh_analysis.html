01 Company Overview

Cognizant is one of the four largest listed global IT services firms, selling consulting, systems integration, application development, engineering and outsourced operations to enterprises — 75.3% of revenue from North America, 18.7% from Europe. FY2025 revenue was $21.11B, up 6.95% from $19.74B in FY2024, the fastest growth since 2021, with GAAP EPS of $4.56. The company ranks roughly #891 globally by market capitalisation at a $26.3B market value — a business that books $29.1B of trailing-twelve-month contract value a year and is valued at less than that.

That gap is the entire report. Cognizant is an effort-priced business: 356,700 people whose billable hours are the product. Generative AI attacks precisely that pricing model — if a model writes the code, the client needs fewer billed hours for the same outcome. In 2026 the market took that argument seriously and repriced the stock from a $83.00 close on December 31, 2025 to a $38.73 close on June 30, 2026 — a 53% drawdown in six months — compounded on June 22, 2026 by removal from the NASDAQ-100 index, which forced mechanical selling from index-tracking funds. Meanwhile the reported numbers went the other way: Q1 2026 bookings rose 21%, Q2 2026 delivered 4.1% constant-currency growth and 40 bps of adjusted margin expansion, and management raised the full-year outlook. The stock has since recovered to $58.32. The central debate: is AI a deflation shock that permanently shrinks the revenue pool per unit of client outcome, or a mix shock that Cognizant can monetise through what management calls "skills plus inference" pricing? Bookings conversion and attrition — not the revenue line — are where the answer shows up first.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Multi-year contracted backlog TriZetto payer platform lock-in Global delivery scale (356,700) Regulated-industry domain depth Switching cost on run-the-business ops Security & compliance clearances Balance-sheet capacity (D/E 0.14)

The real moat is not technology — it is embeddedness. A large share of Cognizant's revenue is "run" work: keeping claims systems, trading platforms and manufacturing ERP environments alive. Replacing that vendor means re-badging hundreds of people, re-documenting undocumented systems and accepting operational risk in a regulated environment, which is why these contracts renew even when the client is unhappy with pricing. The $29.1B TTM bookings number against $21.6B of TTM revenue quantifies the same point: the forward book is larger than the current revenue run-rate. The vulnerability is that embeddedness protects the relationship, not the price — and price is what AI attacks.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The pricing model is the product. Revenue is a function of billable hours × rate. Berenberg's June 2026 downgrade to Hold cited exactly this — "risks tied to the company's transition as AI reshapes traditional outsourcing and consulting work." If clients demand the productivity gain be passed through, revenue falls even as delivery improves. Management's answer, unveiled at the June 5, 2026 AI Forum, is "skills plus inference" pricing — charging for human effort plus AI model consumption — but it is early, and a pricing model announced is not a pricing model realised.
02
Growth is one segment deep. Financial Services grew 12.0% in Q2 2026; the other three verticals — 68% of revenue — grew a combined ~1.4%. If the FS cost-takeout cycle matures or a single large FS client re-scopes, group growth goes to roughly zero without anything new going wrong elsewhere.
03
Attrition is turning the wrong way. Voluntary attrition rose from 12.3% to 13.0% over one quarter while headcount fell 900. Shrinking a workforce and losing people voluntarily at the same time is a harder combination to manage than either alone: you lose choice over which people leave, and on a 356,700-person base each point of attrition is roughly 3,500 additional replacements to hire and train.
04
Index removal removed a buyer. Exclusion from the NASDAQ-100 effective June 22, 2026 permanently removed a large, price-insensitive bid from the shareholder register and forced a one-off wave of mechanical selling. The de-rating that followed was not a fundamental event, but the lost passive demand does not come back unless the stock re-qualifies.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 close of $74.21 to the September 10, 2026 settled close of $58.32, CTSH lost 21.4% of its price. The shape matters more than the endpoints: this was not a slow bleed. It was a 2022 de-rating, three years of range-bound drift between $57 and $83, and then a violent 2026 in which the stock lost more than half its value in six months and regained two-thirds of the loss in two.

The 6-period monthly RSI tells the story cleanly. It printed 18.6 at the June 2026 low — the most oversold reading anywhere in the five-year series, more extreme than the September 2022 bear-market trough of 23.6 — and has since recovered to 46.7, squarely neutral. The 10-month moving average, at roughly $61.65, still sits above the current price: the long-term trend has not yet turned, and the July–August rally has taken the stock back to the underside of it rather than through it. Momentum has normalised; it has not reversed.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 12.6x trailing earnings and roughly 10x forward for a business with $29.1B of trailing bookings against $21.6B of revenue — a 1.3x book-to-bill — accelerating constant-currency growth guided to 4.0–5.5%, expanding adjusted operating margin guided to 16.0–16.2%, a ~9.9% free-cash-flow yield, effectively no debt at 0.14x D/E, and a board retiring nearly 5% of the share count in a single quarter. The 2026 collapse was driven by index-removal mechanics and a thesis about the future, not by a single quarter of missed numbers. If "skills plus inference" pricing works — the $1B context-engineering pipeline target is the test — AI becomes a mix-shift toward higher-value work rather than a revenue deflator, and a business growing mid-single digits with 16% margins does not belong at a 10x multiple.

Bear Case

The bear case does not dispute a single reported number; it disputes what they will be worth. Revenue is billable hours × rate, and generative AI attacks the quantity of hours directly. Q2 bookings already fell 6% YoY after a +21% Q1, voluntary attrition rose to 13.0% from 12.3%, and 68% of revenue — everything outside Financial Services — grew about 1.4%. A 7.9% short float and Morgan Stanley's $51 target say a meaningful part of the market believes the multiple is not cheap but appropriate for a business whose revenue pool is about to shrink. Cheap-on-trailing-earnings is precisely what a structurally impaired business looks like on the way down, and the lost NASDAQ-100 bid is not coming back.

Top 3 Risks

01
AI-driven price deflation on the staffing delivery model. This is the thesis that took the stock from $83.00 to $38.73 in six months. Berenberg downgraded on it explicitly in June 2026, and it remains unresolved: management has announced a consumption-linked pricing answer but has not yet shown revenue proof at scale.
02
Bookings deterioration. Q2 2026 bookings fell 6% YoY. Revenue lags bookings by roughly four to eight quarters, so a second and third down quarter would mechanically translate into 2027 revenue below the current 4.76% consensus growth estimate — regardless of what margins do. The October 28 print is the next read.
03
Single-segment growth dependence with rising attrition. Financial Services at +12.0% is carrying a group whose other three verticals grew ~1.4%. Simultaneously, voluntary attrition climbed to 13.0% on a headcount that fell by 900 — meaning the firm is losing people it did not choose to lose, on a base of 356,700, at a moment when delivery continuity on large fixed-scope contracts is the thing clients are watching.
Industry Commentary
Generative AI vs. the effort-priced services model
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The IT services industry's revenue equation has been billable hours × rate for thirty years. The market's 2026 verdict, as summarised by analysts covering the name, was blunt: "if AI writes code and automates processes, enterprises need fewer consultants." Cognizant fell 36% year-to-date by June 9, 2026 with a maximum drawdown of 46.98% recorded on May 13 — with no earnings miss anywhere in the sequence. Q1 2026 in fact beat: revenue +5.8% YoY to $5.4B, adjusted EPS +13.8% to $1.40 against a $1.33 consensus, and bookings +21% including one deal above $500M. Management's June 5 answer was "skills plus inference" — pricing that bundles human effort with AI model consumption, already live on large deals — plus a context-engineering system with ~$200M of pipeline targeting $1B by year-end. The evidence so far runs both ways: headcount down 900 with revenue up 4.1% constant currency is exactly what productive AI adoption looks like, and also exactly what price pass-through looks like. The two are indistinguishable in one quarter and fully distinguishable over four.

Sector-wide demand softness and index mechanics
ONGOING HEADWIND

Cognizant's June 2026 collapse was not idiosyncratic. A major IT-services competitor narrowed its fiscal-2026 revenue growth outlook and reported weaker bookings, triggering a broad sector selloff that dragged the entire peer group down together. Layered on top was a purely technical event: removal from the NASDAQ-100 effective before the open on June 22, 2026, which forced index-tracking funds to liquidate positions irrespective of valuation. CTSH fell a further 3.8% on June 25. The index effect is non-recurring, but its consequence is durable — a large tranche of price-insensitive passive demand has left the register permanently unless the company re-qualifies. Note that market capitalisation fell 32.84% year-over-year, materially more than the 12.47% one-year price decline, because the buyback shrank the share count at the same time.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

CTSH trades at roughly 5.85x next-twelve-month EV/EBITDA against a peer average near 8.86x — the steepest discount in its group despite leading peer revenue growth — and at a ~9.9% free-cash-flow yield with a 2.26% dividend and essentially no leverage. The bull framing: this is a mechanical dislocation. Index removal and a sector-wide sentiment shock detached the price from a business that raised guidance twice, is signing $29.1B of trailing contract value and is retiring almost 5% of its shares a quarter while its CEO says the stock "significantly undervalues" its prospects. Re-rating to peer multiples alone is worth ~50%. The bear framing: the discount is the market correctly pricing terminal value, not current earnings. A 5.85x multiple on a business whose unit of sale is a billable hour, in the year the cost of producing that hour collapsed, is not an anomaly to be arbitraged — it is a forecast. The way to adjudicate is not the P/E; it is four consecutive quarters of TTM bookings growth and a stable attrition rate. Both are reported. Neither has yet given a clean signal.
