01 Company Overview

Corteva is now a crop-protection company and nothing else. On October 1, 2026 it completed the spin-off of its seed business as Vylor Inc. (NYSE: VYLR), handing shareholders one Vylor share for each Corteva share they held on September 24 (Corteva 8-K, filed October 5). What kept the Corteva name and ticker sells the chemistry and biology farmers spray on fields: herbicides (weed killers), insecticides, fungicides and biologicals (pest and disease control made from living organisms or natural products). Its own description, on the day of the split: nearly 9,000 employees serving growers in about 110 countries, an $11 billion crop protection pipeline, and differentiated technologies (products the company sets apart from generic copies) at about 65% of sales.

The size of what remains is the first surprise. On the carve-out financial statements Corteva filed on October 1 (the business's own accounts, separated from the old group's), the crop-protection company sold $7.84B in 2025, up 3.8% from $7.55B, and $3.85B in the first half of 2026, up 3.2%. At the October 8 close the market valued it at $9.18B, against $48.6B for Vylor (our calculation: closing price × 667.3M shares, the count expected at the spin; Vylor's count matches one for one). So roughly 84 cents of every dollar the market used to pay for Corteva now sits in the seed company. The question this page asks is whether the half that stayed is cheap for a reason, and the reasons on offer are real: price pressure from generic chemistry in Latin America, legacy PFAS liabilities that come with the old DuPont entity it keeps, and a seasonal cash cycle that no longer has a seed business to smooth it.

The index world has already moved. S&P Dow Jones Indices added Vylor to the S&P 500 on October 1 in Corteva's place and moved Corteva into the S&P MidCap 400 before the open on October 6, replacing Olin, saying that post spin-off Corteva would be more representative of the mid-cap market space. One accounting detail matters for anyone reading old filings: Vylor is the "accounting spinnor", so Corteva's historical consolidated statements now count as Vylor's history, and the crop-protection company's history lives in the carve-out statements (8-K, October 1).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
~65% DIFFERENTIATED PRODUCTS $11B PIPELINE 12 NEW ACTIVES PLANNED BIOLOGICALS PLATFORM REGULATORY REGISTRATIONS ~110-COUNTRY REACH

A crop-protection moat is made of time and paperwork. Every new active ingredient needs years of discovery, field trials and safety work, then registration country by country, before it can be sold; patents and data protection then give it a window before generics arrive. Corteva's defence is to keep that window full: it says about 65% of sales now come from differentiated technologies and that 12 new actives, five of them biologicals, are planned for launch in the next decade. Between 2020 and 2025, by the company's account, the business added more than $1 billion of revenue and widened its operating EBITDA margin by about 250 basis points. The moat is narrower than the seed franchise that just left, and it has to be refilled with new molecules as the old ones age.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Generic pricing in Latin America. Price fell 4% in Q2 2026 and 3% in the first half, which the company attributed mainly to the competitive pricing environment and market dynamics in Latin America, a region that is 30.9% of sales. Organic sales were down 6% in the quarter. New products held volume up over the half, but the older book keeps losing price.
02
PFAS liabilities travel with EIDP. Corteva keeps EIDP, the former DuPont operating company, and with it a share of legacy PFAS ("forever chemical") claims under a 2021 cost-sharing agreement with Chemours and DuPont capped at $4B of qualified spend. On September 9 the three agreed to pay North Carolina $455M over 15 years, Corteva's share about $66M (8-K, September 10). California and other states argued in court that the separation could leave creditors short; Corteva called the allegations unfounded and said its balance sheet will be well-equipped to cover any liability it might face (release, September 14).
03
No seed business to smooth the year. Crop chemistry is sold into the planting season and paid for later, so cash flows out in the first half. Carve-out operating cash flow was −$965M in the first half of 2026 against +$80M a year earlier, and 2025 free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital spending) was $214M on $7.84B of sales. Before October 1 the seed business's cash cushioned that swing; now the company carries it alone, with the net cash it got at the spin as the buffer.
04
Standalone costs and the Vylor contracts. The pro forma accounts already shave $248M of 2025 sales and $189M of pre-tax income for the commercial agreements with Vylor, and the 2025 pro forma diluted EPS is $0.77 against $0.97 implied by the carve-out's $659M of continuing-operations income on 680.0M shares. A new head office, new board and new contracts all have to work at the same time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The market has priced the crop-protection half like a broken business, and the numbers say it isn't one. At $13.75 the company trades at about 4.5 times trailing operating EBITDA and 0.71 times book, with roughly $2.0B of net cash after the Vylor payment (pro forma). Margins are rising, not falling: operating EBITDA margin was 21.2% in the first half of 2026 against 19.6% a year earlier, even with prices down. About 65% of sales come from differentiated products, 12 new actives are planned, biologicals grew 9% in 2025, and the Globachem venture adds formulation capacity. Eight of ten analysts rate it a Buy, with an average target 25.7% above the price, and the stock rose 12.3% on its first day in the S&P MidCap 400.

Bear Case

It is cheap because it kept what nobody wanted. EIDP's legacy PFAS exposure stayed with Corteva, and several states went to court arguing the split could leave creditors short. Price is falling in Latin America (−4% in Q2, organic sales −6%), the part of the business with the least protection from generics. The first-half cash outflow (−$965M operating cash flow) now has no seed business behind it, the pro forma accounts take $248M of sales and $189M of pre-tax income away for the Vylor contracts, and the only guidance on record covers the old combined group, so the valuation rests on carve-out history.

Top 3 Risks

01
Legacy PFAS claims. Corteva shares PFAS costs with Chemours and DuPont under the 2021 agreement capped at $4B of qualified spend, and it keeps the EIDP entity those claims attach to. Settlements so far (New Jersey in 2025, North Carolina in September 2026) have been paid over many years; a large new judgment, or a state succeeding in its challenge to the separation, would land on the smaller company alone.
02
Generic price erosion. Price fell 3% in the first half and 4% in Q2, driven by Latin America, which is 30.9% of sales. If new products stop out-running the decline, margins that rose 1.6 points in the first half will turn.
03
Standalone execution. New CEO, new CFO, new board, new revenue-sharing contracts with Vylor and a first standalone outlook still to come. The pro forma accounts already cut 2025 EPS to $0.77; any further standalone costs or a weak first outlook on November 11 would test a valuation that rests on carve-out history.
Industry Commentary
Generic chemistry and the Latin American price war
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Crop chemistry ages: once a molecule's patent and data protection lapse, generic makers can register copies and sell them for less. Latin America is where Corteva feels it most: Corteva's Q2 release put its 4% price decline mainly down to the competitive pricing environment there. FMC shows how bad it gets for a company that is behind on new products: a $1.39B market cap on $3.25B of revenue and a $2.76B net loss over the last twelve months. Corteva's answer is mix. New products lifted first-half volume 2% while price fell 3%, and margins still rose. The industry question for the next few years is whether new actives can keep arriving fast enough to replace the old ones as they turn generic.

PFAS: the liability that stayed behind
ONGOING OVERHANG

PFAS are the "forever chemicals" behind years of water-contamination lawsuits against the old DuPont family. Corteva says it never made, sold or traded PFOA or PFOS in its own seven-year history, but its EIDP subsidiary inherited obligations from DuPont's past and shares costs with Chemours and DuPont under a 2021 agreement. The North Carolina deal in September shows how these claims are being resolved: large headline totals ($455M) spread over many years, with Corteva's share (about $66M) a fraction of it. The states' attempt to stop the separation failed in court on September 30, but it shows the argument creditors will keep making: that liabilities should follow the assets, and that most of the assets just left with Vylor.

What the split resolved — and what the market is paying
EMERGING TAILWIND

The separation was sold on the idea that seed genetics and crop chemistry are different businesses that deserve different valuations, and the first week of trading made that concrete: at the October 8 closes the market valued Vylor at about $48.6B and Corteva at $9.18B, on $7.84B of 2025 sales for Corteva. Holders who kept both shares were 11.4% better off than at the last pre-spin close. For Corteva the result is a focused chemicals company with net cash, rising margins and a single-digit EBITDA multiple. Whether that multiple is a discount to be closed or a fair price for generic pressure and legacy liabilities is what the next few quarters will decide.
