01 Company Overview

Corteva is now a crop-protection company and nothing else. On October 1, 2026 it completed the spin-off of its seed business as Vylor Inc. (NYSE: VYLR), handing shareholders one Vylor share for each Corteva share they held on September 24 (Corteva 8-K, filed October 5). What kept the Corteva name and ticker sells the chemistry and biology farmers spray on fields: herbicides (weed killers), insecticides, fungicides and biologicals (pest and disease control made from living organisms or natural products). Its own description, on the day of the split: nearly 9,000 employees serving growers in about 110 countries, an $11 billion crop protection pipeline, and differentiated technologies (products the company sets apart from generic copies) at about 65% of sales.

The size of what remains is the first surprise. On the carve-out financial statements Corteva filed on October 1 (the business's own accounts, separated from the old group's), the crop-protection company sold $7.84B in 2025, up 3.8% from $7.55B, and $5.54B in the first nine months of 2026, up 0.3% (carve-out statements for the quarter, 8-K filed November 12). At the November 13 close the market valued it at $6.29B, against $48.4B for Vylor (our calculation: closing price × 667.3M shares, Corteva's count on November 5 per its 10-Q; Vylor's count matches one for one from the distribution). So roughly 88 cents of every dollar the market now pays for the two halves sits in the seed company. The question this page asks is whether the half that stayed is cheap for a reason, and its first quarter alone made the reasons plainer: Brazilian farmers short of credit stopped buying, older products lost price to generic copies in Latin America, legacy PFAS liabilities come with the old DuPont entity it keeps, and a seasonal cash cycle no longer has a seed business to smooth it.

The index world has already moved. S&P Dow Jones Indices added Vylor to the S&P 500 on October 1 as Corteva's replacement, and before the open on October 6 moved Corteva out of the S&P 500 and into the S&P MidCap 400, replacing Olin, saying that post spin-off Corteva would be more representative of the mid-cap market space. One accounting detail matters for anyone reading old filings: Vylor is the "accounting spinnor", so Corteva's historical consolidated statements now count as Vylor's history, and the crop-protection company's history lives in the carve-out statements (8-K, October 1).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
~65% DIFFERENTIATED PRODUCTS $11B PIPELINE 12 NEW ACTIVES PLANNED BIOLOGICALS PLATFORM REGULATORY REGISTRATIONS ~110-COUNTRY REACH

A crop-protection moat is made of time and paperwork. Every new active ingredient needs years of discovery, field trials and safety work, then registration country by country, before it can be sold; patents and data protection then give it a window before generics arrive. Corteva's defence is to keep that window full: it says about 65% of sales now come from differentiated technologies and that 12 new actives, five of them biologicals, are planned for launch in the next decade. Between 2020 and 2025, by the company's account, the business added more than $1 billion of revenue and widened its operating EBITDA margin by about 2.5 percentage points, measured on the old segment basis. The moat is narrower than the seed franchise that just left, and it has to be refilled with new molecules as the old ones age.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Brazil and generic pricing in Latin America. Latin America was 30.9% of 2025 sales and is where both problems meet. In Q3 2026 the region's sales fell 28% (organic −31%): volume dropped 24%, which the CFO put down to distributors working off excess stock, weather and weaker grower profits, and price fell 7% on competition with older, late-in-life molecules. Group price was down 7% in the quarter and 4% over nine months. The cut to 2026 guidance is all Brazil, by the CFO's account. Kissam said on the call he expects the stock of product sitting with Brazilian distributors to be at a more acceptable level by the end of the growing season there.
02
PFAS liabilities travel with EIDP. Corteva keeps EIDP, the former DuPont operating company, and with it a share of legacy PFAS ("forever chemical") claims under a 2021 cost-sharing agreement with Chemours and DuPont capped at $4B of qualified spend. On September 9 the three agreed to pay North Carolina $455M over 15 years, Corteva's share about $66M (8-K, September 10). California and other states argued in court that the separation could leave creditors short; Corteva called the allegations unfounded and said its balance sheet will be well-equipped to cover any liability it might face (release, September 14). A second suit, by Indiana and 14 other states plus Guam, seeks to unwind the transfer of the seed business as a fraudulent transfer; a temporary order of October 9 barring dividends and buybacks is stayed while Corteva appeals (8-K, October 13; Seventh Circuit stay, October 20).
03
No seed business to smooth the year. Crop chemistry is sold into the planting season and paid for later, so cash flows out in the first half. Carve-out operating cash flow was −$1,310M in the first nine months of 2026 (−$965M in the first half), and 2025 free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital spending) was $214M on $7.84B of sales. Before October 1 the seed business's cash cushioned that swing; now the company carries it alone. On the pro forma September 30 balance sheet, net cash (cash minus debt) was about $0.8B, down from about $2.0B at June 30.
04
Standalone costs and the Vylor contracts. The pro forma accounts already shave $248M of 2025 sales and $189M of pre-tax income for the commercial agreements with Vylor, and the 2025 pro forma diluted EPS is $0.77 against $0.97 implied by the carve-out's $659M of continuing-operations income on 680.0M shares. A new head office, new board and new contracts all have to work at the same time, and Kissam told the Q3 call "we simply cannot afford the infrastructure we inherited"; the company expects $130–$140M of pre-tax restructuring charges in 2026 and said more detail on cost actions would come with its fourth-quarter results.

Notable one-off items. Four things distort these numbers. Fourth, the September 30 balance sheet is pro forma: it shows the separation, the debt repayment and the Vylor cash as if they had happened on that date. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

One bad region in one bad quarter has been priced as if it were the whole company. Outside Latin America, Q3 sales rose: North America 7% (volume +19%), EMEA 12%, Asia Pacific 3%, and over nine months the adjusted pro forma operating EBITDA margin was 17.6%, up 0.3 points. At $9.43 the stock trades at about 4.9 times the midpoint of the company's own cut 2026 EBITDA guidance and about half its book value, with roughly $0.8B of net cash (pro forma, September 30) and a 2.1% forward dividend yield. The CEO expects the product sitting with Brazilian distributors to be back at a more acceptable level by the end of the growing season there and thinks the company can grow in 2027, and the CFO said on the Q3 call that it is still on a path to the $1.5B of EBITDA it committed to at the Investor Day. Thirteen of sixteen analysts rate it a Buy, with an average target 56.7% above the price.

Bear Case

It is cheap because it kept what nobody wanted, and the first quarter on its own confirmed it. Eight weeks after setting about $1.3B of 2026 EBITDA at its Investor Day, management cut the figure to $1.100–$1.125B. Latin America sales fell 28% and price fell 7% across the company, the older products losing ground to generics. On the company's pro forma accounts the stock is not cheap on earnings: 21.9 times trailing EPS of $0.43. Net cash fell from about $2.0B to $0.8B between the June and September pro forma balance sheets, fungicides and biologicals are shrinking this year, and EIDP's legacy PFAS exposure stayed with Corteva while 15 states and Guam ask a court to unwind the split.

Top 3 Risks

01
Legacy PFAS claims and the states' suit. Corteva shares PFAS costs with Chemours and DuPont under the 2021 agreement capped at $4B of qualified spend, and it keeps the EIDP entity those claims attach to. Settlements so far (New Jersey in 2025, North Carolina in September 2026) have been paid over many years; a large new judgment would land on the smaller company alone. The states' suit to unwind the separation is on appeal; the October 9 order that barred dividends and buybacks is stayed, not overturned.
02
Brazil and generic price erosion. Price fell 7% in Q3 and 4% over nine months, led by Latin America (30.9% of 2025 sales) and the expected acetochlor price drop in North America. Q3 operating EBITDA margin fell 1.4 points. If the excess product sitting with Brazilian distributors is not worked down by the end of the growing season there, or new products stop out-running the price decline, the 2026 cut will carry into 2027.
03
Stand-alone execution and credibility. New CEO, new CFO, new board, new revenue-sharing contracts with Vylor, and a 2026 outlook already cut below the Investor Day figure within two months. Restructuring is under way ($130–$140M of charges expected in 2026) and more cost detail is due with the fourth-quarter results; a second miss would undermine the 2029 targets.
Industry Commentary
Generic chemistry and the Latin American price war
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Crop chemistry ages: once a molecule's patent and data protection lapse, generic makers can register copies and sell them for less. Latin America is where Corteva feels it most: its Q3 release put the price pressure there down to competition on late-in-life molecules, and Brazil added a demand problem on top, as distributors sat on last year's stock. FMC shows how bad it gets for a company that is behind on new products: a $0.81B market cap on $2.81B of revenue and a $4.26B net loss over the last twelve months. Corteva's answer is mix: the Q3 release credits new-product growth with more than offsetting the acetochlor price fall in North America, and demand for new products with the growth in EMEA and Asia Pacific. The industry question for the next few years is whether new actives can keep arriving fast enough to replace the old ones as they turn generic.

PFAS: the liability that stayed behind
ONGOING OVERHANG

PFAS are the "forever chemicals" behind years of water-contamination lawsuits against the old DuPont family. Corteva says it never made, sold or traded PFOA or PFOS in its own seven-year history, but its EIDP subsidiary inherited obligations from DuPont's past and shares costs with Chemours and DuPont under a 2021 agreement. The North Carolina deal in September shows how these claims are being resolved: large headline totals ($455M) spread over many years, with Corteva's share (about $66M) a fraction of it. California's attempt to stop the separation failed in court on September 30, but a second group of states, led by Indiana, is making the argument creditors will keep making: that liabilities should follow the assets, and that most of the assets just left with Vylor. Its October 9 restraining order is stayed while the Seventh Circuit hears the appeal.

What the split resolved — and what the market is paying
EMERGING TAILWIND

The separation was sold on the idea that seed genetics and crop chemistry are different businesses that deserve different valuations, and six weeks of trading have made that concrete: at the November 13 closes the market valued Vylor at about $48.4B and Corteva at $6.29B, on $7.84B of 2025 sales for Corteva. Holders who kept both shares were 5.6% better off than at the last pre-spin close. For Corteva the result is a focused chemicals company with net cash and a single-digit EBITDA multiple, but its first stand-alone quarter cut the year's outlook. Whether that multiple is a discount to be closed or a fair price for Brazil, generic pressure and legacy liabilities is what the next few quarters will decide.
