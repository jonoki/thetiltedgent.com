01 Company Overview

CVS Health is one company holding four businesses: an insurer (Aetna), a pharmacy benefit manager (Caremark), a retail pharmacy chain of roughly 9,000 stores, and a growing primary-care and home-health business. Fiscal 2025 total revenues were $402.1B, up 7.8% YoY, and the last twelve months reached $420.6B (+6.7%). Yet the market capitalization is only $107.3B, #107 in the S&P 500: CVS keeps about 2.6 cents of net profit on each revenue dollar, so the size of the business and the value of its shares have little to do with each other.

For anyone valuing this stock, the number that matters most is not consolidated EPS. It is the medical loss ratio (MLR) at the Aetna insurance segment. That single ratio wrecked earnings between 2023 and 2025 and has driven the recovery since. Section 02 starts there.

The November 2026 news is a breakup plan. On November 4, alongside third-quarter results, CVS said it intends to separate into two independent listed companies in the first half of 2028. CVS Health (an interim name) would keep Aetna, Caremark, CVS Specialty, Oak Street Health and Signify Health, with David Joyner as Chairman and CEO. CVS Pharmacy would take the roughly 9,000 stores, mail pharmacy, MinuteClinic and the Health100 technology business, with Group President Prem Shah as CEO. Shareholders would own shares in both, and the company intends the separation to be tax-free. It still needs final board approval, an effective Form 10 registration with the SEC and the usual tax confirmation. The stock fell 12.5% that day, from $91.41 to $79.96, even though the quarter beat forecasts and guidance went up; CNBC named one investor concern: whether the stores lose their edge once they are no longer tied to a PBM.

This is the segment that matters. Aetna sells commercial employer coverage, Medicaid managed care and — critically — Medicare Advantage (MA, the private-plan alternative to traditional Medicare, in which the government pays an insurer a fixed per-member amount to take on the member's full medical risk). The governing metric is the MBR (medical benefit ratio, i.e. the MLR): claims paid as a percentage of premiums collected. Each percentage point of MBR on the almost $135B of premiums CVS collected in 2025 is about $1.35B of pre-tax income, which is why this ratio moves the stock more than consolidated EPS does.

Where it sits now. FY2025 MBR was 91.2%, an improvement from 92.5% in FY2024, when the segment earned just $307M of adjusted operating income. Even at 91.2% it produced only $2.9B on $143.4B of revenue (about a 2% margin). 2026 has gone better: Q3 2026 MBR was 91.3% versus 92.8% a year earlier, 1.5 percentage points better, and segment adjusted operating income nearly tripled to $912M from $314M on revenue of $36.9B (+2.4%). CFO Brian Newman said the results were "primarily driven by improved underlying performance in our Medicare business." Full-year 2026 MBR is now guided to the lower half of 89.75% ± 0.25 points, or about 89.5% to 89.75%, with segment adjusted operating income of at least $5.4B. Part of the year's improvement came from older claims: prior years' claims estimates developed favorably by $1.3B in the nine months to September 30.

Star ratings — the hidden cash-flow input. Plans rated 4.0 stars and above earn a quality bonus payment (a percentage uplift on the government benchmark rate), and star ratings also drive rebate dollars that fund richer member benefits. The lag is what readers miss: ratings announced in October of year T set the payment rates for plan year T+2, so a downgrade shows up in revenue two years later and cannot be repriced away in the interim. Aetna's recovery here has partly reversed: for the 2027 plan year (announced October 8, 2026), more than 69% of Aetna MA members are in plans rated 4 stars or higher, down from over 81% for 2026, and those ratings set 2028 payments. On October 26, 2026 CVS sued the federal government in the Southern District of Georgia, arguing Medicare's regulator, CMS, relied on improperly adopted measures and that the lower scores will cost it more than $2B in 2028. The same court ruled for Clover Health in May 2026 over measures used in 2026 scores; Healthcare Dive reports CMS still used half of those contested measures in the 2027 ratings for every insurer except Clover. Aetna head Steve Nelson said on the Q3 call that the Medicare recovery remains a multi-year job but "it’s ahead of expectations," and that he expects another year of margin improvement in 2027 with "some modest growth."

Membership was 26.1 million at September 30, down about 580,000 from a year earlier, largely because CVS exited the individual ACA exchange business for 2026. It chose to shrink there because the people buying that coverage had become more expensive to insure than the premiums allowed for.

How a PBM actually makes money — this is opaque by design, and it is now the subject of live political attack from both parties, so it is worth spelling out. A pharmacy benefit manager sits between drug manufacturers, health plans and pharmacies and earns from three distinct pools. First, rebates: manufacturers pay to have their drug placed favorably on the formulary (the covered-drug list), and the PBM passes most — but historically not all — of that back to the plan sponsor, retaining a share plus administrative fees. Second, spread: the PBM bills the plan more for a prescription than it reimburses the dispensing pharmacy and keeps the difference. Third, and increasingly the largest, specialty pharmacy: dispensing high-cost drugs through its own mail and specialty pharmacies, where the PBM captures the dispensing margin itself rather than paying it to a third party. Because rebates are calculated off list price, the model has been accused of creating an incentive for higher list prices — the core of the political case against it.

Caremark is one of the three big PBMs (Drug Channels estimates Express Scripts was the largest for a second straight year in 2025), processing 1,900.7 million 30-day-equivalent claims in FY2025 and 481.7 million in Q3 2026 (+1.3% YoY). FY2025 adjusted operating income was $7.2B, down 1.3%: a high-dollar, low-margin volume business whose profit pool is under pressure. CVS's answer has been to get ahead of regulation with transparency products: CostVantage, a cost-plus retail pharmacy reimbursement model, and TrueCost, which prices claims at net cost with visible fees. Cordavis, its subsidiary for biosimilars (lower-cost near-copies of biologic drugs), backed Caremark's move to take Humira off its main commercial drug lists in April 2024, a real demonstration of buying power when it is pointed at a target.

The regulatory attack is central, not a footnote. PBM reform was signed into law within the 2026 appropriations bill. From 2028, PBM compensation in Medicare Part D (the drug benefit) is delinked from drug prices: PBMs may only receive bona fide service fees, flat-dollar amounts at fair market value tied to actual services, which ends the practice of keeping part of the rebates in Part D. From 2029, "any willing pharmacy" rules force Part D sponsors to admit any pharmacy meeting standard terms, with a protected "essential retail pharmacies" category, which undercuts the narrow preferred networks that steer volume to CVS's own stores. Semiannual net-spend, rebate and spread-pricing disclosure to employer plans begins sooner. At state level, Tennessee's FAIR Rx Act, signed in May 2026, takes effect July 1, 2028 and bars one company from owning a PBM or insurer and a pharmacy in the state; existing pharmacies may keep operating through December 31, 2028 if they are actively pursuing a sale. CVS sued in federal court in May 2026, and Express Scripts and the PBM trade group PCMA followed in June. Arkansas passed a similar law, which a federal judge halted with a preliminary injunction in July 2025; the state appealed. Separating the stores from the PBM by the first half of 2028 would land before Tennessee's date. CEO David Joyner said on the Q3 call that CVS is doing it "because we believe this is the right thing to do for the long-term growth of the business, not because there’s any issue that may be on the horizon in ’28."

The segment also contains the care-delivery assets: Oak Street Health (primary care clinics for Medicare patients, paid for keeping them healthy rather than per visit) and Signify Health (in-home health evaluations). Looking ahead, management guides Health Services to at least $7B of adjusted operating income in 2027, which it calls trough earnings, meaning the low point before growth returns. The drags it names are a smaller 340B business (the federal drug-discount program for safety-net hospitals), a weaker season for winning PBM contracts and health-plan clients losing members.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Top-three US PBM 26.1M medical members ~9,000 store footprint 4+ star plans: 69% of MA members for 2027 Internal rebate + dispensing capture Cordavis biosimilar leverage Regulated-capital barriers to entry

The moat is closed-loop economics. When an Aetna member fills a prescription, Caremark processes the claim, a CVS pharmacy dispenses it, and — if it is a specialty drug — a CVS specialty pharmacy captures the dispensing margin too. Rebates negotiated with manufacturers accrue against volume that CVS both insures and dispenses. Layer on Oak Street's value-based primary care, which is paid to keep Medicare patients out of hospital, and Signify's in-home assessments, which feed both care management and risk adjustment (Medicare pays insurers more for sicker members, so recorded diagnoses raise payments), and the theory is a system that captures margin at four points on the same healthcare dollar and can profitably sell insurance at prices a standalone insurer cannot match. The barriers are real: capital requirements at the insurer, national pharmacy-network scale at the PBM, and long star-rating track records that a new entrant cannot quickly build. The split plan keeps part of that loop and gives up the rest. Aetna, Caremark, specialty pharmacy and the clinics stay together; the stores leave. CEO David Joyner said the products built between CVS and Caremark "are going to be preserved in commercial agreements between the 2 organizations," and that retail has operated behind a firewall for two decades. Contracts can be renegotiated in a way that common ownership cannot, and that is the investor concern CNBC highlighted on the print day.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Medicare Advantage costs are repriced only once a year. Bids are locked a year ahead; if seniors use more care than assumed, the insurer absorbs it. That is what happened from 2023, taking the FY2024 MBR to 92.5%. The 2026 recovery is real but rests on cost trends behaving, and management still calls those trends elevated. Star ratings have also slipped: 69% of members in 4-star-plus plans for 2027, down from 81% for 2026, with more than $2B of 2028 payments at stake by CVS's own count.
▸
The separation itself. CVS has not put numbers on the one-time costs or the ongoing cost of running two public companies; the CFO called them manageable and said CVS will come back with detail. It needs board approval, an SEC Form 10 and tax confirmation, and management expects to spend the next 15 to 18 months negotiating the commercial agreements that keep the two companies working together.
▸
PBM regulation now has dates attached. Part D delinking to flat-fee compensation lands in 2028; any-willing-pharmacy in 2029; Tennessee's ownership ban on July 1, 2028, with CVS challenging it in federal court and an Arkansas law blocked by a preliminary injunction in July 2025. The split would remove the pharmacy-and-PBM common ownership that those state laws target, but delinking still hits Caremark's economics.
▸
Retail is filling more prescriptions for fewer dollars. Prescriptions filled rose 1.9% in Q3 2026 while segment revenue fell 2.0% and same-store front-of-store sales fell 0.6%. CVS closed 900 stores between 2022 and 2024. As a standalone company, CVS Pharmacy would carry this without an insurer and a PBM alongside it.
▸
Debt limits the response to any of the above. StockAnalysis counts $75.3B of total debt against $14.9B of cash and short-term investments, an enterprise value of about $167.7B on a $107.3B market cap, the legacy of the $78B Aetna acquisition (debt included) and the $10.6B Oak Street deal. Creditors have the claim on more than a third of the enterprise value, and both new companies will need debt levels their credit ratings can support. Management says each will have solid investment-grade credit ratings, the tier that signals lower risk of default.

Notable One-Off Items. The distortions here are material and run in both directions. FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS was $1.39 against adjusted EPS of $6.75, a $5.36 per-share gap, the biggest reason to read this company's reported earnings with care. Q3 2025 carried a $5.7B goodwill impairment (a write-down of the premium paid for past acquisitions), which is why that quarter was a $3.13 per-share loss, and Q2 2025 carried $833M of legacy litigation charges, a $471M premium deficiency reserve (money set aside for plans expected to lose money) on Group Medicare Advantage, and a $41M loss on the wind-down of Accountable Care assets. In Q3 2026 the gap was smaller: $425M of intangible amortization, a $193M Omnicare guarantee charge and $13M of acquisition integration costs, less $103M of related tax benefit, accounted for the $0.41 per share between adjusted ($2.09) and GAAP ($1.68). On March 11, 2026, CVS paid $117.7M to settle Department of Justice False Claims Act allegations that Aetna submitted unsupported diagnosis codes to inflate Medicare risk scores (chart-review coding in 2015; obesity diagnoses 2018–2023); CVS did not admit liability and said it continues to disagree with what it called industry-wide allegations. Finally, the 2026 exit from the individual ACA exchange business was the main reason medical membership fell from 26.6M to 26.0M in the first quarter of 2026, and it makes year-over-year membership and premium comparisons less like-for-like.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The margin recovery is real: MBR of 92.5% in FY2024, 91.2% in FY2025, and a guide of about 89.5% to 89.75% for 2026, with Q3 at 91.3% against 92.8% a year ago. Management says it has now delivered 8 quarters of beat and raise. FY2026 adjusted EPS is guided to at least $8.20, operating cash flow to at least $15.5B, and 2027 adjusted EPS to at least $8.65; buybacks restart in Q4. At $83.85 the shares trade at about 9.7x StockAnalysis's FY2027 consensus, with a 3.17% dividend. The split lets the market value a pharmacy business and a health-benefits business separately, 25 of 28 analysts rate it Buy or better, and the average target is 31.6% above the close.

Bear Case

The stock fell 12.5% on a beat and raise, with CNBC naming the stores' standing without the PBM as one investor concern. CVS has not quantified the one-time or ongoing costs, and the 2027 floor of $8.65 is only 5.5% above the 2026 floor of $8.20. Health Services is guided to trough earnings in 2027, with weaker PBM selling and a shrinking 340B business. Star ratings slipped to 69% of members in 4-star plans for 2027, putting more than $2B of 2028 payments at stake by CVS's own count, and $1.3B of favorable prior-year claims development helped this year's results. The PBM profit pool has dates on it: Part D delinking in 2028, any-willing-pharmacy in 2029, Tennessee's ownership ban in July 2028. ROIC is 8.22% against a 12.39% peer median, and $75.3B of debt has to be divided between two companies.

The central debate. The board has now answered the old question of whether this conglomerate should be kept together: the stores go. The live question is whether the separation adds value or gives some up. Joyner says the links that matter survive, either inside CVS Health or through commercial agreements, and that CVS is acting "from a position of strength." The stock's first reaction was a fall of about $14.7B in market value on the day (12.5%, on the October 28 share count), and analysts on the call pressed on lost cost savings and capital plans. The test is what comes next: the size of the one-time and running costs, the terms of the contracts between the two companies, how the debt is divided, and whether the Medicare recovery keeps running while management is doing all of this. At 9.7x FY2027 consensus the market is pricing in a good deal of doubt on those points.

01
Medical costs and star ratings. Management keeps a "cautious view" of the rest of 2026 on elevated cost trends. The 2026 guidance implies the year finishes around 89.5% to 89.75%, and the 2027 star ratings cut 2028 payments unless CVS wins its lawsuit. This stock has shown it can fall 58% on medical costs alone.
02
Execution of the separation. Two companies, two balance sheets, two management teams and a web of contracts between them, all to be done by the first half of 2028 while the Medicare recovery is still in progress. Costs are not yet quantified.
03
PBM regulation and debt. Federal delinking in 2028 and any-willing-pharmacy in 2029 attack Caremark's economics whichever way the company is structured. $75.3B of debt on StockAnalysis's count leaves less room if profits slip, and creditors hold a claim on more than a third of the $167.7B enterprise value.
  Industry Commentary
The Medicare Advantage margin reset is still in progress
ONGOING HEADWIND

The whole managed-care industry mispriced post-pandemic senior health use. For CVS, the MBR ran to 92.5% in FY2024, leaving Health Care Benefits with just $307M of adjusted operating income that year and still only $2.9B on $143.4B of revenue in FY2025, about a 2% margin on the segment carrying almost all the risk. After two bid cycles of tighter pricing, the ratio was 91.2% for FY2025 and is guided to about 89.5% to 89.75% for 2026. Steve Nelson says Aetna's Medicare business is moving into profit in 2026 but remains on a multi-year path. Peers have rallied unevenly over the past year: Elevance +35.4%, UnitedHealth +22.6%, Cigna +14.5%, CVS +6.6% on price. Several other insurers have also sued over their 2027 star ratings, Healthcare Dive reports.

PBM reform has moved from rhetoric to statute
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

This policy issue has support in both parties, and it now has dates. Signed within the 2026 appropriations bill: from 2028, Medicare Part D PBM compensation is delinked from drug prices and limited to flat-dollar service fees at fair market value; from 2029, "any willing pharmacy" rules force Part D sponsors to admit any pharmacy meeting standard terms, with a protected "essential retail pharmacies" category; and semiannual disclosure of net drug spend, rebates and spread pricing to employer plans arrives sooner. At state level, Tennessee's FAIR Rx Act takes effect July 1, 2028 and bars common ownership of a PBM or insurer and a pharmacy in the state. CVS, Express Scripts and PCMA have sued, and a federal judge blocked Arkansas's comparable law with a preliminary injunction in July 2025, which the state appealed. Health Services, which includes Caremark, generated $7.2B of adjusted operating income in FY2025, down 1.3%, and CVS expects Health Services to bottom in 2027 at at least $7B. CVS's split answers the ownership question but not the fee question.

Where the two forces meet: bull versus bear implications
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull reading: Aetna's margins are recovering now, and the PBM rules do not bite until 2028–29, so an investor pays about 10.2 times the FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance of at least $8.20 while the regulatory clock runs. Walgreens is gone from the public market, the ACA exchange losses have stopped, cash flow has reached the level where buybacks can restart, and the split could let each business be valued on its own terms. CostVantage, TrueCost and Cordavis give CVS a transparency story if the model is forced open.

The bear reading: the market may be right to discount this. An 8.22% ROIC says the integrated model has trailed peers, and the split concedes that keeping the stores inside was not worth what it cost. If delinking and any-willing-pharmacy land as written, the rebates and the steering of patients to its own pharmacies that justified building this company are legislated away. The plain summary: a business recovering from a bad cycle is now also going through a corporate breakup, and the investment question is whether the recovery pays you before the regulation and the separation costs arrive.
