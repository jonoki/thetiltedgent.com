01 Company Overview

Chevron is one of the two American integrated oil majors — it finds and produces crude oil and natural gas (Upstream), then refines, transports and markets fuels, lubricants and petrochemicals (Downstream). FY2025 sales and other operating revenues were $184.4B, down 4.7% from 2024's $193.4B as realized prices fell, even though volumes hit a record; net income of $12.3B was the weakest since 2021. The picture inverted violently in 2026. The July 2025 close of the $53B all-stock Hess acquisition — completed only after an arbitration panel rejected ExxonMobil's and CNOOC's claimed right of first refusal over Hess's 30% Guyana stake — added Guyana and the Bakken just before the war with Iran disrupted the Strait of Hormuz and sent oil and refining margins soaring. Q2 2026 brought 4,070 MBOED of production (thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day), with record U.S. output, and $12.1B of net income, Chevron's best quarter in at least six years. Q3, reported October 30, was the second-best of the run: $9.9B of net income ($5.06 a share; $5.28 adjusted), against a Nasdaq consensus of $4.78, on sales of $65.2B, up 35% YoY. Production slipped to 3,980 MBOED: when prices rise, some contracts hand Chevron fewer barrels, and the Middle East conflict cut output, while U.S. refineries ran at a record pace. At $444.4B (1,951M shares at the Nov 2 close, our calculation), Chevron is the second-largest US energy company behind ExxonMobil ($732.9B).

In a commodity business, moat means cost position and duration, not pricing power — nobody sets the price of Brent. Chevron's advantage is that it owns unusually long-lived, unusually cheap barrels. Guyana's Stabroek development costs are among the lowest of any offshore basin on earth and, with Yellowtail, Uaru, Whiptail and Hammerhead sequenced through the early 2030s, they extend the reserve life of the portfolio at high margin without exploration risk. Tengiz — 50% owned, with processing capacity now slightly over 1 million barrels a day — is a decades-long annuity. In the Permian, a large slice of the acreage carries legacy low- or no-royalty terms, so Chevron keeps more of each barrel than most operators do. Wrapped around all of it: a balance sheet with a 9.6% net debt ratio at September 30 after $8.4B of debt was repaid in Q2 and another $3.5B in Q3, the structural cost cuts and Hess synergies management reported at the Q2 results, and 39 consecutive years of dividend increases. That combination is what lets Chevron survive $50 Brent without cutting the dividend — which is the only moat that actually matters in this industry.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Earnings are a leveraged bet on a price nobody controls. Q3 already showed the arithmetic running in reverse: international liquids realizations fell from $96.41/bbl in Q2 to $86.09, and international upstream earnings halved from $4.6B to $2.4B. The EIA's October outlook has Brent averaging $96 in 2026 and $84 in 2027, sliding to $74 by the fourth quarter of 2027 as Gulf production returns — about 30% below its $105 forecast for the current quarter. Nothing about Chevron's operations would have deteriorated; the windfall simply ends.

2. The best assets sit in the hardest jurisdictions. Tengiz exports predominantly through the CPC pipeline across Russian territory to Novorossiysk — a chokepoint that has been struck and shut before. Venezuela's joint ventures, now set to invest $7B over five years, operate under U.S. licence terms that have been revoked, restored and rewritten repeatedly since 2019 and could change again with an administration or a headline. Guyana itself faces a standing territorial claim from Venezuela over the Essequibo region. Chevron's growth is concentrated precisely where sovereign risk is highest.

3. Non-operated position in the crown jewel. Chevron owns 30% of Stabroek; ExxonMobil operates it. Development pace, capital budget, tieback sequencing and cost control are decided by a competitor that spent two years in arbitration trying to block Chevron from owning the stake at all. Chevron pays its share and takes its barrels, but has limited say over the asset that justified a $53B acquisition.

4. Terminal-value and reinvestment tension. Chevron returned $6.0B to shareholders in Q3 ($3.5B of dividends, $2.5B of buybacks) and expects 2026 capex at the low end of its $18–19B range — a deliberate choice to harvest rather than expand; the 2027 budget comes in December. That is the right answer if oil demand plateaus in the 2030s and the wrong one if it does not, and the market will not tell you which until the reserve replacement math becomes visible. Meanwhile the low-carbon portfolio remains a rounding error inside a −$3.5B "All Other" line; Project Kilby is the first credible earnings stream that does not depend on the oil price, and it has not reached its final investment decision.

Notable One-Off Items

Q3 2026 carried small charges. Reported net income of $9.9B versus adjusted earnings of $10.4B — a $437M gap made up of a $160M legal reserve, a $110M impairment, $24M of pension settlement costs and foreign-currency effects that cut earnings by $143M. None is large; the fall from Q2 is price- and volume-driven, not accounting-driven.

A large one-off is coming. Chevron expects a one-time after-tax loss of $3–4B when the Hess Midstream deal closes, expected by year-end 2026, treated as a special item: Q4 reported earnings will likely sit well below adjusted earnings. The same deal takes about $3.7B of Hess Midstream's debt off Chevron's balance sheet (8-K, Oct 6, 2026).

FY2025 was not clean. Reported net income of $12.3B against adjusted earnings of $13.5B (Q4 2025 release); the gap includes severance from the 2025 restructuring and Hess transaction costs. Any comparison of 2026 versus 2025 reported earnings therefore overstates the improvement.

Hess purchase accounting is the persistent distortion. The $53B all-stock deal closed July 18, 2025, with Chevron issuing about 301 million shares from treasury to Hess holders (1.025 Chevron shares per Hess share). That enlarged the denominator of every per-share and return metric, and the deal stepped up acquired asset values — which inflates depreciation, depletion and amortisation (the yearly write-down of wells and equipment) and therefore understates ROIC relative to legacy assets earning on written-down book value. The trailing twelve months (Q4 2025 to Q3 2026) are now entirely post-merger, so the TTM P/E of 16.7 compares like with like; what it mixes is two weak quarters (Q4 2025 and Q1 2026) with two windfall quarters.

Venezuela remains a policy variable, not a modelling input. In Q3 Chevron moved its Venezuelan interests to equity accounting, adding one month of production to the reported figures, and announced improved commercial terms and expanded acreage, with the joint ventures planning to invest $7B over five years. Licence terms have been altered repeatedly by successive U.S. administrations; the growth plan carries a political risk that no discounted cash flow can price.

Five years, and essentially two regimes separated by a three-year sideways market. From $112.87 at the end of November 2021, Chevron rose 62% in twelve months as the post-COVID demand recovery collided with the Russian invasion of Ukraine: $162.83 by March 2022, then $183.31 in November 2022 at the end of the most profitable year in the company's history. What followed was one of the longest range-bound stretches a mega-cap has produced in the modern era — from January 2023 through December 2025, thirty-six months, the stock traded almost entirely between $136 and $174 and ended 2025 at $152.41 — 15% below where it began 2023. Oil normalised, the Hess deal sat in arbitration limbo for eighteen months, and shareholders were paid to wait. The second regime began in January 2026: the stock gained 16% that month alone, then ran $176.90 (Jan) → $186.76 (Feb) → $206.90 (Mar) as war with Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, printing an intramonth high of $214.71 on March 30. The correction was equally violent — $165.76 by June 2026, a 20% drawdown, as Hormuz flows briefly resumed and Brent fell back to $72.68. July and August recovered almost all of it on the blowout Q2 print and renewed Iranian brinkmanship; September's attacks on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline sent Brent to $131 on September 15 (EIA), and October added 15.3% to a $235.52 month-end close, the highest on this chart, with an intraday high of $236.48 on the day of the Q3 print. Price return over the chart window: +101.8% ($112.87 at Nov 30, 2021 → $227.77 at Nov 2, 2026), against +65.1% for SPY and +72.9% for QQQ over the same dates (price only, dividends excluded). Chevron has, over five years, beaten its benchmark — but most of its gain came in 2026: at the end of 2025 the stock stood at $152.41, 35% above the window's start.

Momentum read. The 6-period monthly RSI held above 70 for three consecutive months from January to March 2026, peaking at 83.6 at the war-spike high, unwound to 44.0 by June as the drawdown ran, and climbed back to 76.7 at the October close; at the November 2 close it reads 69.8. That shape — overbought, washed out, and overbought again within seven months — is characteristic of a commodity stock trading its input price rather than its business. On daily data the picture is calmer: a 14-day RSI of 58.7, with the price 6.6% above its 50-day average ($213.60) and 17.6% above its 200-day average ($193.65). The most useful historical marker is April 2025, when the monthly RSI fell to 36.1 at $136.06; buying that reading would have returned about 67% by November 2, 2026. Today's reading offers no such edge: it is a stock near the top of its range, in the strongest earnings environment it has seen in six years, priced accordingly.

Relative performance. Over the chart window Chevron's price rose 101.8% against 65.1% for SPY, with far lower correlation to the index (beta 0.52) and a materially higher cash yield — which is exactly the role integrated energy is meant to play in a portfolio. Over the last year the gap is wider still: +44.4% for CVX against +10.3% for SPY and +8.3% for QQQ (Oct 31, 2025 close → Nov 2, 2026, price only). The distribution of that return is the caution. Chevron beat the index enormously in 2021–22, went sideways for three years, and has pulled ahead again only since January 2026 on a geopolitical event. An investor buying today at $227.77 is not buying a business whose long-run value has suddenly been re-assessed; they are buying a well-run, cash-generative company whose next twelve months of earnings are being set in the Strait of Hormuz.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

Two windfall quarters in a row have proved the Hess thesis: production of 4,070 MBOED in Q2 with record U.S. output, U.S. production still above 2 million BOE a day in Q3, record U.S. refinery runs, and $30.1B of free cash flow in Q2 and Q3 combined against $8.5B of capex. Guyana and Tengiz supply low-cost growth into the 2030s, Venezuela adds a self-funding $7B programme, and the Hess Midstream deal halves Bakken pipeline costs. The balance sheet is the sector's best — 0.17 debt-to-equity and a 9.6% net debt ratio after $11.9B of debt repaid in two quarters — so the 3.1% yield and 39-year growth streak are safe well below current prices. At 14.0x 2027 consensus earnings with 17.7% ROCE, you are paying a mid-cycle multiple for a business with above-mid-cycle volumes, plus a free option on Project Kilby turning cheap Permian gas into data-center power.

▼ Bear Case

Almost every number above is a function of one variable Chevron does not control. Q3 showed it: international liquids realizations fell about $10 a barrel from Q2 and adjusted earnings fell $1.6B, which CFO Eimear Bonner put down to lower realized prices, fewer cargoes sold (lower liftings) and timing effects; production also fell year over year. The EIA expects Brent to slide from $105 this quarter to $74 by late 2027 as Gulf supply returns and OPEC output recovers from 23.9 to 29.2 mmb/d, and analysts already pencil in 2027 EPS 12% below 2026. The stock sits 55% above its December low and 3.7% below its October 30 high, and the Street's average target of $241.67 offers only 6% upside. November 2 was a reminder: one day of peace-talk headlines cut Brent 7.6% and CVX 3.3%. June 2026 was the rehearsal: Brent fell to $72.68 and CVX lost 20% in ten weeks with nothing wrong operationally. Add non-operated Guyana, CPC export dependence through Russia, and Venezuelan licence risk, and you are underwriting geopolitics at the high end of a range.

Top 3 Risks
01
Oil price normalisation. The EIA's October base case — Brent $96 in 2026 and $84 in 2027, down to $74 by the fourth quarter of 2027 as most Gulf production returns by mid-2027 — implies 2027 earnings well below the 2026 windfall. Consensus already has 2027 EPS 12% lower. For scale: international upstream earnings halved between Q2 and Q3 on a $10/bbl drop in international liquids realizations.
02
Concentrated geopolitical exposure. Tengiz exports through the CPC pipeline across Russia; Venezuela's joint ventures run on a U.S. licence that has been revoked and reinstated repeatedly; Guyana faces a Venezuelan territorial claim; and Chevron does not operate Stabroek. Any single adverse event in Kazakhstan, Caracas or Georgetown affects an asset central to the plan for 2–3% a year production growth to 2030.
03
Capital-return pace is windfall-funded. Q3 distributions of $6.0B ($3.5B dividends, $2.5B buybacks) plus $3.5B of debt repayment were comfortably covered by $12.0B of free cash flow. Buybacks are running at the bottom of the $2.5–3.0B range management maintains through the cycle, and Bonner put growing the dividend first. At $74 Brent, the buyback is the first line to go — and the dividend — $7.12 a year, 52% of trailing EPS but 107% of FY2025's $6.63 — becomes the constraint rather than the cushion.
  Industry Commentary: The Macro Backdrop
The Hormuz Premium: The Largest Energy Disruption on Record
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The U.S.-Israeli war with Iran that began in late February 2026 has produced a supply shock without modern precedent. The EIA estimates crude production shut in by the conflict peaked at 10.9 mmb/d in May and was still 4.8 mmb/d in September; its October outlook has global liquids production at 101.1 mmb/d in 2026 against consumption of 102.4 mmb/d, with inventories drawn an estimated 1.9 mmb/d in Q3. OPEC output averages 23.9 mmb/d in 2026, against 29.3 mmb/d in 2025. Brent's path has been brutal: spiking through March, round-tripping to $72.68 by late June when flows briefly resumed, then climbing again — a $114 September average and a $131 peak on September 15 after attacks on Saudi Arabia's East-West export pipeline. On November 2 it settled at $104.77, down 7.6% in a day, as Qatari mediators shuttled to Tehran for talks on ending the war (Reuters). Chevron captures the price on the barrels it still produces; the war's direct cost to it shows up in volumes — Q3 production was lower partly because of "impacts from the Middle East conflict", in the company's words — while earnings nearly tripled year over year. The analytical discipline: a windfall is not a franchise, and the EIA expects most of the region's production back by mid-2027.
Capital Discipline Has Won — And That Is Why Prices Stay Volatile
ONGOING HEADWIND
A decade ago, $100 Brent would have triggered a U.S. shale spending boom that killed the price within eighteen months. That reflex is gone. Chevron is doing the opposite: full-year capex at the low end of its $18–19B guidance, Q3 capex lower than a year ago "largely due to lower spend in the Permian Basin", the basin deliberately held near its ~1 mmboe/d plateau, and $11.9B of debt repaid in two quarters rather than drilled. Every major is behaving the same way, which has two consequences. First, the supply response to high prices is slower and weaker than in any previous cycle — supportive of prices, and of Chevron's cash flows. Second, with shale no longer the swing supplier that adds barrels whenever prices rise, the market's shock absorber is OPEC+ spare capacity and inventories, which is why crude round-tripped in ten weeks this summer and then fell 7.6% in a single day on November 2. Investors get better free cash flow and more price volatility at the same time. The tell in Chevron's own behaviour: with $30.1B of free cash flow across Q2 and Q3, management chose debt reduction and cash over an accelerated buyback, holding repurchases at $2.5B in Q3, the bottom of its range. That is not the allocation of a team that believes $86–96/bbl international realizations persist.
Natural Gas Meets AI: The Cheap-Gas Arbitrage
EMERGING TAILWIND
The strangest number in Chevron's 2026 has not been the profit — it has been U.S. natural gas realizations of $0.91/mcf in Q2 and $1.88/mcf in Q3, against $9.44/mcf internationally in Q3. Permian associated gas comes up whether you want it or not, pipeline takeaway is constrained, and the molecule is worth very little at the wellhead. Meanwhile U.S. electricity demand is growing for the first time in roughly two decades, and the big cloud companies face grid connection queues measured in years. Chevron's answer is Project Kilby: a 20-year agreement with Microsoft for about 2.67 GW of on-site generation in West Texas, with the final investment decision expected by the end of 2026 and first power targeted for 2028. Converting a $1–2 molecule into contracted electricity is the highest-return use of that gas by a wide margin. The frame for investors: the bull sees a company that has just added Guyana's growth, holds the best balance sheet in the sector, is cutting Bakken costs and building an earnings stream that does not depend on oil — all at 14.0x 2027 consensus earnings with a 3.1% yield backed by 39 years of increases. The bear sees $22B of profit in two quarters that exists because a strait is disrupted, an EIA forecast of $74 Brent by late 2027, a stock 55% off its lows with 6% of consensus upside, and a share price that fell 3.3% on a single day of peace-talk headlines. Both are describing the same $444.4B company. The disagreement is not about Chevron's execution — which has been excellent — but about how long the Strait of Hormuz stays shut, and that is not an equity-research question.
