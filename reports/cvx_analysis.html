01 Company Overview

Chevron is one of the two American integrated oil majors — it finds and produces crude oil and natural gas (Upstream), then refines, transports and markets fuels, lubricants and petrochemicals (Downstream). FY2025 sales and other operating revenues were $184.4B, down 4.7% from 2024's $193.4B as realized prices fell, even though volumes hit a record; net income of $12.3B was the weakest since 2021. The picture inverted violently in 2026. Following the July 2025 close of the $53B all-stock Hess acquisition — completed only after an arbitration panel rejected ExxonMobil's and CNOOC's claimed right of first refusal over Hess's 30% Guyana stake — and the ramp of Tengiz in Kazakhstan, Q2 2026 production reached a record 4,070 MBOED (thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, +20% YoY). Combined with an oil and refining-margin shock driven by the disruption of the Strait of Hormuz, that produced $12.1B of quarterly net income ($6.06 adjusted EPS) — Chevron's best quarter in at least six years. At $385.6B, Chevron is roughly the 40th most valuable listed company in the world and the second-largest US energy company behind ExxonMobil ($652.8B).

In a commodity business, moat means cost position and duration, not pricing power — nobody sets the price of Brent. Chevron's advantage is that it owns unusually long-lived, unusually cheap barrels. Guyana's Stabroek development costs are among the lowest of any offshore basin on earth and, with Yellowtail, Uaru, Whiptail and Hammerhead sequenced through the early 2030s, they extend the reserve life of the portfolio at high margin without exploration risk. Tengiz — 50% owned, now producing slightly above 1 million b/d after a very low-capital debottleneck from 260 to 320 kb/d on the third-generation plant — is a decades-long annuity. In the Permian, a large slice of the acreage carries legacy low- or no-royalty terms, so Chevron keeps more of each barrel than most operators do. Wrapped around all of it: a balance sheet with a 13.1% net debt ratio after an $8.4B single-quarter paydown, a $3B structural cost reduction delivered six months early (management says more than 70% came from durable efficiency rather than deferral), $1.5B of Hess synergies captured 50% above target and ahead of schedule, and 39 consecutive years of dividend increases. That combination is what lets Chevron survive $50 Brent without cutting the dividend — which is the only moat that actually matters in this industry.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Earnings are a leveraged bet on a price nobody controls. Q2 2026 international liquids realizations of $96.41/bbl (+64% YoY) delivered a 255% increase in international upstream earnings. That arithmetic runs in reverse just as fast. The EIA's own outlook has Brent averaging $87 in 2026 and $69 in 2027 — a 21% decline that, applied to Chevron's Q2 realizations, would erase the majority of the current earnings uplift. Nothing about Chevron's operations would have deteriorated; the windfall simply ends.

2. The best assets sit in the hardest jurisdictions. Tengiz exports predominantly through the CPC pipeline across Russian territory to Novorossiysk — a chokepoint that has been struck and shut before. Venezuela's 280 kb/d of joint-venture output operates under U.S. licence terms that have been revoked, restored and rewritten repeatedly since 2019 and could change again with an administration or a headline. Guyana itself faces a standing territorial claim from Venezuela over the Essequibo region. Chevron's growth is concentrated precisely where sovereign risk is highest.

3. Non-operated position in the crown jewel. Chevron owns 30% of Stabroek; ExxonMobil operates it. Development pace, capital budget, tieback sequencing and cost control are decided by a competitor that spent two years in arbitration trying to block Chevron from owning the stake at all. Chevron pays its share and takes its barrels, but has limited say over the asset that justified a $53B acquisition.

4. Terminal-value and reinvestment tension. Chevron is returning roughly $6.5B per quarter to shareholders against $18–19B of annual capex — a deliberate choice to harvest rather than expand. That is the right answer if oil demand plateaus in the 2030s and the wrong one if it does not, and the market will not tell you which until the reserve replacement math becomes visible. Meanwhile the low-carbon portfolio remains a rounding error inside a −$3.5B "All Other" line; Project Kilby is the first credible non-hydrocarbon-margin earnings stream, and it has not reached FID.

Notable One-Off Items

Q2 2026 was unusually clean. Reported net income of $12.1B versus adjusted earnings of $12.0B — a gap of roughly $100M, comprising a $230M asset-sale gain offset by $86M of pension settlement expense, with foreign-currency effects reducing earnings by $49M. There is no large impairment hiding in the quarter; the earnings jump is operational and price-driven, not accounting-driven.

FY2025 was not clean. Special items totalled −$753M after tax, including roughly $696M of severance (the 2025 restructuring that cut 15–20% of the workforce), $355M of Hess integration costs, $223M of pension settlement charges, and assorted asset write-offs and impairments. Any comparison of 2026 versus 2025 earnings therefore overstates the improvement by roughly $0.40 of EPS.

Hess purchase accounting is the persistent distortion. The $53B all-stock deal closed July 18, 2025, adding ~1.96B shares' worth of equity dilution to the denominator of every per-share and return metric, and stepping up acquired asset values — which inflates depreciation, depletion and amortisation and therefore understates ROIC relative to legacy assets earning on written-down book value. It also means trailing-twelve-month figures blend six months of pre-merger and six months of post-merger Chevron: the TTM P/E of 18.9 is comparing today's share count against yesterday's earnings.

Venezuela remains a policy variable, not a modelling input. Licence terms governing the three joint ventures (280 kb/d, +15% in six months) are described by management as confidential and have been altered repeatedly by successive U.S. administrations. Chevron guides to ~50% production growth there by end-2028; that guidance carries a political option embedded in it that no discounted cash flow can price.

Five years, and essentially two regimes separated by a three-year sideways market. From $101.45 in September 2021, Chevron doubled in fifteen months as the post-COVID demand recovery collided with the Russian invasion of Ukraine: $162.83 by March 2022, then $183.31 in November 2022 at the end of the most profitable year in the company's history. What followed was one of the longest range-bound stretches a mega-cap has produced in the modern era — from January 2023 through December 2025, thirty-six months, the stock traded almost entirely between $136 and $174 and ended 2025 at $152.41 — 15% below where it began 2023. Oil normalised, the Hess deal sat in arbitration limbo for eighteen months, and shareholders were paid to wait. The second regime began in January 2026: the stock gained 16% that month alone, then ran $176.90 (Jan) → $186.76 (Feb) → $206.90 (Mar) as war with Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, printing a 52-week high of $214.71 intramonth in March. The correction was equally violent — $165.76 by June 2026, a 20% drawdown, as Hormuz flows briefly resumed and Brent fell back to $72.68. July and August then recovered all of it on the blowout Q2 print and renewed Iranian brinkmanship. Total 5-year price return: approximately +94% ($101.45 → $196.60), or roughly +130% with dividends reinvested, against approximately +100% price / ~+115% total return for the S&P 500 and roughly +130% total return for the S&P 500 Energy sector (index figures approximate). Chevron has, over five years, matched its benchmark — with all of the outperformance earned in the last eight months.

Momentum read. The 6-period monthly RSI held above 70 for three consecutive months from January to March 2026, peaking at 83.6 at the war-spike high, unwound sharply into June as the drawdown ran, and has snapped back into the 60s on the July–August recovery. That shape — overbought, washed out, and back to strong-but-not-extreme within six months — is characteristic of a commodity equity trading its input price rather than its business. The most useful historical marker is April 2025, when RSI bottomed near oversold at $136.06; buying that reading would have returned roughly 45% in sixteen months. Today's reading offers no such edge: it is a stock at the upper end of its range, in the strongest earnings environment it has seen in six years, priced accordingly.

Relative performance. Over the full five years Chevron has roughly matched the S&P 500 on total return while delivering it with far lower correlation to the index (beta 0.49) and a materially higher cash yield — which is exactly the role integrated energy is meant to play in a portfolio. The distribution of that return is the caution. Chevron beat the index enormously in 2021–22, lagged badly for three years, and has beaten it again only since January 2026 on a geopolitical event. An investor buying today at $196.60 is not buying a compounding franchise re-rating; they are buying a well-run, cheap, cash-generative company whose next twelve months of earnings are being set in the Strait of Hormuz.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

Chevron has just proved the Hess thesis: production up 20% YoY to a record 4,070 MBOED, $1.5B of synergies captured 50% above target and six months early, $3B of structural cost cuts delivered early, and $18.1B of quarterly free cash flow against $4.5B of capex. Guyana and Tengiz supply low-cost growth into the 2030s while Permian capital falls 25%. The balance sheet is the sector's best — 0.19 debt-to-equity, a 13.1% net debt ratio after a record $8.4B quarterly paydown — so the 3.6% yield and 39-year growth streak are safe well below current prices. At 12.8x forward earnings and a 0.96 PEG with 21.4% ROCE, you are paying a mid-cycle multiple for a business with above-mid-cycle volumes, plus a free option on Project Kilby monetising stranded Permian gas as data-center power.

▼ Bear Case

Almost every number above is a function of one variable Chevron does not control. Q2's $12.1B came from liquids realizations up 48–64% YoY because the Strait of Hormuz is closed — and the EIA expects Brent to average $87 in 2026 and $69 in 2027 as 5.5 mmb/d of shut-in supply returns and OPEC output recovers from 24.0 to 29.7 mmb/d. The stock already sits 34% above its December low, 8.4% below the war high, and the Street's average target of $216.96 offers only 10% upside with Barclays, Bernstein and TD Cowen all at or below the current price. June 2026 was the rehearsal: Brent fell to $72.68 and CVX lost 20% in ten weeks with nothing wrong operationally. Add non-operated Guyana, CPC export dependence through Russia, and Venezuelan licence risk, and you are underwriting geopolitics at the high end of a range.

Top 3 Risks
01
Oil price normalisation. The EIA's own base case — Brent $87 in 2026 falling to $69 in 2027, with production and trade patterns returning to pre-conflict form by early 2027 — implies 2027 earnings materially below the 2026 windfall. Consensus is currently marking up 2026 estimates into that forecast. A return to $70 Brent plausibly halves upstream earnings from Q2's run-rate.
02
Concentrated geopolitical exposure. Tengiz exports through the CPC pipeline across Russia; Venezuela's 280 kb/d runs on a U.S. licence that has been revoked and reinstated repeatedly; Guyana faces a Venezuelan territorial claim; and Chevron does not operate Stabroek. Any single adverse event in Kazakhstan, Caracas or Georgetown affects an asset central to the 2–3% production CAGR to 2030.
03
Capital-return pace is windfall-funded. Q2 distributions of $6.5B ($3.5B dividends, $3.0B buybacks) plus $8.4B of debt repayment were affordable only because free cash flow was $18.1B. Q3 buyback guidance has already been cut to $2.5–3.0B, and the 2024 pace of $3.75B per quarter has not returned. At $69 Brent, the buyback is the first line to go — and the dividend, at 68% of trailing earnings, becomes the constraint rather than the cushion.
  Industry Commentary: The Macro Backdrop
The Hormuz Premium: The Largest Energy Disruption on Record
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The U.S.-Israeli war with Iran that began in late February 2026 has produced a supply shock without modern precedent. Only 8–15 vessels per day were transiting the Strait of Hormuz in early August against roughly 130 before the conflict, with at least 64 violent incidents and 17 deaths involving commercial shipping since the war began. The EIA estimates shut-in production averaged 5.5 mmb/d in July 2026 and now forecasts global liquids production of 100.8 mmb/d in 2026 against 106.1 mmb/d in 2025, with consumption at 102.7 mmb/d — a deficit that drew inventories down 4.2 mmb/d in Q2 and an expected 3.8 mmb/d in Q3. OPEC output collapses to 24.0 mmb/d in 2026 from a forecast 29.7 mmb/d in 2027. Brent traded at $84.11 on August 10, about 16% above pre-war levels — but the path has been brutal, spiking through March, round-tripping to $72.68 by late June when flows briefly resumed, and re-rating again in August on Iran's demand for sanctions relief and reparations. Chevron has no Iranian or Persian Gulf production of consequence, so it captures the price without carrying the operational risk — the purest possible windfall. The analytical discipline: a windfall is not a franchise, and the EIA expects normalisation by early 2027.
Capital Discipline Has Won — And That Is Why Prices Stay Volatile
ONGOING HEADWIND
A decade ago, $84 Brent would have triggered a U.S. shale spending boom that killed the price within eighteen months. That reflex is gone. Chevron is doing the opposite: full-year capex guided to the low end of $18–19B, Permian spending below $3.5B and 25% lower than 2025 with the basin deliberately held at ~1 mmboe/d plateau, and $8.4B of debt repaid in a single quarter rather than drilled. Every major is behaving the same way, which has two consequences. First, the supply response to high prices is slower and weaker than in any previous cycle — supportive of prices, and of Chevron's cash flows. Second, with shale no longer functioning as the marginal swing producer, the market's shock absorber is OPEC+ spare capacity and inventories, which is why crude round-tripped 20% in ten weeks this summer. Investors get better free cash flow and more price volatility at the same time. The tell in Chevron's own behaviour: given $18.1B of quarterly free cash flow, management chose debt reduction over an accelerated buyback and guided Q3 repurchases down to $2.5–3.0B. That is not the allocation of a team that believes $96/bbl international realizations persist.
Natural Gas Meets AI: The $0.91/mcf Arbitrage
EMERGING TAILWIND
The strangest number in Chevron's Q2 was not the profit — it was U.S. natural gas realizations of $0.91/mcf, down 48% YoY, against $7.84/mcf internationally. Permian associated gas comes up whether you want it or not, pipeline takeaway is constrained, and the molecule is worth almost nothing at the wellhead. Meanwhile U.S. electricity demand is growing for the first time in roughly two decades, and hyperscalers face grid interconnection queues measured in years. Chevron's answer is Project Kilby: a 20-year agreement with Microsoft for 2.67 GW of behind-the-meter generation in West Texas at mid-teens returns, with FID expected later in 2026 and management describing it as a repeatable model. Converting a $0.91 molecule into contracted electricity is the highest-return use of that gas by a wide margin. The frame for investors: the bull sees a company that has just added Guyana's growth, delivered $1.5B of synergies early, cut $3B of structural cost, holds the best balance sheet in the sector, and is now building a non-commodity earnings stream — all at 12.8x forward earnings with a 3.6% yield backed by 39 years of increases. The bear sees $12.1B of quarterly profit that exists because a strait is closed, an EIA forecast of $69 Brent in 2027, a stock 34% off its lows with 10% of consensus upside, and three of the loudest recent analyst calls sitting at or below the current price. Both are describing the same $385.6B company. The disagreement is not about Chevron's execution — which has been excellent — but about how long the Strait of Hormuz stays shut, and that is not an equity-research question.
