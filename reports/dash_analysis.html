01 Company Overview

DoorDash operates a local-commerce platform that connects merchants, consumers and independent couriers ("Dashers"), spanning restaurant delivery, grocery, convenience and general retail, plus merchant software and an advertising business. FY2025 revenue was $13.72B, up 27.9% from $10.72B in FY2024, and revenue for the twelve months through September 2026 is $17.06B, up 35.0% — growth inflated by the October 2025 completion of the Deliveroo acquisition. Its market capitalisation is $82.87B at the November 2, 2026 close.

The reason this name is worth a careful look is narrower than "growth stock." Food delivery was for a decade the textbook business that grew fast and lost money — DoorDash itself lost $1.37B in FY2022 on $6.58B of revenue. It printed $935M of GAAP net income in FY2025 and $466M in Q3 2026 alone, up 91% on a year earlier. The question on this page is whether that reflects a business whose orders now genuinely make money or an accounting-and-scale effect. Section 04 and the prose below take that apart.

The Instacart deal. On October 29, 2026, alongside its Q3 results, DoorDash agreed to buy Maplebear Inc. (Instacart) for $65.00 a share, an equity value of about $17 billion, at least 75% in cash and up to 25% in DoorDash stock. The price is a 26% premium to Instacart's October 22 close, the last trading day before media reports of a possible deal. The cash comes from cash on hand and new debt (JPMorgan has committed a 364-day bridge loan, a short-term loan meant to be replaced by bonds), and closing is expected at the end of 2027 or in the first half of 2028, after Instacart's shareholders vote and antitrust reviews in the U.S., Canada and Israel. CEO Tony Xu's case, in the joint release: "Grocery is one of the hardest things to get right online." For a company whose own grocery push has not yet been shown to make money per order, buying Instacart, a grocery-delivery business that already turns a GAAP profit, is the biggest change to the story since the Deliveroo deal.

The expansion beyond restaurants. Grocery and retail baskets are larger than a burrito, so they lift average order value — but they carry different economics: more items to pick, longer shop times, higher substitution and refund rates, and a colder start on merchant integrations. The company is explicit that this is not yet paying: it told investors that grocery and retail unit economics are expected to turn positive in the second half of 2026. That is a dated, falsifiable claim and the single most checkable item on the whole thesis. In December 2025, over 30% of U.S. monthly active users and nearly 30% of global MAUs engaged with these categories. In Q2 2026, DashPass members placed roughly 75% of U.S. grocery and retail orders — the subscription and the expansion into new categories feed each other rather than running as two separate bets. In Q3 2026 the company said Marketplace GOV in U.S. grocery and retail grew more than twice as fast as U.S. restaurants; its Q3 release does not say whether the unit-economics crossover promised for the second half has happened.

White-label delivery (Drive), merchant-branded ordering (Storefront), reservations and CRM (SevenRooms, acquired for $1,152M, completed June 13, 2025), and advertising. On September 15, 2026 DoorDash agreed to buy Grubhub Campus Dining from Wonder for $300M (order-ahead ordering at more than 450 colleges, expected to close in the first half of 2027) and invested $125M in Wonder (Nation's Restaurant News). DoorDash does not disclose advertising revenue, so no figure is given here — but this is analytically the most important small line on the page. Ads are sold against demand the platform already has; there is no incremental Dasher cost, no incremental food cost, and essentially no incremental delivery risk. Every advertising dollar therefore falls to contribution profit at a rate the delivery business can never match. After the Symbiosys acquisition, the number of advertising partners using Symbiosys nearly doubled between June and December 2025. If GAAP profitability is going to become structural rather than marginal, advertising and merchant software are the most credible route — and the absence of a disclosed revenue number is a real gap in the disclosure.

Disclosure gap: no advertising revenue line, no advertising take rate, no ad ARPU. Investors are asked to take the margin story on trust.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
DELIVERY DENSITY DASHER SUPPLY LIQUIDITY DASHPASS HABIT LOCK-IN SUBURBAN-FIRST FOOTPRINT MERCHANT SOFTWARE STACK ROUTING & DATA SCALE ADVERTISING FLYWHEEL

The moat here is density, not technology. In any given zip code the platform with the most orders per square mile can batch deliveries more efficiently, keep Dashers busier per hour of pay, and quote shorter delivery times — which wins more consumers, which raises density again. That loop is local, which is why market share is fought street by street and why DoorDash's historical strength in suburban U.S. markets, where the competition arrived later, is worth more than a national share number would suggest. Sitting on top of density are two hardening layers: DashPass (the paid subscription that waives delivery fees and reduces service fees, converting price-sensitive one-off users into habitual repeat buyers) and the merchant software stack — Storefront, Drive and now SevenRooms — which raises the cost of a restaurant leaving the ecosystem entirely. The contribution-profit disclosure supports the density argument: Q3 2026 contribution profit was $1,780M, or 5.2% of GOV, up from $1,268M a year earlier (contribution profit = revenue less the direct variable costs of fulfilling an order — Dasher pay, payment processing, support — before overhead; it is the cleanest read on whether an extra order makes money). About five cents of contribution on every dollar of gross order value, at 993 million orders a quarter, is a real machine. What it is not is a wide moat against a well-capitalised competitor in a single dense city — and buying Instacart takes one such competitor out of grocery rather than fighting it city by city.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Driver classification law. Dashers are independent contractors (driver classification = the legal question of whether gig couriers are self-employed contractors or employees entitled to minimum wage, overtime, payroll taxes and benefits). Reclassification would reset the cost base of the entire delivery layer, not trim it. California's Proposition 22, which preserved contractor status with limited benefits, was upheld by the California Supreme Court in July 2024 — but that is one state, and city- and country-level rules continue to move independently. DoorDash recorded $98M of legal, tax and regulatory expense in Q2 2026 and $16M in Q3 2026, which it adds back to adjusted EBITDA; a line item that appears every quarter is arguably a cost of doing business, not a one-off. On September 22, 2026 it agreed a $131.5M settlement with New York City's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection — about $115M of relief for some 264,000 Dashers plus a $16.7M fine — after admitting some couriers were underpaid or paid late (Reuters, via NBC News).
▸
City-level fee caps. Several U.S. municipalities have capped the commission platforms may charge restaurants. Caps compress the take rate directly in exactly the densest, highest-GOV markets, and the platform's usual response — pushing costs onto the consumer as fees — invites the next round of regulation. This is a slow, grinding, politically durable risk rather than a single event.
▸
Competition from Uber Eats — and the Instacart antitrust review. Uber can subsidise Eats from a $55.23B revenue base and $9.58B of trailing net income (stockanalysis.com, before Uber's Q3 report on November 4), which means it can afford to undercut DoorDash in a given city for longer than DoorDash can. In grocery, DoorDash has stopped attacking Instacart and agreed to buy it ($475M of trailing net income on $4.15B of revenue). That swaps a competitive risk for a regulatory one: the deal needs U.S. antitrust clearance (the Hart-Scott-Rodino review), plus approvals in Canada and Israel, and analysts cited by CNBC said the two companies' overlap could draw antitrust scrutiny.
▸
Adjusted-versus-GAAP disclosure risk. The company leads with adjusted EBITDA, which excludes stock-based compensation. At $1,051M of SBC in FY2025 on $13.72B of revenue — 7.7% — the exclusion is not a rounding convention, it is the difference between a $2.78B business and a $935M one. If investors ever switch to valuing the company on GAAP profit, the price they pay per dollar of earnings falls without a single operating metric changing.

Notable One-Off Items

There are material non-recurring distortions in this period and they all run the same direction — they flatter the top line and depress GAAP earnings.

Deliveroo, $3,724M, completed October 2, 2025. This is a structural change, not a bolt-on. It added roughly eleven percentage points to reported order growth (Q3 2026: +28% total orders versus +17% excluding Deliveroo) and roughly thirteen to GOV growth (+36% versus +23%). It brought a large chunk of the $2.9B of new goodwill recognised in 2025, along with identified intangibles — developed technology ($216M), merchant, customer, rider and new-vertical relationships, and trade names. Those intangibles amortise, which is why depreciation and amortisation ran $312M in Q3 2026, with FY2026 D&A now guided at about $1.2B, including about $450M of amortisation of acquired intangibles. In February 2026 management said it expected Deliveroo to contribute about $200M of adjusted EBITDA in 2026, after more than $45M in Q4 2025 alone, and guided Q1 2026 to under $25M because of extra investment (Q4 2025 release).

Instacart, about $17B equity value, agreed October 29, 2026. Not yet a one-off in the numbers, but it will be: deal costs (Q3 carried $20M of transaction-related costs across all deals, added back to adjusted EBITDA), new debt from the bridge loan and its refinancing, and — if Instacart holders elect stock, capped at 25% of the price — roughly 22 million new DoorDash shares, about 5% of today's count (our estimate: $17B ÷ $65 ≈ 261.5M Instacart shares × 25% × 0.3372 DoorDash shares each). DoorDash says it targets a capital structure that keeps investment-grade credit ratings.

SevenRooms, $1,152M, completed June 13, 2025. Restaurant reservation, waitlist and CRM software. Smaller, but strategically it is the merchant-lock-in play, and it contributes further intangible amortisation.

Symbiosys (advertising technology) was also acquired; the company says the number of advertising partners using it nearly doubled between June and December 2025.

Legal, tax and regulatory expense of $98M in Q2 2026 and $16M in Q3 2026 is added back to adjusted EBITDA, as are transaction-related costs ($13M in Q2, $20M in Q3). The transaction costs are genuinely one-off. The legal and regulatory line has appeared repeatedly, and for a business whose central open question is driver classification, adding it back every quarter is a choice worth noticing.

Buyback: 6.8M shares repurchased in the first nine months of 2026 for $1,049M — an average of roughly $155 per share, below the current $190.07 — and none in Q3; about $4.0B of the February 2025 authorisation remains. Weighted-average diluted shares were 440.3M in Q3 2026 against 435.98M shares outstanding on October 26, so the buyback has roughly offset SBC dilution rather than shrunk the count meaningfully.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The stack, in the company's own order. Start with Total Orders: 993M in Q3 2026, up 28%, up 17% excluding Deliveroo. Multiply by average order value and you get Marketplace GOV (Gross Order Value — the full retail value of everything transacted on the platform): $33.9B, up 36%, up 23% excluding Deliveroo. GOV divided by orders gives an average order of about $34.18, against $32.24 a year earlier — the grocery and retail push is doing what it is supposed to do to basket size. Revenue is the take rate on that GOV (the share of gross order value DoorDash keeps as its own revenue, after paying merchants): $4,616M ÷ $33.9B = 13.6%, matching the company's stated net revenue margin, against 13.8% a year earlier. Then adjusted EBITDA of $1,060M, up 41% — growing faster than both orders and GOV, which is the pattern the bull case needs: profit rising faster than the business it comes from.

And then GAAP net income: $466M, up 91% year over year. In the previous edition this was the problem: in Q2, adjusted EBITDA rose 40% while GAAP net income fell 30% to $200M. In Q3 the two moved together, and GAAP profit was 44% of adjusted EBITDA against 22% in Q2. The reconciliation still matters: from $466M of net income, DoorDash adds back D&A of $312M, stock-based compensation and related payroll tax of $269M, legal/tax/regulatory of $16M, transaction costs of $20M and taxes of $19M, and takes out $37M of net interest income and $5M of other income, to reach $1,060M. SBC (stock-based compensation — shares and options granted to employees; a real transfer of ownership from existing shareholders to staff, which costs no cash but dilutes every holder) was $1,051M in FY2025 — 7.7% of revenue — and is guided to about $1.2B for FY2026, roughly 6.7% of the $17.95B consensus revenue forecast (stockanalysis.com). Put differently: FY2025 adjusted EBITDA was $2.78B (the sum of the four quarters) and GAAP net income was $935M; SBC alone accounts for about 57% of that $1.84B gap. Anyone valuing this business on adjusted EBITDA is valuing it before paying its employees in full.

Thread 1 — cohort economics: what is and is not disclosed. The bull case rests on a specific mechanism: each annual group of new customers costs marketing money once, then orders more often every year afterward, so a maturing customer base should throw off rising profit without rising acquisition spend. DoorDash confirms the direction. Its Q2 2026 letter states that as consumer cohorts age, "DashPass penetration increases, order rates increase, and Adjusted Gross Profit per MAU increases". The Q3 release adds that order rates grew by double digits year over year for nearly all mature cohorts. Those are real disclosures and they are consistent with the reported numbers. But note carefully what is not disclosed: no cohort retention table, no per-cohort order-frequency curve, no customer acquisition cost, no payback period, no gross-profit-per-cohort-year figures. Investors are given the shape of the curve in words and asked to accept its steepness on trust. The one place the argument is checkable is new verticals, where management put a date on it — 2H 2026 for positive unit economics. The Q3 release is silent on it, so the Q4 report is the test.

Thread 2 — DashPass. The subscription (DashPass — a paid membership that waives delivery fees and reduces service fees on qualifying orders) is the mechanism that turns a price-comparison shopper into a habit. Combined membership across DashPass, Wolt+ and Deliveroo Plus exceeded 35 million at the end of FY2025, and the Q3 release says: "We continued to drive strong growth in U.S. DashPass paid memberships in Q3 2026, which helps reduce transactional friction for consumers." It credits DashPass growth with helping cut transaction fees per order in U.S. restaurants by 9% over two years. DashPass is also where the vertical expansion actually lands — members placed roughly 75% of U.S. grocery and retail orders in Q2 2026. The subscription, frequency and new-vertical stories are one story, and the Instacart deal adds a second membership base: the deal release estimates that one membership in place of both DashPass and Instacart+ would save members about $100M a year in membership fees.

Thread 3 — beyond restaurants, quantified. Over 30% of U.S. MAUs and nearly 30% of global MAUs engaged with grocery and retail in December 2025, and in Q3 2026 U.S. grocery and retail GOV grew more than twice as fast as U.S. restaurant GOV. Bigger baskets lift the average order ($32.24 to $34.18 year over year), which lifts revenue per order at a constant take rate. But the margin profile differs: more items per order means more Dasher shop time, more substitutions, more refunds. The company's own admission that unit economics here were not yet positive — with a fix promised for 2H 2026 — is the honest version of what "expanding into grocery" costs. Buying Instacart, which earned $147M of GAAP net income in Q3 on $1,095M of revenue, is the faster and more expensive answer to the same problem.

Thread 4 — advertising, the real margin path. DoorDash's advertising revenue is not separately disclosed, so no number appears here. Structurally, though, it is the most credible route to lasting GAAP profit: ad dollars carry almost no extra cost, unlike a delivery, where every dollar of revenue drags Dasher pay behind it. After the Symbiosys acquisition, the number of advertising partners using it nearly doubled between June and December 2025. Instacart does disclose its line — $331M of advertising and other revenue in Q3 2026, up 23% — and DoorDash pitches the combined reach to advertisers as a reason for the deal. Alongside it, merchant software is growing fast — in Q2 2026 digital ordering revenue grew over 40% YoY across 150,000+ merchants, and new signed venues at SevenRooms grew over 100% YoY. These lines are small; they are also the only ones whose growth turns almost fully into profit.

Valuation — and which earnings base. Be explicit, because the answer changes by more than a factor of three depending on the denominator. (i) On trailing GAAP earnings of $2.41 per share, DASH trades at 79×. That is the honest, fully-loaded number: it charges the company for stock compensation and for the amortisation of what it paid for Deliveroo and SevenRooms. (ii) On the 2026 consensus EPS of $5.81 on stockanalysis.com, it trades at 33×, and at 24× the 2027 figure of $8.02; stockanalysis.com says these are adjusted (non-GAAP) estimates, against GAAP diluted EPS of $1.93 for the first nine months. The $3.40 gap between $2.41 and $5.81 is where all the argument lives. (iii) On trailing free cash flow of $2.20B (company definition), it trades at about 38× market cap (EV/FCF ≈ 36×) — but free cash flow adds SBC back as non-cash, so it flatters an owner who cares about dilution. (iv) On adjusted EBITDA, Q3's $1,060M times four gives about $4.2B (est.), or about 19× the $80.18B enterprise value (market cap less net cash); the reported EV/EBITDA of 46.9× (stockanalysis.com) uses the GAAP measure instead. The defensible base is somewhere between (i) and (iii): GAAP earnings are real but held down by acquisition amortisation that will run off, while free cash flow is real but ignores about $1.2B of annual dilution. Charge the business for SBC and credit it for the non-cash amortisation, and the multiple lands between about 38× and 79×. For comparison, DoorDash is paying about 36× Instacart's trailing GAAP net income (our arithmetic: $17B ÷ $475M). At any point in that band, the stock is priced for per-order profits to keep improving — not merely to have turned positive once.

Bull Case

The economics have genuinely turned and are still improving: contribution profit of $1,780M is 5.2% of GOV, adjusted EBITDA grew 41% against 28% order growth, and in Q3 GAAP net income followed, up 91% to $466M. Cohorts mature into higher frequency, DashPass locks in that frequency, and advertising and merchant software (digital ordering revenue up over 40% and new SevenRooms venues up over 100% in Q2 2026) layer profit on top of a delivery business that already covers its variable costs. Instacart adds a profitable grocery business with its own $331M-a-quarter advertising line and, on management's figures, a path to $500M or more of annual synergies. Net cash at September 30 ($5.98B of cash and short-term investments against $3.30B of debt) gives room to fund the cash part of the deal while targeting investment-grade ratings.

Bear Case

One good GAAP quarter does not change the cost base: about $1.2B of FY2026 stock-based compensation and about $1.2B of D&A, and the Q4 adjusted EBITDA guide's midpoint ($1.0B) is below Q3's $1,060M. Headline growth is inflated by Deliveroo and will step down from Q4, just as the company stops reporting the ex-Deliveroo figures. The Instacart deal adds a $17B price, new debt, up to about 5% share dilution and an antitrust review that runs to 2027 or 2028 — while DoorDash's own grocery unit economics are still unproven at the deadline it set. At 79× trailing GAAP earnings with a 1.89 beta, driver-classification law that could reset the cost base, and Uber able to subsidise from $9.58B of trailing net income, the margin for error on a 6.2% net margin is small.

Top 3 Risks

01
Driver classification and pay rules. Reclassifying Dashers as employees would add minimum wage, overtime, payroll taxes and benefits to the largest variable cost in the model — it would not shave the cost base, it would reset it. Prop 22 survived in California (upheld July 2024), but city and international rules move separately: New York City's $131.5M pay settlement in September 2026 and the legal/tax/regulatory line ($98M in Q2, $16M in Q3) show the cost of those rules is already live.
02
The Instacart deal — blocked, delayed or overpaid. Closing is more than a year away and depends on U.S., Canadian and Israeli approvals; the two companies overlap in U.S. grocery delivery. Until then DoorDash carries the financing commitment and the deal costs, and management attention is split across Deliveroo, Grubhub Campus Dining and Instacart integrations. If approved, the synergies are on management's figures and come after a first year it does not describe as positive.
03
The adjusted-to-GAAP gap closing the wrong way. The market currently pays about 33× a consensus figure well above GAAP and 79× the GAAP one. If SBC stays near 7% of revenue while growth slows toward the high teens after Deliveroo annualises, the price investors pay per dollar of earnings has to fall — no operating metric needs to miss for that to happen.
  Industry Commentary
Labour Law Is the Sector's Cost Base
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every on-demand delivery platform runs on contractor labour, and the entire industry's margin structure is a bet that this arrangement holds. California's Proposition 22 was upheld by the state Supreme Court in July 2024, which removed the largest single-jurisdiction threat — but the question is now fragmented across cities and countries rather than settled. New York City's $131.5M settlement with DoorDash in September 2026, over how Dashers were paid, and the city's accusation that DoorDash and Uber Eats used app design to deprive workers of more than $550M in tips (Reuters, via NBC News), are the visible running cost of that fragmentation. This is the one risk that could change the sign of the industry's economics rather than the size.
Consolidation Has Replaced the Subsidy War
ONGOING HEADWIND
The 2018–2021 phase of buying share with discounts is over; the 2025–2026 phase is buying share outright. DoorDash spent roughly $4.9B on Deliveroo ($3,724M, closed Oct 2, 2025) and SevenRooms ($1,152M, closed June 13, 2025), booking about $2.9B of new goodwill in 2025, then agreed in autumn 2026 to buy Grubhub Campus Dining ($300M) and Instacart (about $17B). That is cheaper than a price war and harder on GAAP earnings — it converts a cash cost into years of amortisation that adjusted EBITDA never sees. It also moves the industry's main risk from competition to antitrust review: analysts cited by CNBC said the overlap between DoorDash and Instacart could draw antitrust scrutiny.
Retail Media Is the Margin, Delivery Is the Traffic
EMERGING TAILWIND
The lesson of Amazon and now Instacart is that a marketplace's profits come from advertising against its own demand, not from fulfilling orders: Instacart's advertising and other revenue was $331M in Q3 2026, about 30% of its revenue, and grew faster than its transaction revenue (+23% against +14%). DoorDash is running the same play — advertising partners using Symbiosys, which it acquired, nearly doubled between June and December 2025, and its memberships (DashPass, Wolt+ and Deliveroo Plus, over 35M at the end of 2025) give it a logged-in audience with known purchase habits. Bull framing: if advertising and merchant software scale, investors start valuing DoorDash more like a media-and-software business than a thin-margin logistics one, and today's 33× consensus earnings looks reasonable. Bear framing: DoorDash does not disclose its own advertising revenue at all, so there is currently no way to verify the ramp; until it breaks the line out, the margin story is an assertion, and the fully-loaded 79× trailing GAAP multiple is the only number you can actually check.
