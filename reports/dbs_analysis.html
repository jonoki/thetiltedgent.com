01 Company Overview

DBS — "Southeast Asia's largest lender" in CNA's description, and the largest of Singapore's three listed banks by market value — runs deposits, lending, payments and trade finance for companies, mortgages and cards for households, and — increasingly the story — wealth management for Asia's rich and mass-affluent. It operates in 19 markets, but Singapore still produces 65% of its profit and Hong Kong another 17% (1H26). It is a constituent of Singapore's Straits Times Index, and the state investor Temasek owns 28.30% of it. FY2025 total income rose 3% to a record S$22.9B, profit before tax was a record S$13.1B, but net profit fell 3% to S$11.0B as the new 15% global minimum tax lifted the tax bill; return on equity was 16.2%.

The latest results — 3Q26, reported November 5, 2026 — missed. Net profit was S$2.81B (−5% on a year earlier, −9% on the quarter), about 3% under the S$2.90B mean estimate of four analysts in LSEG data (as reported by The Star). The shortfall was one line: markets trading income, what the bank earns from trading and structuring products for clients rather than from lending, fell 65% from a year earlier to S$152M, which DBS put down to unfavourable equity market conditions. The rest held up. Net interest income (NII) rose 4% on the quarter to S$3.71B as loans and deposits grew, wealth management fees were S$864M (+9% on the year, −6% on the quarter), and wealth assets under management reached a record S$544B. Nine-month net profit was a record S$8.82B (+2%), and the 15¢ quarterly Capital Return dividend stays. CEO Tan Su Shan said in the release that the outlook "has become more uncertain amid elevated energy prices, tighter financial conditions and geopolitical risks"; DBS expects total income to grow at a mid-single-digit rate in 2027, assuming two more US rate rises by the end of 2027 (The Star, Nov 5). Chairman Peter Seah retires at the end of 2026 after 16 years, and board member Anthony Lim takes over on January 1, 2027 (The Star, Nov 9). The shares had already been falling: on October 8 they closed down 4.7% as JPMorgan analysts recommended trimming all three Singapore banks (The Edge Malaysia). The results came out before the market opened on November 5; the shares rose 0.6% that day and fell 2.1% on November 6. At S$66.11 on November 20 the stock is down 14.4% from the previous edition's S$77.21 (October 2), but still up 139.9% from a S$27.56 month-end in September 2021 (adjusted for the 2024 bonus issue) on price alone, against +61.2% for the Straits Times Index. It trades at 2.7x book value and 17.0x trailing earnings. The question this report turns on is whether the wealth engine can carry the bank through a lower-rate world, and how much of that is still in a S$66 price.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

S$650B deposit base Singapore home-market scale Asia wealth franchise (S$544B wealth AUM) AA- / Aa1 credit ratings Digital and data investment Temasek as anchor holder

DBS's advantage starts with cheap money at home. Customer deposits of S$650B fund S$479B of loans (a 73.7% loan-to-deposit ratio at September 30, 2026), and deposits grew 10% in a year in constant currency (DBS 3Q26 release). On top of that sits the newer moat: Singapore as a wealth haven. Shee Tse Koon told analysts that DBS benefits from being Singapore-headquartered, a jurisdiction associated with rule of law and stability, and that geopolitical instability "tends to benefit us given our Singapore branding." The bank's "AA-" and "Aa1" credit ratings (per its own release) mean depositors and wealth clients treat it as a safe place to park money. CEO Tan Su Shan's own claim about where the edge lies, quoted by The Business Times on August 6: "Our data is another differentiator, and we've spent a lot of time, effort and money to really create our data lake …"

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Rates cut both ways. Net interest income is still 63% of group income (S$3.71B of S$5.85B in 3Q26). Management's own sensitivity is +S$11M per basis point of SGD rates but −S$4M per basis point of USD rates, so a Fed hike — like the September 16, 2026 one — helps only once it feeds through to SORA.
02
Wealth fees are market-dependent. The CEO herself called fee income "cyclical and market dependent" on the August 6 call. 1H26 fee growth was led by equities and equity structures in a volatile, IPO-heavy market; a quiet or falling market would slow the line that now carries the growth story. In 3Q26 wealth fees slipped 6% on the quarter and markets trading income fell 65% on the year.
03
China cross-border rules. Analysts on the call pressed on Chinese measures affecting offshore trusts and outbound investment. Management said the trust exposure of Chinese nationals is "quite small" and declined to break out net new money by nationality or domicile, which limits what outsiders can check.
04
Legal: the 1MDB-linked claim. On September 9, 2026 DBS disclosed that liquidators of four companies linked to 1MDB recovery efforts are suing DBS Bank for an estimated S$1.298B; DBS rejected the claim, said it would fight it, and has made no provision (CNA, citing DBS's SGX statement). The claim equals about 22% of 1H26 net profit.

Capital returns and one-offs

Dividends: 3Q26 total dividend 81¢ per share, unchanged from 2Q26 (nine months: S$2.43, DBS 3Q26 release) — a 66¢ ordinary dividend plus a 15¢ Capital Return dividend, a payout of surplus capital the board plans at 15¢ a quarter for FY2026 and FY2027 "barring unforeseen circumstances" (FY2025 results release, February 9, 2026). FY2025 dividends totalled S$3.06 per share, S$8.68B in all, up 38%. CFO Chng Sok Hui on August 6: "we do not intend to keep the Capital Return dividend as a continual feature of our capital management." Buyback: as of the August 6 call, of the S$3B programme running to end-2027, about S$0.4B had been used; she said the unused S$2.6B could be converted into Capital Return dividends, subject to the board. Capital rules: on November 4, 2026 the Monetary Authority of Singapore lowered DBS's operational-risk multiplier, which scales the capital the bank must hold against losses from failed systems and processes, from 1.8x to 1.5x; DBS credited its progress in technology and operational risk controls (DBS 3Q26 release, as published by The Asian Banker). Capital tools: in 2Q26 DBS completed its first synthetic securitisation (a transfer of loan credit risk to investors that frees capital), the first by a Singapore-headquartered bank. Tax: the 15% global minimum tax, effective 2025, is why FY2025 net profit fell while profit before tax rose. Share basis: the only bonus issue in the chart window was one new share for every ten held, issued April 26, 2024 (258 million shares); per-share figures and the chart are on the post-bonus basis.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 20, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end of S$27.56 (bonus-adjusted) to the November 20, 2026 close of S$66.11, DBS rose 139.9% on price alone, against 61.2% for the Straits Times Index. The chart has three phases. From 2021 to early 2024 the stock went nowhere — it closed between S$26.98 and S$32.57 at every month-end from September 2021 to February 2024 — even as rising rates lifted profits. Then it climbed: up through 2024, a sharp April 2025 dip (−8.7%), and a steep run through 2026 to a September 4 closing high of S$78.65, with July 2026 (+13.2%) the biggest monthly gain in the window. Since the peak it has given back 15.9%: −5.6% in October, and −9.6% in November to the 20th, already a bigger fall than any full month in the window (the worst was −8.8% in May 2022). Year to date it is still +17.3%.

The 6-period monthly RSI now reads 49.0 — neutral, down from 94.8 in August and September 2026, so the stock went from deep overbought to middling in two months. The 3-month moving average (~S$72.25) is well above the price, and the price sits 1.2% under the 10-month average (~S$66.89). The first leg of the fall was the October 8 bank selloff; it continued through early November and after the third-quarter results, with a Singapore index that fell 11.7% between October 2 and November 20 (5,634.82 → 4,975.17).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

DBS's franchise kept growing through a hard quarter: loans up 11% and deposits up 10% in a year in constant currency, wealth assets under management at a record S$544B, net interest income up 4% on the quarter, nine-month profit a record S$8.82B, and the 15¢ Capital Return dividend planned through FY2027. The miss was one volatile line, markets trading income, not lending. After a 14.4% fall since October 2 the stock trades at 17.0x trailing and 16.3x forward earnings and 2.7x book, 15.5% under the S&P Global consensus average target of S$78.27 (last updated Nov 6). Capital is ample (15.9% CET1 transitional, with a small lift from the regulator's cut in the operational-risk multiplier). Asia's wealth is growing and Singapore is where much of it wants to sit; DBS is the biggest local beneficiary.

Bear Case

The results showed how much of the growth story rides on markets: trading income fell 65% and wealth fees slipped 6% on the quarter, and the CEO says the outlook "has become more uncertain". Net interest income is still 63% of income, and the 2027 guide, mid-single-digit income growth, assumes two more US rate rises. At 17.0x trailing earnings and 2.7x book — a 6.3% return on the price paid at a 17.0% ROE — the stock still trades above OCBC (15.1x) and UOB (10.7x) after a fall that came with the market (the Singapore index is down 11.7% since October 2). Fully phased-in CET1 is 14.0% rather than 15.9%, a S$1.3B legal claim landed in September with no provision, and at least one analyst (Phillip Securities, Nov 6) has cut its target.

Top 3 Risks

01
A market downturn hits fees and the multiple at once. 1H26 growth came from wealth fees, treasury customer sales and trading, all of which shrink in a falling market — and the 2.7x book multiple assumes that growth continues. 3Q26 gave a preview: trading income −65%, wealth fees −6% on the quarter.
02
Rates stay low in Singapore and Hong Kong. SORA, the Singapore dollar benchmark rate, roughly halved in a year (The Business Times, Sep 17). If the US hikes do not feed through, the fixed-rate assets the bank locked in earlier mature and are replaced at lower yields, and NII keeps declining.
03
Credit and legal surprises. 4Q25 specific allowances jumped to 36bps after a watch-listed real estate loan was downgraded; 3Q26 came in at 17bps, the low end of the 17–20bps assumed for 2H26. The S$1.298B 1MDB-linked claim is unprovided.
Industry Commentary
The rate cycle: from headwind to a possible turn
ONGOING HEADWIND

Singapore's three banks all spent 2026 losing net interest income: in 2Q26 DBS's NII fell 2%, OCBC's 1% and UOB's 2%, while their net profits rose 9%, 22% and 10% on fee and wealth income (The Business Times, Sep 17, 2026). SORA roughly halved in a year. The Fed's 25bps hike on September 16 was its first since 2023; Macquarie told BT it expects SORA to rise about 70bps from its 2Q26 low by the second half of 2027. For DBS the gain is diluted by its US-dollar book, which management says loses NII when USD rates rise. In 3Q26 the three diverged: DBS's net profit fell 5% to S$2.81B and missed estimates on weak trading income; UOB's rose 182% to S$1.25B on much lower provisions but was 15% below the previous quarter and missed the S$1.38B Bloomberg consensus; OCBC's rose 3% to S$2.04B (The Star, Nov 9, 2026).

Singapore and Hong Kong as Asia's wealth hubs
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

All three Singapore banks leaned on wealth management to offset falling rates in 2026. DBS is building 18 new wealth centres and upgrading 36 across its six core markets, and CEO Tan Su Shan singled out Taiwan, where the semiconductor and AI boom is creating new fortunes, and India as the growth markets. The risk on the other side is policy: China's new rules on offshore trusts and outbound investment were a recurring topic on the August call.

Valuation after the run-up and the fall
KEY DEBATE

DBS trades at 2.68x book and 17.0x trailing earnings; OCBC and UOB at 15.1x and 10.7x. After the August results OCBC Group Research raised the book multiple it applies to DBS from 2.4x to 3x. The bull framing is that a bank whose growth comes from wealth fees deserves to be valued more like a fee business than a lender; the bear framing is arithmetic — at 2.7x book, a 17.0% ROE is a 6.3% earnings yield on the price paid, and any slip in returns comes straight off it. Since the previous edition (October 2) DBS is down 14.4% and the Straits Times Index 11.7%.
