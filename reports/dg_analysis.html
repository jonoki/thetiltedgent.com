01 Company Overview

Dollar General is the store at the crossroads. It runs 21,148 small-box discount stores, mostly in rural and small-town America across the South, Southwest, Midwest and East, where it is often the nearest place to buy detergent, a loaf of bread or a bottle of ibuprofen. Fiscal 2025 (year ended January 2026) net sales were $42.7B, up 5.2%, with same-store sales up 3.0% and diluted EPS of $6.85, up 34.1% from $5.11. More than four of every five dollars it takes in come from consumables — food, paper, cleaning products, health and beauty, pet supplies — the things a household buys whether or not the month went well.

That is the whole hand, and it has been played badly and well in the same five years. The stock closed November 2022 at $255.68, then lost more than half its value through 2023, and the CEO it had just appointed was gone within a year; on October 12, 2023 the board brought back Todd Vasos, who had run the company from 2015 to 2022, to restore stability. It fell again in August 2024 when management cut its outlook, blaming "financially constrained" customers, and bottomed at a $71.06 month-end in January 2025. Since then the repair has been visible in the numbers — gross margin up, operating profit up 28.6% in fiscal 2025, a fifth consecutive quarter of traffic growth in Q2 2026 — and the stock has rebuilt to $121.91. The open question is how much of the recovery is operational and how much was a one-time gift: Q2 2026 EPS of $2.48 included roughly $0.25 of tariff refunds.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

21,148-store rural footprint Proximity / convenience Low-cost small-box format Owned distribution network Consumables traffic base Purchasing scale

The moat is geography. In a town of a few thousand people there is room for one discount store, and whoever gets there first usually keeps it; the next supercenter may be a twenty-minute drive away. That proximity lets a small box with a limited assortment compete on convenience rather than purely on price. The weakness is the same geography seen from the other side: the core customer has less financial cushion than the average shopper, so when household budgets tighten, the basket shrinks toward lower-margin necessities — which is exactly what happened in 2024.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A financially stretched core customer. In August 2024 management cut full-year EPS guidance to $5.50–$6.20 from $6.80–$7.55, blaming customers who were "financially constrained"; the stock fell more than 26% that day. The customer base has not changed, only the conditions around it.
02
Part of 2026's margin is not repeatable. The company estimates tariff refunds, after reinvestment, added about 81 bps to Q2 gross margin, 66 bps to operating margin and ~$0.25 to EPS, and the raised full-year guide includes a similar ~$0.25 benefit. Strip it out and Q2 EPS was about $2.23 — still up ~20% on $1.86, but the headline 33% overstates the run-rate.
03
Leadership handover mid-repair. The CEO who led the turnaround steps down January 1, 2027, and the incoming CEO comes from outside the company, with nine further senior changes already made. Execution on 4,000+ remodels a year does not pause for a handover.
04
Competition for the same shopper. Walmart, at a $852B market value, and Dollar Tree compete for the same budget-conscious shopper. Rising competition was one of the factors cited when the 2024 forecast was cut.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The turnaround is showing up in the numbers. Traffic has grown for five straight quarters, every category comps positive, inventory per store is down 2.7% while sales grow 5%, and FY2026 EPS guidance has been raised twice to $7.80–$8.00. You are paying 15.8x trailing and about 15.3x forward earnings for a 21,148-store network that is hard to replicate in rural America, with a ~7.5% free-cash-flow yield, a restarted buyback of up to $700M and a 1.94% dividend. If the 4,000+ remodels a year keep lifting the stores and the new CEO brings grocery know-how to a business that is 82% consumables, the stock is still less than half its 2022 high.

Bear Case

The 2026 margin story leans on something that will not repeat: about $0.25 of this year's EPS is tariff refunds. The core customer is the one management itself called "financially constrained" in 2024, and markdowns were already rising in Q2. Leverage is higher than the headline long-term debt suggests once leases are counted (debt-to-equity 1.70), ROIC is only 8.2%, and the CEO who led the repair leaves on January 1, 2027. Nineteen of thirty analysts say Hold. The stock has already rallied 72% off its low; at 15.8x trailing earnings the easy part of the recovery may be priced.

Top 3 Risks

01
Consumer stress in the core customer base. DG's 2024 outlook cut was blamed on financially constrained customers; the same customer is still the business. A weaker low-income consumer shows up first as a basket shifting toward low-margin consumables and higher markdowns.
02
Earnings quality in 2026. Tariff refunds added ~81 bps of Q2 gross margin and ~$0.25 of EPS, and the full-year guide includes a similar benefit. Next year's comparisons will have to be made without it.
03
Execution through a CEO handover. An outside CEO takes over on January 1, 2027, in the middle of a ~4,730-project real-estate program and after nine senior leadership changes in June 2026.
Industry Commentary
The low-income consumer as the swing factor
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Dollar stores are a direct read on the budget of the lower-income household. In August 2024 DG told the market its customers were "financially constrained" and cut its EPS outlook by roughly a fifth, and the shares fell more than a quarter in one session. Two years later the tone has reversed — traffic-led comps of +3.5%, positive comps in every category — but the margin of safety sits with the customer, not with the retailer. Seasonal (+7.4%) and home (+4.8%) growing in Q2 2026 is the healthiest signal available: those are the categories people cut first.

Tariffs — a cost that came back as a refund
ONE-OFF TAILWIND

Much of what DG sells in seasonal, home and apparel is imported. In Q2 2026 the company booked tariff refunds that it estimates added about 81 bps to gross margin and 66 bps to operating margin after reinvestment, and about $0.25 to EPS; the FY2026 guide includes a similar ~$0.25. It is real cash and real earnings, but it is not a run-rate, and any fresh tariff costs on imported goods would work in the opposite direction.

Reinvesting in the box instead of adding boxes
EMERGING TAILWIND

For most of its history DG grew by opening stores. The 2026 plan is weighted the other way: roughly 4,250 remodels (Project Renovate and Project Elevate) against about 460 new stores. The bull framing: better-kept, better-stocked stores are what drove five straight quarters of traffic growth, and the returns on fixing an existing store beat those on building a marginal new one. The bear framing: heavy remodel spending is also an admission that the base was neglected, and capex of $1.4–1.5B a year is the price of standing still in a market Walmart is contesting. The way to judge it is traffic in remodelled stores versus the rest — a number the sources fetched did not disclose.
