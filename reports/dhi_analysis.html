01 Company Overview

A note on the calendar before anything else. D.R. Horton's fiscal year ends September 30, not December 31. When this page says "FY2025" it means the twelve months ended September 30, 2025. The most recently reported quarter, fiscal Q3 2026, ended June 30, 2026 and was reported on July 21, 2026. Fiscal 2026 closes in roughly three weeks from the data date on this page and will be reported October 29. Every builder comparison in the sector suffers from this: Lennar's year ends in November, PulteGroup's and NVR's in December. Do not line up "2025" across four homebuilders and assume you are comparing the same weather.

D.R. Horton is the largest homebuilder in the United States by volume and has been since 2002, building and selling homes in 126 markets across 36 states under the D.R. Horton, Emerald Homes, Express Homes and Freedom Homes badges. It is not only a builder: it consolidates a majority-owned public land developer (Forestar Group, NYSE: FOR), runs a captive mortgage and title operation, and develops single-family and multi-family rental communities for sale to investors. FY2025 revenue was $34.25B, down 6.9% from $36.80B in FY2024, with net income of $3.59B and diluted EPS of $11.57. On a TTM basis through June 30, 2026, revenue is $33.35B, down 3.5%, and EPS is $10.51, down 15.8%.

The central debate on this page is simple to state and hard to resolve: how much of D.R. Horton's reported revenue is a real price, and how much is a rebate the company pays to make a mortgage payment work? The company sells a home at a listed price, then buys the buyer's mortgage rate down from roughly 6.5% to something in the 4.99%–5.5% range. That buydown is a real cash cost, it lands in cost of sales, and it is the single largest reason home sales gross margin has fallen from 30.1% in FY2022 to 20.7% in the June 2026 quarter. Volume is being defended; price realisation is being surrendered to defend it. Section 02 and Section 04 take apart how much that costs, and Section 08 asks whether a 12.9× earnings multiple on a peak-incentive earnings base is cheap or is a value trap.

Revenue Mix

The engine, and the whole cycle exposure. In fiscal Q3 2026 D.R. Horton closed 23,983 homes for $8.68B at an average selling price (ASP — total home sales revenue divided by homes closed) of about $362,000, down 2% year over year and flat sequentially. That ASP is the tell: D.R. Horton is deliberately the cheapest large builder in America, aimed squarely at the entry-level buyer. 65% of its mortgage closings in the quarter were first-time homebuyers — the cohort with the least equity, the least income cushion, and by far the most sensitivity to a mortgage rate.

The margin story is the story. Home sales gross margin was 20.7% in the quarter, against 30.1% for all of FY2022. The gap is incentives. Management's own quantification: mortgage rate buydowns ran 1.6% in Q3, down from 1.7% in Q2 — the company is writing buyers into 4.99%–5.5% mortgages against a ~6.5% market rate, and paying the difference out of gross margin. Two things pushed Q3 margin above its own guidance: incentives eased slightly, and stick-and-brick construction costs fell 5% year over year on framing labour and materials. Management guided Q4 FY2026 home sales gross margin to 20.5%–21% — flat, not recovering.

The orders line is where the caution sits. Net sales orders were 23,084 homes for $8.4B in Q3 — below the 23,983 homes closed. A builder whose orders run below its closings is shrinking its backlog, and the backlog confirms it: 15,983 homes worth $6.18B at June 30. The cancellation rate rose to 20% from 17% a year earlier. Romanowski said demand "softened mid-quarter" after tracking normal seasonality early, and guided Q4 starts lower than Q3. Community count grew 9% year over year and management expects that to moderate to mid-single digits — the land pipeline that drove the last three years of share gain is being throttled to match demand.

Inventory discipline is the offsetting positive. D.R. Horton ended Q3 with 38,000 homes in inventory (down 1% both sequentially and year over year), 23,300 of them unsold, 7,600 completed specs, and only 600 completed more than six months ago. For a speculative-build model in a softening market, 600 aged units out of 38,000 is genuinely tight. This is the single strongest operational number in the release and the main reason the company has not had to fire-sale.

The land bank. 126,600 lots owned and 441,900 controlled through options and purchase contracts — 568,500 total, roughly 6.8 years of supply at the guided ~84,000 closings pace, with only about 1.5 years of it on balance sheet. Keeping four-fifths of the land under option rather than owned is the structural reason this builder can shrink fast without a writedown cycle. It is also what Forestar exists to make possible.

Industry size: US new single-family home sales run roughly 650–700k units annually in recent years (est. — not verified from a fetched source). D.R. Horton's guided FY2026 closings of 83,800–84,300 imply roughly a 12–13% share of US new-home closings (est.). The company does not disclose a market-share figure.

DHI Mortgage and the title operation originate and sell loans to D.R. Horton's own buyers, plus provide title insurance and closing services. Nine-month FY2026 revenue was $598.2M with $180.0M of pre-tax income — a 30.1% margin. Two things matter here. First, it is a capture business, not an origination business: its volume is a derivative of homes closed, so it does not diversify the cycle, it levers it. Second, and more subtly, it is the delivery mechanism for the incentive. The rate buydown that compresses homebuilding gross margin is executed through this captive lender. Investors who look at the 31.9% margin and see a clean fee annuity are looking at only one side of an internal transfer: the builder pays the points, the lender books the loan. The consolidated result is what counts, and the consolidated result is a 20.7% home sales gross margin.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE PURCHASING OPTIONED LAND PIPELINE CAPTIVE MORTGAGE ENTRY-LEVEL PRICE POINT 36-STATE GEOGRAPHIC SPREAD INVENTORY TURN DISCIPLINE FORTRESS BALANCE SHEET

The core advantage is that D.R. Horton is the low-cost producer in a commodity business, and it has built the financing and land machinery to stay that way through a downturn. Three mechanisms do most of the work. First, scale in procurement: at roughly 84,000 homes a year it buys lumber, appliances, framing labour and trades at prices a regional builder cannot match — which is how stick-and-brick costs came down 5% year over year while the industry was complaining about input inflation. Second, land optionality: 441,900 of 568,500 lots are controlled rather than owned, largely via Forestar, so the balance sheet carries about 1.5 years of dirt rather than seven. In 2007–2009, land on the balance sheet is what killed builders; keeping it off is a structural lesson learned and applied. Third, the captive lender, which lets the company convert a price cut into a monthly-payment cut — a far more efficient way to spend a dollar on an entry-level buyer who is qualifying on payment, not on price. Add a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.29, a 23.0% debt-to-total-capital ratio, $6.1B of total liquidity and only $600M of notes due within twelve months, and the company can survive a bad two years that would force weaker builders to liquidate inventory into its markets — which is, historically, exactly when D.R. Horton takes share.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
There is no moat against a mortgage rate. This is the vulnerability that dwarfs the others and it is not company-specific. D.R. Horton's buyer is qualifying on a monthly payment; the payment is set by a rate the company does not control. Its only defence is to buy the rate down out of gross margin, which it is doing at a cost management puts at 1.6% — and that defence has already taken home sales gross margin from 30.1% (FY2022) to 20.7% (Q3 FY2026). If the 30-year mortgage moves from ~6.5% to 7.5%, the buydown gets more expensive at exactly the moment order volume falls. Scale does not help here; it just makes the exposure bigger.
02
The entry-level buyer is the most fragile buyer. 65% of Q3 mortgage closings were first-time buyers at a ~$362,000 average price. This cohort has no existing-home equity to roll into a down payment, the thinnest income buffer, and the highest sensitivity to unemployment. The cancellation rate rising from 17% to 20% is the early-warning instrument for exactly this, and it is already flashing. Toll Brothers' luxury buyer pays cash; D.R. Horton's buyer does not have that option.
03
Orders below closings means the backlog is a wasting asset. Q3 net sales orders of 23,084 were below 23,983 closings. Backlog stands at 15,983 homes / $6.18B — under one quarter of production. Builders with thin backlogs have very little visibility and very little pricing leverage; they build specs and then discount them. Management guiding Q4 starts lower than Q3 is the rational response, but it converts an operating problem into a revenue problem in the following fiscal year.
04
Community count growth is being switched off. Active selling communities grew 9% year over year, which is most of what kept closings flat while per-community absorption fell. Management has now guided that growth down to "mid-single digits" longer term. Community count is the volume lever a builder can actually control; throttling it protects margin and cash but removes the offset that has been masking soft demand in the reported closings line.

Notable One-Off Items

D.R. Horton's fiscal 2026 accounts are, unusually for a cyclical, largely free of one-off distortions. No impairments, no acquisitions, no restructuring charges were disclosed in the fetched sources. What is distorting the per-share numbers is structural rather than exceptional, and runs in two directions.

The buyback is doing heavy lifting on EPS. In Q3 FY2026, revenue was flat year over year (+0.01%) and net income fell 11.7% to $904.9M — but diluted EPS fell only 4.8%, to $3.20 from $3.36. The difference is the share count: 14.6M shares repurchased for $2.2B in nine months (4.2M for $615.7M in Q3 alone), taking shares outstanding down about 6% year over year to 280.7M. FY2026 guidance is ~$2.5B of repurchases — roughly 6.6% of the current $37.9B market capitalisation — plus ~$500M of dividends. Total capital return of ~$3.0B is about 7.9% of market cap. That is a genuine return of capital, funded by liquidating inventory in a downturn, and it is flattering the reported EPS decline by roughly seven percentage points a quarter. Strip it out and the underlying earnings deterioration is worse than the headline.

The incentive load is the real "non-recurring" item — and it may not be non-recurring. Mortgage rate buydowns quantified by the CFO at 1.6% in Q3 (1.7% in Q2) are booked in cost of sales, not below the line, so they never appear as an adjustment. Jessica Hansen's framing on the call is worth reading precisely: incentives "remain elevated relative to historical levels" with only slight sequential improvement. If rates normalise, that 1.6% comes back to gross margin and the earnings base steps up materially. If they do not, this is simply the new cost of selling a home to a first-time buyer, and 20.7% is the normal margin — in which case there is nothing to add back and the current multiple is not a trough multiple at all.

Cost deflation is helping and management has flagged its expiry. Stick-and-brick costs fell 5% year over year on framing labour and materials. Hansen expects those savings to persist into Q4 and possibly early calendar 2027, but explicitly warned of "maybe a slight lumber headwind" later. That is a tailwind with a stated shelf life.

Book value: $84.85 per share at June 30, up 5% year over year. At $135.57 the stock trades at 1.60× book — worth holding in mind, because homebuilders have historically traded below book at genuine cycle troughs.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time, and a homebuilder in a rate cycle usually does.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $89.27 to the September 10, 2026 close of $135.57, DHI has returned +51.9% in price — a compound annual rate of about 8.7%, before a dividend that has run near 1% of price throughout. That is a respectable five-year number and it completely disguises the path. The stock fell 39% from the December 2021 close of $108.45 to the June 2022 close of $66.19 as the Federal Reserve began raising rates. It then rose 188% from that trough to the September 2024 close of $190.77 — the cycle peak, printed in the month of the first rate cut, which is exactly when a rate-sensitive stock tops. From there it fell 38% to the May 2025 close of $118.06, rallied back to $169.48 by August 2025, and has been chopping in a $135–$163 band ever since. The most recent leg is a −16.8% decline from the June 2026 close of $162.88, and the $135.57 close sits 28.9% below the September 2024 peak.

Relative performance. A verified total-return comparison against the S&P 500 over the identical five-year window was not obtained from the sources fetched and is therefore not stated — nor is a NASDAQ-100 comparison. What can be verified is the relative position within the homebuilder group over the last twelve months, and it favours D.R. Horton: its market capitalisation is down 27.8% year over year, against −46.2% for Lennar and −18.1% for PulteGroup. On the price line, DHI's 52-week change is −21.59%. The group is being repriced together, and the ordering is what you would expect from margin resilience: PulteGroup (higher-priced, move-up-weighted buyer) held up best, D.R. Horton second, Lennar — which chased volume hardest and saw net income fall 50.4% — worst. With a beta of 1.37 and an S&P 500 that has not fallen 20% over this window, DHI has clearly underperformed the index; the magnitude is deliberately left unquantified rather than estimated.

Testing the lows
08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the arithmetic of a home sale, because everything else follows from it. D.R. Horton sold 23,983 homes in its June 2026 quarter at an average of $362,000, generating $8.68B of home sales revenue and 20.7% of gross margin. That means roughly $287,000 of direct cost per home and about $75,000 of gross profit. From that $75,000, SG&A of $720.6M across the quarter takes roughly $30,000 per home, leaving homebuilding pre-tax income of $1.1B, or about $45,800 per home at a 12.3% segment margin. Now insert the incentive: at a buydown cost management puts at 1.6%, the company is spending on the order of $5,800 per home purely to move a buyer's mortgage rate from ~6.5% to the 4.99%–5.5% range. That is roughly 13% of the pre-tax profit on every house, handed back at the closing table, and it does not appear as an adjustment anywhere in the income statement.

The gross-margin bridge is the whole valuation argument. FY2022: 30.08% gross margin, $16.51 EPS. FY2025: 22.52%, $11.57. Q3 FY2026: home sales gross margin 20.7%, TTM EPS $10.51. That is 940 basis points of margin and 36% of earnings, gone in four fiscal years, on volume that is only modestly lower. Nearly all of the decline is price realisation surrendered to keep the volume. So the question a buyer at $135.57 is really answering is: is 20.7% the floor of a cycle or the new normal? If it is a floor and margin reverts even halfway to FY2023's 25.13%, earnings power is well north of $14 per share and the stock is trading under 10× a normalised number. If 20.7% is what selling entry-level homes in a 6.5%-mortgage world simply costs, then $10.44 of FY2026 consensus EPS is the base, 13× is an ordinary multiple for a cyclical at mid-cycle, and there is no discount at all. Management guiding Q4 to "relatively flat" at 20.5%–21% is a vote for the second reading over the next two quarters at least.

Thread 1 — the demand signals are deteriorating faster than the margin signals. Three numbers, all from the same July release, all pointing the same way. Net sales orders of 23,084 were below 23,983 closings, so the company sold less than it delivered and the backlog shrank to 15,983 homes / $6.18B — under one quarter of production. The cancellation rate rose to 20% from 17%. And management guided Q4 starts lower than Q3. Against that, community count grew 9% year over year, which means per-community absorption fell by more than the flat closings line suggests — and management has now guided community growth down to mid-single digits. The margin beat in Q3 was real, but it came from cost deflation (stick-and-brick −5%) with a stated expiry date, not from demand.

Thread 2 — the inventory and land position is genuinely excellent, and that is the reason to take the bull case seriously. 38,000 homes in inventory, down 1% both sequentially and year over year. 23,300 unsold. 7,600 completed specs. Only 600 completed more than six months ago. A speculative builder with 1.6% of its inventory aged past six months in a demonstrably softening market is running a tight ship. On land: 126,600 lots owned against 441,900 controlled — about 1.5 years of owned supply inside a 6.8-year total pipeline. The 2006–2009 builder failures were balance-sheet failures caused by owned land, and D.R. Horton has structurally engineered that exposure away, largely through Forestar. Combine that with debt-to-equity of 0.29, a 23.0% debt-to-total-capital ratio, $6.1B of liquidity and $600M of near-term maturities, and the downside here is an earnings problem, not an existence problem.

Thread 3 — cash generation inverts in a downturn, and the buyback is exploiting it. Trailing free cash flow is $3.20B, or $11.45 per share — above GAAP EPS of $10.51. That is not an accounting artefact; it is what happens when a builder stops replacing inventory as fast as it sells it. FY2026 guidance is ≥$3.0B of operating cash flow, ~$2.5B of buybacks and ~$500M of dividends — a combined 7.9% of market capitalisation returned in one year. Nine-month repurchases of 14.6M shares for $2.2B have already cut the count about 6% year over year, which is why Q3 EPS fell only 4.8% while net income fell 11.7%. Cut both ways: this is a real, compounding benefit to a patient holder, and it is currently disguising how fast underlying profitability is eroding.

Thread 4 — Forestar and the mortgage arm are not diversification. It is tempting to treat the four segments as a portfolio. They are not. Forestar's lots are ~88% absorbed by D.R. Horton itself; Financial Services originates loans exclusively for D.R. Horton's buyers and is the vehicle through which the margin-destroying buydown is delivered; Rental sells to institutions whose bid is set by the same financing costs that set the retail buyer's payment. All four segments are levered to one variable. The 31.9% pre-tax margin in Financial Services and the 12.0% at Forestar look like earnings diversity on a segment table and are, in economic substance, the same bet expressed three more times.

Valuation, stated plainly. At $135.57: 12.90× trailing EPS of $10.51; ~12.2× forward; 1.60× book value of $84.85; 10.45× EV/EBITDA on a $43.74B enterprise value; 11.8× trailing free cash flow of $11.45 per share; a 1.33% dividend yield on a 17.1% payout, with another ~6.6% of market cap coming back via buyback. Against a homebuilder peer set at 11.9–12.4× earnings, DHI is not cheap relative to its industry — it is in line, with better margins and a better balance sheet, at a modest premium to the two large peers whose earnings fell far harder. The PEG of 2.68 is the honest counterweight to the low headline multiple: you are not paying 13× for growth, you are paying 13× for a decline. The 52-week range is decisive on positioning — at 7.7% of the way up a $131.75–$181.47 range, the market has already marked this down a long way. Whether that is opportunity or information depends entirely on the answer to the gross-margin question above.

Bull Case

The largest, lowest-cost builder in America is trading at 12.9× earnings and 1.60× book with a fortress balance sheet (0.29 debt-to-equity, $6.1B liquidity, 23% debt-to-total-capital) and is returning 7.9% of its market cap in a single year through a ~$2.5B buyback and ~$500M of dividends — funded by $3.2B of free cash flow that exceeds GAAP earnings. Operationally it is doing everything right into a bad market: 5% stick-and-brick cost deflation, only 600 aged specs out of 38,000 homes, 78% of its 568,500-lot pipeline controlled rather than owned, and a margin beat against its own guidance. Net income fell 23% over the TTM against Lennar's 50%. If the 30-year mortgage rate falls, the 1.6% buydown cost reverses straight back into gross margin and the earnings base steps up sharply — with a 6%-a-year smaller share count to divide it into.

Bear Case

Home sales gross margin has fallen from 30.1% to 20.7% in four years and management guides it flat, not recovering. Net sales orders are running below closings, backlog is under one quarter of production, cancellations have risen to 20% from 17%, starts are guided down, and community-count growth — the lever that kept closings flat — is being throttled to mid-single digits. Earnings are down 36% from the FY2022 peak and consensus has FY2026 falling another 9.8%; the 2.68 PEG says the low multiple is a decline, not a discount. All four segments are the same bet on one mortgage rate the company cannot influence, 65% of its buyers are first-timers with no equity cushion, the Street is 13-of-18 Hold with the two most recent target actions both cuts, and the stock sits 2.9% above its 52-week low at 1.60× book — well above the sub-book levels at which homebuilders have historically bottomed.

Top 3 Risks

01
Mortgage rates stay at or above ~6.5%. This is the risk that contains all the others. The incentive load compressing gross margin exists only because the market rate is unaffordable to a buyer at a $362,000 price point; the buydown to 4.99%–5.5% is the bridge. If long rates hold or rise, the buydown gets more expensive per home at the same time the qualified buyer pool shrinks — margin and volume deteriorate together, which is precisely the combination that turns a 12.9× multiple into a 12.9× multiple on much lower earnings.
02
The backlog runs out before demand returns. Orders below closings, a $6.18B / 15,983-home backlog worth less than a quarter of production, cancellations at 20%, and guided-down starts together mean fiscal 2027 revenue is being set right now by order intake that is deteriorating. Management guiding community growth down to mid-single digits removes the volume lever that masked soft absorption through fiscal 2026. Consensus already has FY2026 EPS down 9.8% to $10.44; the risk is that FY2027 is not the recovery year the average $162.92 price target implies.
03
Entry-level credit quality, if unemployment turns. 65% of mortgage closings are first-time buyers, the cohort with the least equity and the thinnest income buffer, financed through a captive lender that also carries the buydown cost. A rise in unemployment would hit cancellations, spec absorption and Financial Services profitability simultaneously — and would do so in the one segment mix where D.R. Horton has no move-up or luxury business to cushion the blow, unlike Toll Brothers or the higher-priced end of PulteGroup's book.
  Industry Commentary
The Payment, Not the Price, Is the Product
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
American homebuilders have spent four years discovering that they no longer sell houses — they sell monthly payments. When the 30-year mortgage sits near 6.5% and the entry-level buyer qualifies on payment rather than price, a builder has two choices: cut the sticker, or buy the rate down. Cutting the sticker impairs the value of every unsold home in the same community and every lot behind it. Buying the rate down is invisible to the comparables and shows up only in gross margin. The entire industry has chosen the second, which is why D.R. Horton's home sales gross margin fell from 30.08% (FY2022) to 20.7% while its average selling price fell just 2% year over year to $362,000. The industry did not cut prices; it cut margins and called it an incentive. Watch the buydown percentage, not the ASP — it is the only place the real price cut is visible, and at D.R. Horton it was 1.6% in Q3, 1.7% in Q2.
Easing Arrived and Mortgage Rates Did Not Follow
ONGOING HEADWIND
The chart in Section 07 makes the sector's central disappointment unmissable: DHI's five-year monthly-close high of $190.77 was printed in September 2024, the month the first rate cut landed, and the stock has not closed above it in the twenty-four months since. Homebuilder equities discount the path of long rates, and they had already fully priced an easing cycle that the 30-year mortgage declined to deliver. Policy rates and mortgage rates are set in different places, and 2024–2026 has been an extended, expensive lesson in that distinction. Practically, it means a DHI holder should not treat a Fed cut as a catalyst — the relevant instrument is the 10-year Treasury and the mortgage spread over it, and both are set by bond investors who have their own view on inflation.
Scale Consolidation Inside a Shrinking Market
EMERGING TAILWIND
Every soft housing market in the last forty years has ended with the public builders larger than they started, because scale players can absorb a margin squeeze that bankrupts a regional builder. The evidence is already in the TTM numbers: revenue fell 3.5% at D.R. Horton against 7.4% at Lennar and 7.3% at PulteGroup, while net income fell 23.0% versus 50.4% and 30.5% respectively. D.R. Horton has added 30 new markets in five years and grown community count 9% year over year into a downturn — funded by 5% cost deflation, an optioned land pipeline and $6.1B of liquidity. Bull framing: the company is buying permanent share at the cost of two years of margin, and when rates eventually cooperate it will operate more communities at a higher margin with 6% fewer shares outstanding. Bear framing: share of a shrinking market is still shrinking revenue, and the market is currently paying 12.9× for the privilege — the same multiple it pays for Lennar, which is executing worse. There is no valuation reward for the better operator right now, which tells you the market is discounting the sector, not the company.
