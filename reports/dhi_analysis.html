01 Company Overview

A note on the calendar before anything else. D.R. Horton's fiscal year ends September 30, not December 31. When this page says "FY2026" it means the twelve months ended September 30, 2026, which the company reported on October 29, 2026; that full year is also the page's trailing twelve months. Every builder comparison in the sector suffers from this: Lennar's year ends in November, PulteGroup's in December. Do not line up "2026" across homebuilders and assume you are comparing the same weather.

D.R. Horton is the largest homebuilder in the United States by volume and has been since 2002, with operations in 128 markets across 38 states. It is not only a builder: it consolidates a majority-owned public land developer (Forestar Group, NYSE: FOR), runs a captive mortgage and title operation, and develops single-family and multi-family rental communities for sale to investors. FY2026 revenue was $33.04B, down 3.5% from $34.25B in FY2025, with net income of $2.89B (down 19%) and diluted EPS of $10.11, down 13% from $11.57. It closed 84,202 homes in the year; about 43,500 of its buyers were first-time buyers, per Executive Chairman David Auld in the release.

The central debate on this page is simple to state and hard to resolve: how much of D.R. Horton's reported revenue is a real price, and how much is a rebate the company pays to make a mortgage payment work? The company sells a home at a listed price, then pays to buy the buyer's mortgage rate down — on the October call CEO Paul Romanowski put the bought-down rate at about a point and a half below the market rate, and Jessica Hansen, senior vice president of communications, said the high 80s to low 90s percent of the company's mortgage customers get one. That buydown is a real cash cost and it lands in cost of sales. On the company's own release tables, the gross margin on home sales has fallen from 29.3% in FY2022 to 21.1% in FY2026 (our calculation: home sales revenue less home sales cost of sales); the figure management quotes fell to 19.5% in the September 2026 quarter and is guided to 18%–18.5% for the December quarter. Volume is being defended; price is being given up to defend it. Section 02 and Section 04 take apart how much that costs, and Section 08 asks whether a 14.4× earnings multiple on a falling earnings base is cheap or only looks cheap.

The engine, and the whole cycle exposure. In fiscal Q4 2026 D.R. Horton closed 22,915 homes (down 2%) for $8.38B at an average closing price of $365,600 — up 1% on the June quarter and flat on a year earlier, per COO Mike Murray on the call. That price is the tell: D.R. Horton is deliberately among the cheapest large builders in America, aimed squarely at the entry-level buyer; about 43,500 of its fiscal 2026 buyers were first-time buyers, the cohort with the least equity, the least income cushion, and by far the most sensitivity to a mortgage rate.

The margin story is the story. Home sales gross margin was 19.5% in the quarter, down 120 basis points (1.2 percentage points) from 20.7% in June, and management guided the December quarter to 18%–18.5% — a further step down, not a recovery. For the full year the release tables give 21.1% (our calculation, home sales revenue less home sales cost of sales), against 29.3% on the same basis in FY2022. The gap is mostly incentives: Romanowski said incentives "remain elevated well above our historical norm", and Executive Chairman David Auld wrote in the release that the company expects them to stay elevated in fiscal 2027. The cost help of the spring has also turned: Hansen said stick-and-brick costs (the materials and labour of the house itself) rose 1% from the June quarter, and Romanowski tied that to lumber price increases from earlier in the year.

The orders line is where the caution sits. Net sales orders were 18,619 homes for $6.6B in Q4, down 7% and 9% — and 4,296 homes below the 22,915 closed. A builder whose orders run below its closings is shrinking its backlog, and the backlog confirms it: 11,714 homes worth $4.46B at September 30, fewer than the 15,500–16,500 homes guided to close in the December quarter. The cancellation rate rose to 23% from 20%. Romanowski said demand weakened as the quarter went on and that traffic was running a little below normal for the season. Average active selling communities were up 10% year over year and 2% on the June quarter, so sales per community fell further than the orders total. For the full year, orders rose 2% to 84,995 while closings slipped 1% to 84,202.

Inventory discipline is the offsetting positive. D.R. Horton ended the fiscal year with 28,200 homes in inventory, 17,700 of them unsold, 8,800 of those completed, and only 600 completed more than six months ago. For a build-before-you-sell model in a softening market, 600 aged homes is tight — and it is the main reason the company has not had to fire-sale.

The land bank. 130,500 lots owned and 440,100 controlled through purchase contracts — 570,600 total, about 7.0 years of supply at the midpoint of the 79,000–84,000 closings guided for fiscal 2027, with only about 1.6 years of it owned outright. Keeping 77% of the land under contract rather than owned is the structural reason this builder can shrink fast without a cycle of writedowns. It is also what Forestar exists to make possible.

DHI Mortgage and the title operation originate and sell loans to D.R. Horton's own buyers, plus provide title insurance and closing services. Full-year FY2026 revenue was $817.3M with $258.1M of pre-tax income — a 31.6% margin. Two things matter here. First, it lends to D.R. Horton's own buyers rather than competing for loans in the open market: its volume follows homes closed, so it does not spread the cycle risk, it magnifies it. Second, and more subtly, it is the delivery mechanism for the incentive. The rate buydown that compresses homebuilding gross margin is executed through this captive lender. Investors who look at the 35.7% margin and see a clean fee stream are looking at only one side of an internal transfer: the builder pays for the lower rate, the lender books the loan. The consolidated result is what counts, and the consolidated result is a 19.5% home sales gross margin.

Forestar is a publicly traded residential lot developer that D.R. Horton majority-owns and fully consolidates — its revenue, its debt and its inventory all appear in D.R. Horton's statements, with the minority stake backed out as non-controlling interest. The structure is elegant and slightly uncomfortable at the same time. Elegant, because it lets D.R. Horton convert raw land into finished lots off its own balance sheet and buy them back under option — which is how 440,100 of its 570,600 lots stay controlled rather than owned, and why a downturn forces fewer writedowns here than at a builder that owns all its own land. Uncomfortable, because much of Forestar's output is sold to its own parent: the group cancelled out $458.6M of sales between its segments in Q4, against $580.8M of Forestar revenue. Forestar's own shares trade at a market cap of $1.22B (our calculation: 51.02M shares × the $23.94 October 30 close) on $1.64B of fiscal 2026 revenue, down 1.6%, and $142.6M of net income (stockanalysis.com). A year ago it was the group's growth engine; this year its revenue slipped with everything else.

D.R. Horton builds rental communities — 509 single-family homes and 2,182 multi-family units sold in Q4 — and sells them whole to institutional investors. Revenue is booked when an entire community is sold, which makes this the lumpiest line in the group, and Q4 showed the downside: the segment lost $25.3M before tax after $91.4M of impairment charges on nine multi-family projects (writing their book value down to what they can now be sold for). For the full year Rental earned just $18.2M on $1,234.5M of revenue, down from $1,640.4M in FY2025. Strategically it is a shock absorber. When retail buyers cannot afford today's mortgage payments, an investor with its own financing and a rent roll still can, so the rental channel takes homes the retail channel will not. The limit is the same limit: what investors will pay for rental communities also depends on financing costs, and the Q4 writedowns show that price can fall too. In a genuine rate shock, both channels close at once.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE PURCHASING OPTIONED LAND PIPELINE CAPTIVE MORTGAGE ENTRY-LEVEL PRICE POINT 38-STATE GEOGRAPHIC SPREAD INVENTORY TURN DISCIPLINE FORTRESS BALANCE SHEET

The core advantage is that D.R. Horton is the low-cost producer in a commodity business, and it has built the financing and land machinery to stay that way through a downturn. Three mechanisms do most of the work. First, scale in buying: at roughly 84,000 homes a year it buys lumber, appliances, framing labour and trades at prices a regional builder cannot match. Scale does not make it immune to input prices — stick-and-brick costs rose 1% from the June quarter as earlier lumber increases came through — but it keeps its costs below a smaller builder's. Second, land under contract rather than owned: 440,100 of 570,600 lots are controlled through purchase contracts, largely via Forestar, so the balance sheet carries about 1.6 years of land rather than seven. In 2007–2009, land on the balance sheet is what sank builders; keeping it off is a lesson learned and applied. Third, the captive lender, which lets the company turn a price cut into a monthly-payment cut — a far more efficient way to spend a dollar on an entry-level buyer who qualifies on payment, not on price. Add a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.26, a 20.8% debt-to-total-capital ratio and $7.2B of total liquidity at September 30 (it then repaid $600M of 1.3% notes at maturity in October), and the company can survive a bad two years that would force weaker builders to dump inventory into its markets — which is, historically, when D.R. Horton takes share.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
There is no moat against a mortgage rate. This is the vulnerability that dwarfs the others and it is not company-specific. D.R. Horton's buyer is qualifying on a monthly payment; the payment is set by a rate the company does not control. Its only defence is to buy the rate down out of gross margin — about a point and a half below market, per the CEO in October — and on the company's own tables home sales gross margin has gone from 29.3% (FY2022) to 21.1% (FY2026), with the quarterly figure at 19.5% and guided to 18%–18.5% next. Auld's release blamed a harder fourth quarter partly on rising mortgage rates. When rates rise, the buydown gets more expensive at the moment order volume falls. Scale does not help here; it just makes the exposure bigger.
02
The entry-level buyer is the most fragile buyer. About 43,500 of D.R. Horton's fiscal 2026 buyers — roughly half of the 84,202 homes closed — were first-time buyers, at an average price around $365,600. This group has no existing home to sell for a down payment, the thinnest income buffer, and the most exposure to job losses. The cancellation rate rising to 23% from 20% is the early warning for exactly this, and it rose again this quarter.
03
Orders below closings means the backlog is running down. Q4 net sales orders of 18,619 were 4,296 homes below 22,915 closings. Backlog stands at 11,714 homes / $4.46B — fewer homes than the 15,500–16,500 the company expects to close in the December quarter alone. Builders with thin backlogs have little visibility and little pricing power; they build homes before they have buyers and then discount them. Guiding fiscal 2027 closings to 79,000–84,000, below or at best level with fiscal 2026's 84,202, is the rational response, but it turns an operating problem into a revenue problem.
04
More communities are not producing more sales. Average active selling communities were up 10% year over year in Q4, yet orders fell 7% — sales per community are falling faster than the headline. The company says it has raised its community count 65% over five years. Opening communities is the volume lever a builder can control; when new ones sell less than the old ones did, the lever stops offsetting soft demand.

Notable One-Off Items

D.R. Horton's fiscal 2026 accounts carry one clear one-off item and two structural distortions that run in opposite directions.

The rental writedown. The fourth quarter included $91.4M of impairment charges on nine multi-family rental projects, booked in cost of sales, which turned the Rental segment to a $25.3M pre-tax loss. Consolidated inventory and land option charges were $134.3M in the quarter against $57.3M a year earlier. Even adding the whole $91.4M back, Q4 pre-tax income would have been about $1.11B against $1.20B a year earlier (our arithmetic) — the writedown deepened the miss but did not cause it.

The buyback is doing heavy lifting on EPS. In Q4, revenue fell 3.2% and net income fell 18.1% to $741.8M — but diluted EPS fell only 12.2%, to $2.67 from $3.04. The difference is the share count: 24.0M shares repurchased for $3.6B in FY2026 (9.4M for $1.4B in Q4 alone), taking shares outstanding down 8% to 271.4M. FY2027 guidance is about $3.0B of repurchases — roughly 7.6% of the current $39.6B market capitalisation — plus about $500M of dividends. Total capital return of about $3.5B is about 8.8% of market cap. That is a genuine return of capital, funded by turning inventory into cash in a downturn, and it is flattering the reported EPS decline: for the full year net income fell 19.4% while EPS fell 12.6%. Strip it out and the underlying earnings decline is worse than the headline.

The incentive load is the real "non-recurring" item — and it may not be non-recurring. Mortgage rate buydowns are booked in cost of sales, not below the line, so they never appear as an adjustment. Romanowski said incentives remain well above the historical norm, and Auld's release says the company expects them to stay elevated in fiscal 2027, to a degree that depends on market conditions. If rates fall, part of that cost comes back to gross margin and the earnings base steps up. If they do not, this is simply the new cost of selling a home to a first-time buyer, and a margin around 18%–19.5% is normal — in which case there is nothing to add back and the current multiple is not a trough multiple at all.

Cost deflation has run out. The previous edition counted a 5% year-over-year fall in stick-and-brick costs as a tailwind with a shelf life. In Q4 those costs rose 1% from the June quarter, per Hansen on the call; Romanowski tied it to lumber price increases from earlier in the year working through the building process.

Book value: $85.14 per share at September 30, up 4% year over year. At $146.00 the stock trades at 1.71× book.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time, and a homebuilder in a rate cycle usually does.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $89.27 to the October 30, 2026 close of $146.00, DHI has returned +63.6% in price — a compound annual rate of about 10.3%, before dividends. Over the same dates SPY returned +64.6% and QQQ +78.8% in price, so five years of homebuilder drama roughly matched the S&P 500 and trailed the Nasdaq-100. The stock fell 39% from the December 2021 close of $108.45 to the June 2022 close of $66.19 as the Federal Reserve began raising rates. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the arithmetic of a home sale, because everything else follows from it. D.R. Horton closed 22,915 homes in its September 2026 quarter at an average of $365,600, generating $8.38B of home sales revenue at a 19.5% home sales gross margin. That means roughly $294,000 of direct cost per home and about $71,300 of gross profit. Homebuilding overheads (selling, general and administrative costs) of $729.3M across the quarter take roughly $31,800 per home, and homebuilding pre-tax income of $891.0M works out to about $38,900 per home (our arithmetic from the release). Each percentage point of home sales gross margin is about $3,700 a home, nearly a tenth of that pre-tax profit — so the 1 to 1.5 points management has guided away for the December quarter (to 18%–18.5%) cost $3,700–$5,500 on every house, and none of it appears as an adjustment anywhere in the income statement.

The gross-margin bridge is the whole valuation argument. FY2022: 29.3% home sales gross margin on the release tables, $16.51 EPS. FY2026: 21.1% on the same basis, $10.11 EPS — and the company's own quarterly figure has gone from 20.7% in June to 19.5% in September, with 18%–18.5% guided next. That is about 830 basis points of margin and 39% of earnings gone in four fiscal years, on slightly more homes (84,202 closed in FY2026 against 82,744 in FY2022). Nearly all of the decline is price given up to keep the volume. So the question a buyer at $146.00 is really answering is: is this margin the floor of a cycle or the new normal? If it is a floor and margin recovers about halfway back to FY2022's, to around 25% on the table basis, earnings power is roughly $13.50 a share and the stock trades near 11× it (our arithmetic: each point of home sales margin is about $306M of pre-tax profit on FY2026's $30.6B of home sales, or about $0.84 a share after a 25.5% tax rate on 271.4M shares). If 19.5% or lower is simply what selling entry-level homes now costs, then the $9.35 FY2027 consensus EPS is the base, 15.6× is a full multiple for a builder whose earnings are still falling, and there is no discount at all. Management guiding the December quarter lower and incentives "elevated" through fiscal 2027 is a vote for the second reading for now.

Thread 1 — the demand signals worsened again. In July the worry was orders below closings; in October it got bigger. Net sales orders of 18,619 were 4,296 homes below 22,915 closings, so the backlog shrank to 11,714 homes / $4.46B — fewer homes than the company expects to close in the December quarter alone. The cancellation rate rose to 23% from 20%. Average active selling communities were up 10% year over year, so sales per community fell by more than the 7% drop in orders. Romanowski said demand weakened as the quarter went on, and Auld's release pointed to rising mortgage rates. The one offset: for the full year, orders rose 2% while closings fell 1%.

Thread 2 — the inventory and land position is genuinely strong, and that is the reason to take the bull case seriously. 28,200 homes in inventory. 17,700 unsold. 8,800 of those completed. Only 600 completed more than six months ago. A build-before-you-sell company with 2.1% of its homes aged past six months in a softening market is running a tight ship. On land: 130,500 lots owned against 440,100 controlled — about 1.6 years of owned supply inside a 7.0-year total pipeline. The 2006–2009 builder failures were balance-sheet failures caused by owned land, and D.R. Horton has largely engineered that exposure away, mostly through Forestar. Combine that with debt-to-equity of 0.26, a 20.8% debt-to-total-capital ratio and $7.2B of liquidity, and the downside here is an earnings problem, not a survival problem.

Thread 3 — cash generation rises in a downturn, and the buyback is using it. FY2026 free cash flow was $3.35B, or $12.33 per share — above GAAP EPS of $10.11. That is not an accounting quirk; it is what happens when a builder stops replacing inventory as fast as it sells it. The company returned $4.1B in FY2026 ($3.6B of buybacks, $513.3M of dividends) and guides FY2027 to about $3.5B of operating cash flow, about $3.0B of buybacks and about $500M of dividends — a combined 8.8% of market capitalisation in one year. Buying back 24.0M shares cut the count 8% in a year, which is why FY2026 EPS fell 12.6% while net income fell 19.4%. It cuts both ways: this is a real, compounding benefit to a patient holder, and it is disguising how fast underlying profit is eroding.

Thread 4 — Forestar, the mortgage arm and Rental are not diversification. It is tempting to treat the four segments as a portfolio. They are not. Much of Forestar's output is bought by D.R. Horton itself; Financial Services lends to D.R. Horton's own buyers and is the channel through which the margin-eating buydown is delivered; Rental sells to investors whose bids depend on the same financing costs that set the retail buyer's payment — and its $91.4M of Q4 writedowns show what happens when those bids fall. All four segments ride on one variable. The 35.7% pre-tax margin in Financial Services and the 13.5% at Forestar look like variety on a segment table and are, in substance, the same bet three more times.

Valuation, stated plainly. At $146.00: 14.4× trailing EPS of $10.11; 15.6× the FY2027 consensus of $9.35; 1.71× book value of $85.14; 11.45× EV/EBITDA (enterprise value divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; stockanalysis.com) on an enterprise value of about $43.6B (our calculation: $39.6B market cap plus $6.05B of notes payable less $2.06B of cash); 11.8× free cash flow of $12.33 per share; a 1.23% trailing dividend yield (1.37% at the new $0.50 rate) on a 17.8% payout, with another ~7.6% of market cap guided to come back through buybacks. Against Lennar at 16.4× and PulteGroup at 13.3× trailing earnings, DHI sits in between, with a stronger balance sheet than either needs to have. The PEG of 2.01 is the honest counterweight to the modest headline multiple: you are not paying 14× for growth, you are paying 14× for a decline. On the 52-week range the stock sits 38.6% of the way up from $130.40 to $170.79, after bouncing 12% off its October 14 low. Whether that is opportunity or information depends entirely on the answer to the gross-margin question above.

Bull Case

The largest builder in America by volume trades at 14.4× earnings and 1.71× book with a balance sheet built for a downturn (0.26 debt-to-equity, $7.2B of liquidity, 20.8% debt-to-total-capital) and guides to return about 8.8% of its market cap in a single year through a ~$3.0B buyback and ~$500M of dividends — funded by free cash flow ($3.35B in FY2026) that exceeds GAAP earnings, and backed by a fresh $5.0B authorisation and an 11% dividend raise. Operationally it is holding the line where it can: only 600 aged homes out of 28,200, 77% of its 570,600-lot pipeline under contract rather than owned, and full-year orders up 2%. Net income fell 19% over the year against Lennar's 51%. If the 30-year mortgage rate falls, the buydown cost flows back into gross margin and the earnings base steps up — with a share count already 8% smaller than a year ago to divide it into.

Bear Case

Home sales gross margin is still falling — 20.7% in June, 19.5% in September, 18%–18.5% guided for December — and management expects incentives to stay elevated through fiscal 2027. Net sales orders are running well below closings, the backlog is smaller than one quarter's guided closings, cancellations have risen to 23%, and fiscal 2027 revenue is guided down a third straight year to $30–32B. Earnings are down 39% from the FY2022 peak and consensus has FY2027 falling another 7.5%; the forward P/E is higher than the trailing one. All four segments are the same bet on a mortgage rate the company cannot influence, and Rental just wrote down nine projects. The Street is 15-of-21 Hold, five of the six post-print target moves were cuts, and the average target sits about 2% above the price, and the stock trades at 1.71× book.

Top 3 Risks

01
Mortgage rates stay high or rise. This is the risk that contains all the others. The incentive load squeezing gross margin exists because market mortgage rates are unaffordable to many buyers at a $365,600 price point; the buydown of about a point and a half is the bridge. If long rates hold or rise, the buydown costs more per home at the same time the pool of qualified buyers shrinks — margin and volume fall together, which is the combination that turns a 14× multiple into a 14× multiple on much lower earnings.
02
The backlog runs out before demand returns. Orders 4,296 homes below closings, an 11,714-home / $4.46B backlog smaller than the next quarter's guided closings, cancellations at 23% and a margin guided lower together mean fiscal 2027 revenue is being set right now by order intake that is still weakening. Community growth (+10% year over year) has stopped producing order growth. Consensus already has FY2027 EPS down 7.5% to $9.35; the risk is that FY2027 lands below that and is not the year the market starts pricing a recovery.
03
Entry-level credit quality, if unemployment turns. About half of fiscal 2026's buyers were first-time buyers, the group with the least equity and the thinnest income buffer, financed through a captive lender that also carries the buydown cost. A rise in unemployment would hit cancellations, unsold-home sales and Financial Services profits at the same time — and would do so in a business mix with little move-up or luxury business to cushion the blow.
  Industry Commentary
The Payment, Not the Price, Is the Product
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
American homebuilders have spent four years discovering that they no longer sell houses — they sell monthly payments. When mortgage rates are high and the entry-level buyer qualifies on payment rather than price, a builder has two choices: cut the sticker, or buy the rate down. Cutting the sticker impairs the value of every unsold home in the same community and every lot behind it. Buying the rate down is invisible to the comparables and shows up only in gross margin. The entire industry has chosen the second, which is why D.R. Horton's home sales gross margin fell from 29.3% (FY2022, release tables) to 19.5% in the September 2026 quarter while its average closing price was flat year over year at $365,600. The industry did not cut prices; it cut margins and called it an incentive. Watch the margin and the incentive commentary, not the average price — that is where the real price cut shows. In October D.R. Horton guided the margin lower again, to 18%–18.5%, and said incentives would stay elevated through fiscal 2027.
Easing Arrived and Mortgage Rates Did Not Follow
ONGOING HEADWIND
The chart in Section 07 makes the sector's central disappointment unmissable: DHI's five-year monthly-close high of $190.77 was printed in September 2024, the month the first rate cut landed, and the stock has not closed a month above it in the 25 months since. Homebuilder shares move on where investors expect long-term rates to go, and they had already priced in a cycle of rate cuts that the 30-year mortgage rate did not deliver. Policy rates and mortgage rates are set in different places, and 2024–2026 has been an extended, expensive lesson in that distinction. Practically, it means a DHI holder should not treat a Fed cut as a catalyst — the relevant instrument is the 10-year Treasury and the mortgage spread over it, and both are set by bond investors who have their own view on inflation.
Scale Consolidation Inside a Shrinking Market
EMERGING TAILWIND
The case for the largest builders in a soft market is that they can absorb a margin squeeze that can bankrupt a regional builder. The trailing numbers fit it: revenue fell 3.5% at D.R. Horton against 8.0% at Lennar and 9.3% at PulteGroup, while net income fell 19.4% versus 51.3% and 31.7% respectively (Lennar and PulteGroup from stockanalysis.com). D.R. Horton says it has grown its community count 65% over five years and grew it another 10% year over year into this downturn, funded by an optioned land pipeline and $7.2B of liquidity. Bull framing: the company is buying lasting share at the cost of a few years of margin, and when rates eventually cooperate it will operate more communities at a higher margin with fewer shares outstanding. Bear framing: share of a shrinking market is still shrinking revenue — fiscal 2027 is guided down again — and the market pays 14.4× for D.R. Horton, between PulteGroup's 13.3× and Lennar's 16.4×. There is no clear valuation reward for the better operator right now, which tells you the market is pricing the sector, not the company.
