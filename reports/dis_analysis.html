01 Company Overview
The Walt Disney Company is the world's largest diversified media and entertainment conglomerate, earning money from a century-deep library of characters and stories across three reporting segments — Entertainment (studios, TV networks, and Disney+/Hulu streaming), Sports (ESPN), and Experiences (theme parks, resorts, cruise line, and consumer products). Fiscal 2026 revenue (a 53-week year ended October 3, 2026) was $102.34B, up 8.4% YoY; Disney estimates the extra week added about 2 points of that growth. At a $208.99B market capitalization, the debate around the stock is unchanged: an irreplaceable set of assets valued below the market's average multiple while its traditional TV business shrinks and streaming and parks have to make up the difference.
The fiscal Q4 2026 print (reported November 17, 2026) closed the year ahead of guidance: total segment operating income rose 43% to $4.97B on 16% revenue growth (helped by the extra week), streaming earned $824M at a 13.6% margin, and Sports operating income rose 53%. For the full year, segment operating income rose 12% to $19.73B and adjusted EPS 18% to $7.02 (16% without the extra week). Management guided to double-digit adjusted EPS growth again in fiscal 2027, raised the dividend 33% to $2.00, and set a $3B yearly cost-savings target for fiscal 2029 that CFO Hugh Johnston said will include more layoffs.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Many Ways to Earn From One Story
Disney's moat is best understood as a chain of ways to earn from each story rather than a single advantage: a character is created once in the studio, then earns again in theatres, streaming, TV, merchandise, park attractions, cruise itineraries and games. Toy Story shows how it works — Toy Story 5 lifted fiscal Q4 content sales while the franchise anchors whole park lands. Competitors can outbid Disney for content or sports rights, but none can match what happens downstream. The Experiences segment is the hardest asset to attack: thousands of acres of approved land near major cities, decades of permitting, and a cruise fleet whose growth is limited industry-wide by shipyard capacity. That segment's 28.3% fiscal 2026 operating margin, in a year Disney says set an all-time record for guests, is evidence the moat still holds. What has clearly eroded is the distribution moat: the cable bundle that once made ESPN and ABC compulsory purchases is dissolving, and Disney is rebuilding that income one direct subscriber at a time, at lower revenue per customer.
Key Vulnerabilities
TV network decline is long-term, not cyclical. Cable cancellations remove high-margin fees that cost Disney almost nothing extra to earn. Sports operating income fell 14% over the first nine months of fiscal 2026 even as revenue grew, because rights costs rise on contract while the cable audience that paid for them shrinks; only the 53-week fourth quarter lifted the full year to +7%.
Sports rights keep getting dearer. The NBA renewal raised the cost base, and the NFL, college football and international rights rounds ahead are contested by Amazon, Apple, Netflix and the newly merged Skydance (Paramount plus Warner Bros. Discovery) — bidders that can borrow more cheaply or that value sports for reasons beyond its own profit. ESPN cannot give up marquee rights without weakening its streaming service, but it cannot win them at any price without hurting the segment's margin.
The highest-margin segment depends on consumer spending. Experiences produced 57% of fiscal 2026 segment operating income. International parks already show the risk: attendance at Hong Kong and Shanghai worsened in the fourth quarter, international operating income fell 7%, and management expects the softness to continue into fiscal 2027. A US slowdown would hit profits harder than revenue, because the parks' costs are largely fixed, and about $9B of yearly capital spending is committed years in advance.
Less disclosure and a new team. Disney no longer reports Disney+, Hulu or ESPN streaming subscriber counts, and from fiscal 2027 it stops reporting streaming operating income separately, so outsiders lose the main check on the streaming turnaround. This lands in Josh D'Amaro's first full year as CEO, alongside Dana Walden's new President and Chief Creative Officer role, Bob Iger's departure from the board on Dec 31, 2026, and a cost program that management says will include more layoffs.

Notable One-Off Items
Reported earnings carry several material non-recurring distortions that make GAAP GAAP figures a poor guide to run-rate economics:
$317M of restructuring and impairment charges in fiscal Q4 2026: $212M of severance, a $53M impairment of the A+E Global Media investment and $52M of facility exit costs. With acquisition amortization, they explain the gap between Q4 GAAP EPS of $1.51 and adjusted EPS of $1.77 ($0.13 each).
Fiscal 2025's one-time gains distort the full-year comparison. Fiscal 2025 GAAP EPS of $6.85 ran above adjusted EPS of $5.93; so fiscal 2026 GAAP EPS fell 18% to $5.63 while adjusted EPS rose 18% to $7.02 and income before taxes rose 18% to $14.21B.
A+E stake sold. Disney sold its 50% interest in A+E Global Media to an affiliate of co-owner Hearst on Sep 30, 2026 for about $1.3B in cash. From fiscal 2027, equity income (Disney's share of profits at companies it partly owns) is excluded from Entertainment segment operating income, mainly because of this sale.
Restructuring continues. Disney now expects its cost program to deliver about $3B of yearly savings by fiscal 2029; Johnston said most will be reinvested in the business and more layoffs are expected, so further severance charges are likely.
53rd fiscal week. Fiscal 2026 had an extra week, which Disney estimates added about $1.6B (2 points) to revenue growth and about $0.17 to adjusted EPS. All fiscal 2027 guidance excludes it, so any fiscal 2026-to-2027 comparison must strip it out.

Five-year trajectory. DIS closed at $144.90 at the end of November 2021 and $122.36 on November 18, 2026 — a price decline of −15.6% (dividends excluded). Over the same dates SPY, an S&P 500 index fund, rose +60.3% and QQQ (Nasdaq-100) +67.5% on price, leaving Disney about 76 percentage points behind the S&P 500. The fall from the $201.91 all-time closing high (Mar 8, 2021) to the $79.32 close on Oct 4, 2023 was −60.7%. Since November 2023 the stock has closed between $81.07 (Nov 1, 2023) and $124.01 (Jun 30, 2025); the Nov 18 close sits 1.3% below that top.
The shape of the chart is a two-act story. Act one (2021–2023): the price investors would pay collapsed. The market stopped rewarding streaming subscriber growth at any cost and demanded profits, and Disney — which had spent heavily to build Disney+ while its TV networks declined — took the full hit, made worse by an activist campaign, a messy CEO succession and the return of Bob Iger. Act two (2024–present): profits recovered faster than the share price. Segment operating income has grown steadily (+12% to $19.73B in fiscal 2026), streaming became profitable and Experiences set records, yet the stock is still about 40% below its 2021 peak and trades at 16.19 times fiscal 2027 consensus earnings, below the S&P 500 aggregate in Section 04.

The past year looks different: Disney is +15.1% against +10.6% for SPY, while Netflix, the streaming benchmark, is −47.6% (and −6.8% over the full chart window, split-adjusted; Yahoo Finance). For now the market is paying more for Disney's mix of parks, sports and streaming than for a pure streaming play.
08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The streaming turnaround is real and measured: operating income more than doubled to $824M in the fourth quarter at a 13.6% margin, and the full-year margin reached 11.5%. Experiences earns a 28.3% operating margin, set a guest record in fiscal 2026, and Walt Disney World's first-quarter bookings are pacing ahead of last year. At 16.19 times consensus fiscal 2027 EPS of $7.56, against management's double-digit EPS growth guidance, the stock is priced for modest growth. About $11B of expected free cash flow funds a ~$9B buyback (about 4.3% of the $208.99B market cap) and a dividend raised 33% to $2.00, and the $3B savings program adds room. The average street target of $131.34 implies 7.3% upside.

Bear Case

The returns are still not good enough: 9.13% ROE and 8.00% ROIC on a $111.6B equity base, after the recovery. Sports profits rose 7% in fiscal 2026 only thanks to the extra week, after a 14% nine-month decline, and the NFL and college rights rounds ahead now face a bigger Skydance as well as Amazon, Apple and Netflix. Disney is cutting disclosure again — no subscriber counts, and from fiscal 2027 no separate streaming profit line — just as investors most need to check the streaming case. Experiences carries 57% of segment operating profit and about $9B of yearly capital spending, with Hong Kong and Shanghai expected to stay soft. Content spending rises 9% to $26B, and management expects most of fiscal 2027's growth in the first half, leaving the second half to prove itself.

Top 3 Risks
01
Sports-rights costs outrun ESPN's move to streaming. The NBA renewal cut Sports operating income 14% over the first nine months of fiscal 2026 before the 53-week fourth quarter rescued the year. If the ESPN streaming service does not grow fast enough — and Disney does not publish its subscriber count — the segment becomes a shrinking, cost-heavy drag on the roughly 18% of revenue it represents.
02
Consumers pull back from parks and cruises. Experiences earns 57% of segment operating profit at a ~28% margin, and its costs are largely fixed, so a drop in visits hits profit harder than revenue. International operating income already fell 7% in Q4 on soft Shanghai and Hong Kong demand. A US slowdown, with about $9B of yearly capital spending committed years ahead, would hit the one part of Disney the market currently trusts.
03
Streaming margins stall, or the Disney+ and Hulu merger misfires. The case for a higher valuation rests on streaming margins rising from ~13% toward the 20%+ range. Competition from the merged Skydance (Paramount+ and HBO Max) is stiffer, content spending rises 9% in fiscal 2027, and the move of Disney+ and Hulu into one app brings migration and cancellation risk; from fiscal 2027 investors will no longer see a separate streaming profit figure to judge it. A stall removes the reason to pay more than ~16× forward earnings.
  Industry Commentary — Macro Themes
Streaming Turns Profitable
EMERGING TAILWIND
The industry has moved from subscriber growth at any cost to profit discipline, and Disney benefits directly. Streaming operating income more than doubled to $824M in fiscal Q4 at a 13.6% margin versus 7.9% a year earlier, on 19% revenue growth, and the full-year margin reached 11.5%. Netflix, the category benchmark, still runs a far higher margin on $49.7B of trailing revenue growing 14.7%, so Disney has room to improve. The constraints: cash content spending rises 9% to about $26B in fiscal 2027, the newly merged Skydance puts Paramount+ and HBO Max under one owner, and from fiscal 2027 Disney stops reporting streaming profit separately, so the market will have to judge the progress through the wider Entertainment segment.
Sports Rights Inflation Meets Cord-Cutting
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the industry's hardest unsolved problem and Disney owns the most exposed asset. Sports operating income fell 14% over the first nine months of fiscal 2026 on rising rights costs, then rose 53% in a fourth quarter boosted by the extra week, ending the year +7% to $3.09B. Rights costs rise on contract while the cable audience that funded them shrinks every quarter. The bidder pool now includes Amazon, Apple, Netflix and a merged Skydance — buyers that value sports for subscribers, ad slots and engagement rather than its own profit, and can rationally outbid a pure media company. ESPN's product is strong: advertising rose 20% in Q4 (about 5 points from the extra week), and it holds exclusive rights to every round of the expanded College Football Playoff. The gap closes only if ESPN's streaming prices can replace cable fees at scale — and Disney does not publish the subscriber data to prove it.
A Split Consumer & a Consolidating Industry — Bull vs. Bear
ONGOING HEADWIND
Experiences shows a split consumer: domestic parks revenue +19% in Q4 (attendance +11%, or +3% without the extra week; spending per guest +4%), against international operating income down 7% on soft Shanghai and Hong Kong demand that management expects to last into fiscal 2027, while Disneyland Paris keeps growing. Meanwhile the industry has consolidated: Paramount Skydance completed its purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery on Oct 6, 2026, and Disney sold its A+E stake to Hearst on Sep 30.

Bull framing: the US consumer is still spending on Disney, Walt Disney World bookings are pacing ahead, the cruise fleet has two new ships, and a consolidated industry has fewer bidders driving up content prices. At 16.19× forward earnings with a ~$9B buyback and a 33% dividend raise, little of this is priced in.

Bear framing: Asia weakness could be the first sign of a wider pullback in a segment carrying 57% of segment operating profit and about $9B of hard-to-cut yearly capital spending; Skydance is a larger, better-funded bidder for exactly the sports and content rights ESPN cannot afford to lose; and an 8.00% ROIC says this capital-heavy model has not been earning much above its cost even in good conditions.
