01 Company Overview

Digital Realty Trust is a data-center REIT: it owns 311 data centers (88 of them held through partnerships with other investors) in 55+ metro areas across 30+ countries on six continents, and rents out space and power to enterprises, cloud providers and network operators. The portfolio holds about 3.2 gigawatts of IT capacity (electricity available to customers' computers) and a further 9.8 GW of buildable capacity under development or held for future development.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $6.11 billion; the trailing-twelve-month figure is now $7.10 billion (+20.0%, or +16.6% without $201 million of one-time joint-venture incentive fees in Q2) as AI-driven leasing starts paying rent. The third quarter of 2026, reported after the close on October 29, produced revenue of $1.902 billion (+21% YoY), record bookings of $855.3 million of annual base rent at 100% share ($626.6 million at DLR's share) and a record $2.5 billion backlog of signed leases not yet paying rent. Management raised full-year 2026 core FFO per share guidance, excluding net promote, to $8.30–$8.35 from $8.15–$8.20 (and $7.90–$8.00 in February).

At a $65.9 billion market capitalisation (our calculation: 378.3 million shares from the Q3 10-Q cover, as of Oct 27, times the Nov 6 close), DLR ranks #186 in the S&P 500.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
SECURED POWER — ~9.8 GW BUILDABLE 55+ METRO FOOTPRINT INTERCONNECTION NETWORK EFFECT SWITCHING COSTS INVESTMENT-GRADE RATINGS PRIVATE-CAPITAL PLATFORM LAND BANK

The binding constraint in data centres today is not capital, land or construction labour — it is grid power. In the major hubs (Northern Virginia, Dublin, Frankfurt, Singapore, Santa Clara) utilities quote multi-year waits for new connections, and some places restrict new large loads. That makes DLR's accumulated land and power — about 3.2 GW in place plus ~9.8 GW buildable — the moat: a newcomer with unlimited money cannot buy a place in line. DLR keeps adding to it: about $742 million of land bought in Q3 (Indianapolis, Chicago, Charlotte, Atlanta, Frankfurt), on top of a $475 million land purchase in the Kansas City area, with a utility agreement for 600 MW of power by early 2028, rising to 2 GW at full delivery. Layered on top are interconnection density, where each new network raises a facility's value to every other tenant, and a footprint across 30+ countries that lets multinational customers use one provider. The financial expression is the renewal spread: +11.8% cash / +17.0% GAAP across the portfolio in Q3, cooler than Q2's +25.4% / +32.0% but above the +9–11% full-year guide, which management lifted to +10–12%.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Hyperscaler self-build. The cloud platforms and AI companies that rent DLR's largest halls are also its largest competitors. Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta each build their own capacity at scale and rent third-party space for speed and overflow, not as a permanent plan. If their budgets tilt toward owned buildings, the >1 MW pipeline shrinks quickly. In Q3, 75.7% of bookings came from that large-format business, a higher concentration than Q2's 48%.

2. Cost of capital and leverage. A REIT pays out most of its cash flow, so it cannot fund a development pipeline from retained earnings; it must keep issuing shares and debt. DLR sold about $2.9 billion of shares in 2026 YTD at a $185.98 average, now above the $174.20 price, and net debt / adjusted EBITDA rose to 5.2× from 4.7× in Q2. The October euro notes carry a 5.125% coupon, and the company's guidance assumes about $1.75 billion of long-term debt issued in October, priced near 4.0%. If long-term rates rise further, both the share price and the borrowing cost move against the company, squeezing the gap between the 10.0%+ yield it targets on new buildings and its cost of money.

3. Power, permitting and local political risk. The same scarcity that creates the moat creates execution risk. Utility connection dates slip; Northern Virginia, Ireland and the Netherlands have imposed or debated limits on new data-centre load and water use. Kansas City's 600 MW, due by early 2028, is a multi-year bet on utility delivery that DLR does not control.

4. Currency. DLR earns a large share of its revenue outside US dollars, so reported core FFO per share moves with exchange rates. The company guides reported core FFO ($8.30–$8.35) and constant-currency core FFO ($8.25–$8.30) separately; the $0.05 gap is its own measure of the effect. Its guide assumes USD/GBP of $1.32–$1.37 and USD/EUR of $1.13–$1.18.

Notable One-Off Items

Q3 2026 had no promote income, so headline and underlying match. Core FFO per share was $2.10 both with and without net promote. Q2 had reported $2.65 headline and $2.13 without a $188 million promote payment from the Blackstone joint venture ($0.52 per share). On the underlying number, Q3 slipped a penny from Q2 ($2.13 to $2.10) but is 11% above Q3 2025 ($1.89). Full-year guidance of $8.30–$8.35 excludes net promote.

Second, reported earnings per share and core FFO per share pulled in different directions. Net income per diluted share was $0.48 against $0.15 a year earlier and $1.21 in Q2. MarketBeat's summary of the results lists a $0.52 consensus for that figure and a $1.75 billion revenue consensus (the company reported $1.90 billion). Meanwhile guidance for full-year net income per share was cut to $2.90–$2.95 from $3.10–$3.15 even as the NAREIT FFO guide (a standard industry definition of FFO) was raised to $8.55–$8.60 from $8.40–$8.45. The company's release does not tie the cut to a single item; gains on property sales fall in GAAP income but not in core FFO, which is why the two diverge.

Third, currency: the $0.05 gap between reported ($8.30–$8.35) and constant-currency ($8.25–$8.30) 2026 guidance is the company's own quantification of exchange-rate effects.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been upward versus downward. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a forecast.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 close of $167.74 to $174.20 on November 6, 2026, DLR returned +3.9% on price alone — roughly 0.8% a year, before dividends. The S&P 500 (SPY, price only, November 30, 2021 close of $455.56 to $735.29 on November 6, 2026) gained +61.4%, about 10% a year. Inside that flat line is a violent round trip. On month-end closes it peaked at $176.87 in December 2021, then fell 43.9% to $99.18 by September 2022 as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates and investors marked down every asset valued on long-dated income, including a data-center builder that has to keep raising money. Month-end closes went slightly lower, to $98.31 in March 2023 (-44.4%). From the September 2022 close the recovery is +75.6%, first on the late-2023 rate-peak rally and then on the AI building boom.

The 2025 fall is the template for the bear case. After a $195.69 monthly close in November 2024, the stock fell to $143.29 by March 2025 — a 26.8% decline — as cheaper AI models (DeepSeek in January 2025) raised worries that cloud companies had ordered too much capacity. It then drifted through the back half of 2025, closing December 2025 at $154.71, before the 2026 leg higher took it to a monthly close of $200.94 in April. Since then it has pulled back: $179.58 in June, $188.52 in July, $185.59 in August, $175.53 in September and $180.70 in October, before the Nov 6 close of $174.20, +12.6% year-to-date — and 16.3% below the $208.14 intraday high of April 24.

Relative performance. Over the five years DLR has lagged the S&P 500 by a wide margin (+3.9% against +61.4%, both price only). Within its sector, DLR's +20.0% trailing revenue growth is well ahead of Equinix's +11.8%, yet DLR's shares closed 10.3% below the previous edition's Aug 20 price ($194.30). The pattern across the period is consistent: DLR trades like a bond when interest rates dominate the news and like an AI-infrastructure play when they do not.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

DLR owns the scarce input in a fast-growing infrastructure market — about 3.2 GW in place plus ~9.8 GW buildable — at a time when electricity supply, not money, limits every competitor. The numbers show it: record Q3 bookings of $855.3 million at 100% share, a record $2.5 billion backlog, portfolio renewal spreads of +11.8% cash, and guidance raised for the third time this year to $8.30–$8.35 of core FFO, almost 13% growth over 2025 at the midpoint. The company says the backlog and pipeline support double-digit FFO growth into 2027. At 20.9× 2026E core FFO with a 2.8% dividend that uses 58.6% of that FFO, an investor pays a moderate price for visible growth, and every analyst target in the tables read sits above the share price.

Bear Case

Leverage has moved the wrong way — 5.2× net debt/EBITDA from 4.7× a quarter earlier — while free cash flow is -$1.47 billion over twelve months and 2026 development spending is guided to $4.25–$4.75 billion. Shares sold so far this year went at an average $185.98, above today's price, and a new tranche of about 3.4 million shares is headed to Teraco's sellers. Large-deal demand comes from cloud and AI customers who also build their own capacity, and 75.7% of Q3 bookings were hyperscale. Renewal spreads cooled from Q2's +25.4% to +11.8%, and GAAP earnings guidance was cut even as core FFO guidance rose. The stock has fallen 10.3% since August, and March 2025 (-26.8% in four months) shows how fast sentiment can turn.

Top 3 Risks
01
Hyperscaler demand concentration and self-build. In Q3, 75.7% of bookings came from >1 MW deals, the kind signed with cloud and AI customers who also build capacity of their own at enormous scale. A change in their lease-versus-build mix, or a pause after the recent ordering boom, would cut the highest-volume part of the pipeline first, and the $2.5 billion backlog would stop being replenished even as it converts to rent.
02
Cost of capital and leverage. A REIT paying out 58.6% of its core FFO cannot self-fund a development pipeline of this size. DLR has sold about $2.9 billion of shares in 2026 at a $185.98 average and carries about $19.5 billion of debt, with net debt/EBITDA at 5.2×. Higher long-term rates would raise refinancing costs, lower the share price and narrow the gap between the 10.0%+ yield on new buildings and the cost of money that is the entire economic engine.
03
Power delivery, permitting and currency. The moat and the risk are the same asset. Utility commitments like Kansas City's 600 MW, due by early 2028, depend on delivery DLR does not control, and limits on data-center load and water use are live in Northern Virginia, Ireland and the Netherlands. Separately, reported core FFO moves with exchange rates; the company's own $0.05 gap between reported and constant-currency 2026 guidance measures it.
  Industry Commentary
Power Is the Binding Constraint on the Entire Sector
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Data-center demand is no longer held back by money, land or construction capacity — it is held back by megawatts the grid can actually deliver. Utilities in the main hubs quote multi-year waits for new connections, and some jurisdictions have moved from queues to outright limits. That flips the old industry structure: for two decades data centres were a commodity building business where the cheapest builder set the price; today the operator holding secured power sets the price. DLR's response is to buy the constraint directly: about $742 million of land in Q3 (including a site near Indianapolis of more than 1 GW, 360 MW in Chicago and up to 600 MW in Charlotte), about $475 million for land in the Kansas City area, with a utility agreement for 600 MW by early 2028, rising to 2 GW, and a 288 MW Northern Virginia hyperscale portfolio bought from Blackstone funds (Form 8-K, June 29, 2026). Total controlled capacity is ~3.2 GW in place plus ~9.8 GW buildable. The risk is in the same fact: utility delivery dates slip, and a pipeline priced on 2028 power that arrives in 2030 is worth less.
Hyperscaler Self-Build: Customer and Competitor in the Same Contract
ONGOING HEADWIND
The structural oddity of this industry is that the largest buyers of leased capacity are also the largest builders of their own. Cloud platforms rent third-party data centres for speed, geographic reach and balance-sheet flexibility, and move to owned buildings where demand is predictable enough to justify the capital. That makes leasing volumes the leftover after the hyperscalers decide how much to build themselves, not a measure of total AI demand. DLR's defence is to sell what hyperscalers cannot easily replicate: sub-megawatt colocation and interconnection, which together produced a record $152 million of bookings in Q3. The Q3 mix shows how lumpy the large deals are, though: hyperscale bookings rose to $474.2 million from $99.8 million in Q2, so the smaller-deal share of bookings dropped from 52% to 24% even as its dollars grew.
Interest Rates, the Cost of Funding Growth, and How the Bull–Bear Debate Resolves
MIXED — CONDITIONAL
DLR is both a growth company and a bond-like asset, and which one the market prices depends mostly on the interest-rate path. The 2022 experience is the reference case: a 43.9% fall from the December 2021 month-end high to September 2022 as interest rates rose. In 2026 the company is leaning on outside capital again: partnerships and funds (Columbia Capital closed Aug 18; $1.4 billion of first commitments to a new fund), asset sales (2026 guide raised to $1.5–$2.0 billion), shares (~$2.9 billion YTD) and bonds (about $1.75 billion this year at roughly 4.0% in guidance). Leverage nonetheless rose to 5.2×.

How it resolves. Bull: if rates ease while AI leasing holds, the share price can rise while the gap between building yields and funding costs widens — the $2.5B backlog turns into double-digit core FFO growth and the analysts' $190–$250 targets prove conservative. Bear: if long-term rates rise while the big cloud companies pause, the market treats DLR as a heavily borrowed landlord with negative free cash flow and debt refinancing at higher coupons; the 2025 drop shows how quickly that can happen. The signal that separates the two is quarterly bookings: as long as new leases signed outrun the backlog converting to rent, the growth case holds.
