01 Company Overview

Digital Realty Trust is the world's second-largest listed data-center REIT, operating 300+ facilities across 55+ metros in 30+ countries on six continents. It leases physical data-center capacity — measured in megawatts of IT load — plus colocation space and interconnection services to a customer base of roughly 6,000 enterprises, cloud providers and network operators. The portfolio carries approximately 3 gigawatts of in-place IT capacity with a further 9 GW of future development capacity in land and secured power, for a total controlled footprint of about 12 GW.

Full-year 2025 revenue was $6.08 billion, up 11.9% YoY; the trailing-twelve-month figure has since accelerated to $6.76 billion (+18.8%) as AI-driven hyperscale leasing commenced. Q2 2026 was a record quarter on essentially every leading indicator: revenue of $1.924 billion (+28.9% YoY), record total bookings, a record signed-but-not-commenced backlog, and cash renewal spreads above 25%. Management raised full-year 2026 core FFO per share guidance to $8.15–$8.20 from a prior $7.90–$8.00.

At a $73.4 billion market capitalisation, DLR is the 6th-largest real-estate company globally by market value (behind Welltower, Prologis, Equinix, Simon Property Group and American Tower) and ranks roughly #347 globally / #172 in the US across all sectors.

Q2'26 Promote
The capital-recycling engine — and the single most important structural feature of the DLR story. Rather than fund a $20 billion development pipeline entirely with balance-sheet debt and dilutive equity issuance, DLR develops assets, then sells majority stakes into joint ventures and managed funds while retaining management and a minority interest. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
SECURED POWER — 9 GW PIPELINE 55+ METRO FOOTPRINT INTERCONNECTION NETWORK EFFECT SWITCHING COSTS INVESTMENT-GRADE COST OF CAPITAL PRIVATE-CAPITAL PLATFORM >$12B ENTITLED LAND BANK

The binding constraint in data centres today is not capital, land or construction labour — it is grid power. Utilities in the major availability zones (Northern Virginia, Dublin, Frankfurt, Singapore, Santa Clara) are quoting multi-year interconnection queues, and in several markets moratoria on new large loads. That converts DLR's decade of accumulated, entitled, power-secured land into the moat: the company controls roughly 9 GW of future development capacity on top of ~3 GW in place, and continues to add — a $475 million powered-land purchase in Kansas City tied to 2 GW of utility power, with an initial 600 MW secured and ramping from early 2028. A new entrant with unlimited capital cannot buy that queue position. Layered on top are two reinforcing effects: interconnection density, where each incremental network and cloud on-ramp raises the facility's value to every existing tenant, and a genuinely global footprint that lets multinational customers standardise on a single provider across 30+ countries. The financial expression of the moat is the renewal spread — +25.4% cash / +32.0% GAAP portfolio-wide, and +66.7% cash on >1 MW leases — which is what pricing power looks like when supply cannot respond.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Hyperscaler self-build. DLR's largest customers — the cloud platforms and AI labs — are simultaneously its largest competitors. Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta each build owned capacity at scale and use third-party leasing as an overflow and speed-to-market lever, not a permanent architecture. If hyperscaler capital budgets pivot toward owned shells, the >1 MW leasing pipeline compresses quickly. Management's stated defence is to lean into the segments hyperscalers cannot replicate — sub-megawatt enterprise colocation and interconnection — but 48% of Q2 bookings still came from the large-format business.

2. Cost of capital and rate sensitivity. A REIT distributing the bulk of its cash flow cannot retain earnings to fund a $20 billion construction pipeline; it must issue equity and debt continuously. DLR raised $2.5 billion via its ATM programme in 2026 YTD at a $184.94 average price. If long rates back up, both the equity currency and the debt cost deteriorate at once, and the spread between development yield (~11.5% expected stabilised) and cost of capital — which is the entire economic engine — narrows. The 2.9% weighted-average coupon is a legacy of the 2020–21 issuance window and will reprice upward on refinancing.

3. Power, permitting and local political risk. The same scarcity that creates the moat creates execution risk. Utility interconnection dates slip; local jurisdictions in Northern Virginia, Ireland and the Netherlands have imposed or debated restrictions on new data-centre load and water use; and power price inflation flows through to tenants and eventually to demand. A 600 MW award "ramping in early 2028" is a multi-year bet on utility delivery that DLR does not control.

4. Currency and geographic concentration. Only 54% of revenue is US-dollar denominated; roughly 23% is euro, with the remainder across Singapore dollars, sterling and other currencies. That is unusual among US REITs and makes reported core FFO per share materially FX-sensitive — Q2 2026 included a $0.02 per share FX benefit, and management guides constant-currency core FFO ($8.10–$8.15) separately from reported ($8.15–$8.20) precisely because the gap is real.

Notable One-Off Items

Q2 2026 contained two large non-recurring items, and management itself reported core FFO both ways. Headline core FFO per share was $2.65; excluding net promote income it was $2.13 (versus $1.87 in Q2 2025, +14%). The $0.52 per-share gap is a $188 million promote payment from the Blackstone joint venture — an incentive fee earned when the JV's assets cleared a return hurdle. Promotes are real cash and a genuine validation of the capital-recycling model, but they are lumpy and deal-dependent; the $2.13 figure is the right run-rate anchor, and full-year guidance of $8.15–$8.20 is likewise stated excluding net promote.

Second, DLR received $113 million of insurance proceeds relating to a Singapore incident claim, of which $27 million ($0.07 per share) was included in core FFO as business-interruption compensation for lost rent. That is a recovery of foregone revenue rather than incremental earnings, and it will not repeat.

Third, currency. With only 54% of revenue in US dollars and roughly 23% in euros, FX is a recurring swing factor — Q2 2026 carried a $0.02 per share favourable FX contribution, and the $0.05 gap between reported ($8.15–$8.20) and constant-currency ($8.10–$8.15) full-year guidance is the company's own quantification of the effect.

Finally, note that GAAP net income per diluted share fell to $1.21 in Q2 2026 from $2.94 a year earlier despite a 28.9% revenue increase. That is almost entirely the timing of gains on property dispositions between periods; it is not an operating signal, and it is the clearest single illustration of why GAAP EPS is the wrong lens for this security.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been upward versus downward. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a forecast.

Five-year trajectory. From the September 2021 close of $122.70 to $194.30 on August 20, 2026, DLR returned +58.4% on price alone — roughly 9.6% annualised, before a dividend that has yielded between about 2.5% and 4% across the period. That headline number conceals a violent round trip. The stock peaked at $151.27 in December 2021 as the last of the zero-rate era, then fell 42.3% to $87.31 by September 2022 as the Federal Reserve's tightening cycle repriced every long-duration real-estate asset and DLR's development pipeline shifted from an asset to a funding liability in investors' minds. From that trough the recovery has been +122.5%, driven first by the rate-peak rally of late 2023 and then, decisively, by the AI infrastructure build-out.

The 2025 drawdown deserves attention because it is the template for the bear case. After peaking at a $186.08 monthly close in November 2024, the stock fell to $138.31 by March 2025 — a 25.7% decline — on the AI-capex reassessment that followed cheaper-model announcements and questions about whether hyperscalers had over-ordered capacity. Nothing in DLR's leasing book deteriorated; the multiple compressed. It then chopped sideways through the back half of 2025, closing December 2025 at $152.65, before the 2026 leg higher took it to $194.30, +27.3% year-to-date, on two consecutive quarters of record bookings and two guidance raises.

Relative performance. Against the S&P 500 — the appropriate benchmark for an NYSE large-cap — DLR's ~9.6% annualised five-year price return has lagged the index's post-2022 advance, which was concentrated in a handful of mega-cap technology names that DLR indirectly serves as a landlord. Within its own sector the picture inverts: DLR's +18.85% TTM revenue growth is roughly double Equinix's +10.9%, and DLR trades at 23.8× forward core FFO against a materially higher multiple at Equinix on the same basis. The pattern across the five years is consistent — DLR trades as a rate-duration asset when rates dominate the narrative and as an AI-infrastructure proxy when they do not, which is why its realised drawdowns are larger than its 1.04 beta suggests.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

DLR owns the scarce input in the fastest-growing infrastructure market on earth — roughly 9 GW of secured, entitled future power capacity on top of 3 GW in place — at a moment when utility interconnection queues, not capital, gate every competitor. The pricing consequence is already in the numbers: portfolio cash renewal spreads of +25.4% and +66.7% on >1 MW leases, with a record $1.9 billion backlog equal to about 30% of in-place data-center rent that converts to revenue through 2028. The private-capital platform (now >$12B with Columbia Capital) funds a 1.4 GW / $20 billion pipeline at an ~11.5% expected stabilised yield while leverage fell to 4.7× net debt/EBITDA. At 23.8× 2026E core FFO with a well-covered 2.5% yield and 59.7% payout, an investor is paying a reasonable price for visible double-digit FFO growth.

Bear Case

The stock has already re-rated on the AI narrative — up 27.3% year-to-date and 122.5% off the 2022 trough — and 23.8× forward core FFO leaves little cushion if hyperscale leasing decelerates. That demand comes from a handful of customers who are also DLR's largest competitors: every dollar of hyperscaler self-build is a dollar not leased. Meanwhile the business model requires permanent access to cheap capital — $4.25–4.75B of 2026 net capex against negative free cash flow of -$507M, funded with $2.5B of ATM equity and JV recycling — so a rate back-up compresses the development spread from both ends at once. The 2.9% weighted-average coupon must eventually reprice. March 2025 showed the mechanism: a -25.7% drawdown on sentiment alone, with no deterioration in bookings.

Top 3 Risks
01
Hyperscaler demand concentration and self-build substitution. Roughly 48% of Q2 2026 bookings came from >1 MW deployments to a small set of cloud and AI counterparties who simultaneously build owned capacity at enormous scale. A shift in their build-versus-lease mix — or simple capex digestion after two years of record ordering — would compress the highest-volume part of the pipeline first, and the $1.9B backlog would stop replenishing even as it converts.
02
Cost of capital and continuous-issuance dependency. A REIT paying out 59.7% of core FFO cannot self-fund a $20 billion pipeline. DLR raised $2.5B of ATM equity in 2026 YTD at $184.94 and carries $18.6B of debt at a 2.9% weighted-average coupon with a 4.5-year weighted-average maturity. Higher long rates would simultaneously raise refinancing costs, lower the equity currency, and narrow the spread between the ~11.5% development yield and the cost of capital that is the entire economic engine.
03
Power delivery, permitting and FX execution risk. The moat and the risk are the same asset. Utility commitments like the 600 MW Kansas City award ramping in early 2028 depend on delivery DLR does not control, and local restrictions on data-center load and water use are live in Northern Virginia, Ireland and the Netherlands. Separately, with only 54% of revenue in US dollars and ~23% in euros, reported core FFO carries recurring currency risk — the company's own $0.05 gap between reported and constant-currency 2026 guidance quantifies it.
  Industry Commentary
Power Is the Binding Constraint on the Entire Sector
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Data-center demand is no longer gated by capital, land or construction capacity — it is gated by megawatts the grid can actually deliver. Utilities in every tier-one availability zone are quoting multi-year interconnection queues, and several jurisdictions have moved from queuing to outright restriction. That inverts the historical industry structure: for two decades data centres were a commodity development business where the marginal builder set price; today the operator holding secured power sets price, and the renewal data proves it. DLR's +66.7% cash renewal spreads on >1 MW leases are not a negotiating triumph — they are what happens when a tenant renewing a 2015-vintage lease discovers there is nowhere else to go. The company is responding by buying the constraint directly: $475 million for Kansas City powered land tied to 2 GW of utility power, with 600 MW secured and ramping in early 2028, plus a 288 MW Virginia hyperscale acquisition. The total controlled footprint is now ~3 GW in place plus 9 GW of future development capacity. The risk embedded in the same fact: utility delivery dates slip, and a pipeline priced on 2028 power that arrives in 2030 is worth considerably less.
Hyperscaler Self-Build: Customer and Competitor in the Same Contract
ONGOING HEADWIND
The structural oddity of this industry is that the largest buyers of leased capacity are also the largest builders of owned capacity. Cloud platforms use third-party data centres for speed-to-market, geographic reach and balance-sheet flexibility, and shift toward owned shells wherever demand is predictable enough to justify the capital. That makes third-party leasing volumes inherently second-derivative: they reflect not total AI demand but the residual after hyperscalers allocate to self-build. DLR's stated defence is deliberate segment selection — management said on the Q2 call it targets "investment-grade credit names" for large deployments rather than competing on price for every workload, and it is pushing hard into the parts of the market hyperscalers structurally cannot serve. The evidence that this is working is the record $108 million of combined 0–1 MW and interconnection bookings — 52% of DLR-share bookings, above the hyperscale contribution for the first time — plus 142 new logos in a single quarter across a ~6,000-customer base. Sub-megawatt colocation and cross-connects carry higher rent per kilowatt, far lower churn, and no hyperscaler alternative.
Rates, REIT Cost of Capital, and How the Bull–Bear Debate Resolves
EMERGING TAILWIND — CONDITIONAL
DLR is simultaneously a growth asset and a duration asset, and which one the market prices depends almost entirely on the rate path. The 2022 experience is the reference case: a 42.3% drawdown with no operating deterioration, purely because the discount rate moved. What is different in 2026 is that the capital-recycling model has partially decoupled growth from balance-sheet capacity — DLR grew capacity under construction to 1.4 GW / $20 billion at 63% pre-leased while reducing leverage to 4.7× net debt/EBITDA, down 0.4 turns YoY, by selling majority JV stakes and retaining management economics. The Blackstone joint venture returned a $188 million promote in Q2 alone, and the pending Columbia Capital acquisition takes private-capital capacity above $12 billion.

How it resolves. Bull: if rates ease while AI leasing holds, DLR gets multiple expansion and a widening development spread at the same time — the $1.9B backlog converts into double-digit core FFO growth through 2028 and the analyst cluster at $220–$240 proves conservative. Bear: if long rates back up while hyperscalers digest capacity, the stock re-rates as a leveraged REIT with negative free cash flow and a 2.9% coupon rolling toward market — March 2025's -25.7% move on sentiment alone is the honest downside template. The signal that distinguishes them is not the macro print but the quarterly bookings number: as long as backlog replenishes faster than it converts, the growth framing holds.
