01 Company Overview

For ten years Dollar Tree was two companies wearing one ticker. In July 2014 it agreed to buy Family Dollar for $8.5 billion; on July 7, 2025 it announced it had finished selling that business to Brigade Capital Management and Macellum Capital Management for an aggregate base price of $1,007.5 million, with estimated net proceeds of about $800 million. What is left is the original idea: 9,436 Dollar Tree stores in the U.S. and Canada (as of August 1, 2026), selling consumables, variety goods and seasonal merchandise at low fixed price points. Continuing-operations revenue for the fiscal year ended January 31, 2026 was $19.41B, up 10.43%; trailing-twelve-month revenue is $20.07B and trailing EPS is $8.15.

The exit was expensive to admit. When Family Dollar was classified as held for sale in Q4 of fiscal 2024, Dollar Tree booked a $3,438.7 million loss on the classification, on top of a $1,400.0 million trade-name impairment and a $490.5 million goodwill impairment — the fiscal year ended February 1, 2025 closed with a $3.03B net loss. The stock had already priced it in: the month-end close fell from $165.36 in July 2022 to $64.64 in October 2024, a 61% drawdown. Since then it has almost doubled, to $127.21 at the end of July 2026, before giving back 11.7% to $112.33 by September 21. The question now is simple to state: once the one-off tariff-refund gains wash out of the numbers, what does a clean, single-banner Dollar Tree actually earn?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

9,436-store footprint 19 distribution centers Fixed-price-point brand Buying scale ($20.07B TTM sales) Small-box convenience Treasure-hunt assortment

The moat is a price promise backed by logistics. A shopper walking into a Dollar Tree knows roughly what the shelf will cost before they look at it. That certainty is the brand, and the network of 19 distribution centres across 48 states and seven Canadian provinces is what lets the company keep it. The weakness is that the promise is a ceiling. Every year of inflation squeezes more products out of the price band, and every price change risks the thing that made the brand. The 2021 move from $1.00 to $1.25 and the multi-price rollout are both answers to that squeeze, and both put the promise at risk.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Earnings are flattered by a one-off. Q2 diluted EPS of $2.70 included a $1.31 benefit from the net impact of IEEPA tariff refunds; first-half EPS of $4.44 included $1.29. Gross margin rose 850bps to 42.9% in Q2, and 680bps of that was the refund. The FY2026 adjusted EPS guide of $7.70–$8.05 includes about $0.60 of it. Take the refund out and the trailing P/E is closer to 16 than 14.
02
Growth is ticket, not traffic. Q2 comps were +3.7%, but 3.3 points came from average ticket and only 0.4 from traffic. First-half traffic was negative. A price-led comp is fine while shoppers accept it. The model depends on traffic holding up.
03
The balance sheet is not light. StockAnalysis shows total debt of $7.67B against $1.06B cash and $3.43B of book equity, a debt-to-equity ratio of 2.24. The company reports no commercial paper and no revolver borrowings, so the total likely includes store lease liabilities (my inference, not verified). Buybacks of $1.2B in six months are being funded from free cash flow of $1.98B TTM.
04
The low-income customer is stretched. News coverage on the StockAnalysis feed (PYMNTS, Aug 2026) says traditional low-income dollar-store shoppers "remain under pressure from the cost of necessities." Higher-income trade-down customers are filling the gap. They are also the most likely to leave when conditions improve.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items distort almost every ratio above. First, tariff refunds. The Q2 release lists IEEPA tariff refunds of $383 million ($369M in cost of sales and $14M of interest income), offset by $37M of reinvestment spending and $13M of duties on aluminium pans and paper plates, all net of tax. The result is a $1.31 EPS benefit in Q2, and the FY2026 guide includes about $0.60 of net benefit for the full year. The Q3 guide already includes about $0.50 of reinvestment cost, so part of the Q2 windfall is being spent in Q3. Second, the Family Dollar history. Revenue fell from $26.32B in the fiscal year ended January 2022 to $15.41B the following year on StockAnalysis's continuing-operations basis. Family Dollar was restated as discontinued operations, not lost. Any per-share or revenue series that crosses 2025 compares two different companies. The Family Dollar business still contributes through the transition services agreement, which produced $18M of TSA income, net, in Q2.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $95.72 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $112.33, DLTR gained 17.4% on price. It pays no dividend, so that is also the total return. The endpoints hide the path. The stock ran to $165.36 by July 2022, lost 61% to $64.64 by October 2024, and then nearly doubled to $127.21 by July 2026. Over the full five years, someone who held throughout got a modest gain; someone who bought at the top in 2022 is still down about 32%.

The 6-period monthly RSI bottomed at 17.1 in October 2024, the most oversold reading in the series, and peaked in the high 80s in spring 2022. It now reads 47.3, neutral, after September's 11.3% drop from the August close. The 10-month moving average is about $117.72, above the current price, so the September decline has pushed the stock back below its medium-term trend. StockAnalysis's daily 14-day RSI is 32.92, near oversold on the shorter timeframe.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The ten-year Family Dollar distraction is over and has already been written down. What remains is a single-banner chain with 9,436 stores, positive traffic in Q2 for the first time this year, +3.7% comps on top of +6.5%, square footage growing 3.4%, and sales per square foot rising. It generates $1.98B of free cash flow (a 9.39% yield) and has retired 6.94% of its shares in a year, with $2.5B more authorised. At 14.35x forward earnings, with the stock 21% below its 52-week high and consensus targets 21% above the price, the market is valuing it as if the refund-free earnings base is the whole story. If multi-price conversions keep increasing sales per square foot, the refund-free earnings base will grow on its own.

Bear Case

The headline numbers are inflated. $1.29 of first-half EPS and 680bps of Q2 gross margin came from tariff refunds, which will not recur, and Q3 is guided to $0.80–$0.95 against a prior $1.39 consensus. Consensus EPS for next year is up just 1.0%. On refund-free earnings the stock is closer to 16x trailing, not 14x, for a retailer with 2.24x debt-to-equity, a customer base under pressure, and comps driven by ticket rather than traffic. The sales guide was held, not raised. Every price-point change also risks the certainty that brings shoppers in.

Top 3 Risks

01
Earnings normalisation after the tariff refunds. The FY2026 guide includes about $0.60 of net refund benefit, and Q3 includes about $0.50 of reinvestment cost. When the one-off disappears, the P/E is measured on a lower base: ≈16.4x trailing on refund-adjusted EPS (estimate).
02
Traffic. First-half traffic was −0.3% and Q2 was +0.4%. If ticket growth has to carry comps while traffic turns negative again, the price-point strategy is costing shoppers.
03
Leverage alongside buybacks. $7.67B of total debt against $3.43B of equity, and $1.2B spent on buybacks in six months. That works while free cash flow holds at around $2B. A weak holiday quarter would squeeze buybacks first.
Industry Commentary
Trade-down: the value channel's wider customer base
EMERGING TAILWIND

Coverage on the StockAnalysis feed (PYMNTS, August 2026) describes dollar stores "getting more business from middle- and high-income households at the same time that their traditional low-income customers remain under pressure from the cost of necessities," and a separate PYMNTS piece headlined Dollar Tree's Q2 as attracting higher-income customers as sales climbed 7%. For a fixed-price-point retailer, that is the best kind of growth: new customers who come for the price and stay for the assortment. The catch is that trade-down is cyclical. These customers came when budgets tightened and can leave when they loosen.

Tariffs: the cost that came back as a refund
ONE-OFF DISTORTION

A retailer built around imported variety goods sold at fixed prices is heavily exposed to tariffs. The Q2 release credits lower tariff rates with part of the underlying gross-margin gain, and books $383M of IEEPA tariff refunds as a one-time benefit. The company is reinvesting part of it, about $0.50 of EPS in Q3. For the analysis, the refund is a balance-sheet event recorded in the income statement. It says nothing about future tariff policy and should be removed before any multiple is applied.

Dollar Tree vs. Dollar General — two answers to one customer
COMPETITIVE CONTEXT

Dollar General ($26.90B market cap) and Dollar Tree ($21.08B) sell to overlapping customers. Wikipedia's history recalls that they bid against each other for Family Dollar in 2014. Dollar Tree won that auction and spent a decade unwinding it. On the day of Dollar Tree's Q2 print, Schwab Network framed the two as "a tale of two retailers" moving in opposite directions. Relative comps, traffic and margin trajectories for the two chains were not compared from sources fetched this session, so no ranking is offered here.
