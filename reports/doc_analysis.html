01 Company Overview

Healthpeak is a REIT that owns, operates and develops real estate "for healthcare discovery and delivery" — in plain terms, three kinds of building: outpatient medical offices next to hospitals, life-science labs where biotech companies do research, and senior housing, now held through a separately listed subsidiary, Janus Living. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $2.95B, up 5.5%. The company that trades as DOC today was Healthpeak (ticker PEAK) until it merged with Physicians Realty Trust — which had itself used the DOC ticker. The merger closed on March 1, 2024 and the combined company kept the Healthpeak name and took the DOC symbol on March 4, 2024. Every price in this report is Healthpeak's own history, not the old Physicians Realty stock.

The story of the last five years is one number falling and another holding. The share price went from a $36.09 month-end close in December 2021 to $15.55 in October 2023 — a 57% fall — as — in our reading — rising rates repriced REITs and the lab business, the growth engine, ran into a biotech funding downturn. The cash flow did not follow it down: FFO as Adjusted was $1.81 a share in 2024 and $1.84 in 2025, and management guides $1.73–$1.77 for 2026. At $20.72, the stock trades at about 11.8x the midpoint of that guide and yields 5.9%. The GAAP P/E of 60.9 is not the valuation that matters here; depreciation across a $21.7B asset base swamps accounting profit. The question is whether lab occupancy (78.5%) has turned, and whether the senior-housing IPO and the asset sales turn a discounted portfolio into cash at close to what it is worth on paper.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hospital-campus locations Health-system relationships Core life-science clusters 80% outpatient tenant retention Scale after the $5B merger Investment-grade access to capital

In real estate the moat is location plus the tenant relationship. A medical office building attached to a hospital campus cannot be reproduced across the street. The physicians need to be next to the operating rooms, which is why 80% of outpatient tenants renew. Health systems also bring repeat development work: the Northside Hospital building in Atlanta, announced in June 2026, is Healthpeak's fifth ground-up project for that system, totaling 565,000 sq ft. Lab is different. A cluster location helps, but when funding dries up the tenants are small companies that can shrink or disappear, and a well-located empty lab still earns nothing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Lab tenants live on biotech funding. Healthpeak's own risk factors cite "changes within the life science industry, and significant regulation, funding requirements, and uncertainty faced by our lab tenants." One-third of same-store NOI rests on a tenant base whose capital comes from venture and public equity markets that open and close.
02
Rates set the ceiling on value. REIT prices compete with bond yields. In CEO Brinker's words, the cost of debt "isn't much lower than the fair market cap rate of our real estate". Every new purchase is close to break-even until rates fall or rents rise.
03
Complexity and conflicts with Janus. Healthpeak externally manages a listed subsidiary it owns 73.6% of. The 10-K risk factors flag "potential conflicts of interest in our relationship with Janus Living" and "economic exposure to shifts in the price of Janus Living common stock." A structure built to unlock value can also add a holding-company discount.
04
Shrinking to grow. The 2026 FFO guide ($1.73–$1.77) is below 2025's $1.84 because assets are being sold faster than new income arrives. That is fine if the proceeds are recycled at better returns and a problem if they are not.

Notable One-Off Items

GAAP earnings in 2026 are unusually noisy. Six-month 2026 net income applicable to common shares was $246.2M ($0.36 a share) against $73.9M ($0.11) a year earlier. The increase comes from $60.7M of gains on property sales and $156.5M of "other income", which the FFO reconciliation shows is mostly a $138.3M gain upon change of control. Q2 also includes a one-time $9M of interest income from the accelerated repayment of a seller-financing loan. FFO strips most of this out, which is why FFO per share was flat while EPS rose. The Janus IPO also changes the share base per unit of senior-housing income: about 26.4% of Janus's earnings now go to outside shareholders as noncontrolling interest. Finally, Healthpeak bought back 5.9M shares at $16.81 (~$100M) in April 2026, and the board authorized a new $500M program in July.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $33.48 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $20.72, the stock lost 38.1% of its price. At the current $1.22 a year, five years of dividends come to about $6 a share (an estimate at today's rate, not a sourced total), which recovers part of the loss but leaves the total return clearly negative. It was not a steady decline. There was a 2022–2023 collapse as rates rose, a merger-era recovery to $22.87 in September 2024, a second slide to $16.08 by December 2025, and a sharp 2026 rebound around the Janus Living IPO.

The 6-period monthly RSI printed 73.3 in July 2026, the highest reading in the five-year series and the first above 70. The low was 20.7 in October 2023. It has since eased to 62.0. Price is above both the 3-month average (about $21.12) and the 10-month average (about $18.75). The trend has turned up, and the latest move off the high is consolidation, not a reversal so far.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay about 11.8x 2026 guided FFO, with a 5.9% yield covered about 1.4 times by FFO, for a portfolio whose parts are being priced privately at higher values. Brookfield paid a ~5.9% cap rate for half the outpatient book, Janus Living went public and has since spent ~$1.0B on acquisitions, and management values Healthpeak's stake in it at roughly $6.5B. Leverage is 4.7x. Lab occupancy has risen for two straight quarters with ~480,000 sq ft of LOIs behind it. If lab NOI turns positive in 2027, the drag on the portfolio becomes a contributor. The starting multiple, 11.8x guided FFO, is still well below where the stock traded before 2023.

Bear Case

2026 FFO is guided below 2025, and Lab, a third of same-store NOI, is still shrinking at −3.2%. The recovery depends on biotech funding that Healthpeak does not control, and the CEO himself says the cost of debt is close to property yields, so external growth adds little. Janus Living adds a layer of complexity and potential conflicts, and a quarter of its growth now belongs to outside shareholders. After a 35% rally in three months, 14 of 22 analysts rate it Hold and the two most recent target changes were cuts (Jefferies to $21 on Sep 15, Evercore ISI to $22 on Sep 21). The easy part of the re-rating may be done.

Top 3 Risks

01
Lab recovery stalls. Occupancy at 78.5% and same-store NOI at −3.2% leave little room for error. If signed LOIs do not convert, or tenants shrink as funding tightens, the second-half commencement story slips into 2027.
02
Interest rates. A REIT yielding 5.9% competes directly with bonds. Higher long-term yields would lower property values and make refinancing more expensive, and with debt costs already near cap rates there is little cushion.
03
Execution of a complex restructuring. A listed subsidiary, a Brookfield JV with a year-seven call right, ongoing asset sales and a buyback all compete for capital. The 2026 FFO decline is accepted in exchange for a better portfolio later. If proceeds are redeployed poorly, the lower FFO stays and the improvement does not come.
Industry Commentary
Life science: from development to acquisition
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For a decade, lab REITs grew by building. Brinker's Q2 2026 view is that "for the next few years, it will be an acquisition game", meaning the value is in buying existing buildings from distressed or exiting owners rather than adding supply. Healthpeak's February 2026 plan fits that: sell stabilized lab assets into strong private demand and recycle into campuses in core submarkets, as with the $600M South San Francisco purchase. The constraint is tenant demand, which depends on biotech capital markets. Healthpeak's filings list this among the first risks.

Public vs private real-estate pricing
ONGOING DEBATE

Healthpeak spent 2026 testing whether private buyers would pay more for its assets than public investors. Brookfield valued an 86-building outpatient portfolio at $2.1B (about $380 per sq ft, ~5.9% trailing cash cap rate), and $400M of seller-financing loans were repaid early. Year-to-date proceeds reached $1.75B, well ahead of the $1B plan. Each such sale sets a private-market reference price for the rest of the portfolio. Selling also shrinks near-term FFO, which is why the 2026 guide sits below 2025.

Senior housing demand
EMERGING TAILWIND

Janus Living's same-store NOI grew 19.2% with a 250 bps margin expansion in Q2 2026, and total revenue rose 45%, the strongest growth anywhere in the portfolio. The bull framing is that Healthpeak put its fastest-growing business into a listed vehicle whose share price makes its value visible. The bear framing is that Healthpeak now owns less of that growth, collects it through a management agreement and a 73.6% stake, and carries the conflicts that come with the structure. Senior housing was only 9.6% of Healthpeak's same-store NOI in Q2, so the effect on group numbers will be slow unless Janus keeps buying.
