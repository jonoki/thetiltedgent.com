01 Company Overview

Domino's is the pizza brand most people picture on a delivery scooter, but the listed company is something quieter: a franchisor that collects royalties on other people's pizza sales and runs the supply chain that feeds those stores. At the end of Q2 2026 the system had 22,531 stores — 7,231 in the U.S. and 15,300 internationally — and in FY2025 it rang up $20.13B of global retail sales. Most of that is franchisees' revenue, not Domino's. The company's FY2025 revenue was $4.94B, up 4.96%, with diluted EPS of $17.57.

For years the market treated this as a compounder: a pizza chain that behaved like a toll road on other people's sales. The five-year chart tells a different story. From a $564.33 month-end close in December 2021, the stock has fallen to $295.94 — including seven straight monthly declines from December 2025 to June 2026, the longest losing run in the five-year series. The reason is visible in one number: U.S. same store sales growth went from +3.0% in FY2025 to +0.9% in Q1 2026 to +0.1% in Q2 2026, while international same store sales slipped slightly negative. Store count keeps rising and order counts are growing, per management — but the average store is no longer selling more. Add a CEO handover on October 1, 2026 and the exit of its most famous shareholder, and the question for this report is simple: is Domino's a great system going through a flat patch, or a mature one being re-priced as such?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

22,531-store scale Owned supply chain Asset-light franchise royalties National ad fund ($134.9M in Q2) Delivery + carryout density Brand recognition

The moat is density. More stores in a town means shorter delivery radii, faster pizzas and lower delivery cost per order; lower cost per order funds a national promotion; the promotion drives orders that justify more stores. Domino's owns the two parts of that loop a franchisee cannot replicate alone — the supply chain and the national ad fund — and franchisees pay for both. The result is a business with a TTM ROIC of 62.24%. The vulnerability is that density is a flywheel for volume. It does not protect the ticket: if customers trade down and the category is in a price war, the loop spins but earns less per turn — which is roughly what the Q2 2026 numbers show.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Same store sales have gone flat. U.S. comps fell from +3.0% (FY2025) to +0.1% (Q2 2026) and international comps turned slightly negative for two quarters. A franchisor valued as a compounder needs the average store to grow; unit growth alone produces mid-single-digit revenue growth, which is what the FY2027 consensus (+2.37% revenue) already assumes.
02
Franchisee economics sit upstream of everything. Flat sales per store on rising food and labour costs squeeze the franchisee first and the franchisor later, through slower openings and closures. In September one franchisee, Mile High Pizza Company, closed 13 stores in northern Ohio without public explanation; Domino's called it "an isolated franchisee matter" and said it is working to move the stores to new owners and reopen them (Pizza Marketplace, Sep 14, 2026). Thirteen stores out of 7,231 is small, but it is the kind of signal to watch.
03
The balance sheet is built for growth, not for a stall. Book equity is −$3.98B and the balance sheet carries $4.88B of long-term debt at June 14, 2026, $4.77B of it fixed-rate securitised notes (company release; StockAnalysis shows $5.12B of total debt, a broader measure). Leverage of this kind is comfortable when royalties rise every year; in a flat year it limits how much can go to buybacks and dividends without the leverage ratio creeping back up.
04
A new CEO and a departed anchor holder in the same year. Joe Jordan takes over on October 1, 2026, after running the U.S. business during the slowdown he now inherits. Berkshire Hathaway, which had built a near-10% stake, sold all of it in Q1 2026 — removing a high-profile, long-horizon holder from the register.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 16.8x trailing and 15.5x forward earnings for a franchisor with a 62% ROIC, a ~6.7% free-cash-flow yield, a dividend raised 15% this year to a 2.69% yield, 776 net new stores in FY2025 and 389 more in the first half of 2026. Order counts are growing, per management; flat comps on rising orders is a ticket problem, and tickets recover when promotions roll off. The stock sits within about 2% of its five-year low with RSI back in oversold territory, 48% of analysts rate it Buy, and the average target is $381. A new CEO who ran the U.S. business gets a clean slate at a low base.

Bear Case

The reported numbers already show the problem: U.S. comps went +3.0% → +0.9% → +0.1% and international comps turned negative, putting a 32-year streak at risk. Growth is now almost entirely new stores, which depends on franchisees' willingness to keep building — and in September one franchisee abruptly closed 13 Ohio stores (Domino's calls it isolated). The balance sheet has −$3.98B of equity and a 4.3x leverage ratio, fine for a compounder and uncomfortable for a utility-like one. The most famous long-term holder left, short interest is 9.59% of float, and consensus sees FY2027 revenue growth of just 2.37%. A mid-teens multiple may not be cheap; it may be the right price for a mature restaurant brand.

Top 3 Risks

01
Same store sales stall becomes a decline. U.S. +0.1% and international −0.1% in Q2 2026 leave no cushion. Negative comps would hit royalties, ad-fund revenue and franchisee profitability together.
02
Franchisee health and the store-growth engine. With comps flat, unit growth carries the story. Slower openings or more closures would take away the one growth lever still working.
03
Leadership transition under a levered balance sheet. A CEO change on October 1, 2026, $4.88B of long-term debt and negative equity leave less room for error if the new team needs to invest in price or technology to restart comps.
Industry Commentary
A value fight in U.S. quick-service pizza
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Domino's sells a cheap, habitual product, and in 2026 it has been leaning on price to keep the habit: a week of half-price menu pizzas from July 27 to August 2 and a new single-serve pizza launched on August 31. Growing orders while holding comps flat is what a value fight looks like from inside a winner — the share is held, the ticket gives. Yahoo Finance's May 19 report described the backdrop as weak consumer sentiment, ongoing inflation and more intense competition from rival pizza chains. The peer set's market values make the point from another direction: Papa John's, a direct competitor, is valued at roughly $0.67B, about one-fifteenth of Domino's.

Franchise economics and store growth
ONGOING HEADWIND

For a franchisor, the customer who matters most is the franchisee deciding whether to open the next store. Domino's is still adding units — 209 net globally in Q2 2026 — but the U.S. added only 26 in the quarter, and sales per store are flat while food and labour costs are not. September's abrupt closure of 13 northern Ohio stores by a single franchisee is small against the system and Domino's calls it isolated, but it names the risk precisely: if the marginal franchisee's return falls, the net-store target is the first number to move.

The valuation reset — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $295.94, DPZ trades at 16.8x trailing EPS with a 2.69% dividend yield. The bull framing: the market has priced a cyclical comps stall as a permanent one; the system is still growing units, cash generation is intact, and a new CEO inherits a low bar. The bear framing: a franchisor whose same store sales have stopped growing is a slower business than the one the old multiple paid for, and the discount is the market adjusting to that. The test is the next two quarters of U.S. comps and U.S. net openings — both reported every quarter, and neither has yet given a clean signal.
