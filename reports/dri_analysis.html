01 Company Overview

Darden is the company behind the breadsticks. It runs 2,218 company-owned restaurants (August 30, 2026) across Olive Garden, LongHorn Steakhouse, Cheddar's Scratch Kitchen, Chuy's, Yard House, Ruth's Chris Steak House, The Capital Grille, Seasons 52, Eddie V's, the last 10 Bahama Breeze units and three Capital Burgers. Fiscal 2026 (the year to May 31, 2026) was the first in company history above $13 billion of sales: $13.21B, up 9.4%, with GAAP diluted EPS from continuing operations of $10.44 and adjusted EPS of $10.64 (+11.4%). One asterisk sits on every FY2026 growth rate: it was a 53-week year, and the extra week added 2.1 points of sales growth and $0.25 of EPS.

The story is a portfolio that has quietly swapped engines. For thirty years Olive Garden was Darden. It still is 42% of sales — but in fiscal 2026 LongHorn grew sales 13.1% on 7.2% same-restaurant sales, and in the fourth quarter it posted a 9.5% comp against Olive Garden's 2.4%. The company is also a serial acquirer — Cheddar's (2017), Ruth's Chris (June 2023), Chuy's (October 2024) — and a serial pruner: Red Lobster was sold in 2014, Olive Garden Canada was sold in fiscal 2026, and Bahama Breeze is being closed or converted by the end of fiscal 2027. At $195.11 the market pays 19.1x trailing earnings for that machine. The central debate: can LongHorn and the acquired brands keep carrying growth while Olive Garden's comps fade toward 1% — and does a balance sheet carrying $8.57B of debt including leases leave room to keep buying the next Chuy's?

The first quarter of fiscal 2027 (13 weeks to August 30, 2026) put numbers on that debate. Sales rose 5.1% to $3.20B and diluted EPS from continuing operations was $2.05, both a hair under expectations. Olive Garden's same-restaurant sales rose just 1.1%; on the call CFO Raj Vennam put the hit to its guest counts at 150 to 200 basis points from the World Cup and consumer worries about lettuce. LongHorn rose 6.2%, which Vennam called "its 22nd consecutive quarter of positive same-restaurant sales growth." Management reaffirmed every line of the fiscal 2027 outlook.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: 2,218 restaurants Multi-brand portfolio Integration playbook (Ruth's, Chuy's) Owned guest data (no marketplaces) Brand recognition Cash generation ($1.78B TTM OCF)

Restaurants do not have moats in the textbook sense — nobody is locked into Olive Garden, and a new competitor is one lease away. What Darden has is operating scale spread across brands: one purchasing, technology and real-estate machine serving nine-plus concepts, and a proven routine for buying a regional chain and running it through that machine. The evidence is in the numbers: LongHorn beating the industry by 800bps with record steak "cook-correct" scores, Olive Garden's Q4 traffic outpacing the industry by roughly 200bps, and 9% annualised adjusted EBITDA growth since 2019 per the CFO. The weakness is that none of this protects against the diner simply staying home, and the portfolio is still 42% one brand.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Olive Garden is decelerating. From a 4.0% full-year comp in fiscal 2026 to 2.4% in Q4 and 1.1% in Q1 FY2027 — management blames the World Cup and lettuce worries for 150 to 200 basis points of lost guest counts. At 42% of sales and the largest profit pool, every point of Olive Garden comp moves the group more than a point of anything else.
02
Leverage is not trivial. $8.57B of total debt including lease liabilities against $220.5M of cash at August 30, 2026 — net debt of $8.35B, 3.9x trailing EBITDA of $2.14B (StockAnalysis figures, our division); debt/equity 4.14. Of that, $2.62B is borrowings ($979.7M short-term, $1,636.1M long-term, per the Q1 release) and $5.96B is lease liabilities (StockAnalysis); on borrowings alone, net debt is about 1.1x EBITDA. Equity is small because of years of buybacks, which is also why ROE reads 55.4%.
03
Beef cuts both ways. High grocery beef prices send steak dinners to LongHorn; they also raise LongHorn's own food cost. Full-year LongHorn segment margin slipped to 18.6% from 19.3%. In Q1 FY2027 food and beverage costs ran about 30 basis points higher as a share of sales; on the call the CFO said some beef prices, especially sirloin, were starting to come down — a commodity call, not an operating one.
04
Younger guests are softening. The CEO said on the Q4 call that traffic from consumers under 35 weakened slightly. Casual dining lives on the next generation of regulars.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the FY2026 figures. First, the 53rd week: 7.6% of Q4 sales growth, 2.1% of full-year sales growth and $0.25 of EPS came from an extra week that will not repeat in FY2027 — which is why FY2027 guidance looks slow. Second, adjustments of $0.20 per share between GAAP ($10.44) and adjusted ($10.64) continuing-operations EPS, mostly restaurant closures and impairments (chiefly Bahama Breeze), Chuy's integration costs and income-tax items, partly offset by a gain on the Olive Garden Canada sale. Third, buybacks: the diluted share count fell 1.77% year-over-year (116.3M from 118.4M), $138M was repurchased in Q4 alone, and a new, non-expiring $1.5B authorisation was approved on June 24, 2026.

Q1 FY2027 was clean: no adjustments, so reported and adjusted diluted EPS from continuing operations are both $2.05. The year-ago quarter's reported $2.19 included a $0.26 gain on the Olive Garden Canada sale, which is why reported EPS fell 6.4% while adjusted EPS rose 4.1% (from $1.97). Darden bought back about 1.1 million shares for $222.3M in the quarter, leaving $1.3B of the $1.5B authorisation; diluted shares averaged 114.4M against 117.6M a year earlier.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Darden is the house that keeps winning small pots: positive comps at every segment in Q1 FY2027 and every brand in Q4 FY2026, LongHorn's 22nd straight quarter of positive same-restaurant sales, 53 net new restaurants in a year and 75–80 more coming, 9% annualised adjusted EBITDA growth since 2019, and a 3.32% dividend yield backed by $1.05B of free cash flow. After the post-print slide it trades at 17.3x forward earnings with a 0.59 beta, and management reaffirmed every line of the FY2027 outlook while blaming Olive Garden's soft quarter on the World Cup and a lettuce scare, both one-offs if they are right. A proven acquirer with $1.3B left on its buyback authorisation does not need heroic comps to compound.

Bear Case

The flagship is slowing — Olive Garden comps went from 4.0% for fiscal 2026 to 2.4% in Q4 and 1.1% in Q1 FY2027 — and it is 42% of sales. Fine Dining's profit was flat in a 53-week year, business dining is still shrinking, and management allows that some diners may be trading down to LongHorn. FY2027 guidance is only +5.5% EPS at the midpoint, Q1 came in just under consensus on both EPS ($2.05 vs. $2.06) and sales ($3.20B vs. $3.21B), and even after falling 8.7% from its pre-print close the stock sits at 19.1x trailing earnings. Add $8.57B of debt including leases, a 0.30 current ratio and weakening under-35 traffic, and the valuation leaves little room for a consumer who finally does cut back.

Top 3 Risks

01
Olive Garden traffic. The largest profit pool is decelerating while management deliberately trades check for traffic with Lighter Portions. If the slowdown is traffic rather than mix, group comps fall toward the bottom of the 2.5–3.5% guide.
02
Commodity and labor inflation. The FY2027 plan assumes ~3.0% total inflation. Beef is the swing factor for LongHorn and Fine Dining; a miss on that assumption flows straight through a 21.7% gross margin.
03
Consumer and balance-sheet leverage together. Net debt of $8.35B including leases (3.9x TTM EBITDA; about 1.1x on borrowings alone) is comfortable while sales grow; casual dining is a discretionary purchase, and a spending slowdown would hit earnings and leverage at the same time.
Industry Commentary
The cautious consumer who keeps showing up
WATCH CLOSELY

Darden's fiscal 2027 plan, set in June and reaffirmed in every line on September 24, calls for 2.5%–3.5% same-restaurant sales growth and about 3.0% total inflation; Q1 delivered 3.1%. On the June Q4 call the CEO said guests under 35 had pulled back slightly. The guidance is a bet on the status quo — reasonable, but it means any real consumer slowdown is not in the numbers.

Beef: tailwind for traffic, headwind for cost
KEY VARIABLE

High retail beef prices are part of why LongHorn is winning: when a grocery steak costs more, the restaurant version looks like value. The same prices sit in LongHorn's and Fine Dining's cost of goods. In Q1 FY2027 food and beverage costs rose about 30 basis points as a share of sales while labor fell by about the same, leaving restaurant-level EBITDA at 18.8%, "flat to last year and consistent with our expectations," in the CFO's words; he added that some beef prices, sirloin especially, were starting to come down. The industry-wide question is which way the cycle turns first — menu prices or input costs.

Scale wins in a fragmented industry
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Darden's model — buy regional brands, run them on shared purchasing and systems, open more units — works best when smaller operators are squeezed by the same inflation. At $22.15B it is valued at about twice its nearest listed casual-dining peer. The bull framing: scale compounds, and every segment posted positive comps again in Q1 FY2027. The bear framing: scale did not stop Olive Garden slowing to a 1.1% comp or Fine Dining stalling, and 19x trailing earnings already pays for the compounding. Olive Garden's Q2 comp is the next piece of evidence.
