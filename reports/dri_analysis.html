01 Company Overview

Darden is the company behind the breadsticks. It runs 2,202 company-owned restaurants (May 31, 2026) across Olive Garden, LongHorn Steakhouse, Cheddar's Scratch Kitchen, Chuy's, Yard House, Ruth's Chris Steak House, The Capital Grille, Seasons 52, Eddie V's, the last 13 Bahama Breeze units and three Capital Burgers. Fiscal 2026 (the year to May 31, 2026) was the first in company history above $13 billion of sales: $13.21B, up 9.4%, with GAAP diluted EPS from continuing operations of $10.44 and adjusted EPS of $10.64 (+11.4%). One asterisk sits on every FY2026 growth rate: it was a 53-week year, and the extra week added 2.1 points of sales growth and $0.25 of EPS.

The story is a portfolio that has quietly swapped engines. For thirty years Olive Garden was Darden. It still is 42% of sales — but in fiscal 2026 LongHorn grew sales 13.1% on 7.2% same-restaurant sales, and in the fourth quarter it posted a 9.5% comp against Olive Garden's 2.4%. The company is also a serial acquirer — Cheddar's (2017), Ruth's Chris (June 2023), Chuy's (October 2024) — and a serial pruner: Red Lobster was sold in 2014, Olive Garden Canada was sold in fiscal 2026, and Bahama Breeze is being closed or converted by the end of fiscal 2027. At $211.31 the market pays 20.4x trailing earnings for that machine. The central debate: can LongHorn and the acquired brands keep carrying growth while Olive Garden's comps fade toward 1% — and does a balance sheet carrying $8.05B of debt leave room to keep buying the next Chuy's?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: 2,202 restaurants Multi-brand portfolio Integration playbook (Ruth's, Chuy's) Owned guest data (no marketplaces) Brand recognition Cash generation ($1.85B OCF)

Restaurants do not have moats in the textbook sense — nobody is locked into Olive Garden, and a new competitor is one lease away. What Darden has is operating scale spread across brands: one purchasing, technology and real-estate machine serving nine-plus concepts, and a proven routine for buying a regional chain and running it through that machine. The evidence is in the numbers: LongHorn beating the industry by 800bps with record steak "cook-correct" scores, Olive Garden's Q4 traffic outpacing the industry by roughly 200bps, and 9% annualised adjusted EBITDA growth since 2019 per the CFO. The weakness is that none of this protects against the diner simply staying home, and the portfolio is still 42% one brand.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Olive Garden is decelerating. From a 4.0% full-year comp to 2.4% in Q4, and Oppenheimer models just +0.9% for Q1 FY2027 versus a 1.8% consensus. At 42% of sales and the largest profit pool, every point of Olive Garden comp moves the group more than a point of anything else.
02
Leverage is not trivial. $8.05B of total debt against $219.5M of cash — net debt of $7.83B, 3.09x EBITDA, debt/equity 3.65 (StockAnalysis). Equity is small because of years of buybacks, which is also why ROE reads 53.7%. The composition of that debt figure (bonds vs. leases) was not broken out in the sources fetched.
03
Beef cuts both ways. High grocery beef prices send steak dinners to LongHorn; they also raise LongHorn's own food cost. Full-year LongHorn segment margin slipped to 18.6% from 19.3%. Oppenheimer's bull case for FY2027 leans on easing beef inflation — a commodity call, not an operating one.
04
Younger guests are softening. The CEO said on the Q4 call that traffic from consumers under 35 weakened slightly. Casual dining lives on the next generation of regulars.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the FY2026 figures. First, the 53rd week: 7.6% of Q4 sales growth, 2.1% of full-year sales growth and $0.25 of EPS came from an extra week that will not repeat in FY2027 — which is why FY2027 guidance looks slow. Second, adjustments of $0.20 per share between GAAP ($10.44) and adjusted ($10.64) continuing-operations EPS, mostly restaurant closures and impairments (chiefly Bahama Breeze), Chuy's integration costs and income-tax items, partly offset by a gain on the Olive Garden Canada sale. Third, buybacks: shares outstanding fell 1.77% year-over-year, $138M was repurchased in Q4 alone, and a new, non-expiring $1.5B authorisation was approved on June 24, 2026.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Darden is the house that keeps winning small pots: positive comps at every brand in Q4, a blended +4.5% for the year, LongHorn beating the industry by 800bps, 43 net new restaurants and 75–80 more coming, 9% annualised adjusted EBITDA growth since 2019, and a 3.07% dividend yield backed by $1.11B of free cash flow. It trades at 18.7x forward earnings with a 0.59 beta, and the pre-earnings setup is Oppenheimer's: management has reiterated guidance after Q1 in each of the past four years, and easing beef inflation could lift margins even if Olive Garden traffic is choppy. A proven acquirer with a $1.5B buyback authorisation does not need heroic comps to compound.

Bear Case

The flagship is slowing — Olive Garden comps went from 4.0% for the year to 2.4% in Q4, with +0.9% modelled for Q1 — and it is 42% of sales. Fine Dining's profit was flat in a 53-week year and management admits trade-down to LongHorn. FY2027 guidance is only +5.5% EPS at the midpoint and came in below Street hopes, while the stock sits 20.4x trailing earnings and within 3% of its five-year month-end high. Add $8.05B of debt, a 0.31 current ratio and weakening under-35 traffic, and the valuation leaves little room for a consumer who finally does cut back.

Top 3 Risks

01
Olive Garden traffic. The largest profit pool is decelerating while management deliberately trades check for traffic with Lighter Portions. If the slowdown is traffic rather than mix, group comps fall toward the bottom of the 2.5–3.5% guide.
02
Commodity and labor inflation. The FY2027 plan assumes ~3.0% total inflation. Beef is the swing factor for LongHorn and Fine Dining; a miss on that assumption flows straight through a 21.7% gross margin.
03
Consumer and balance-sheet leverage together. Net debt of $7.83B (3.09x EBITDA) is comfortable while sales grow; casual dining is a discretionary purchase, and a spending slowdown would hit earnings and leverage at the same time.
Industry Commentary
The cautious consumer who keeps showing up
WATCH CLOSELY

On the Q4 call, CEO Rick Cardenas summed up the tension: consumer mood is "still a little cautious," but weaker sentiment "hasn't necessarily translated into reduced spending." Some casual-dining brands saw more visits from all income groups, possibly helped by tax refunds, while guests under 35 pulled back slightly. CFO Raj Vennam built FY2027 on "no material change to industry performance." That makes Darden's guidance a bet on the status quo — reasonable, but it means any real consumer slowdown is not in the numbers.

Beef: tailwind for traffic, headwind for cost
KEY VARIABLE

High retail beef prices are part of why LongHorn is winning: when a grocery steak costs more, the restaurant version looks like value. The same prices sit in LongHorn's and Fine Dining's cost of goods. Oppenheimer's pre-earnings case explicitly relies on easing beef inflation lifting restaurant-level margins. The industry-wide question is which way the cycle turns first — menu prices or input costs.

Scale wins in a fragmented industry
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Darden's model — buy regional brands, run them on shared purchasing and systems, open more units — works best when smaller operators are squeezed by the same inflation. At $23.99B it is valued at more than twice its nearest listed casual-dining peer. The bull framing: scale compounds, and FY2026 proved every brand can post positive comps at once. The bear framing: scale did not stop Olive Garden slowing or Fine Dining stalling, and 20x trailing earnings already pays for the compounding. Thursday's Olive Garden comp is the next piece of evidence.
