01 Company Overview

DTE Energy is Detroit's power company. Its electric utility serves about 2.3 million customers in southeastern Michigan from a fleet that still runs on coal alongside nuclear, gas, hydro, wind and solar; its gas utility serves about 1.4 million customers across Michigan. Around those two regulated franchises sit two smaller businesses — DTE Vantage (industrial energy services, coke products and renewable plants), which DTE announced on November 5 it will sell for about $3 billion, and an Energy Trading desk. FY2025 revenue was $15.81B and GAAP EPS was $7.03; the company guides investors on operating EPS, which it expects at $7.59–$7.73 for 2026 (confirmed November 5, with management positioned for the high end). Its long-term target changed at the same time: operating EPS growth of more than 9% a year from 2027 through 2031, up from 6%–8% through 2030.

A regulated utility is a simple machine to describe. The state lets it earn a set percentage — the allowed ROE — on the capital it has sunk into poles, wires, pipes and plants (the rate base). Earnings grow when the rate base grows and the regulator keeps the allowed return. DTE's story for 2026 is that the first half of that equation has never looked better — a $44.2B five-year capital plan for 2027–2031, up from $34.5B for 2026–2030 (utility spending only; the September figure of $36.5B included about $2B for Vantage), pushed higher by 1.4 GW of Oracle and 1.0 GW of Google data-center load — while the second half has been getting harder. Michigan regulators granted $242.4M of a $574.1M electric request in February at a 9.9% ROE, then $74.5M of a $162.7M gas request on September 10 at 9.8%, and in August asked lawmakers to end annual rate hikes and change how DTE and Consumers Energy make money. The stock went from a $152.37 June month-end to $121.80 at the end of September, then recovered to $135.08 by November 6. The central debate: does data-center growth outrun a regulator that is openly trying to lean against capital spending? The Vantage sale is DTE's answer on funding: the proceeds are meant to cut how much new stock it has to issue.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territory 2.3M electric + 1.4M gas customers Regulated return on capital Nuclear + diversified generation 2.4 GW contracted data-center load Owner of the grid connection

Nobody can string a competing set of wires down a Detroit street. That is the whole moat, and it is absolute — for the volume. What it does not protect is the price, because the price is set by the Michigan Public Service Commission. A utility's real competitive position is therefore its relationship with its regulator, and the 2026 record reads as a regulator pushing back: 42% of the electric ask, 46% of the gas ask, a rejected compressor station, and a formal recommendation to the legislature to "counter the inherent bias for utilities to invest in capital projects," in the words of MPSC Chair Dan Scripps. The data-center contracts are DTE's answer — new load that spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours, which management argues lowers bills for everyone else (~$300M a year of affordability benefit from Oracle once ramped, ~$1.7B over the life of the Google contract, company figures).

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The regulator is changing the rules, not just the numbers. The MPSC's August 2026 recommendations to lawmakers — multiyear rate plans tied to performance metrics instead of annual increases, a smaller bonus on purchased power, competitive bidding for renewables, statutory data-center rules — target the capital-investment model itself. Mizuho cut its target from $164 to $152 on August 27, 2026, after the recommendations, and to $136 on September 28.
02
Negative free cash flow funded by new shares. Trailing operating cash flow of $3.50B against $5.48B of capex leaves free cash flow at −$1.98B (StockAnalysis, trailing twelve months). The gap is closed with debt ($28.53B at September 30 per the 10-Q, 2.25x equity) and about $1 billion a year of new equity on average in 2027–2031. Every dollar of rejected rate relief widens that gap.
03
The rate freeze is conditional. DTE says the Oracle load ramp supports no new electric rate request until at least 2028, and the Google ramp could extend that (company presentation, Nov 5). The freeze is a political asset while it holds and a credibility cost if a construction delay breaks it. Michigan's Attorney General is also asking the commission to rehear the Google contracts.
04
Storms are a Michigan operating cost. An early-July 2026 storm hit nearly 400,000 customers and broke more than 600 poles. Storm restoration is where reliability spending gets judged in public — and where regulators form a view before the next request.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At $135.08 you pay 17.6x the midpoint of 2026 operating EPS guidance for a utility with a 3.69% yield, a $44.2B capital plan and 2.4 GW of signed data-center load — load that spreads fixed costs, with the Google contract now approved by the commission and management targeting operating EPS growth of more than 9% a year from 2027 through 2031 off a $7.66 baseline. The regulatory news is largely known: the stock is 11.3% below its June month-end, the average StockAnalysis target of $148.39 is 9.9% above the price, and the company says it has met or beaten guidance in 18 of the past 19 years. If another data-center agreement lands by year-end and the Vantage sale closes, the market is left pricing a utility that is guiding to 9%+ growth.

Bear Case

A utility grows as fast as its regulator lets it, and Michigan's is saying no more often: 42% of the last electric ask, 46% of the gas ask, a rejected $284.1M compressor station, a 9.8% gas ROE, and a commission asking the legislature to counter the "inherent bias" toward capital spending. A faster growth target needs a bigger rate base that regulators have to approve. DTE runs negative free cash flow (−$1.98B TTM), carries 2.25x debt-to-equity and now plans about $1 billion of new stock a year in 2027–2031, up from $500–$600M a year in the September plan; it is also selling a business that earned $203M in the first nine months of 2026. More than 30 outside parties have joined the pending electric case and Harris does not expect a settlement; Michigan's Attorney General has asked for a rehearing on the Google contracts.

Top 3 Risks

01
Slow rate approvals and a falling allowed return. The pending $474.3M electric case at a requested 10.25% ROE is the next test, with an order expected in February. Another partial grant or an electric ROE below 9.9% would compress the earnings the capital plan is supposed to produce.
02
Data-center execution. The Oracle project is under construction with load ramping in 2027 and the Google contract was approved on October 1, but the Attorney General has petitioned for a rehearing. The load beyond the signed 2.4 GW is pipeline, not contract: about 2 GW in advanced discussions.
03
Financing a cash-negative build. −$1.98B TTM free cash flow, $28.53B of debt and a ~15% FFO/debt target leave limited room, and the plan leans on the Vantage sale (not before December 31, 2026; subject to regulatory approvals) to cut the equity needed. Rejected capital, storm costs or higher interest rates all show up as more equity or more debt.
Industry Commentary
Michigan's regulator leans against the capex model
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The MPSC's August 2026 recommendations are unusual because they come from the regulator itself. Chair Dan Scripps: "The most impactful opportunity may be to counter the inherent bias for utilities to invest in capital projects." The package — performance-linked multiyear rate plans, a smaller purchased-power bonus (about $868M collected by DTE and Consumers since 2019), required grid-utilisation improvements before new infrastructure, and competitive bidding for renewables — goes at the core of how a regulated utility grows earnings. A Citizens Utility Board expert calculated that 8.91% instead of the 10.75% DTE wanted in its last electric case would save customers about $200M a year (Planet Detroit). None of it is law yet — Harris expects multiyear rate-making legislation toward the end of 2027 — and the September 10 gas order suggests the commission is already applying the spirit of it.

Data centers as the affordability argument
EMERGING TAILWIND

DTE's reply is that large new load makes everyone else's bill cheaper. Harris on the July 28 call: as these projects move forward, "they will deliver meaningful affordability benefits for our existing customers, absorbing a significant portion of fixed system costs." Signed: Oracle 1.4 GW (approved, under construction) and Google 1.0 GW (conditionally approved by the commission on October 1; the Attorney General has asked for a rehearing), with ~2 GW in advanced discussions and 3–4 GW earlier in the pipeline. The conditional electric rate freeze is the political wrapper. If regulators accept the argument, data-center capital is the one kind of spending they are inclined to approve; if they do not, it is the largest capital commitment in the plan.
