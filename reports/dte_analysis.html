01 Company Overview

DTE Energy is Detroit's power company. Its electric utility serves about 2.3 million customers in southeastern Michigan from a fleet that still runs on coal alongside nuclear, gas, hydro, wind and solar; its gas utility serves about 1.4 million customers across Michigan. Around those two regulated franchises sit two smaller businesses — DTE Vantage (industrial energy services, coke products and renewable plants) and an Energy Trading desk. FY2025 revenue was $15.81B and GAAP EPS was $7.03; the company guides investors on operating EPS, which it expects at $7.59–$7.73 for 2026, with a 6%–8% long-term growth target through 2030.

A regulated utility is a simple machine to describe. The state lets it earn a set percentage — the allowed ROE — on the capital it has sunk into poles, wires, pipes and plants (the rate base). Earnings grow when the rate base grows and the regulator keeps the allowed return. DTE's story for 2026 is that the first half of that equation has never looked better — a $36.5B five-year capital plan, up from $30B, pushed higher by 1.4 GW of Oracle and 1.0 GW of Google data-center load — while the second half has been getting harder. Michigan regulators granted $242.4M of a $574.1M electric request in February at a 9.9% ROE, then $74.5M of a $162.7M gas request on September 10 at 9.8%, and in August asked lawmakers to end annual rate hikes and change how DTE and Consumers Energy make money. The stock went from a $152.37 June month-end to $126.13 in under three months. The central debate: does data-center growth outrun a regulator that is openly trying to lean against capital spending?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territory 2.3M electric + 1.4M gas customers Regulated return on capital Nuclear + diversified generation 2.4 GW contracted data-center load Owner of the grid connection

Nobody can string a competing set of wires down a Detroit street. That is the whole moat, and it is absolute — for the volume. What it does not protect is the price, because the price is set by the Michigan Public Service Commission. A utility's real competitive position is therefore its relationship with its regulator, and the 2026 record reads as a regulator pushing back: 42% of the electric ask, 46% of the gas ask, a rejected compressor station, and a formal recommendation to the legislature to "counter the inherent bias for utilities to invest in capital projects," in the words of MPSC Chair Dan Scripps. The data-center contracts are DTE's answer — new load that spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours, which management argues lowers bills for everyone else (~$300M a year of affordability benefit from Oracle once ramped, ~$1.7B over the life of the Google contract, company figures).

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The regulator is changing the rules, not just the numbers. The MPSC's August 2026 recommendations to lawmakers — multiyear rate plans tied to performance metrics instead of annual increases, a smaller bonus on purchased power, competitive bidding for renewables, statutory data-center rules — target the capital-investment model itself. Mizuho cut its target from $164 to $152 on August 27, 2026, following the recommendations (StockAnalysis news summary).
02
Negative free cash flow funded by new shares. Trailing operating cash flow of $3.36B against $5.31B of capex leaves free cash flow at −$1.95B. The gap is closed with debt ($27.84B total, 2.29x equity) and $500–$600M a year of new equity through 2028. Every dollar of rejected rate relief widens that gap.
03
The rate freeze is conditional. DTE has said it will not file another electric rate request until at least 2028 — but only if the Oracle data center is online by the end of 2027 and approvals are received. The freeze is a political asset while it holds and a credibility cost if a construction delay breaks it.
04
Storms are a Michigan operating cost. An early-July 2026 storm hit nearly 400,000 customers and broke more than 600 poles. Storm restoration is where reliability spending gets judged in public — and where regulators form a view before the next request.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At $126.13 you pay 16.5x the midpoint of 2026 operating EPS guidance for a utility with a 3.69% yield, a $36.5B capital plan and 2.4 GW of signed data-center load — load that spreads fixed costs and that management says lifts growth "solidly to eight" percent once Google is approved. The regulatory news is known and in the price: the stock is 17% off its June month-end, every StockAnalysis target is above it (lowest $141), and the company says it has met or beaten guidance in 18 of the past 19 years. If Google is approved and another agreement lands by year-end, the market is left pricing a 6% grower that is guiding toward 8%+.

Bear Case

A utility grows as fast as its regulator lets it, and Michigan's is saying no more often: 42% of the last electric ask, 46% of the gas ask, a rejected $284.1M compressor station, a 9.8% gas ROE, and a commission asking the legislature to counter the "inherent bias" toward capital spending. DTE runs negative free cash flow (−$1.95B TTM), carries 2.29x debt-to-equity and needs $500–$600M of new stock a year; a lower allowed return on a bigger rate base is a worse deal for those new shares. Analyst targets are still falling, and they have not yet caught up with the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory lag and ROE drift. The pending $474.3M electric case at a requested 10.25% ROE is the next test. Another partial grant or an electric ROE below 9.9% would compress the earnings the capital plan is supposed to produce.
02
Data-center execution. The Oracle project must be online by the end of 2027 for the electric rate freeze to hold, and the Google contract still needs MPSC approval. The load beyond the signed 2.4 GW is pipeline, not contract.
03
Financing a cash-negative build. −$1.95B TTM free cash flow, $27.84B of debt and a ~15% FFO/debt target leave limited room. Rejected capital, storm costs or higher interest rates all show up as more equity or more debt.
Industry Commentary
Michigan's regulator leans against the capex model
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The MPSC's August 2026 recommendations are unusual because they come from the regulator itself. Chair Dan Scripps: "The most impactful opportunity may be to counter the inherent bias for utilities to invest in capital projects." The package — performance-linked multiyear rate plans, a smaller purchased-power bonus (about $868M collected by DTE and Consumers since 2019), required grid-utilisation improvements before new infrastructure, and competitive bidding for renewables — goes at the core of how a regulated utility grows earnings. The Citizens Utility Board's Michael Larson argued that cutting DTE's requested ROE to 8.91% would save customers roughly $200M a year. None of it is law yet; the September 10 gas order suggests the commission is already applying the spirit of it (my reading).

Data centers as the affordability argument
EMERGING TAILWIND

DTE's reply is that large new load makes everyone else's bill cheaper. Harris: data-center projects "will deliver meaningful affordability benefits for our existing customers, absorbing a significant portion of fixed system costs." Signed: Oracle 1.4 GW (approved, under construction) and Google 1.0 GW (awaiting approval), with ~2 GW in advanced discussions and 3–4 GW earlier in the pipeline. The conditional electric rate freeze is the political wrapper. If regulators accept the argument, data-center capital is the one kind of spending they are inclined to approve; if they do not, it is the largest capital commitment in the plan.
