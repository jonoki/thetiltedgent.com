01 Company Overview

Deutsche Telekom is Germany's former state phone monopoly, converted into a stock corporation at the start of 1995 and listed in 1996, and today one of the largest telecom groups in the world. It runs mobile and fixed-line networks in Germany and in a string of European countries, an IT-services arm (T-Systems), and — the part that dominates the numbers — a stake of about 55% in T-Mobile US, America's growth leader in wireless. In 2025 it booked €119.1B of net revenue and €44.2B of adjusted EBITDA AL; 78% of revenue came from outside Germany. In the first nine months of 2026 the US segment supplied 65.0% of segment revenue and 66.1% of segment EBITDA AL.

That ownership structure is the whole debate. Because Deutsche Telekom controls T-Mobile US, it consolidates 100% of the American carrier's revenue and cash flow, but roughly 45% of that business belongs to other shareholders. At the November 13 closes, Deutsche Telekom's stake of about 55% (the CEO's figure for September 30) was worth about $99.0B against a total Deutsche Telekom market value of $142.6B — about 69% of the parent's price is the US stake (our calculation from T-Mobile US's share count and both closes). So the stock moves with T-Mobile US: when the American shares fell 20.8% over the twelve months to November 13, Deutsche Telekom fell 2.5% on price (+1.2% with the dividend added). In 2026 a second question was added: press reports on April 22, citing two sources, said Deutsche Telekom was exploring a full merger with T-Mobile US through a new holding company, and on September 2 the activist fund Elliott was reported to have built a stake in Deutsche Telekom to oppose it. On October 29 Bloomberg News, citing people familiar with the matter, reported that Deutsche Telekom had not submitted a formal offer for T-Mobile US for now, partly because of opposition from its works council (the employee representatives), and that it could still make one later; the companies declined to comment. On the November 5 call the CEO said he does not comment on specific merger speculation and gave his test for any deal: it must create incremental value for shareholders — "Size alone is not a strategy."

The latest print (Q3 2026, reported November 5): net revenue €31.10B (+7.5% reported, +5.5% organic, meaning excluding currency swings and acquisitions or sales), adjusted EBITDA AL €12.21B (+9.9%; +8.0% organic), adjusted EPS €0.63 (+14.3%) and free cash flow AL €5.70B (+1.4%). Against Deutsche Telekom's own compiled analyst consensus for the quarter (the Q3 file on its investor-relations site) that was revenue 2.2% ahead, EBITDA AL 2.0%, adjusted net profit 4.6% and free cash flow 7.7% ahead (our calculation from the file's averages). Guidance for 2026 was raised for EBITDA AL (to around €47.6B from around €47.5B) and free cash flow AL (to around €20.1B from around €20.0B) and left at around €2.20 for adjusted EPS; the company said the increase reflects higher expectations at T-Mobile US. For 2027 the board plans to propose a €1.10 dividend (+10%) and a buyback of up to €2B; the average 2027 buyback in the consensus file was €3.2B across 10 estimates. The stock fell 2.1% on the day, close to close (€26.76 → €26.20), and 1.1% the next day; Reuters quoted an unnamed trader attributing the move to a dividend lower than expected and to renewed concerns about a full merger with T-Mobile US.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Control of T-Mobile US Spectrum licences Germany's mobile leader by service revenue Fixed-line and fibre infrastructure A− credit rating Multi-country scale in Europe Sovereign-cloud credentials

Telecom moats are built from licences and concrete. Spectrum is auctioned in finite blocks, networks take decades and tens of billions to build, and once a household is on a contract it rarely leaves. On top of that sits control of T-Mobile US, whose earnings growth management says is significantly outpacing its major US competitors, which supplies two-thirds of segment EBITDA AL and which pays growing dividends up to Bonn. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin. Deutsche Telekom's growth, valuation and now its strategic agenda all run through a company it does not wholly own, in a market where competition is getting harder, and through a possible merger that would reshape who owns the parent.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The US premium has been shrinking. T-Mobile US's share price fell 20.8% in the twelve months to November 13 ($214.57 → $169.95). Höttges acknowledged in his August 6 remarks that T-Mobile "has lost much of its former valuation premium to its U.S. peers" and that "the market now appears to be looking more closely at customer and volume growth." On November 5 Reuters reported he said the group is preparing for SpaceX entering US wireless and that investors are already pricing in lower long-term profits for the established carriers. Because the stake is about 69% of the parent's market value, a 10% move in T-Mobile US's shares changes the value of that stake by an amount equal to roughly 7% of Deutsche Telekom's market value.
02
Merger uncertainty. Press reports on April 22, 2026, citing two sources, said Deutsche Telekom was exploring a full merger with T-Mobile US via a new holding company; the stock fell 4.8% that day. Barclays wrote on July 1 that such a holding structure could come at the expense of Deutsche Telekom shareholders, and Bernstein estimated in September that buying out T-Mobile US's other shareholders at a 20% premium would dilute Deutsche Telekom holders by about 11%. On October 29 Bloomberg reported that Deutsche Telekom had not submitted a formal offer for now and could still make one later; the stock rose 1.9% that day and 1.8% the next. Deutsche Telekom and T-Mobile US declined to comment to Bloomberg.
03
German broadband is stagnating. Broadband lines fell by 20,000 in Q2 and by 49,000 across 2025 before edging up by 11,000 in Q3, while cable and alternative fibre builders compete for the same homes. Höttges told the press conference that fibre must be aligned "even more closely to demand, penetration, churn, wholesale utilization, and return on capital employed." Wholesale revenue fell 2.5% in Q3 after contractually agreed price increases expired.
04
Leadership turnover in the finance and technology seats. The technology board member left in March 2026, the CFO leaves in April 2027, and the product chief is departing at his own request. JPMorgan's Akhil Dattani wrote on September 2 that the CFO's exit came earlier than he expected and that changes at the top carry some risk given the team's record.

Notable One-Off Items

Two gaps matter when reading these figures. First, reported versus adjusted profit: in the first nine months of 2026 reported net profit fell 8.0% to €7.26B while adjusted net profit rose 10.2% to €8.39B (Q3 alone: reported +13.8%, adjusted +12.7%). At the half-year the CFO attributed the reported decline to restructuring and integration costs at T-Mobile US (mainly UScellular) and to the absence of 2025's book gains from reversing impairments on the GD Towers and Glasfaser+ stakes. Second, consolidation: revenue, EBITDA, free cash flow and net debt include 100% of T-Mobile US, whose own buybacks add to group net debt even though they raise Deutsche Telekom's stake. T-Mobile US also sold spectrum licences to Grain for about €2.5B in cash, which the CFO said, with strong free cash flow, brought net debt excluding leases down by €4B in Q3.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Relative performance

Against its home benchmark, Deutsche Telekom has done better over five years and worse over one. Over five years its price-plus-dividends return of about +79.7% beats the DAX performance index's +66.2%; over the last twelve months it returned about +1.2% (−2.5% on price) while the DAX gained +11.3%. The gap of the last year is almost entirely the subsidiary: T-Mobile US's shares fell 20.8% in dollars over the same twelve months (Nov 13, 2025 close $214.57 → $169.95). Deutsche Telekom held up far better than its main asset because about 31% of its value is the business outside the US — the part Höttges argues is undervalued: in August he said the implied EV/EBITDA multiple (enterprise value, the company's total worth including debt, divided by operating profit) of the business outside the US is "around 1-2 times lower" than for European peers.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 12.1x management's 2026 adjusted-EPS target for a group whose adjusted EPS grew 11.7% in the first nine months, that has met or beaten its guidance (FY2025 beat its own raised targets; the 2026 outlook raised for the third time this year, per Reuters) and that holds an A− rating with leverage of 2.55x, under its 2.75x ceiling. About 69% of the market value is a stake in T-Mobile US at a price that has fallen 20.8% in a year, so the rest of the group — Europe's 35 straight quarters of rising profits, the German mobile leader, T-Systems — is priced cheaply on top. Management is acting on that view with up to €5B of buybacks this year and a 3.76% dividend (a 10% rise to €1.10 is planned), and both a shelved merger (Elliott's ask; Bloomberg reported on October 29 that no formal offer has been made for now) and a recovery in T-Mobile US would lift the shares.

Bear Case

The stock moves largely with a US carrier whose valuation premium is fading: Höttges says US competition is getting fiercer, the market is looking harder at T-Mobile US's customer volumes, SpaceX is preparing to enter US wireless (Reuters, November 5), and Barclays and Deutsche Bank point to satellite competition. On top sits an unresolved ownership question — a possible all-share merger that Bernstein estimates could dilute holders by about 11%, a German state that owns 28.6%, an activist shareholder, and a finance and technology leadership in transition. At home, broadband lines were shrinking until Q3 (+11,000) and fibre penetration was 17.5% at June 30 after years of building. Reported EPS is falling (TTM €1.86) even as adjusted EPS rises, and €134.2B of net debt including leases leaves the group exposed to rates and the dollar. Bernstein's €28.10 target, barely above the price, is the bear view in numbers.

Top 3 Risks

01
A lower price for T-Mobile US. About 69% of Deutsche Telekom's market value is its stake. A further fall in T-Mobile US — on weaker account growth, tougher competition or satellite rivals — hits the parent directly; in June the two fell together, and Deutsche Telekom's sharpest September fall came the session after a 5.6% drop in T-Mobile US.
02
An unfavourable merger. A holding-company merger with T-Mobile US would require paying T-Mobile US's other shareholders, change the parent's shareholder base and dilute the German state's stake. Until management says what it will do, Barclays and Bernstein both flag the risk that the terms would favour T-Mobile US holders over Deutsche Telekom's.
03
German fixed-line erosion. Broadband lines that only stopped shrinking in Q3, a weaker wholesale business and slow fibre take-up cap growth in the segment that is 22.6% of segment EBITDA AL, while capital spending on fibre continues. The CEO's own goal for the unit is to "monetize more".
Industry Commentary
US wireless: growth leader under pressure
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

T-Mobile US still grows faster than its rivals — Q3 2026 service revenue +6.3% and adjusted EBITDA AL +11.4% — but the market has shifted from rewarding profit growth to questioning volumes. In his August 6 remarks Höttges said that in the US "competition is getting fiercer" and that the group does "not buy growth at any cost". On November 5 Reuters reported that Höttges said the group is preparing for SpaceX entering US wireless and that investors are already pricing in lower long-term profits for the established carriers. Deutsche Bank's Robert Grindle wrote in July that Starlink's satellite service and the Stargate AI project had dimmed Deutsche Telekom's standing among European telecoms. For a parent whose value is about 69% US stake, US competition is the industry trend that matters most.

European telecoms: consolidation and capital returns
ONGOING DEBATE

Europe has many national operators per market, and consolidation is the recurring investment theme: Berenberg's Nick Lyall looked for its winners on September 30 and named Deutsche Telekom among eight favourites, calling AI fears overdone. Deutsche Telekom's own path is different — it already owns its US growth asset, and the question is whether to fold it in or keep returning cash. Elliott's reported position puts that choice in front of shareholders. On the Q3 call Höttges said Deutsche Telekom is exploring a joint bid with three other operators for European satellite spectrum. Separately, EU member states proposed easing the deadline for replacing Huawei and ZTE equipment, according to a Reuters report Bernstein commented on September 30; per that report, Deutsche Telekom and Vodafone would benefit most.

Sovereign cloud and AI in Europe
EMERGING TAILWIND

European governments and companies want cloud and AI infrastructure run under European law. T-Systems is winning that work — Volkswagen's private cloud, the federal citizen app and a sovereign AI platform with SAP, and a sovereign cyber-defence service with Palo Alto Networks — and Höttges calls the unit "a building block of digital sovereignty in Europe." It is still small (3.5% of segment revenue, 0.9% of segment EBITDA AL) and order entry fell 13.4% in Q2 before rising 28.1% in Q3, so this is an option on the future rather than a driver today. At its first AI Investor Day on October 5, 2026 the company set out AI targets that include €2.5B of gross cost savings by 2030, according to the CEO on the Q3 call.
