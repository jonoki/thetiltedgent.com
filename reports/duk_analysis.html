01 Company Overview

Duke Energy is one of the largest regulated electric and gas utility holding companies in the United States, operating vertically integrated electric utilities across the Carolinas, Florida, Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky, plus a natural gas local distribution business under the Piedmont Natural Gas brand. Full-year 2025 total operating revenues were $32,237M, up +6.2% from $30,357M in 2024, with 2025 GAAP and adjusted EPS both landing at $6.31. Through the first nine months of 2026, revenues were $25,874M (versus $24,299M) and adjusted EPS was $5.35 (versus $4.81). At a $99.63B market capitalization (our calculation: $127.78 × 779.71M shares) Duke ranks #116 in the S&P 500, and it is the third-largest listed U.S. regulated electric utility behind NextEra ($182.38B) and Southern ($107.46B), both our calculation at the Nov 6 close.

The investment case is unusually simple to state and unusually hard to execute: Duke is carrying out what CEO Harry Sideris calls the “industry's largest regulated capital plan” — $103 billion for 2026–2030, an 18% increase over the prior $87B plan, which management expects to grow the earnings base from $114B in 2025 to $180B in 2030 (≈9.6% CAGR) and underwrite 5–7% annual adjusted EPS growth through 2030 off a 2025 base of $6.30. On the Q3 call CFO Brian Savoy said spending runs about $1.5 billion a month and will step up to about $2 billion a month in 2027, against $92.79B of existing debt. Paying for that plan is the central risk.

This is Duke Energy, full stop. The segment covers vertically integrated electric utilities in three geographic blocks: the Carolinas (Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress — the growth engine), Florida (Duke Energy Florida), and the Midwest (Duke Energy Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky). Segment income rose to $5,337M in FY2025 from $4,770M in FY2024 (+11.9%) — about 2.6× as fast as regulated electric revenue (+4.6%), which is what a utility earning a return on a fast-growing asset base looks like. In Q3 2026 the segment earned $1,817M versus $1,658M a year earlier (+$0.20 per share), which Duke attributed to recovery of infrastructure investment and favorable weather, partly offset by higher depreciation, interest expense and the minority investor's share of Duke Energy Florida's earnings — the costs that follow a $103B build.

The demand story is concentrated here. Duke has signed approximately 7.8 GW of electric service agreements (ESAs — binding contracts obligating a large customer to take and pay for capacity) with data-center customers, and a ~15.4 GW high-confidence pipeline leaves 7.6 GW still to convert; CFO Brian Savoy said Duke remains confident of converting the full pipeline to signed agreements by the first half of 2027, while CEO Harry Sideris said negotiations are taking longer because the contracts are complex. 5.2 GW of data centers are under construction, due to start drawing power in the second half of 2027 and into 2028 (Q3 call). Q3 electricity demand rose 2%, the strongest quarterly growth since 2022, and management's outlook calls for 3–4% load growth over the rest of the five-year plan — for context, U.S. utility load was essentially flat for the fifteen years before this cycle. To serve it, Duke's Q3 release says it is executing a record build to add 16 GW of generation capacity by 2031. On the Q3 call management said more than 5 GW of natural gas is under construction in the Carolinas and Indiana with another 5 GW in development, and that two combined-cycle gas plants totaling nearly 2,800 MW were pre-filed for the Davie-Davidson site in North Carolina, with the formal filing expected early next year. On the Q2 call it said 26 gas turbines are available under a GE Vernova framework agreement, about 300 MW of nuclear upgrades are under way at existing units, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission has approved license extensions for two plants with a Brunswick application due by year-end, Duke holds a license for two AP1000 reactors at its Lee site, and it has applied for an early site permit for small modular reactors (SMRs) at Belews Creek.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Regulated Franchise Monopoly $114B → $180B Earnings Base Multi-Year Rate Plans (NC / IN) 9.8% ROE in DEC Settlement Vertically Integrated Generation Nuclear Fleet + Licensed Sites Storm-Cost Securitization Turbine Supply Locked (GE Vernova)

Duke's moat is legal, not commercial. In each service territory it holds an exclusive franchise to distribute electricity, and in exchange a state commission sets rates designed to let it recover prudently incurred costs plus an authorized return on equity. That construct converts capital spending directly into earnings. Under the July 2026 Duke Energy Carolinas settlement with the North Carolina Public Staff, which the Commission has yet to approve, Duke would earn a 9.8% authorized ROE on a 53% equity capital structure, continue the multi-year rate plan (MYRP) framework that lets Duke file forward-looking rates for up to three years at once, and add an earnings-sharing mechanism permitting Duke to keep up to 0.5 percentage point above the allowed ROE, to 10.3%. Duke Energy Progress filed a comprehensive settlement with the Public Staff on August 5, 2026 and a hearing was held on August 11. The North Carolina attorney general declined to sign the Progress settlement, which would raise residential bills about 6.8% over two years, and had earlier declined to sign the Carolinas settlement, which would raise residential rates about 9.5% (WITN, Aug 13, 2026). On the Q3 call CEO Harry Sideris said he expects orders on both cases by mid-November, with rates effective in January 2027.

Layered on top are two genuinely differentiating assets in the current cycle. First, sites already in the licensing queue: Duke holds a license for two AP1000 reactors at the Lee site and has applied for an early site permit for SMRs at Belews Creek, so part of the slow licensing work on new nuclear is done, although management says it won't build without protections for customers and investors. Second, secured supply chain: 26 gas turbines are available under a GE Vernova framework agreement. Finally, Duke has repeatedly used securitization (bonds repaid from a dedicated charge on customer bills) to turn storm costs into AAA-rated debt — a $582.1M Duke Energy Carolinas issue in 2025 covering Hurricane Helene (2024), Hurricanes Ian and Zeta (2020) and Winter Storm Izzy (2022) — which spreads the cost over many years of bills.

Key Vulnerabilities

Regulatory lag and political affordability risk. Every dollar of the $103B plan must be found prudent by a commission before it earns a return, and rates are rising into an environment where residential affordability is already a live political issue in Indiana (where Duke is explicitly framing its next MYRP filing around affordability). A single adverse order — a lower authorized ROE, a disallowance, or a slower recovery schedule — compounds across a decade of spending.

Balance sheet fragility. Duke carries $92.79B of debt against $451M of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.61 and a current ratio of 0.68. Free cash flow over the last twelve months is −$5.16B (operating cash flow $11.55B less $16.71B of capex). CFO Brian Savoy said on the Q3 call that Duke is on track for a 14.5% FFO/Debt ratio in 2026 (cash flow as a share of debt, the measure credit-rating agencies watch); the long-term target is 15%, and Savoy said on the Q2 call that the target has “substantial cushion to our downgrade thresholds.”

Equity dilution as a permanent feature. Duke has committed to $10B of new common equity between 2027 and 2030 via at-the-market (ATM) and dividend reinvestment programs — roughly 10% of the current market cap. Separately, on August 13, 2026 it closed the sale of 40 million equity units at $50 each (about $2.0B): each unit pairs a contract to buy Duke stock for $50 no later than August 1, 2029 with a share of Duke notes, and pays 7.75% a year in total (8-K). Management has said no large block offerings are planned, but issuing equity at about 18× forward earnings to fund assets earning a 9.8% regulatory ROE adds to earnings per share only if the share price holds.

Concentration in the data-center bet. The load-growth thesis rests on ~15.4 GW of pipeline converting to signed contracts by mid-2027. If AI capex cools, or if hyperscalers relocate to cheaper or faster-to-power jurisdictions, Duke is left having committed capital to generation that must still be recovered from a residential and industrial base — the exact scenario that turns a growth story into a rate-shock story.

Notable One-Off Items

Brookfield's $6B investment in Duke Energy Florida (announced August 2025). Brookfield acquired a 19.7% non-controlling indirect equity interest in Duke Energy Florida for $6 billion, at what Duke described as a significant premium to its own public equity valuation. Funding is phased: $2.8B at first closing in early 2026, $200M by year-end 2026, $2B in 2027 and $1B in 2028. On the Q3 call, CFO Brian Savoy said Brookfield plans to pull $1B forward from 2028 to December 2026, with another $2B expected in 2027, which would reduce holding-company financing needs. Roughly $2B funds the capital plan and $4B displaces holding-company debt. The transaction raised Duke's FFO/Debt target by 1 percentage point to 15% and increased Duke Energy Florida's own five-year plan by $4B. Duke retains 80.3% ownership and operational control.

Sale of Piedmont Natural Gas Tennessee to Spire — $2.48B (announced July 29, 2025; completed March 31, 2026). ~205,000 customers, ~3,800 miles of pipeline and one LNG facility. About $800M offset Piedmont debt; about $1.5B of net proceeds replaced common stock Duke would otherwise have issued. The comparison with 2025 is distorted by the missing Tennessee business and will stay so through Q1 2027.

$2.0B equity units (priced August 10, closed August 13, 2026). Duke sold 40,000,000 equity units at $50 (including a 5,000,000-unit over-allotment). Each unit holds a stock purchase contract requiring the holder to buy Duke shares for $50 in cash no later than August 1, 2029, plus interests in 4.85% remarketable senior notes due 2032 and 2036; distributions total 7.75% a year (2.90% contract payments plus 4.85% note interest). Source: Duke Energy Form 8-K dated August 13, 2026, sec.gov.

Storm cost deferrals and securitization. Duke has used securitization repeatedly to remove storm costs from the balance sheet: a $582.1M Duke Energy Carolinas North Carolina storm recovery bond issued in 2025 (Aaa/AAA rated, two tranches maturing 2036 and 2048) covering Hurricane Helene, Hurricanes Ian and Zeta, and Winter Storm Izzy. Duke Energy Florida has separately flagged $1.1–1.3B of restoration costs from the 2024 hurricanes for recovery. These are cash-flow-timing items, not earnings items, but they materially affect FFO in the year they occur.

Coal ash remediation. Under a January 2021 North Carolina settlement, Duke shareholders absorbed more than $755M in coal ash cost write-offs ($485M in the 2019 retail rate case plus more than $270M in subsequent cases), delivering approximately $1.1B of customer savings through 2030. The shareholder charge is behind Duke, but ash basin closure spending continues to run through the capital plan.

Clean 2025, distorted 2024. FY2025 GAAP EPS and adjusted EPS were identical at $6.31, which is unusual and makes the year a clean comparison base. FY2024 GAAP EPS of $5.71 versus adjusted $5.90 reflects $0.19/share of special items.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

Duke is carrying out what management calls the “industry's largest regulated capital plan” — $103B for 2026–2030 — in territories where data-center demand is rising, with 7.8 GW of data-center load already under contract and 7.6 GW more expected to convert by mid-2027. Rate base compounds at ~9.6% to $180B by 2030, which mechanically supports 5–7% EPS growth that management says will run in the top half of that range from 2028. Investors get that growth at an 18.25× forward P/E — below the large-cap utility peer median — plus a 3.40% dividend with about 20 straight years of increases, and Brookfield has already validated the asset value by paying a premium for 19.7% of Duke Energy Florida and is pulling $1B of its payments forward.

▼ Bear Case

This is a $92.79B debt load with $451M of cash, a 1.61 debt-to-equity ratio, a 0.68 current ratio, and −$5.16B of free cash flow that gets worse as spending rises toward about $2B a month in 2027. Funding requires $10B of new equity by 2030 — roughly 10% dilution — plus $2.0B of equity units sold in August, issued to build assets earning a 9.8% regulatory ROE, which only works while the stock price holds. With a beta of 0.31 the stock has moved about a third as much as the market: it returned +3.05% over the past year against the S&P 500's +9.69% (price return), and ROIC of 3.95% shows how little economic return the capital actually generates. If the load pipeline disappoints or a commission balks at the rate path, there is no cushion.

Top 3 Risks
01
Financing risk — the plan outruns the balance sheet. Duke must fund $103B of capex against $92.79B of existing debt while holding FFO/Debt at 14.5% in 2026 and 15% long-term, which management says leaves substantial cushion to its downgrade thresholds. If storm costs, a rate-case delay, or higher borrowing costs erode that cushion, the choices are a bigger equity raise, a slower capital plan, or a downgrade — all three of which hit the stock.
02
Regulatory and affordability backlash. The 9.8% ROE / 53% equity settlement in North Carolina is constructive, but the Commission's orders on the Carolinas settlements are not yet in hand (expected by mid-November), the state attorney general declined to sign either settlement, and rates across six states are rising into a politically sensitive affordability environment — Duke is already framing its Indiana filing around affordability. A disallowance, a reduced authorized ROE, or a legislative challenge to a securitization financing order would compound over a decade of spending.
03
Interest rate sensitivity and load-pipeline concentration. With a 0.31 beta and a 3.40% yield, DUK can trade like a long-term bond: the 2022–2023 slump to $88.26 came as Treasury yields climbed. Layer on a growth story that depends on ~7.6 GW of still-unsigned data-center pipeline converting to binding contracts, and you have a stock exposed to both a rate shock and an AI capex pause, either of which alone would be sufficient to reset what investors will pay for it.
  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Load Growth in the Southeast
EMERGING TAILWIND
After roughly fifteen years of flat U.S. electricity demand, load is growing again, and Duke's territory is drawing data-center developers. Duke has 7.8 GW of signed electric service agreements with data-center customers, a ~15.4 GW high-confidence pipeline expected to convert by H1 2027, and 5.2 GW of data centers under construction in its territory. Q3 2026 demand rose 2%, the strongest quarter since 2022, and management's outlook calls for 3–4% load growth over the rest of the plan. In the first half of 2026 alone Duke secured $5B of economic development investment supporting more than 9,000 jobs. The strategic point: large-load customers arrive with long-term take-or-pay obligations, which is the highest-quality demand a regulated utility can add.
The Capex Supercycle — and How It Gets Paid For
ONGOING HEADWIND
Duke's $103B plan, which management calls the industry's largest regulated capital plan, is an 18% increase in a single year, with management flagging a further $5–10B of upside. Deployment runs at about $1.5 billion per month, rising to about $2 billion in 2027 (Q3 call). The funding stack is deliberately diversified: $10B of equity (2027–2030) via ATM and DRIP rather than block offerings, $2.0B of equity units sold in August 2026, $6B from Brookfield for 19.7% of Duke Energy Florida (of which $4B displaced holding-company debt, and $1B of the later payments is being pulled forward to December 2026), $2.48B from the Tennessee gas sale to Spire, a Department of Energy loan application, and possibly a Cayuga coal plant transaction in Indiana. The asset sales and Brookfield's payments happen once; the capital spending recurs.
Interest Rates and the Utility Multiple
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A utility can trade much like a bond. Duke's beta of 0.31 and 3.40% yield make it an income alternative to long-term Treasury bonds, and its $92.79B of debt means the cost of the capital plan rises and falls with those yields too. The 2022–2023 slump from $112.52 to $88.26 came as Treasury yields climbed. Bull framing: if long rates ease, DUK gains twice — the yield becomes relatively more attractive and the incremental cost of financing $103B falls, which improves the spread between the 9.8% authorized ROE and the cost of capital. The one-year lag (+3.05% versus the S&P 500's +9.69%, price returns) means there is some catch-up room, and at 18.25× forward earnings versus a ~20.0× peer median, the starting valuation is not high. Bear framing: if rates stay high or rise, Duke is issuing $10B of equity and rolling tens of billions of debt into a hostile market, a 3.95% return on invested capital looks thinner as borrowing costs rise, and a bond-like, low-volatility stock tends to lag when growth stocks lead the market.
