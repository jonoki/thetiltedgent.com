01 Company Overview

Duke Energy is one of the largest regulated electric and gas utility holding companies in the United States, operating vertically integrated electric utilities across the Carolinas, Florida, Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky, plus a natural gas local distribution business under the Piedmont Natural Gas brand. Full-year 2025 total operating revenues were $32,237M, up +6.2% from $30,357M in 2024, with 2025 GAAP and adjusted EPS both landing at $6.31. At a $96.37B market capitalization Duke ranks roughly #249 globally and #128 among U.S. companies, and it is the third-largest listed U.S. regulated electric utility behind NextEra ($179.78B) and Southern ($106.75B).

The investment case is unusually simple to state and unusually hard to execute: Duke has put the largest five-year capital plan on file at any U.S. regulated utility — $103 billion for 2026–2030, an 18% increase over the prior $87B plan, which management expects to grow the earnings base from $114B in 2025 to $180B in 2030 (≈9.6% CAGR) and underwrite 5–7% annual adjusted EPS growth through 2030 off a 2025 base of $6.30. The funding of that plan — roughly $1 billion of capital deployed every month against $92.21B of existing debt — is the central risk.

This is Duke Energy, full stop. The segment covers vertically integrated electric utilities in three geographic blocks: the Carolinas (Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress — the growth engine), Florida (Duke Energy Florida), and the Midwest (Duke Energy Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky). Segment income rose to $5,337M in FY2025 from $4,770M in FY2024 (+11.9%) — note that segment income grew nearly 3× faster than segment revenue, which is the signature of a utility earning a return on a rapidly growing rate base rather than on volume. In Q2 2026 the segment produced $1,271M reported / $1,310M adjusted income versus $1,194M a year earlier, with management attributing the gain to recovery of infrastructure investment, partially offset by higher depreciation and interest expense — the two costs that inevitably follow a $103B build.

The demand story is concentrated here. Duke has signed approximately 7.8 GW of electric service agreements (ESAs — binding contracts obligating a large customer to take and pay for capacity) with data-center customers, with a further ~15.4 GW high-confidence pipeline management expects to convert to ESAs by the first half of 2027 and 5.2 GW of data centers already under construction. That pipeline drove Duke to shift the Carolinas Resource Plan to its high load scenario. Enterprise load growth is guided to 1.5–2% in 2026, accelerating to 3–4% for 2027–2030 enterprise-wide and 4–5% in the Carolinas — for context, U.S. utility load was essentially flat for the fifteen years before this cycle. To serve it, Duke is adding ~15 GW of capacity by 2031: roughly 5 GW of natural gas combined-cycle/combustion turbine under construction plus 2.5 GW in development (26 turbines secured under a GE Vernova framework agreement, first unit delivered to the Person County site in July 2026), a ~4,500 MW battery storage portfolio, 300 MW of nuclear uprates (more than 1 GW of fleet uprates targeted through 2031), two approved subsequent license renewals with a Brunswick filing due by year-end, two AP1000 combined operating licenses held at the Lee site, and an early site permit application filed for small modular reactors (SMRs) at Belews Creek.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Regulated Franchise Monopoly $114B → $180B Earnings Base Multi-Year Rate Plans (NC / IN) 9.8% Authorized ROE (DEC) Vertically Integrated Generation Nuclear Fleet + Licensed Sites Storm-Cost Securitization Turbine Supply Locked (GE Vernova)

Duke's moat is legal, not commercial. In each service territory it holds an exclusive franchise to distribute electricity, and in exchange a state commission sets rates designed to let it recover prudently incurred costs plus an authorized return on equity. That construct converts capital spending directly into earnings: the July 2026 Duke Energy Carolinas settlement in North Carolina set a 9.8% authorized ROE on a 53% equity capital structure, continued the multi-year rate plan (MYRP) framework that lets Duke file forward-looking rates for up to three years at once, and added an earnings-sharing mechanism permitting Duke to keep up to 50 basis points above the allowed ROE, to 10.3%. Duke Energy Progress is negotiating toward a "substantially similar" framework, with a hearing held August 11, 2026 and a commission order expected by mid-November.

Layered on top are two genuinely differentiating assets in the current cycle. First, optionality on generation sites: Duke already holds two AP1000 combined operating licenses at the Lee site and has filed an early site permit for SMRs at Belews Creek — permitting, not construction, is the binding constraint on new nuclear, and Duke has already spent that time. Second, secured supply chain: 26 gas turbines are locked under a GE Vernova framework agreement at a moment when turbine lead times are the single biggest bottleneck to serving data-center load in the Southeast. Finally, Duke has repeatedly used securitization to convert storm costs into AAA-rated bonds — a $582.1M Duke Energy Carolinas issue in 2025 covering Hurricane Helene (2024), Hurricanes Ian and Zeta (2020) and Winter Storm Izzy (2022) saved North Carolina customers roughly $300M — which reduces both rate shock and regulatory friction.

Key Vulnerabilities

Regulatory lag and political affordability risk. Every dollar of the $103B plan must be found prudent by a commission before it earns a return, and rates are rising into an environment where residential affordability is already a live political issue in Indiana (where Duke is explicitly framing its next MYRP filing around affordability). A single adverse order — a lower authorized ROE, a disallowance, or a slower recovery schedule — compounds across a decade of spending.

Balance sheet fragility. Duke carries $92.21B of total debt against $673M of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.62 and a current ratio of 0.66. Free cash flow is −$4.27B (operating cash flow $11.56B less $15.84B of capex). Management targets 14.5% FFO/Debt in 2026 and 15% long-term, described as roughly 200 bps above Moody's downgrade threshold — a real but not enormous cushion given the spending trajectory.

Equity dilution as a permanent feature. Duke has committed to $10B of new common equity between 2027 and 2030 via at-the-market (ATM) and dividend reinvestment programs — roughly 10% of the current market cap. Management has said no large block offerings are planned, but issuing equity at ~18× forward earnings to fund assets earning a 9.8% regulatory ROE is only accretive if the multiple holds.

Concentration in the data-center bet. The load-growth thesis rests on ~15.4 GW of pipeline converting to signed contracts by mid-2027. If AI capex cools, or if hyperscalers relocate to cheaper or faster-to-power jurisdictions, Duke is left having committed capital to generation that must still be recovered from a residential and industrial base — the exact scenario that turns a growth story into a rate-shock story.

Notable One-Off Items

Brookfield's $6B investment in Duke Energy Florida (announced August 2025). Brookfield acquired a 19.7% non-controlling indirect equity interest in Duke Energy Florida for $6 billion, at what Duke described as a significant premium to its own public equity valuation. Funding is phased: $2.8B at first closing in early 2026, $200M by year-end 2026, $2B in 2027 and $1B in 2028. Roughly $2B funds the capital plan and $4B displaces holding-company debt. The transaction raised Duke's FFO/Debt target 100 bps to 15% and increased Duke Energy Florida's own five-year plan by $4B. Duke retains 80.3% ownership and operational control. Management has signalled additional DEF minority-interest proceeds still to come.

Sale of Piedmont Natural Gas Tennessee to Spire — $2.48B (announced July 29, 2025; expected close Q1 2026). ~205,000 customers, ~3,800 miles of pipeline and one LNG facility. About $800M offset Piedmont debt; ~$1.5B in net proceeds funded the capital plan. This is why Gas segment income was flat in Q2 2026 despite infrastructure recovery — the comparison is now distorted and will remain so through Q1 2027.

Storm cost deferrals and securitization. Duke has used securitization repeatedly to remove storm costs from the balance sheet: a $582.1M Duke Energy Carolinas North Carolina storm recovery bond issued in 2025 (Aaa/AAA rated, two tranches maturing 2036 and 2048) covering Hurricane Helene, Hurricanes Ian and Zeta, and Winter Storm Izzy, saving customers roughly $300M versus conventional recovery; a separate Duke Energy Progress South Carolina issue saved $35M. Duke Energy Florida has separately flagged hurricane restoration costs in the $1.1–1.3B range for recovery. These are cash-flow-timing items, not earnings items, but they materially affect FFO in the year they occur.

Coal ash remediation. Under a January 2021 North Carolina settlement, Duke shareholders absorbed more than $755M in coal ash cost write-offs ($485M in the 2019 retail rate case plus more than $270M in subsequent cases), delivering approximately $1.1B of customer savings through 2030. The shareholder charge is behind Duke, but ash basin closure spending continues to run through the capital plan.

Clean 2025, distorted 2024. FY2025 GAAP EPS and adjusted EPS were identical at $6.31 (a single −$0.01 discontinued-operations item), which is unusual and makes the year a clean comparison base. FY2024 GAAP EPS of $5.71 versus adjusted $5.90 reflects $0.19/share of special items. Note also that reported total revenue differs by presentation: Duke's 8-K shows FY2025 total operating revenues of $32,237M, while continuing-operations screens report $31,790M — a ~$447M gap attributable to discontinued-operations treatment of the Tennessee gas business. Verify the basis before comparing to any third-party figure.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold off hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation tool.

The five-year trajectory. DUK closed September 2021 at $97.59 and closed August 19, 2026 at $123.60 — a price gain of +26.7% over five years. Add roughly $20.60 per share of dividends paid over that span (quarterly payments rising from $0.985 to $1.085) and the total return lands somewhere in the high-40s percent range depending on reinvestment assumptions. That total-return figure is a calculation, not a sourced number — verify before relying on it. Compounded, that is a mid-single-digit annualized return: precisely what a regulated utility earning its authorized ROE and paying out ~65% of earnings should deliver, and precisely why the stock is owned for income rather than appreciation.

The shape of the path matters more than the endpoint. The stock spent 2022–2023 in a punishing de-rating as the 10-year Treasury yield rose, bottoming at a monthly close of $88.26 in September 2023 — a 21.6% drawdown from the May 2022 peak of $112.52. It then re-rated almost continuously from mid-2024 as the market reassessed utilities as AI-infrastructure plays, peaking at a monthly close of $130.94 in March 2026 after the $103B capital plan was unveiled. It has since given back 5.6% from that peak. The current $123.60 sits 8.1% below the 52-week high of $134.49. The 6-period monthly RSI has fallen to roughly 26 — an oversold reading. Only five other months in the five-year window printed below 30 — September and October 2022 (the rate shock), and May, June and October 2023 (the run into the $88.26 low) — and both of those episodes preceded durable bottoms. Treat that as a momentum observation, not a valuation signal.

Against its own sector the picture is far less bad: Duke's trailing P/E of 18.61 is essentially in line with the 18.13 weighted average for the 44-company Utilities — Regulated Electric group, and its 18.24 forward multiple is below the peer median of the five large-cap comparables (SO, NEE, D, AEP, ETR). In other words: the utility sector as a whole has lagged the index badly in a year when the index was driven by mega-cap technology, and DUK has performed roughly in line with its sector while trading at a slight discount to it. The dispersion within the sector has been meaningful — Entergy's market cap is up 26.6% year-over-year and Dominion's 15.6%, versus Duke's much flatter print — which suggests the market is differentiating on load-growth exposure per dollar of existing rate base, a metric on which Duke's sheer size works against it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

Duke has the largest capital plan of any U.S. regulated utility — $103B for 2026–2030 — pointed at the fastest-growing electricity demand region in the country, with 7.8 GW of data-center load already under contract and 15.4 GW more expected to convert by mid-2027. Rate base compounds at ~9.6% to $180B by 2030, which mechanically supports 5–7% EPS growth that management now says will run in the top half of that range from 2028. Investors get that growth at a 18.24× forward P/E — below the large-cap utility peer median — plus a 3.51% dividend with 20+ years of increases, and Brookfield has already validated the asset value by paying a premium for 19.7% of Duke Energy Florida.

▼ Bear Case

This is a $92.21B debt load with $673M of cash, a 1.62 debt-to-equity ratio, a 0.66 current ratio, and −$4.27B of free cash flow that gets worse as the plan ramps in 2027–2028. Funding requires $10B of new equity by 2030 — roughly 10% dilution — issued to build assets earning a 9.8% regulatory ROE, which only works while the multiple holds. With a beta of 0.37 the stock trades on the 10-year Treasury more than on its own results: it returned +1.76% over the past year against the S&P 500's +20.87%, and ROIC of 3.83% shows how little economic return the capital actually generates. If the load pipeline disappoints or a commission balks at the rate path, there is no cushion.

Top 3 Risks
01
Financing risk — the plan outruns the balance sheet. Duke must fund $103B of capex against $92.21B of existing debt while holding FFO/Debt at 14.5% in 2026 and 15% long-term, described as roughly 200 bps above Moody's downgrade threshold. If storm costs, a rate-case delay, or higher borrowing costs erode that cushion, the choices are a bigger equity raise, a slower capital plan, or a downgrade — all three of which hit the stock.
02
Regulatory and affordability backlash. The 9.8% ROE / 53% equity settlement in North Carolina is constructive, but the DEP order is not yet in hand and rates across six states are rising into a politically sensitive affordability environment — Duke is already framing its Indiana filing around affordability. A disallowance, a reduced authorized ROE, or a legislative challenge to a securitization financing order would compound over a decade of spending.
03
Interest rate sensitivity and load-pipeline concentration. With a 0.37 beta and a 3.51% yield, DUK is a duration asset — the 2022–2023 de-rating to $88.26 happened with nothing wrong at the company. Layer on a growth story that depends on ~15.4 GW of speculative data-center pipeline converting to binding contracts, and you have a stock exposed to both a rate shock and an AI capex pause, either of which alone would be sufficient to reset the multiple.
  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Load Growth in the Southeast
EMERGING TAILWIND
After roughly fifteen years of flat U.S. electricity demand, load is growing again — and the Southeast is the epicentre because it combines available land, comparatively cheap power, and constructive regulators. Duke has 7.8 GW of signed electric service agreements with data-center customers, a ~15.4 GW high-confidence pipeline expected to convert by H1 2027, and 5.2 GW of data centers already under construction in its territory. Guided enterprise load growth steps up from 1.5–2% in 2026 to 3–4% for 2027–2030, and 4–5% in the Carolinas. In the first half of 2026 alone Duke secured $5B of economic development investment supporting more than 9,000 jobs. The strategic point: large-load customers arrive with long-term take-or-pay obligations, which is the highest-quality demand a regulated utility can add.
The Capex Supercycle — and How It Gets Paid For
ONGOING HEADWIND
Duke's $103B plan is the largest of any U.S. regulated utility and represents an 18% increase in a single year, with management flagging a further $5–10B of upside from Indiana and Florida. Deployment runs at over $1 billion per month and accelerates in 2027–2028. The funding stack is deliberately diversified: $10B of equity (2027–2030) via ATM and DRIP rather than block offerings, $6B from Brookfield for 19.7% of Duke Energy Florida (of which $4B displaced holding-company debt), $2.48B from the Tennessee gas sale to Spire, a Department of Energy loan application, and possibly a Cayuga coal plant transaction in Indiana. That structure is smart — but it is also a tacit admission that the plan cannot be financed conventionally. Every one of these levers is a one-time source; the capex is recurring.
Interest Rates and the Utility Multiple
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A utility is a bond with an equity option attached. Duke's beta of 0.37 and 3.51% yield mean the stock is priced off the long end of the Treasury curve, and its $92.21B debt means the cost of the capital plan is priced there too. The 2022–2023 de-rating from $112.52 to $88.26 was a pure discount-rate event. Bull framing: if long rates ease, DUK re-rates twice — the yield becomes relatively more attractive and the incremental cost of financing $103B falls, which improves the spread between the 9.8% authorized ROE and the cost of capital. The one-year underperformance (+1.76% versus the S&P 500's +20.87%) means there is real catch-up room, and at 18.24× forward earnings versus a ~20.1× peer median, the entry is not demanding. Bear framing: if rates stay high or rise, Duke is issuing $10B of equity and rolling tens of billions of debt into a hostile market, ROIC of 3.83% compresses against a rising cost of capital, and a low-beta bond proxy simply does not participate in an equity market led by growth. Rate direction, not operations, will determine the next 20% in either direction.
