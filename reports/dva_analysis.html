01 Company Overview

A patient with failed kidneys comes in for dialysis three times a week, for the rest of their life or until a transplant. DaVita runs the chairs. At June 30, 2026 it treated about 298,500 patients at 3,266 outpatient centers — 2,671 in the United States and 595 across 14 other countries — and delivered an average of 92,649 US treatments a day. Wikipedia, citing the company's annual report, puts DaVita at roughly 36% of all US dialysis patients. FY2025 revenue was $13.64B, up 6.46%; GAAP EPS was $9.84 and adjusted EPS $10.78.

The economics are a two-tier table. Per Wikipedia's summary of the 2025 annual report, 68% of revenue came from Medicare and other government programs, which cover 89% of patients — and the commercial insurers who pay the other 32% of revenue generate nearly all of the profit. Every serious question about DaVita is a question about that small, rich slice of patients: how many there are, and what their insurers will pay. That is why a company with 78,000 employees and a steady, repetitive service can move 17% in a day, as it did on August 5, 2026, when Q2 revenue per treatment slipped on patients falling out of Affordable Care Act plans.

The other half of the story is the share count. DaVita pays no dividend and instead buys back stock relentlessly: total common shares fell from 97.29M at the end of 2021 to 63.96M at June 30, 2026 (−34%). Berkshire Hathaway owns about 45% of what is left — and because an April 2024 agreement caps it there, Berkshire sells shares back to DaVita every quarter. Over five years the stock is up 57% while net income is lower than in 2021. The chips didn't grow; the table got smaller.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: ~36% of US patients 3,266-center footprint Nephrologist referral relationships Commercial payer contracts Purchasing power on drugs & supplies Anchor holder: Berkshire 45%

The moat is density. A dialysis patient needs a chair near home three times a week, and DaVita alone serves about 36% of US dialysis patients (per Wikipedia), with Fresenius Medical Care its main listed rival. Scale buys better terms from suppliers and better bargaining power with commercial insurers — which is exactly why regulators keep looking. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: a business built on the stock of existing kidney-failure patients is exposed to anything that shrinks that stock — slower progression of kidney disease, fewer commercially insured patients, or payers who squeeze the rate.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Profit sits in the commercial minority. Commercial payers are 32% of revenue and nearly all the profit (per Wikipedia's summary of the 2025 annual report). Every patient who moves from a commercial plan to Medicare or Medicaid — through subsidy changes, job loss or policy — lowers the average rate. Q2 2026 showed exactly that mechanism in revenue per treatment.
02
Drugs that slow kidney disease. In October 2023 Novo Nordisk stopped its FLOW trial of semaglutide early for efficacy in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (BioSpace, Oct 13, 2023); DVA's October 2023 month-end close fell 18.3%. DaVita argued that fewer than 10% of current CKD patients would meet the trial's inclusion criteria. It is a slow-moving risk to patient volume, not a quarterly one.
03
Antitrust and legal history. Per Wikipedia, the FTC began investigating DaVita and Fresenius in 2024 over tactics toward smaller competitors, a union benefits fund filed a proposed class action against both in May 2025, and the company has paid $350M (2014), $450M (2015) and $270M (2018) to settle federal claims. Concentration is the moat and the regulatory target.
04
Leverage behind the buybacks. Total debt, including leases, was $13.41B against $689M of cash at June 30, 2026, and common equity attributable to DaVita was negative $765M. The company borrowed an extra $500M Term Loan B-2 tranche in June 2026. Interest coverage is 3.65x per StockAnalysis. This is fine while cash flow holds and costly if it doesn't.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At $182.34 you pay 12.3x this year's consensus adjusted EPS of $14.85 for a provider serving about 36% of US dialysis patients, with an 11% free-cash-flow yield on the company's own definition and a buyback that shrank the share count 14.73% in a year. Management raised guidance in May and reaffirmed it in August; Q2 EPS of $4.02 beat the $3.88 estimate. The ACA headwind is sized at $40M this year against $2.15–2.25B of guided adjusted operating income. The August sell-off priced a mix scare, not a broken business, and the panel's average target of $219.86 is 20.6% above the close.

Bear Case

Take the buybacks away and there is little growth: net income is below 2021 and treatment volume grew 0.3%. The profit hangs on a commercial minority of patients that policy is shrinking, the balance sheet carries $13.41B of debt and negative common equity, and the per-share engine needs borrowing to keep running. GLP-1 drugs and value-based care both aim to keep people off dialysis. The one Sell on the panel and a 13.78% short interest against the float say part of the market expects the August mix shift to repeat.

Top 3 Risks

01
Commercial payer mix. Nearly all profit comes from the commercial 32% of revenue. Subsidy expiry, insurer rate pressure or policy change that moves patients off commercial plans lowers revenue per treatment directly — as the $1.72 Q2 slip and the 17.24% one-day fall showed.
02
Leverage in service of buybacks. $13.41B total debt, 3.79x debt/EBITDA, 3.65x interest coverage and −$765M common equity. The per-share story depends on continued borrowing capacity and stable cash flow; an interest-rate or reimbursement shock hits both at once.
03
Patient pool and regulatory risk. Drugs that slow kidney disease (the October 2023 FLOW trial reaction) and antitrust scrutiny of the DaVita–Fresenius duopoly (the 2024 FTC probe, the May 2025 class action) are slow-moving threats to both volume and pricing.
Industry Commentary
The commercial-patient cliff
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Dialysis reimbursement is a cross-subsidy. Per Wikipedia's summary of the annual report, government programs cover 89% of patients and 68% of revenue, while commercial plans supply 32% of revenue and nearly all the profit. DaVita's commercial patients are a minority but carry the company. Anything that moves a patient from commercial to government coverage — the end of pandemic-era ACA subsidies in this case — shows up as lower revenue per treatment within a quarter. Q2 2026 was the first visible print of that effect, and the market's 17% one-day reaction says it cares about direction more than size.

Fewer patients, by design
ONGOING HEADWIND

The medical system is trying to reduce the number of people who need dialysis: GLP-1 drugs aimed at kidney outcomes, value-based programmes that pay providers to delay kidney failure, and improved care for chronic kidney disease. DaVita is hedging by joining in — IKC's 64,900 risk-based patients and the Humana deal are bets on getting paid to keep patients off the chair. For now, volume is flat rather than falling: non-acquired treatments +0.3%, with StockAnalysis's call summaries citing improved mortality and competitor clinic closures as support.

The shrinking-table math
EMERGING TAILWIND

DaVita works like a slow-motion buyout. With ~45% held by a holder that agrees to be bought down to stay at 45%, and a float of just 33.93M shares, each dollar of buyback retires a larger share of the tradable stock than the headline count suggests. The bull framing: at an 11–14% free-cash-flow yield (company vs StockAnalysis definitions), retiring shares compounds per-share value even with flat profits. The bear framing: it is financed partly with debt against a patient base under pressure, and a smaller float makes the stock move harder both ways — see August. The adjudicator is revenue per treatment and treatment growth over the next four quarters, both of which the company reports.
