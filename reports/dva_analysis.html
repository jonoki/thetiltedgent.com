01 Company Overview

A patient with failed kidneys comes in for dialysis three times a week, for the rest of their life or until a transplant. DaVita runs the chairs. At September 30, 2026 it treated about 298,100 patients at 3,269 outpatient centers — 2,671 in the United States and 598 across 14 other countries — and delivered an average of 92,780 US treatments a day in the third quarter. Wikipedia, citing the company's annual report, puts DaVita at roughly 36% of all US dialysis patients. FY2025 revenue was $13.64B, up 6.46%; GAAP EPS was $9.84 and adjusted EPS $10.78.

The economics are a two-tier table. Per its 2025 annual report, 68% of US dialysis patient revenue came from Medicare and other government programs, which cover 89% of US patients — and the commercial insurers who pay the other 32% generate nearly all of the profit. Every serious question about DaVita is a question about that small, rich slice of patients: how many there are, and what their insurers will pay. That is why a company with 78,000 employees and a steady, repetitive service can move 17% in a day, as it did on August 5, 2026, when Q2 revenue per treatment slipped, in part because patients were leaving Affordable Care Act plans. The October 29 report was calmer: revenue per treatment edged down another $0.62, and the company raised the bottom of its 2026 profit guidance.

The other half of the story is the share count. DaVita pays no dividend and instead buys back stock relentlessly: total common shares fell from 97.29M at the end of 2021 to 63.40M at September 30, 2026 (−35%). Berkshire Hathaway owns about 45% of what is left — and because an April 2024 agreement caps it there, Berkshire sells shares back to DaVita every quarter. Over five years the stock is up 85% while trailing twelve-month net income ($920M) is still below 2021's ($978M). The chips didn't grow; the table got smaller.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: ~36% of US patients 3,269-center footprint Nephrologist referral relationships Commercial payer contracts Purchasing power on drugs & supplies Anchor holder: Berkshire 45%

The moat is density. A dialysis patient needs a chair near home three times a week, and DaVita alone serves about 36% of US dialysis patients (per Wikipedia), with Fresenius Medical Care its main listed rival. Scale buys better terms from suppliers and better bargaining power with commercial insurers — which is exactly why regulators keep looking. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: a business built on the stock of existing kidney-failure patients is exposed to anything that shrinks that stock — slower progression of kidney disease, fewer commercially insured patients, or payers who squeeze the rate.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Profit sits in the commercial minority. Commercial payers are 32% of US dialysis patient revenue and, in DaVita's words, "generate nearly all of our profits" (2025 annual report). Every patient who moves from a commercial plan to Medicare or Medicaid — through subsidy changes, job loss or policy — lowers the average rate. Q2 2026 showed exactly that mechanism in revenue per treatment, and Q3 slipped a further $0.62.
02
Drugs that slow kidney disease. In October 2023 Novo Nordisk stopped its FLOW trial of semaglutide early for efficacy in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (BioSpace, Oct 13, 2023); DVA's October 2023 month-end close fell 18.3%. It is a slow-moving risk to patient volume, not a quarterly one.
03
Antitrust and legal history. Per Wikipedia, the FTC began investigating DaVita and Fresenius in 2024 over tactics toward smaller competitors, a union benefits fund filed a proposed class action against both in May 2025, and the company has paid $350M (2014), $450M (2015) and $270M (2018) to settle federal claims. Concentration is the moat and the regulatory target.
04
Leverage behind the buybacks. Total debt, including leases, was $13.39B against $496M of cash at September 30, 2026 (StockAnalysis). The company's own debt total, which leaves leases out, was $10.83B: a 3.38x leverage ratio (debt against yearly cash earnings, as its credit agreement measures it) against a 5.00x maximum (Q3 release). Common equity attributable to DaVita was negative $591M. Its main term loan was enlarged by $500M in June 2026, partly to pay down its revolving credit line, which still had $80M outstanding at quarter-end (10-Q). Operating profit covers its interest bill 3.72 times over (StockAnalysis). This is fine while cash flow holds and costly if it doesn't: Q3 free cash flow was $276M against $604M a year earlier (company definition).
05
Federal kidney-care policy. On October 20, 2026 HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced a Kidney Care National Plan with three priorities: preventing kidney failure, improving dialysis care while expanding patient choice, and reforming the organ transplant system (as reported by IBTimes). The same article lists lower barriers for new providers, more home-based care and more competition as possible effects on DaVita. DVA closed down 6.23% that day, $187.00 to $175.35 (our calculation from daily closes). DaVita's statement that day said it shares the administration's urgency, adding: "Where we differ is the suggestion that kidney care has lacked innovation or that scale stands in its way." On the October 29 call the CEO said many elements of the plan will need policy development and possibly action by Congress.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported figures. First, the share count: 0.6 million shares were repurchased in Q3 2026 for $111M at an average $186.21, 2.2 million in Q2 for $348M at $154.95 and 3.0 million in Q1 at $133.70, with another 0.5 million bought at $180.57 between October 1 and 27. Per-share growth therefore runs well ahead of profit growth. Second, the China impairment: Q3 GAAP diluted EPS of $3.40 compares with adjusted EPS of $3.82, because a $62M charge (a net $27M to DaVita after minority partners' share and tax, $0.41 a share in the release's reconciliation) is added back in the adjusted figure; nine-month GAAP EPS is $10.27 against $10.67 adjusted. Third, 2025 carried one-off costs, including the April 2025 ransomware attack the Motley Fool cites: nine-month 2025 adjusted EPS was $7.44 against GAAP $6.62, so the easy year-over-year comparisons flatter 2026 growth rates.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $103.24 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $191.35, DVA gained 85.3% on price, with no dividends to add. Now look at the company's value instead of the share price: at the December 2021 close, $113.76 × 97.29M shares was about $11.07B; today, $191.35 × 62.92M shares is $12.04B (both our calculations). The market value of the business rose less than 9% in the time the share price rose by two-thirds. Almost all of the shareholder return came from the share count shrinking by about a third — paid for with cash flow and debt.

The ride was anything but smooth. The 6-period monthly RSI hit 88.3 at the July 2026 peak — the most overbought reading in the series — after running above 80 in May and June, then fell back to about 50, neutral, after the August collapse and stood at 57.2 at the November 6 close. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At $191.35 you pay 13.0x this year's consensus adjusted EPS of $14.68 for a provider serving about 36% of US dialysis patients, with a 10.7% free-cash-flow yield on the StockAnalysis definition and a buyback that retired 5.8 million shares ($863M) in the first nine months of 2026. Management raised guidance in May, reaffirmed it in August and lifted the bottom of its ranges on October 29; Q3 adjusted EPS of $3.82 beat the $3.78 consensus on the Nasdaq calendar. The ACA headwind is sized at $40M this year against $2.175–2.25B of guided adjusted operating income. The August sell-off was a scare over the patient mix, not a broken business, and the panel's average target of $222.29 is 16.2% above the close.

Bear Case

Take the buybacks away and there is little growth: trailing net income is below 2021 and treatment growth excluding acquisitions was 0.4% in Q3. The profit hangs on a commercial minority of patients that policy is shrinking, the balance sheet carries $13.39B of debt including leases and negative common equity, and the buybacks behind the per-share gains need borrowing to keep running. Q3 free cash flow fell to $276M from $604M, and International posted a loss after the China charge. GLP-1 drugs and value-based care both aim to keep people off dialysis, and the October 20 HHS plan points toward more competition. Two Sells on the panel and a 14.67% short interest against the float (October 15 settlement) say part of the market expects the mix shift to repeat.

Top 3 Risks

01
Commercial payer mix. Nearly all profit comes from the commercial 32% of US dialysis patient revenue. Subsidy expiry, insurer rate pressure or policy change that moves patients off commercial plans lowers revenue per treatment directly — as the $1.72 Q2 slip and the 17.24% one-day fall showed.
02
Leverage in service of buybacks. $13.39B total debt including leases (3.80x debt/EBITDA excluding leases), 3.72x interest coverage and −$591M common equity. The per-share story depends on continued borrowing capacity and stable cash flow; an interest-rate or reimbursement shock hits both at once.
03
Patient pool and regulatory risk. Drugs that slow kidney disease (the October 2023 FLOW trial reaction) and antitrust scrutiny of the DaVita–Fresenius duopoly (the 2024 FTC probe, the May 2025 class action) are slow-moving threats to both volume and pricing.
Industry Commentary
The commercial-patient cliff
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Dialysis reimbursement is a cross-subsidy. Per DaVita's 2025 annual report, government programs cover 89% of its US dialysis patients and 68% of that revenue, while commercial plans supply 32% of the revenue and nearly all the profit. DaVita's commercial patients are a minority but carry the company. Anything that moves a patient from commercial to government coverage — the end of pandemic-era ACA subsidies in this case — shows up as lower revenue per treatment within a quarter. Q2 2026 was the first quarter to show that effect, and the stock's 17% one-day fall says investors care about direction more than size.

Fewer patients, by design
ONGOING HEADWIND

The medical system is trying to reduce the number of people who need dialysis: GLP-1 drugs aimed at kidney outcomes, value-based programmes that pay providers to delay kidney failure, and improved care for chronic kidney disease. DaVita is hedging by joining in — IKC's 80,800 risk-based patients and the Humana deal are bets on getting paid to keep patients off the chair. For now, volume is flat rather than falling: treatments excluding acquisitions +0.4%. On the October 29 call the CFO said patient counts rose mainly because mortality improved, and that the benefit from Fresenius closing clinics played out in Q1.

The shrinking-table math
EMERGING TAILWIND

DaVita works like a slow-motion buyout. With ~45% held by a holder that agrees to be bought down to stay at 45%, and just 33.45M shares left for everyone else to trade (the float), each dollar of buyback retires a larger share of the tradable stock than the headline count suggests. The bull framing: at a 10.7% free-cash-flow yield (StockAnalysis definition), retiring shares raises each remaining share's slice of the profit even if profits stay flat. The bear framing: it is financed partly with debt against a patient base under pressure, and a smaller float makes the stock move harder both ways — see August. The test is revenue per treatment and treatment growth over the next four quarters, both of which the company reports.
