01 Company Overview

DexCom makes continuous glucose monitors (CGM — a small wearable sensor that reads glucose in the fluid just under the skin every few minutes and streams it to a phone or an insulin pump, replacing fingerstick blood tests). Its flagship is the G7 platform; alongside it sit Stelo, an over-the-counter biosensor sold without a prescription, and Dexcom Smart Basal for people on long-acting insulin only. FY2025 revenue was $4.662B, up 15.6% from $4.033B, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $4.969B, up 15.5%. The company ranks roughly #749 globally by market capitalisation at $33.17B, and a third-party industry estimate puts it at 33.9% of the 2025 CGM market against Abbott's 52.8%.

This was, for a decade, one of the great compounders in medical devices — and then it broke. In July 2024 the stock fell 40% in a single month (from a $113.38 June close to $67.82) when US revenue growth disappointed and the multi-year growth narrative reset. Two years on, the debate has narrowed to a specific question: is DexCom losing share to Abbott's FreeStyle Libre in the insulin-using core, or is the whole category expanding fast enough — via Stelo, via basal and type 2 reimbursement, via GLP-1 users buying a sensor to watch what food does to them — that both companies grow? The numbers on this page argue that the second thing is happening while the first thing is also true: DexCom is growing 11–16% into a category growing faster than that, and it is winning the argument on margin rather than on share.

This is where the 2024 break happened and where the recovery has to be proved. US revenue grew 15% in FY2025 but only 11% in both Q4 2025 ($891.5M) and Q2 2026 ($933.4M) — a deceleration in the largest and highest-margin geography, at a time when total company revenue is still compounding in the low teens. Stelo is sold without a prescription and without reimbursement — cash, direct to the consumer. It matters for two reasons that have nothing to do with its current revenue. First, it was DexCom's first 15-day product, the proving ground for the sensor chemistry now migrating into the prescription G7 line. Second, it is the answer to the reimbursement gap: management positions it as "an immediate option" for type 2 non-insulin patients who have no coverage today, while it waits on a CMS decision expected mid-2027. In Q2 2026 the company launched a fully reimagined Stelo app with AI-driven insights and macronutrient logging, and received FDA clearance to market Stelo for children. This is also the natural landing zone for GLP-1 users — people on weight-loss and diabetes drugs who want to see what specific foods do to their glucose. DexCom discloses no Stelo revenue, no unit volume and no user count, so the size of this business is currently unknowable from public filings, and nothing here is estimated.

Watch item: the CONNECT trial read out a 0.9% A1c improvement versus routine care in type 2 non-insulin patients, with 97% median CGM usage. That is the clinical evidence base for the mid-2027 CMS decision — the single largest binary on the whole thesis.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
CLINICAL ACCURACY DATA AID PUMP INTEGRATIONS CONSUMABLE SUBSCRIPTION MODEL PBM & MEDICARE COVERAGE FDA CLEARANCE STACK PRESCRIBER RELATIONSHIPS OWN-MANUFACTURING SCALE

The core advantage is that DexCom does not sell a device — it sells a subscription to a consumable. A sensor lasts 10 days, or now 15, and then the patient buys another one, forever. That converts a one-time hardware sale into an annuity with roughly 24–36 reorders a year, which is why a business with $4.97B of revenue throws off $1.41B of free cash flow and a 44.5% ROIC. The switching cost that protects the annuity is not the sensor itself but everything wrapped around it: a prescription, a PBM coverage decision, a pump that only speaks to one sensor, and a patient who has learned to read one particular app. That is a real moat — and it is narrower than it was in 2021, because Abbott has now cleared most of the same regulatory and reimbursement hurdles at a lower price point.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Abbott is bigger in CGM and can subsidise from a $46.6B revenue base. A third-party estimate puts Abbott at 52.83% of the 2025 CGM market against DexCom's 33.89% — Abbott is roughly 1.6× DexCom's size in DexCom's only business, while CGM is a modest slice of Abbott's total. Abbott can price Libre aggressively in a tender or a PBM negotiation and absorb it; DexCom cannot, because CGM is 100% of what it sells. The 11% US growth rate is where that asymmetry shows up.
02
Quality-system regulation, and an open FDA warning letter. The FDA issued DexCom a warning letter dated March 4, 2025 following 2024 inspections of the San Diego and Mesa facilities, citing deficiencies in manufacturing processes and quality management systems and in the company's responses to prior observations. Critically, it does not restrict manufacture, marketing or distribution and required no recall, and the company has been submitting corrective actions since. No public close-out of the letter was verified from the sources fetched — so as of this snapshot it should be treated as open. Comparable remediation programmes at other device makers have run in the mid-eight figures annually.
03
Single-product concentration with a reimbursement single point of failure. Every dollar of revenue depends on someone else agreeing to pay for a glucose sensor. The largest growth pool — 7M+ US type 2 non-insulin patients — is gated on a CMS decision not expected until mid-2027. Between now and then, that cohort can only be reached through cash-pay Stelo, at a much lower revenue per patient.
04
Gross margin is at a cyclical peak by management's own admission. The 400 basis point year-over-year margin expansion in Q2 2026 came from manufacturing efficiencies and the G7 15-day transition. Management has said margin peaks in Q3 2026 and then steps down as the Ireland factory begins production. Anyone extrapolating the Q2 margin trend into 2027 is extrapolating against explicit guidance.

Notable one-off items — and the gross margin story in full. Gross margin is the most important line in this report, so here is the whole arc with the causes attached. GAAP gross margin ran 68.63% in FY2021, then fell every single year: 64.72% (FY2022) → 63.19% (FY2023) → 60.46% (FY2024) → 60.10% (FY2025) — an 850 basis point decline over four years. The company's stated explanation across FY2025 was that "significantly expanded access" and operating leverage were partially offset by manufacturing investments; on a non-GAAP basis FY2025 gross margin actually fell to 60.8% from 61.8%. In plain terms: DexCom was building capacity (Malaysia, then Ireland) ahead of the volume, selling into lower-priced pharmacy and international channels, and carrying the cost of running two sensor platforms at once. The reversal since has been violent and is largely a product-cycle effect: 56.85% (Q1'25) → 59.53% → 60.48% → 62.93% → 62.95% → 63.44% (Q2'26), non-GAAP 64.1% versus 60.1% a year earlier, a 400 basis point improvement management attributes to manufacturing efficiencies and the G7 15-day system transition. The 15-day sensor is the key: one sensor covering 15 days instead of 10 means roughly a third fewer sensors manufactured, shipped and disposed of per patient-year at a similar revenue per patient-year. That is a pure cost-of-goods win. It is also finite — and management has told investors it peaks in Q3 2026 and steps down as Ireland comes online. There were no large non-recurring charges disclosed in the fetched Q2 2026 or FY2025 releases; GAAP and non-GAAP EPS were identical at $2.09 for FY2025, which is unusually clean. The FY2026 non-GAAP guide is ~64% gross margin, 23.5–24% operating margin, 31.5–32% adjusted EBITDA margin.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $155.80 (split-adjusted) to the September 4, 2026 close of $87.90, DXCM has returned −43.6% over the window — roughly −11% a year, with no dividend. That is a brutal number for a company whose revenue grew from $2.45B (FY2021) to $4.97B TTM over the same period, more than doubling, while GAAP EPS went from $0.53 to $2.54 — nearly a five-fold increase. The entire loss is multiple compression. A business earning $0.53 was worth $155.80 (294× trailing); the same business earning $2.54 is worth $87.90 (34.7×). Nothing about that path is an earnings story; it is a repricing of what a 15%-grower in a duopoly is worth versus what a 25%-grower in a monopoly narrative was worth.

The violence is concentrated in two months. July 2024: −40.2% in a single month, from a $113.38 June close to $67.82, when US revenue growth disappointed and FY2024 growth landed at 11.3% against 24.5% the prior year. The specific causes cited at the time — a salesforce restructuring and durable-medical-equipment channel rebate dynamics — could not be re-verified from the sources fetched for this report, so they are named here as the market's contemporaneous account rather than as confirmed fact. What is verified is the growth deceleration and the price move. March 2025: −22.7%, from $88.37 to $68.29, the month the FDA warning letter was issued. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The stack, in order. Start with patients. DexCom does not disclose absolute new patient adds, but it said Q2 2026 starts were in line with the record set in Q1, with a sequential uptick in the US, broad-based across type 1, type 2 insulin-intensive, basal and non-insulin cohorts. Multiply patients by revenue per patient and you get revenue: $1,308M in Q2 2026, +13.1%, split US $933.4M (+11%) and International $375.0M (+19% reported, +16% organic). Apply gross margin — 63.44% GAAP, 64.1% non-GAAP, up 400 basis points — and you get gross profit growing far faster than revenue. Apply operating leverage and you get operating margin of 24.3% GAAP against 18.4% a year earlier, +590 basis points. The result is net income of $249.1M and GAAP EPS of $0.64, up 42% on 13% revenue growth. That is a textbook operating-leverage quarter.

And here is the part that decides the next two years: almost all of it is a product cycle, and management has dated its end. The G7 15 Day sensor replaces a 10-day consumable with a 15-day one. Same patient, same approximate revenue per patient-year, roughly a third fewer sensors manufactured and shipped. That is why gross margin went from 56.85% in Q1 2025 to 63.44% in Q2 2026 while revenue growth decelerated. The company targets converting nearly 50% of the US customer base by year-end 2026 — and separately, its own CFO has told investors margin peaks in Q3 2026 and then steps down as the Ireland factory begins production. So the mechanism driving the 51% rally off the October 2025 low is explicitly guided to stop working within one quarter. After that, earnings growth has to come from revenue growth, and revenue growth is guided to 11–13%.

Thread 1 — is DexCom losing to Abbott, or is the category expanding? Both, and the ratio matters. A third-party estimate puts 2025 CGM share at Abbott 52.83%, DexCom 33.89%, Medtronic 10.10% — about 97% of the market in three hands. The same source sizes the market at $15.77B in 2026 growing at a 15.1% CAGR. DexCom's guided FY2026 growth of 11–13% is below that category rate, which is the arithmetic definition of share loss. But note where it is losing: US +11%, international +19%. The pressure is domestic and it is concentrated in the segment where Abbott competes hardest on price. Meanwhile the category expansion is real and it is arriving through channels that barely existed three years ago — Stelo sold over the counter for cash, basal-only users via Dexcom Smart Basal, and 7M+ type 2 non-insulin patients now covered by the four largest PBMs. The honest framing: DexCom is trading a shrinking share of a fast-growing pie for a much better margin on the slice it keeps.

Thread 2 — the regulatory picture is not what the bear case assumes. DexCom's March 4, 2025 FDA warning letter is real, it followed 2024 inspections at San Diego and Mesa, it cites quality-system deficiencies, and no close-out was verifiable from the sources fetched here — so assume it is open. But it never restricted manufacturing, marketing or distribution and never required a recall, and the stock has risen 51% since the October 2025 low with it outstanding. More to the point, on January 23, 2026 the FDA issued Abbott its own warning letter over FreeStyle Libre, after an October 2025 inspection, for releasing finished sensors without post-assembly accuracy testing and for failing to transfer accuracy requirements to third-party manufacturers. That is arguably a more severe finding than DexCom's, and it lands on the market-share leader. Anyone modelling a regulatory penalty box for DexCom alone is modelling half the picture.

Thread 3 — what you are actually paying for. Three denominators, three answers. On trailing GAAP EPS of $2.54, DXCM trades at 34.7×. On 2026 consensus EPS of $2.66, 33.0× (the reported forward P/E of 31.26 implies a slightly later or higher base). On trailing free cash flow of $1.41B, about 23.5× — a 4.3% free-cash yield, and on enterprise value of $32.62B, roughly 23.1×. The gap between the earnings and cash multiples is not an accounting trick here; GAAP and non-GAAP EPS were identical at $2.09 in FY2025, which is a rare and creditable thing in this sector. The PEG of 1.48 is the tidiest summary: you are paying about a 50% premium to the growth rate for a business with a 44.5% ROIC, a 20.1% net margin, net cash, and a management team that just told you its margin tailwind ends in one quarter. That is neither cheap nor absurd. It is a fair price for a good business whose growth rate has permanently reset — which is exactly what a 34.7× multiple on a 15%-grower is supposed to mean.

Bull Case

The margin structure has genuinely inverted: gross margin from 56.85% (Q1'25) to 63.44% (Q2'26), operating margin +590bps, GAAP EPS +42% on 13% revenue growth, and all four FY2026 guided metrics raised at the July print. The business earns a 44.5% ROIC and 20.1% net margin on $1.41B of free cash flow, holds net cash, bought back ~$600M in a quarter, and pays nothing for the two largest option values it owns: a mid-2027 CMS decision that would open Medicare to 7M+ type 2 non-insulin patients, and Stelo, an over-the-counter cash-pay product with zero disclosed revenue and a natural GLP-1 tailwind. International is compounding at 19%. And the regulator has now put Abbott under a warning letter too, levelling a playing field the bears assumed was tilted.

Bear Case

Guided FY2026 revenue growth of 11–13% is below the ~15% category growth rate — that is share loss, arithmetically, and it is concentrated in the 71%-of-revenue US business growing 11%. The margin expansion driving the entire 51% rally is a one-time product-cycle effect that the company's own CFO says peaks in Q3 2026 before stepping down for Ireland. Abbott is 1.6× DexCom's CGM size and can subsidise from a $46.6B revenue base against a company with one product line. The March 2025 FDA warning letter has no verified close-out. The biggest growth pool is gated on a CMS decision that is still nine months away. And at $87.90 the stock sits above MarketBeat's $88.71 average target by a rounding error, with the panel's raises all crowded into four days after the print.

Top 3 Risks

01
The margin peak arrives on schedule and nothing replaces it. Management has guided gross margin to peak in Q3 2026 and step down as Ireland starts production. If that happens while revenue growth stays at 11–13%, earnings growth decelerates sharply in 2027 and a 34.7× trailing multiple has no support. This is the risk the company has told you about, which makes it the one least likely to be mispriced — and the one most likely to be underweighted anyway.
02
US growth stays at 11% while the category grows 15%. Two more quarters of 11% US growth would confirm structural share loss to Libre rather than a timing artefact, and would put the FY2027 revenue base below where consensus sits. The tell to watch is the relationship between record new patient starts and decelerating revenue: if patients keep hitting records while US revenue growth stays in the low teens, revenue per patient is falling, and the mix shift toward basal, non-insulin and pharmacy is more dilutive than the margin story admits.
03
A regulatory escalation on an already-open warning letter. The March 2025 letter carries no operating restriction today and no close-out was verified. A subsequent inspection finding, a consent decree, or a product-accuracy issue at either DexCom or Abbott would hit a single-product company far harder than a diversified one — and DexCom's manufacturing footprint is simultaneously being expanded into Ireland, which adds new sites and new validation work to a quality system the FDA has already criticised.
  Industry Commentary
A Duopoly Where One Side Has a Day Job
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Third-party estimates put 2025 CGM share at Abbott 52.83%, DexCom 33.89% and Medtronic 10.10% — roughly 97% of the market in three hands, which sounds like a comfortable oligopoly until you look at the balance sheets behind it. CGM is 100% of DexCom's $4.97B and a fraction of Abbott's $46.59B. In any PBM negotiation, government tender or price war, Abbott can accept a margin it would not tolerate elsewhere, and DexCom cannot. This is the structural reason US revenue grows 11% while international grows 19%: the US is where the two collide most directly on price and formulary placement. It is also why DexCom's competitive answer has to be product cycles and clinical differentiation rather than price — the 15-day sensor and the AID pump integrations are exactly that answer.
The FDA Is Auditing the Whole Category
ONGOING HEADWIND
Within eleven months the FDA issued warning letters to both CGM leaders: DexCom on March 4, 2025 (San Diego and Mesa, manufacturing and quality-system deficiencies) and Abbott on January 23, 2026, after an October 2025 inspection, for releasing finished FreeStyle Libre sensors without accuracy testing after assembly, sterilisation and programming, and for failing to transfer accuracy requirements to third-party manufacturers. Neither letter restricted sales. Read together they say something about the industry rather than either company: CGM devices have scaled from niche to mass-market volumes faster than the quality systems around them, and the regulator is now catching up. Expect remediation cost, expect it to be a cost of doing business rather than an existential event — and expect it not to be a differentiator between the two, which is itself a change from the 2025 narrative.
Reimbursement Is the Whole Growth Curve
EMERGING TAILWIND
CGM demand is not really demand — it is coverage. Every leg of growth in this industry has come from a payer decision: type 1, then insulin-intensive type 2, then basal-only, and now the frontier of type 2 non-insulin, where the CONNECT trial showed a 0.9% A1c improvement with 97% median usage and the CMS decision is expected mid-2027. DexCom already has commercial coverage across the four largest PBMs, reaching 7M+ US type 2 non-insulin patients, and it has Stelo as a cash-pay bridge for everyone else — including the GLP-1 population buying a sensor to see what food does to them. Bull framing: a favourable CMS decision converts the largest patient pool in the category from cash-pay to reimbursed overnight, and DexCom already has the product, the trial data and the manufacturing capacity in place. Bear framing: that is nine-plus months out, the market is expanding at ~15% while DexCom guides to 11–13%, and the near-term earnings engine — a 15-day product cycle — is guided to peak this quarter. You are paying 34.7× today for a decision in mid-2027.
