01 Company Overview

DexCom makes continuous glucose monitors (CGM — a small wearable sensor that reads glucose in the fluid just under the skin every few minutes and streams it to a phone or an insulin pump, replacing fingerstick blood tests). Its flagship is the G7 platform, now moving to a 15-day sensor; alongside it sit Stelo, an over-the-counter biosensor sold without a prescription, and Dexcom Smart Basal for people on long-acting insulin only. FY2025 revenue was $4.662B, up 15.6% from $4.033B, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $5.10B, up 13.0%. At the November 2, 2026 close the company was worth $31.61B (372.95M shares × $84.76). Its main rival is Abbott, whose Diabetes Care division, home of FreeStyle Libre, sold $2.19B in Q3 2026 against DexCom's $1.34B (the two companies' Q3 releases).

For a decade this was one of the fastest growers in medical devices — and then it broke. In July 2024 the stock fell 40% in a single month (from a $113.38 June close to $67.82) when US revenue growth disappointed and the growth story reset. Two years on, the debate has narrowed to a specific question: is DexCom losing share to Abbott's FreeStyle Libre in the insulin-using core, or is the whole category expanding fast enough — via Stelo, via basal and type 2 reimbursement, via GLP-1 users buying a sensor to watch what food does to them — that both companies grow? The third quarter of 2026 gave both sides something. US revenue growth sped up to 14% from 11% in the second quarter, the best answer yet to the share-loss worry; and in the same weeks DexCom told patients that some CVS Caremark and Aetna plans move from Dexcom-only to Libre-only coverage in January 2027. Over the last four quarters DexCom grew 11–15% a quarter, and its clearest gain has been in margin rather than in share.

This is where the 2024 break happened and where the recovery has to be proved — and Q3 2026 was the strongest evidence yet. US revenue grew 15% in FY2025, slowed to 11% in both Q4 2025 ($891.5M) and Q2 2026 ($933.4M), then sped up to 14% in Q3 2026 ($969.8M). CFO Jereme Sylvain described "a nice acceleration in our U.S. growth rate compared to the first half of the year" on the Q3 call, with the 15-day G7 rolling out across the US; type 2 patients, including those not on insulin, drove the largest increase in US sales (Drug Delivery Business, October 30, 2026). Coverage is the other half of the US story. At the Q2 print DexCom said it had coverage for all people with diabetes across the four largest commercial PBMs (pharmacy benefit managers — the intermediaries that decide which products a health plan pays for), opening access to over 7 million US type 2 non-insulin patients. The first crack in that: from January 1, 2027 some CVS Caremark and Aetna Commercial plan designs move from Dexcom-only to Abbott FreeStyle Libre-only coverage (DexCom's own patient notice). Sylvain put that PBM at under 4% of US revenue, and CEO Jake Leach said on the Q3 call that most affected patients still have access through their medical benefit, buying through a durable-medical-equipment (DME) supplier.

Stelo is sold without a prescription and without reimbursement — cash, direct to the consumer. It matters for two reasons that have nothing to do with its current revenue. First, it was DexCom's first 15-day product, the proving ground for the sensor chemistry now migrating into the prescription G7 line. Second, it is the answer to the reimbursement gap: management presents it as the product for type 2 non-insulin patients who have no coverage today, while Medicare decides — Leach said on the Q3 call that DexCom expects a CMS coverage decision this year, with coverage taking effect around the middle of 2027. In Q2 2026 the company began launching a fully reimagined Stelo app with AI-driven insights and enhanced food logging (Q2 release); the Q3 release lists the launch of the redesigned Stelo experience. This is also the natural landing zone for GLP-1 users — people on weight-loss and diabetes drugs who want to see what specific foods do to their glucose. DexCom discloses no Stelo revenue, no unit volume and no user count, so the size of this business cannot be read from public filings, and nothing here is estimated.

Watch item: in the CONNECT trial, type 2 patients not on insulin who wore a DexCom sensor cut their A1c (a three-month average of blood sugar) from 8.8% to 7.2% over 26 weeks, against 8.7% to 8.0% with routine fingerstick care: a 0.9-point bigger fall (Managed Healthcare Executive, October 21, 2026). The results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine after the quarter (Q3 release). It is the clinical evidence behind the CMS decision — the single largest yes-or-no event in the whole story.

DexCom's sensors feed the automated insulin delivery (AID — a "closed loop" system where the CGM tells the pump how much insulin to give, without the patient doing the arithmetic) systems sold by Tandem, Insulet and Beta Bionics. This is a genuine advantage over Abbott in the insulin-intensive core, because a patient locked into a pump system does not casually switch sensor brands. It also imports someone else's problems: Insulet's share price is down 50.9% over the past year even as its revenue grew 29.4% to $3.05B TTM, following Omnipod product corrections in March and May and a cut in its FY2026 sales-growth guidance to 20–22% from 21–23% (Drug Delivery Business, August 5, 2026) — a partner under that kind of pressure sells fewer new pumps, and each pump not sold is a DexCom sensor subscription not started. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
CLINICAL ACCURACY DATA AID PUMP INTEGRATIONS CONSUMABLE SUBSCRIPTION MODEL PBM & MEDICARE COVERAGE FDA CLEARANCE STACK PRESCRIBER RELATIONSHIPS OWN-MANUFACTURING SCALE

The core advantage is that DexCom does not sell a device — it sells a subscription to a consumable. A sensor lasts 10 days, or now 15, and then the patient buys another one, forever. That turns a one-time hardware sale into a steady stream of roughly 24–36 reorders a year, which is why a business with $5.10B of revenue throws off $1.10B of free cash flow and a 45.9% ROIC. The switching cost that protects that stream is not the sensor itself but everything wrapped around it: a prescription, a PBM coverage decision, a pump that only speaks to one sensor, and a patient who has learned to read one particular app. That is a real moat — and it is narrower than it was in 2021, because Abbott has now cleared most of the same regulatory and reimbursement hurdles at a lower price point.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Abbott is bigger in CGM and can subsidise from a $48.2B revenue base. Abbott's Diabetes Care division, home of FreeStyle Libre, sold $2.19B in Q3 2026 against DexCom's $1.34B — about 1.6 times as much — yet it was only about a sixth of Abbott's $13.0B of sales that quarter (Abbott Q3 2026 release). Abbott can price Libre aggressively in a tender or a PBM negotiation and absorb it; DexCom cannot, because CGM is 100% of what it sells. The CVS Caremark and Aetna switch to Libre-only coverage from January 2027 is that imbalance at work: Abbott CEO Robert Ford called it "a pretty significant payer formulary coverage that flipped in our favor" (MedTech Dive, November 2, 2026).
02
Quality-system regulation, and a March 2025 FDA warning letter. The FDA issued DexCom a warning letter dated March 4, 2025 following 2024 inspections of the San Diego and Mesa facilities, citing deficiencies in manufacturing processes and quality management systems and in the company's responses to prior observations. It did not restrict manufacture, marketing or distribution and required no recall, and DexCom said at the time that it would take corrective actions and update the agency regularly.
03
Single-product concentration with a reimbursement single point of failure. Every dollar of revenue depends on someone else agreeing to pay for a glucose sensor. The largest growth pool — 7M+ US type 2 non-insulin patients — waits on a CMS decision management expects by the end of 2026, with coverage taking effect around mid-2027. Until then, the Medicare part of that group can only be reached through cash-pay Stelo, at a much lower revenue per patient.
04
Gross margin is at a peak by management's own account. Non-GAAP gross margin reached 65.1% in Q3 2026, up 3.8 percentage points on a year earlier. Sylvain is guiding to "a bit of a step back here in the fourth quarter" as the Ireland factory turns on, and expects "a little bit of an impact of Ireland ramping up into the first part of next year." Anyone projecting the Q3 margin into 2027 is projecting against explicit guidance.

Notable one-off items — and the gross margin story in full. Gross margin is the most important line in this report, so here is the whole arc. GAAP gross margin ran 68.63% in FY2021, then fell every single year: 64.72% (FY2022) → 63.19% (FY2023) → 60.46% (FY2024) → 60.10% (FY2025) — 8.5 percentage points lost over four years. On a non-GAAP basis FY2025 gross margin fell to 60.8% from 61.8% (our calculation from the Q4 2025 release tables). Over those years DexCom was adding manufacturing capacity, including the Ireland plant that starts up in Q4 2026, and making two sensor platforms at once; the Q3 2026 10-Q ties part of this year's inventory charges to the planned end of G6 manufacturing. The reversal since has been sharp: 56.85% (Q1'25) → 59.53% → 60.48% → 62.93% → 62.95% → 63.44% → 64.29% (Q3'26), with non-GAAP gross margin at 65.1% in Q3 2026 against 61.3% a year earlier. The 15-day sensor is the key: one sensor covering 15 days instead of 10 means roughly a third fewer sensors made, shipped and thrown away per patient-year. That is a pure cost-of-goods win. It also pauses: management is guiding to a step back in Q4 2026 as the Ireland factory turns on, with some drag into early 2027. GAAP and adjusted EPS were almost the same in Q3 2026 — $0.72 and $0.73 — so there were no large one-off charges between the two. The FY2026 non-GAAP guide is now ~64% gross margin, ~24% operating margin and ~32% adjusted EBITDA margin, the last two raised at the Q3 print.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 month-end close of $140.65 (split-adjusted), where this chart starts, to the November 2, 2026 close of $84.76, DXCM has returned −39.7% — about −9.8% a year, with no dividend. Over the same dates SPY returned +65.1% and QQQ +72.9% (price only, from Yahoo closes). That is a brutal gap for a company whose revenue grew from $2.45B (FY2021) to $5.10B TTM, more than doubling, while GAAP EPS went from $0.53 to $2.55 — nearly five-fold. The whole loss came from what investors will pay for each dollar of profit. $140.65 was about 265× the $0.53 a share DexCom earned in FY2021; $84.76 is 33.2× the last four quarters' $2.55. Nothing about that path is an earnings story; it is a repricing of what a mid-teens grower sharing its market with Abbott is worth, against what a 25%-plus grower that looked like the category's only serious player was worth.

The worst of it came in two months. July 2024: −40.2% in a single month, from a $113.38 June close to $67.82, when US revenue growth disappointed and FY2024 growth landed at 11.3% against 24.5% the prior year. CEO Kevin Sayer attributed the lower outlook to a salesforce restructuring, lower revenue per customer from rebates, and lost share in the durable-medical-equipment channel (MedTech Dive, July 26, 2024). March 2025: −22.7%, from $88.37 to $68.29, the month the FDA warning letter was issued. The stock then ground lower to a month-end trough of $58.22 in October 2025 — −58.6% from the November 2021 close — and set its 52-week intraday low of $54.11 on November 7, 2025. From the October 2025 close it has risen +45.6% to the November 2 close, alongside the margin recovery documented in Section 04 and, in Q3, faster US growth.

Relative performance. DXCM's one-year price change is +45.6% (October 31, 2025 close → November 2, 2026 close), against +10.3% for SPY and +8.3% for QQQ over the same dates — a window that happens to start at the stock's cycle low. Its 5-year beta of 1.48 fits a name that fell 40% in a month. Among peers over the past 52 weeks (stockanalysis, November 2 close): Abbott's shares are down 10.9% and Insulet's down 50.9%. Diabetes technology as a group has not been a safe place to sit; DXCM's year puts it ahead of its neighbours while it remains about 40% below the November 2021 close where this chart begins.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The stack, in order. Start with patients. DexCom does not disclose absolute new patient adds; on the Q3 call Sylvain said global new patient starts were near record levels in the quarter. Multiply patients by revenue per patient and you get revenue: $1,344.5M in Q3 2026, +11.2%, split US $969.8M (+14%) and International $374.7M (+5%, reported and organic). Apply gross margin — 64.3% GAAP, 65.1% non-GAAP, up 3.8 percentage points — and gross profit grows far faster than revenue: $864.4M, up 18%. Costs below gross profit grew more slowly than sales, so GAAP operating margin reached 24.3% against 20.1% a year earlier. GAAP net income was $275.1M, slightly below last year's $283.8M, and GAAP EPS was $0.72 against $0.70, helped by buybacks: the nine-month diluted share count fell 4.6%, to 389.0M from 407.7M. On the adjusted basis the company leads with, EPS rose 20% to $0.73 from $0.61.

And here is the part that decides the next two years: much of the margin gain is a product cycle, and management has dated its pause. The G7 15 Day sensor replaces a 10-day consumable with a 15-day one: the same patient needs roughly a third fewer sensors a year. Leach said the company is on track "to convert nearly 50% of our U.S. customer base to G7 15 Day by year-end." GAAP gross margin went from 56.85% in Q1 2025 to 64.29% in Q3 2026. Now the step back: Sylvain guided to "a bit of a step back here in the fourth quarter" as Ireland turns on, with "a little bit of an impact of Ireland ramping up into the first part of next year." After that, earnings growth leans more on revenue, and FY2026 revenue is guided to 12–13%, which implies roughly 9.5% growth in Q4 at the midpoint.

Thread 1 — is DexCom losing to Abbott, or is the category expanding? Q3 tilted the answer toward DexCom. On the two companies' own figures, DexCom's revenue grew 11% in Q3 2026 while Abbott's CGM sales grew 7.3%, and Abbott's Diabetes Care division, home of FreeStyle Libre, sold $2.19B against DexCom's $1.34B. Mordor Intelligence, an outside research firm, sizes the global market at $18.15B in 2026 and forecasts about 15% a year from 2027 to 2032, above DexCom's guided 12–13% for FY2026, so DexCom would have to speed up to keep pace with that forecast. The geography flipped in Q3: the US, where DexCom and Abbott collide most directly on price and coverage, sped up to +14%, while international slowed to +5% as it lapped 2025 reimbursement wins. The coverage fight is not over either: from January 2027 some CVS Caremark and Aetna Commercial plan designs move from Dexcom-only to Libre-only coverage, a PBM Sylvain put at under 4% of US revenue. Meanwhile the category expansion is arriving through channels that barely existed three years ago — Stelo sold over the counter for cash, basal-only users via Dexcom Smart Basal, and type 2 non-insulin patients now covered by large commercial PBMs. The fair summary: in Q3 DexCom outgrew Abbott's CGM sales while its own margin rose, and the CVS Caremark and Aetna switch in January is the next test of whether that holds.

Thread 2 — the regulatory picture is not what the bear case assumes. DexCom's March 4, 2025 FDA warning letter is real: it followed 2024 inspections at San Diego and Mesa and cites quality-system deficiencies. But it did not restrict manufacturing, marketing or distribution or require a recall, and the stock has risen 46% from the October 2025 close. More to the point, on January 23, 2026 the FDA issued Abbott its own warning letter over FreeStyle Libre, after an October 2025 inspection, for releasing finished sensors without post-assembly accuracy testing and for failing to transfer accuracy requirements to third-party manufacturers. That is arguably a more severe finding than DexCom's, and it lands on the market-share leader. Anyone expecting the regulator to single out DexCom alone is seeing half the picture.

Thread 3 — what you are actually paying for. Several denominators, several answers. On trailing GAAP EPS of $2.55, DXCM trades at 33.2×. On the $2.70 FY2026 consensus (adjusted, non-GAAP), 31.4×; on the $3.14 FY2027 consensus, 27.0×. On trailing free cash flow of $1.10B, about 28.8× — a 3.5% free-cash yield — and on enterprise value of $31.19B, roughly 28.4×. The cash multiple has risen since the last edition largely because the unusually strong Q3 2025 cash quarter ($579M) left the trailing window; Q3 2026 brought in $273M. GAAP and adjusted EPS were almost the same in Q3 2026 ($0.72 and $0.73), so the earnings multiple is not flattered by adjustments. The PEG of 1.37 is the tidiest summary: you are paying a premium to the growth rate for a business with a 45.9% ROIC, a 19.4% net margin, net cash, and a management team that has told you its margin tailwind pauses next quarter. That is neither cheap nor absurd. It is a fair price for a good business whose growth rate has settled in the low teens.

Bull Case

US growth sped up to 14% in Q3, the clearest answer yet to the share-loss worry, and margins kept climbing: GAAP gross margin from 56.85% (Q1'25) to 64.29% (Q3'26), GAAP operating margin up 4.2 points on a year earlier, and the FY2026 revenue floor and the operating and EBITDA margin guides raised again. The business earns a 45.9% ROIC and a 19.4% net margin, holds net cash, bought back $1.0B of stock in nine months and has authorized up to $1.0B more. Two large options sit on top: a CMS decision management expects this year, with coverage around mid-2027, that would open Medicare to type 2 patients not on insulin, and Stelo, an over-the-counter cash-pay product with a natural GLP-1 tailwind. Management calls international's slowdown a short-term effect of lapping 2025 reimbursement wins. And the regulator has put Abbott under a warning letter too.

Bear Case

International slowed from 16% organic growth in Q2 to 5% in Q3, and guided FY2026 growth of 12–13% is below the ~15% a year an outside forecast (Mordor Intelligence) expects for the category from 2027. The margin expansion behind the rally is a product-cycle effect that management guides to step back in Q4 as Ireland turns on. Abbott's Diabetes Care division sells about 1.6 times what DexCom does, Abbott can subsidise it from a $48.2B revenue base, and it has just won Libre-only coverage on some CVS Caremark and Aetna plans from January 2027. The March 2025 FDA warning letter cited quality-system failings at two of DexCom's plants. The biggest growth pool waits on a Medicare decision that has not been made. And the post-print target raises were small, $3 to $7, with JPMorgan's Neutral at $85 sitting right on the $84.76 price.

Top 3 Risks

01
The margin step back arrives and nothing replaces it. Management has guided gross margin down in Q4 2026 as Ireland starts production, with some impact into early 2027. If that happens while revenue growth sits at 12–13% or lower — the guide implies about 9.5% in Q4 — earnings growth slows sharply in 2027 and a 33.2× trailing multiple has less support. This is the risk the company has told you about, which makes it the one most likely to be in the share price already — and the one investors are most likely to play down anyway.
02
More US coverage moves to Libre. The CVS Caremark and Aetna switch shows a PBM can move a block of patients to Libre at a stroke. DexCom says this one is under 4% of US revenue and that some patients may keep access through their durable-medical-equipment benefit. A second, larger switch would hit the market where Q3's 14% growth came from. The tell to watch is whether US growth holds in the low-to-mid teens through 2027 as the switch takes effect.
03
A regulatory escalation after the 2025 warning letter. The March 2025 letter carried no operating restriction. A later inspection finding, a consent decree, or a product-accuracy issue at either DexCom or Abbott would hit a single-product company far harder than a diversified one — and DexCom's manufacturing is expanding into Ireland at the same time, which adds a new site and new validation work to a quality system the FDA has already criticised.
  Industry Commentary
A Duopoly Where One Side Has a Day Job
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Abbott and DexCom are the two big CGM makers; Abbott's Diabetes Care division, home of FreeStyle Libre, sold about 1.6 times what DexCom did in Q3 2026 ($2.19B against $1.34B). That sounds like a fair fight until you look at the companies behind it. CGM is 100% of DexCom's $5.10B of revenue, while Diabetes Care was about a sixth of Abbott's sales in the quarter, out of $48.23B over the last four quarters. In any PBM negotiation, government tender or price war, Abbott can accept a margin it would not tolerate elsewhere, and DexCom cannot. The CVS Caremark and Aetna Commercial switch to Libre-only coverage from January 2027 is the latest example. DexCom's competitive answer has to be product cycles and clinical evidence rather than price — the 15-day sensor, the AID pump integrations and the CONNECT trial are exactly that answer.
The FDA Is Auditing the Whole Category
ONGOING HEADWIND
Within eleven months the FDA issued warning letters to both CGM leaders: DexCom on March 4, 2025 (San Diego and Mesa, manufacturing and quality-system deficiencies) and Abbott on January 23, 2026, after an October 2025 inspection, for releasing finished FreeStyle Libre sensors without accuracy testing after assembly, sterilisation and programming, and for failing to transfer accuracy requirements to third-party manufacturers. Neither letter restricted sales. Read together they say something about the industry rather than either company: CGM devices have scaled from niche to mass-market volumes faster than the quality systems around them, and the regulator is now catching up. Expect remediation cost, expect it to be a cost of doing business rather than an existential event — and expect it not to separate the two, which is itself a change from the 2025 narrative.
Reimbursement Is the Whole Growth Curve
EMERGING TAILWIND
CGM demand is not really demand — it is coverage. Every leg of growth in this industry has come from a payer decision: type 1, then insulin-intensive type 2, then basal-only, and now the frontier of type 2 non-insulin, where in the CONNECT trial A1c fell 0.9 points more with a sensor than with routine care, now published in the New England Journal of Medicine. DexCom expects the CMS decision by the end of 2026, with coverage taking effect around mid-2027. It said in mid-2026 that it had commercial coverage across the four largest PBMs, reaching 7M+ US type 2 non-insulin patients — though some CVS Caremark plans move to Libre-only from January 2027 — and it has Stelo as a cash-pay bridge for everyone else, including the GLP-1 population buying a sensor to see what food does to them. In early 2027 it adds Dexcom Direct, a service Leach said is "all about helping our patients get access to our technology." Bull framing: a favourable CMS decision converts the largest patient pool in the category from cash-pay to reimbursed, and DexCom already has the product, the trial data and the manufacturing capacity in place. Bear framing: Medicare coverage is still months away, an outside forecast has the market growing about 15% a year from 2027 while DexCom guides to 12–13% for 2026, and the near-term earnings engine — a 15-day product cycle — is guided to step back next quarter. You are paying 33.2× today for a decision that has not been made.
