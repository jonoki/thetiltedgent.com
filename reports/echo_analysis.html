01 Company Overview

A new ticker on an old company. ECHO is not a new listing. It is EchoStar Corporation — the satellite company Charlie Ergen founded in 1980 — which traded as SATS from its January 2008 spin-off out of DISH Network until June 24, 2026, when it switched to ECHO. The symbol honours NASA's Project Echo, the first communications satellite. (ECHO used to belong to Echo Global Logistics, a freight broker taken private in 2021; that history has nothing to do with this company.) EchoStar and DISH merged back together on December 31, 2023, so today's EchoStar owns DISH TV and Sling TV (pay-TV), Boost Mobile (wireless) and Hughes (satellite broadband). It joined the S&P 500 on March 23, 2026, still under the SATS ticker, moving up from the S&P MidCap 400.

The story since 2025 is a spectrum liquidation. For years, DISH/EchoStar bought wireless licences and then burned cash trying to build a fourth US mobile network. In 2025 it gave up on that. It agreed to sell spectrum to AT&T for about $23 billion and to SpaceX for about $17 billion (AWS-4 and H-block), plus a later unpaired AWS-3 sale to SpaceX. Boost became a "hybrid" operator that runs on AT&T's network. The FCC approved the sales on May 12, 2026. The AT&T deal closed on July 28, 2026 and brought in $20.25 billion of cash, with another $2.4 billion going into an escrow the FCC required for claims tied to the sales. The SpaceX deals are still pending: the September 30 10-Q says "The timing and closing of the SpaceX Transactions are not certain." SpaceX itself has been a listed company since its June 12, 2026 IPO. The market paid up for all of this: month-end closes went from $17.73 in May 2025 to $129.19 in May 2026, roughly 7.3x.

Then came the restructurings. On June 30, 2026, the AT&T closing was delayed, and DISH DBS and DISH Wireless filed prepackaged Chapter 11 cases (bankruptcies with terms agreed with creditors before filing) to deal with notes coming due. More than 88% of DISH DBS noteholders had already signed the restructuring agreement. EchoStar deconsolidated those entities on June 30 (removed them from its own books) and booked a non-cash $9.73B gain. That gain is the only reason Q2 shows $8.46B of net income. On August 2, Hughes Satellite Systems also filed, after it could not repay $1.5B of bonds. Its bondholders say EchoStar stripped more than $1.5B out of Hughes. The DISH DBS case then ended: the court confirmed its plan on September 29 and the company emerged on October 1, 2026 with about $4.35 billion less debt, and EchoStar consolidates it again from that date (EchoStar 8-K, filed October 2). DISH Wireless was split off from that plan on August 27 and is still in Chapter 11. The stock closed $94.62 on November 6, 27% below its May month-end. The question is simple to state and hard to answer: after the creditors of the subsidiaries still in court are paid, how much of roughly $40B of spectrum proceeds, including a large block of SpaceX stock, is left for EchoStar's equity?

The Q3 print (November 3). EchoStar reported through its Form 10-Q, accepted by the SEC before the market opened on November 3 (8:15 AM ET, per the SEC's EDGAR record). Revenue was $1,127.6M against $3,614.3M a year earlier, the drop coming from the deconsolidated subsidiaries. Operating income was $147.8M. Net income attributable to EchoStar was $2,782.2M, built on a $3,106.8M gain on the AT&T sale and a $439.2M deconsolidation gain; diluted EPS was $7.97 ($9.57 basic). Cash and equivalents reached $13.44B (from $1.88B at year-end 2025) against $9.66B of debt. The 10-Q says certain subsidiaries lack the cash or committed financing to meet their obligations over the next twelve months, which raises substantial doubt about those subsidiaries' ability to continue; it does not say this of EchoStar as a whole. Boost lost 182,000 wireless customers in the quarter against the 74,000 loss Wall Street expected, leaving about 7.19 million (Broadband Breakfast). EchoStar held no earnings call and issued no press release (Broadband Breakfast). On the stock: it closed +3.78% on November 3 ($98.23 → $101.94), then −3.26%, −8.82% and +5.23% on the next three sessions. On November 2 The Washington Post reported that SpaceX had talked with EchoStar about buying Boost Mobile or using its network arrangements; EchoStar declined to comment and no agreement has been announced.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scarce licensed spectrum FCC-approved buyers (AT&T, SpaceX) Prospective SpaceX equity stake 6.34M pay-TV subscribers Owned satellite fleet & orbital slots Enterprise/defense satellite contracts

EchoStar's real edge was never its operating businesses. It was Ergen's spectrum portfolio: licences bought over two decades that became valuable enough for AT&T and SpaceX to pay about $40B combined. Once the sales close, most of that edge becomes cash, SpaceX stock and a hybrid wireless business that rents its network. The one durable asset left is the SpaceX stake (SpaceX has been listed since its June 12, 2026 IPO), and EchoStar has no say in how SpaceX runs. The weak point is the capital structure. Each business sits in its own subsidiary with its own creditors, and 2026 showed that value moving up to the parent can be challenged.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Creditors ahead of equity, in several courts. DISH DBS emerged on October 1, but DISH Wireless (filed June 30) and Hughes Satellite Systems (filed August 2) are still restructuring. Hughes bondholders say EchoStar took more than $1.5B out of Hughes: a Jupiter 3 satellite lease at about $191M a year, $1.029B of dividends paid to the parent in 2024, about $196M of tax payments, and the way SpaceX-deal value was allocated. The U.S. Trustee asked for an independent examiner and the judge granted the examiner motions on August 26. Any recovery comes out of equity value.
02
The SpaceX leg has not closed. The Q3 10-Q still says the timing and closing of the SpaceX transactions "are not certain." Until they close, a large part of the equity value depends on SpaceX's share price and on closing conditions EchoStar does not control.
03
Every operating business is shrinking. In Q2 2026, Pay-TV lost ~241K subscribers, Boost ~118K and Hughes ~59K; Boost lost another ~182K in Q3. TTM revenue is $12.17B (StockAnalysis), lower mainly because of the deconsolidations. Annual revenue fell from $19.82B in FY2021 to $15.01B in FY2025 on the combined-company basis.
04
Controlled company. The 10-Q says one stockholder, the Chairman and CEO, controls the company. Decisions on capital allocation — keeping or distributing SpaceX shares, related-party deals, and any deal for Boost Mobile such as the one The Washington Post reported SpaceX had explored on November 2 — are his to make.

Notable One-Off Items

Gains now swamp every reported figure. The Q2 2026 non-cash deconsolidation gain of $9.729B from removing DISH DBS and DISH Wireless after their Chapter 11 filings turned a quarter with about $61M of pre-tax income without it (our calculation: $9.79B of pre-tax income less the gain) into a reported $8.46B profit after $1.33B of tax. In Q3 2026 the AT&T sale produced a $3.107B gain and a further $439M deconsolidation gain came through, lifting net income attributable to EchoStar to $2.78B. Year to date the deconsolidation gain is $10.17B. Still to come: gains on the SpaceX sales if they close, and the reconsolidation of DISH DBS from October 1. Before that, the Q3 2025 impairment of $16.48B on the parts of the 5G network being abandoned produced a $12.78B quarterly net loss and most of the $14.50B FY2025 net loss (EPS −$50.41); it has now left the trailing twelve months. Note also that the per-share history before 2024 reflects the combined company after the December 31, 2023 re-merger with DISH. It is not the old standalone EchoStar.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen hard and fast compared with its own recent history ("overbought"). Below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value.

Over five years, from the November 2021 close of $27.37 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $94.62, the stock gained 245.7% on price. It paid no dividends. That number hides two separate stories. For about three and a half years this was a stock in decline: it fell to $10.47 in November 2023, weeks before the DISH re-merger closed, and was still at $17.73 in May 2025. Then, in twelve months, it rose 7.3x to $129.19 in May 2026 as the market valued the spectrum sales. Since then it has given back 27% as the subsidiaries went into bankruptcy, with a partial recovery in September and October.

The 6-period monthly RSI stayed above 83 for ten months in a row, from August 2025 to May 2026, peaking at 94.5 in May 2026. That is an unusually long stretch at extreme readings. It dropped to 40.8 in July and is now 51.2 (November 6 close), which is neutral. The 10-month moving average of about $103.79 is still above the price. The long-term trend has not turned back up.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The hard part is done. The FCC approved the sales, AT&T closed on July 28 and paid $20.25B in cash, DISH DBS has already emerged from Chapter 11, and SpaceX has agreed to pay about $20B including up to $11B in SpaceX stock for the AWS-4/H-block and AWS-3 spectrum. The DISH DBS and DISH Wireless bankruptcies were prepackaged and are there to repay subsidiary debt early from those proceeds, not because the parent is insolvent. EchoStar held $13.44B of cash against $9.66B of debt at September 30. Once the debt is gone, EchoStar equity holds a SpaceX stake, a pay-TV business, and a Boost business that now makes money on AT&T's network. Every covering analyst rates it a Buy, with a $139.20 average target (+47.1%), and even the lowest target, $117, is above $94.62. The 17.8% short interest is fuel for a rally if the SpaceX closing and the DISH Wireless exit go cleanly.

Bear Case

Three subsidiaries went into Chapter 11 in one summer, and their creditors come first. Hughes bondholders are chasing more than $1.5B of alleged transfers to the parent, and an examiner has been appointed. DISH Wireless is still in court with tower companies pressing about $7.6B of lease claims. The SpaceX leg is not closed (expected by November 2027), and its stock component makes EchoStar's value depend on another company's valuation. The 10-Q flags substantial doubt about some subsidiaries' ability to continue. Operationally what is left is shrinking businesses (Q2: −241K, −118K and −59K subscribers; Boost lost another 182K in Q3) with negative free cash flow of $533M TTM. And one person controls the company. With 17.8% of the tradable shares sold short, a sizable group of investors is betting against the $131 median target.

Industry Commentary
Low-earth-orbit broadband vs. geostationary incumbents
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Hughes's chief restructuring officer, Robert Del Genio, told the bankruptcy court that "LEO satellite competition is structural, not cyclical." LEO is low Earth orbit, where SpaceX's Starlink and Amazon's Leo fly; those satellites sit much closer to Earth, so they are faster and more responsive. The same declaration put Hughes at about 641,000 broadband subscribers at the August 2 filing, about 21.7% below the roughly 819,000 of June 30, 2025 (PPC Land). The company's Q2 report counted 622,000 at June 30, 2026. The response is to shift toward enterprise, aviation and defense. That is the standard move for a geostationary operator losing consumers to low-orbit networks. Whether the enterprise pipeline can make up for the consumer decline is still unproven.

Spectrum as a traded asset
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

EchoStar's 2025–26 sales show two kinds of buyer paying for mid- and low-band spectrum: an established carrier (AT&T, adding 5G capacity) and a satellite operator (SpaceX, building a direct-to-phone Starlink constellation). The FCC approved both on May 12, 2026. As a condition, it required a $2.4B escrow for claims tied to the sales, which sits on the September 30 balance sheet (Q3 10-Q). For EchoStar the result is that licences once valued on forecasts for a network that was never finished are now priced by what someone actually paid for them.

Hybrid wireless — renting instead of building
EMERGING TAILWIND

By giving up its own network and running Boost on AT&T's, EchoStar swapped heavy network spending for a wholesale fee. The first result shows up in the numbers: Wireless OIBDA went from −$98.9M in Q2 2025 to +$50.8M in Q2 2026 on flat revenue. The trade-off is subscribers. Boost lost 118,000 in Q2, a year after adding 212,000, and 182,000 more in Q3. An operator that rents its network has to compete on price and distribution, not coverage.
