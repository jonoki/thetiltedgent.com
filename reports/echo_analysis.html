01 Company Overview

A new ticker on an old company. ECHO is not a new listing. It is EchoStar Corporation — the satellite company Charlie Ergen founded in 1980 — which traded as SATS from its January 2008 spin-off out of DISH Network until June 24, 2026, when it switched to ECHO. The symbol honours NASA's Project Echo, the first communications satellite. (ECHO used to belong to Echo Global Logistics, a freight broker taken private in 2021; that history has nothing to do with this company.) EchoStar and DISH merged back together on December 31, 2023, so today's EchoStar owns DISH TV and Sling TV (pay-TV), Boost Mobile (wireless) and Hughes (satellite broadband). It joined the S&P 500 on March 23, 2026, still under the SATS ticker, moving up from the S&P MidCap 400.

The story since 2025 is a spectrum liquidation. For years, DISH/EchoStar bought wireless licences and then burned cash trying to build a fourth US mobile network. In 2025 it gave up on that. It agreed to sell spectrum to AT&T for about $23 billion and to SpaceX for about $17 billion (AWS-4 and H-block), plus a later unpaired AWS-3 sale to SpaceX. Boost became a "hybrid" operator that runs on AT&T's network. The FCC approved the sales on May 12, 2026. The AT&T deal closed on July 28, 2026. The SpaceX deals were still pending at the June 30 10-Q, which said their "timing and closing … are not certain." The market paid up for all of this: month-end closes went from $17.73 in May 2025 to $129.19 in May 2026, roughly 7.3x.

Then came the restructurings. On June 30, 2026, the AT&T closing was delayed, and DISH DBS and DISH Wireless filed prepackaged Chapter 11 cases to deal with notes coming due. More than 88% of DISH DBS noteholders had already signed the restructuring agreement. EchoStar deconsolidated those entities on June 30 and booked a non-cash $9.73B gain. That gain is the only reason Q2 shows $8.46B of net income. In the first days of August, Hughes Satellite Systems also filed, after it could not repay $1.5B of bonds with about $102M of cash. Its bondholders now say EchoStar stripped more than $1.5B out of Hughes. The stock closed $95.36, 26% below its May month-end. The question is simple to state and hard to answer: after the creditors of three subsidiaries are paid, how much of roughly $40B of spectrum proceeds, including a large block of SpaceX stock, is left for EchoStar's equity?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scarce licensed spectrum FCC-approved buyers (AT&T, SpaceX) Prospective SpaceX equity stake 6.39M pay-TV subscribers Owned satellite fleet & orbital slots Enterprise/defense satellite contracts

EchoStar's real edge was never its operating businesses. It was Ergen's spectrum portfolio: licences bought over two decades that became valuable enough for AT&T and SpaceX to pay about $40B combined. Once the sales close, most of that edge becomes cash, SpaceX stock and a hybrid wireless business that rents its network. The one durable asset left is the SpaceX stake, and EchoStar has no say in how SpaceX runs. The weak point is the capital structure. Each business sits in its own subsidiary with its own creditors, and 2026 showed that value moving up to the parent can be challenged.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Creditors ahead of equity, in three separate courts. DISH DBS and DISH Wireless (filed June 30) and Hughes Satellite Systems (filed early August) are each restructuring. Hughes bondholders say EchoStar took more than $1.5B out of Hughes: a Jupiter 3 satellite lease at about $191M a year, $1.029B of dividends paid to the parent in Q1 2024, a $196M tax reimbursement, and the way SpaceX-deal value was allocated. The U.S. Trustee asked for an independent examiner. Any recovery comes out of equity value.
02
The SpaceX leg has not closed. The June 30 10-Q says the timing and closing of the SpaceX transactions "are not certain." Until they close, a large part of the equity value depends on another company's private-market or public valuation and on closing conditions EchoStar does not control.
03
Every operating business is shrinking. In Q2 2026, Pay-TV lost ~241K subscribers, Boost ~118K and Hughes ~59K. TTM revenue is $14.65B, down 5.2%. Revenue has fallen every year since FY2021 on the combined-company basis.
04
Controlled company. The 10-Q says one stockholder, the Chairman and CEO, controls the company. Decisions on capital allocation — keeping or distributing SpaceX shares, related-party deals, and Ergen's reported move to take control of MobileX (a WSJ report headlined on StockAnalysis) — are his to make.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items swamp every reported figure. First, the Q3 2025 impairment of $16.48B on the parts of the 5G network being abandoned after the move to a hybrid operator. It produced a $12.78B quarterly net loss and most of the $14.50B FY2025 net loss (EPS −$50.41). Second, the Q2 2026 non-cash deconsolidation gain of $9.729B from removing DISH DBS and DISH Wireless after their Chapter 11 filings. It turned a quarter that would have earned about $49.5M into a reported $8.46B profit. A third item is coming: gains on the AT&T sale (closed July 28) and, if they close, the SpaceX sales. These will likely produce large one-off gains again, which probably explains the $39.34 FY2026 consensus EPS. Also note that the per-share history before 2024 reflects the combined company after the December 31, 2023 re-merger with DISH. It is not the old standalone EchoStar.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The hard part is done. The FCC approved the sales, AT&T paid about $23B and closed on July 28, and SpaceX has agreed to pay about $17B for AWS-4/H-block — up to half of it in SpaceX stock — plus an AWS-3 sale on top. The bankruptcies are prepackaged and are there to repay subsidiary debt early from those proceeds, not because the parent is insolvent. Once the debt is gone, EchoStar equity holds a SpaceX stake, a pay-TV business still producing about $600M of OIBDA a quarter, and a Boost business that now makes money on AT&T's network. Every covering analyst rates it a Buy, with a $131.44 average target (+37.8%), and even the lowest target, $103, is above $95.36. The 20% short interest is fuel for a rally if the SpaceX closing and the DISH emergence go cleanly.

Bear Case

Three subsidiaries went into Chapter 11 in one summer, and their creditors come first. Hughes bondholders are chasing more than $1.5B of alleged transfers to the parent, and the U.S. Trustee wants an examiner. DISH creditors are reported to be seeking a trustee. The SpaceX leg is not closed, and its stock component makes EchoStar's value depend on another company's valuation. Once the spectrum is sold, all that is left operationally is three shrinking businesses (−241K, −118K and −59K subscribers in Q2) with negative free cash flow of $725M TTM. And one person controls the company. A 20% short interest is the market's view that the $130 sum-of-the-parts targets ignore who gets paid first.

Top 3 Risks

01
Value leaking to subsidiary creditors. Examiner findings or settlements in the Hughes and DISH cases could move part of the spectrum proceeds from equity to creditors.
02
The SpaceX closing and the value of SpaceX stock. The sale is still pending, and up to $8.5B (AWS-4/H-block) of the consideration is SpaceX stock. A delay, a change in terms, or a lower SpaceX valuation hits the largest remaining asset directly.
03
No growth engine left. Pay-TV, Boost and Hughes all lost subscribers in Q2 2026, and TTM free cash flow is −$725M. What is left after the spectrum sales has not yet shown it can generate cash on its own.
Industry Commentary
Low-earth-orbit broadband vs. geostationary incumbents
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Hughes's own Chapter 11 papers, as reported, say competition from SpaceX and Amazon's Leo has "permanently hurt its residential business." Their satellites orbit much closer to Earth, so they are faster and more responsive. Hughes had about 641,000 rural residential customers going into the filing and 622,000 subscribers at June 30. Broadband subscribers fell 21.7% in the year to June 30, 2025. The response is to shift toward enterprise, aviation and defense. That is the standard move for a geostationary operator losing consumers to low-orbit networks. Whether the enterprise pipeline can make up for the consumer decline is still unproven.

Spectrum as a traded asset
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

EchoStar's 2025–26 sales show two kinds of buyer paying for mid- and low-band spectrum: an established carrier (AT&T, adding 5G capacity) and a satellite operator (SpaceX, building a direct-to-phone Starlink constellation). The FCC approved both on May 12, 2026. As a condition, it required a creditor fund, reported as a $2.4B escrow in the SpaceX approval. For EchoStar the result is that licences once valued on a DCF of a network that was never finished are now priced by what someone actually paid for them.

Hybrid wireless — renting instead of building
EMERGING TAILWIND

By giving up its own network and running Boost on AT&T's, EchoStar swapped heavy capex for a wholesale cost. The first result shows up in the numbers: Wireless OIBDA went from −$98.9M in Q2 2025 to +$50.8M in Q2 2026 on flat revenue. The trade-off is subscribers. Boost lost 118,000 in the quarter, a year after adding 212,000. An operator that rents its network has to compete on price and distribution, not coverage.
