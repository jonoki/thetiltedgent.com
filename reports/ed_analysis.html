01 Company Overview

Consolidated Edison is the regulated energy-delivery monopoly for New York City and Westchester County — roughly 3.7 million electric customers, 1.1 million gas customers and 1,490 steam customers, served through its principal subsidiary Consolidated Edison Company of New York (CECONY), plus a smaller utility, Orange & Rockland (O&R), covering a 1,300-square-mile territory in southeastern New York and northern New Jersey. FY2025 revenue was $16.92B, up 10.9% from $15.26B in FY2024, with TTM revenue through June 2026 of $17.69B (+9.5%). The company ranks roughly #660 globally by market capitalisation at $39.5B, and carries a 53-year streak of consecutive annual dividend increases — among the longest in the S&P 500.

The central debate here is not growth versus no growth — a regulated utility's earnings are close to an engineering output of rate base times allowed return. It is price. Con Edison trades at roughly 17.5× trailing earnings with a 3.33% dividend yield while its authorized return on equity under the newly approved CECONY rate plan is 9.40% and its actual ROE is 8.96%. In other words, investors are paying about 1.54× book value for a business legally capped near a 9-handle return. That premium is only rational if the $41.7B 2026–2030 capital plan converts into rate base at close to the authorized return, and if New York regulators and politicians permit the bill increases that plan implies. Section 04 and the Industry Commentary take that apart. A second, quieter issue: reported GAAP earnings have been repeatedly flattered by asset sales, so the trailing multiple you see on a screener is not the multiple you are actually buying.

The core asset: the wires, substations and underground network that deliver electricity to New York City and Westchester. Roughly 3.7 million electric customers, 552 circuit miles of transmission line and 89,675 in-service transformers. Con Edison is a delivery business — it does not compete for customers, and in a franchise territory there is no share to win or lose. Gas service to roughly 1.1 million customers in Manhattan, the Bronx, Queens and Westchester, over nearly 4,400 miles of distribution mains. Con Edison Transmission develops and invests in electric transmission projects under FERC (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) oversight. It contributes almost nothing to revenue — $3M of non-utility revenue in FY2025 — but it has been a repeated source of one-off earnings. In 2026 it sold its equity interest in the Mountain Valley Pipeline, booking a $134M after-tax gain, $0.37 per share, which management explicitly excludes from adjusted EPS. Read alongside the 2023 disposal of the Clean Energy Businesses, the direction of travel is clear: Con Edison is retreating to a pure regulated-delivery balance sheet and monetising everything else to help fund the capital plan. That simplifies the story and removes the only parts of the business that could ever have earned above the allowed return.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
LEGAL MONOPOLY FRANCHISE IRREPLACEABLE UNDERGROUND NETWORK REVENUE DECOUPLING RATE-BASE EARNINGS FORMULA 142-YEAR OPERATING HISTORY 53-YEAR DIVIDEND STREAK BETA 0.26

Con Edison's moat is the strongest kind and the least valuable kind at the same time. It is absolute: no competitor can deliver a kilowatt-hour to a Manhattan apartment, and nobody is going to dig a second network under the densest city in North America. Beta of 0.26 is the market's arithmetic statement of that — this stock has roughly a quarter of the index's sensitivity to market swings. Revenue decoupling removes even weather and conservation risk from the revenue line. But the same regulatory bargain that grants the monopoly caps the return on it: the approved CECONY rate plan sets authorized ROE at 9.40% on a 48% equity ratio. Con Edison originally asked for 10.10% and was cut. So the honest framing of the moat is this: it guarantees that the earnings will exist, and guarantees they will never be spectacular. Everything hinges on how much capital the regulator lets the company put into rate base — which is why the $41.7B 2026–2030 plan is the whole growth story.

Political and affordability risk is the binding constraint. Con Edison serves the most politically scrutinised customer base in America, and its rate requests are public, contested proceedings. In the 2026–2028 case, the company requested a year-one electric increase of $1,612M and gas of $441M; what it received was a phased $222M / $473M / $329M on electric and an initial $46M decrease on gas. That is a very large gap between ask and award, and a 70-basis-point haircut to requested ROE on top of it. Every future case will be litigated against the same New York City affordability politics.

The capital plan requires equity the market must absorb. $41.7B of capital spend over 2026–2030 against a $39.5B market cap cannot be funded from an operating cash flow of $3.96B TTM against $4.82B of capex — which is exactly why free cash flow is negative $862M. The May 8, 2026 announcement of a $2B at-the-market equity programme is the tell. Every share issued below a reasonable multiple of book dilutes per-share growth against the 6–7% target.

Gas is a long-dated stranded-asset problem. New York's building-decarbonisation trajectory puts $11.5B of gas rate base on a path where the customer count shrinks while the remaining book value still has to be recovered. The approved rate plan already treats gas differently from electric — a $46M decrease in year one. Nobody has yet had to answer who pays for the last mile of pipe.

Interest rates set the valuation, not operations. With a 3.33% yield and a 0.26 beta, ED is bought as a bond substitute. The five-year chart in Section 07 is essentially a rates chart: the stock fell from $99 in July 2022 to $85.76 in September 2022 as yields spiked, and made its highs in March–April 2026 when the market was pricing easing. Nothing in the operating business explains those moves.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

An approved three-year rate plan removes the single biggest uncertainty a utility faces: CECONY has a 9.40% authorized ROE on a 48% equity ratio with electric rate base rising from $32.9B to $39.2B by 2028 and scheduled revenue increases of $222M / $473M / $329M. Behind it sits a $41.7B capital plan and management's 6–7% long-term adjusted EPS growth target, funded partly by non-dilutive asset sales (Clean Energy Businesses in 2023, Mountain Valley Pipeline in 2026). Pair that with a 3.33% yield, a 58% payout ratio, a 53-year increase streak and a 0.26 beta, and you own an inflation-linked, regulator-guaranteed income stream in the most electricity-hungry city in America — with electrification and data-centre load giving the rate base a reason to keep growing past 2030.

Bear Case

You are paying 17.5× earnings and 1.54× book for a business the state of New York has legally capped at a 9.40% return — and it is currently earning 8.96%, below even that. The regulator cut the ROE request from 10.10%, awarded $222M of year-one electric revenue against a $1,612M ask, and made gas revenue go down in 2026. FCF is −$862M, the capital plan is larger than the market cap, and the funding answer is a $2B ATM equity programme that dilutes the per-share growth being promised. Reported TTM EPS of $6.09 is flattered by a $0.37 one-off gain. And seven of eighteen analysts rate it Sell against two Buys — the average target of $110.28 implies 3.3% upside, less than the dividend.

01
Regulatory and affordability risk in New York. The 2026–2028 case showed how wide the gap between request and award can be: a $1,612M year-one electric ask became $222M, and a 10.10% ROE request became 9.40%. Every subsequent case will be fought in the same political environment, and the $41.7B capital plan only earns its return if the regulator keeps approving the spend into rate base.

A higher-for-longer rate environment raises both the cost of the debt and the discount rate applied to the stock — hitting earnings and multiple at the same time.

Industry Commentary
New York Affordability Politics vs. a $41.7B Build
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Con Edison's capital plan and its customers' bills are the same number viewed from two sides. The company asked for a $1,612M first-year electric increase and a $441M gas increase; the approved outcome was a phased $222M / $473M / $329M on electric and a $46M decrease on gas in year one, with authorized ROE set at 9.40% against the 10.10% requested. That is the regulator visibly rationing. Yet the plan still requires $6,595M of capex in 2026 and $6,759M in 2027, and rate base still has to climb from $32.9B to $39.2B on electric by 2028 for the growth algorithm to work. The constraint is not engineering or demand — it is how much bill increase New York City will tolerate. Every future rate case is a negotiation about that ceiling, and the last one suggests the ceiling is lower than the company's ask.

The Rate Cycle Owns the Multiple
ONGOING HEADWIND

With a 0.26 beta and a 3.33% yield, ED is priced against the long end of the Treasury curve, not against the S&P 500. The five-year chart makes this unarguable: the stock's worst monthly close of the period ($85.53 in September 2023) came in the same fiscal year as its highest reported EPS ($7.21 in FY2023), and its best monthly close ($113.18 in March 2026) came when easing was being priced. The headwind is double-barrelled — higher rates compress the multiple and raise the cost of the $28.32B debt stack that funds the rate base. Utilities are the one sector where the financing cost and the valuation input are literally the same variable.

Electrification and Load Growth — Bull vs. Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND

After two decades of flat demand, electricity load is growing again — building electrification, transport electrification, and interconnection demand. Con Edison's own marker is 28 new substations in service by 2035, and electric revenue grew 8.9% in FY2025 against gas at 16.2% and steam at 21.6% (the latter two on far smaller bases). The bull reading: load growth is the first genuine reason in a generation for a regulator to approve large rate-base expansion, which is exactly the input the 6–7% EPS growth target needs, and it is a multi-decade runway rather than a cycle. The bear reading: load growth does not change the allowed return, so it can only make the company bigger, not better — and it arrives simultaneously with an affordability fight and a gas network that has to be decommissioned while its book value is still being recovered. At 17.5× earnings and 1.54× book, the price already assumes the bull reading wins.
