01 Company Overview

Consolidated Edison is the regulated energy-delivery monopoly for New York City and Westchester County — roughly 3.7 million electric customers, 1.1 million gas customers and 1,490 steam customers, served through its principal subsidiary Consolidated Edison Company of New York (CECONY), plus a smaller utility, Orange & Rockland (O&R), covering a 1,300-square-mile territory in southeastern New York and northern New Jersey. FY2025 revenue was $16.92B, up 10.9% from $15.26B in FY2024, with TTM revenue through September 2026 of $18.07B; revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was up 8.9% on a year earlier. The company ranks #254 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $44.9B, and in January 2026 it announced its 52nd consecutive annual dividend increase, which the company says extends its record among S&P 500 utilities.

The central debate here is not growth versus no growth — a regulated utility's earnings are close to an engineering output of rate base (the value of the equipment the regulator lets it earn a return on) times the allowed return. It is price. Con Edison trades at roughly 19.3× trailing earnings with a 2.93% dividend yield while its authorized return on equity under the approved CECONY rate plan is 9.40% and its actual ROE is 9.10%. In other words, investors are paying about 1.70× book value (the shareholders' equity on the balance sheet) for a business whose return is legally capped at a little over 9%. That premium is only rational if the $37.7B 2026–2030 capital plan converts into rate base at close to the authorized return, and if New York regulators and politicians permit the bill increases that plan implies. The third quarter did not change that picture. Adjusted earnings per share were $2.07 against $1.90 a year earlier, in line with the $2.07 consensus on the Nasdaq earnings calendar, and the company narrowed its 2026 adjusted EPS guidance to $6.05–$6.15 from $6.00–$6.20 — the same $6.10 midpoint. The stock closed the release day (Nov 5, after-the-close release) at $121.44, fell 2.37% the next session to $118.56, and ended this edition at $121.00, 13.4% above the previous edition's $106.73. Section 04 and the Industry Commentary take apart what that price assumes. A second, quieter issue: reported GAAP earnings have been repeatedly flattered by asset sales, so the trailing multiple you see on a screener is not the multiple you are actually buying.

The core asset: the wires, substations and underground network that deliver electricity to New York City and Westchester. Roughly 3.7 million electric customers, 552 circuit miles of transmission line and 89,675 in-service transformers. Con Edison is a delivery business — it does not compete for customers, and in a franchise territory there is no share to win or lose. Earnings come from the approved average electric rate base of $32,935M in 2026, rising to $35,149M in 2027 and $39,174M in 2028 under the approved rate plan, earning an authorized return on equity of 9.40% on a 48% common equity ratio (the share of the asset base funded by shareholders rather than debt). The rate plan grants electric delivery revenue increases of $222M (2026), $473M (2027) and $329M (2028). The load story underneath is the interesting part: New York City electrification of buildings and transport, plus data-centre and grid-interconnection demand, is why management expects 28 new substations in service by 2035.

Addressable size: defined by regulation, not market — 2026 average electric rate base $32.9B, growing to $39.2B by 2028 per the approved plan. There is no third-party market-size estimate to quote.

A note on competition: no utility can bid for a household inside the CECONY franchise, so there are no direct competitor figures to show here.

Gas service to roughly 1.1 million customers in Manhattan, the Bronx, Queens and Westchester, over nearly 4,400 miles of distribution mains. Financially this is the same machine as electric — rate base times allowed return, with an approved average gas rate base of $11,485M in 2026 rising to $12,615M in 2028. Strategically it is the opposite of electric. Under the approved plan gas delivery revenue falls $46M in 2026 before rising $170M in 2027 and $93M in 2028 — a shape consistent with a regulator unwilling to let gas investment compound the way electric investment does. New York's building-electrification policy points toward a long-dated question no utility in the state has answered: who pays for the gas network's remaining book value once customers are being pushed off it. That is a stranded-asset risk (network cost that may never be recovered from customers) with a multi-decade fuse, not a next-quarter risk.

Addressable size: 2026 average gas rate base $11.5B → $12.6B by 2028 (approved plan). Growth deliberately slower than electric.

The Manhattan district steam system — 1,490 customers generating $703M of FY2025 revenue, or about $472,000 of revenue per customer. That ratio tells the story: these are large commercial and institutional buildings, not households. Steam is the smallest line and the most exposed to commercial real-estate occupancy and to the same decarbonisation pressure as gas, since the system is substantially fossil-fired. It is also the least likely part of the business to be replicated or attacked — nobody is building a new district steam network under Manhattan. Revenue grew fastest of the three lines in FY2025 (+21.6%), which on a base this small is commodity and rate mechanics, not a franchise expansion. In its November 5, 2026 release the company said it has reached agreement with its regulator and other stakeholders on a new investment plan for the Steam business, awaiting approval; nine-month 2026 steam revenue was $612M against $517M a year earlier (+18.4%).

Addressable size: fixed by the physical footprint of the Manhattan steam network.

Con Edison Transmission develops and invests in electric transmission projects under FERC (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) oversight. It contributes almost nothing to revenue — $3M of non-utility revenue in FY2025 — but it has been a repeated source of one-off earnings. In 2026 it sold its equity interest in the Mountain Valley Pipeline, booking a $134M after-tax gain, $0.37 per share, which management explicitly excludes from adjusted EPS. In October 2026 CECONY and Con Edison Transmission sold their interests in Honeoye Storage for $5M in aggregate (Q3 2026 earnings presentation); both sales followed a strategic alternatives review of the two stakes. Read alongside the 2023 disposal of the Clean Energy Businesses, the direction of travel is clear: Con Edison is retreating to a pure regulated-delivery balance sheet and monetising everything else to help fund the capital plan. That simplifies the story and removes the only parts of the business that could ever have earned above the allowed return.

Structural note: Clean Energy Businesses sold in 2023; MVP and Honeoye interests sold in 2026. What remains is CECONY, O&R and a much-reduced Transmission arm.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
LEGAL MONOPOLY FRANCHISE IRREPLACEABLE UNDERGROUND NETWORK REVENUE DECOUPLING RATE-BASE EARNINGS FORMULA 142-YEAR OPERATING HISTORY 52 STRAIGHT DIVIDEND INCREASES BETA 0.22

Con Edison's moat is the strongest kind and the least valuable kind at the same time. It is absolute: no competitor can deliver a kilowatt-hour to a Manhattan apartment, and nobody is going to dig a second network under the densest city in North America. Beta of 0.22 is the market's arithmetic statement of that — this stock has roughly a fifth of the index's sensitivity to market swings. Revenue decoupling removes even weather and conservation risk from the revenue line. But the same regulatory bargain that grants the monopoly caps the return on it: the approved CECONY rate plan sets authorized ROE at 9.40% on a 48% equity ratio. Con Edison originally asked for 10.10% and was cut. So the honest framing of the moat is this: it guarantees that the earnings will exist, and guarantees they will never be spectacular. Everything hinges on how much capital the regulator lets the company put into rate base — which is why the $37.7B 2026–2030 plan is the whole growth story.

Political and affordability risk is the binding constraint. Con Edison serves the most politically scrutinised customer base in America, and its rate requests are public, contested proceedings. In the 2026–2028 case, the company requested a year-one electric increase of $1,612M and gas of $441M; what it received was a phased $222M / $473M / $329M on electric and an initial $46M decrease on gas. That is a very large gap between ask and award, and a cut of 0.70 percentage points to the requested ROE on top of it. Every future case will be litigated against the same New York City affordability politics.

The capital plan requires equity the market must absorb. $37.7B of capital spend over 2026–2030 against a $44.9B market cap cannot be funded from operations: in the first nine months of 2026 operating cash flow was $2,825M against $4,051M of utility capital expenditures (Q3 10-Q) — which is exactly why free cash flow was negative $1,226M over those nine months. The May 8, 2026 announcement of a $2B at-the-market equity programme is the tell, and the 10-Q shows $991M of common shares issued in a public offering in the first nine months of 2026. Every share issued below a reasonable multiple of book dilutes per-share growth against the 6–7% target; the Q3 release itself lists share-issuance dilution of $0.05 a share against CECONY's quarterly earnings.

Gas is a long-dated stranded-asset problem. New York's building-decarbonisation trajectory puts $11.5B of gas rate base on a path where the customer count shrinks while the remaining book value still has to be recovered. The approved rate plan already treats gas differently from electric — a $46M decrease in year one. Nobody has yet had to answer who pays for the last mile of pipe.

Interest rates matter more than operations for the share price. With a 2.93% yield and a 0.22 beta, ED is bought as a bond substitute: an investor compares its dividend with what safe bonds pay. The five-year chart in Section 07 shows the stock falling from $99.27 in July 2022 to $85.76 in September 2022, and reaching its highest month-end closes in October 2026 ($116.64) and on this edition's November 13 close ($121.00).

Notable one-off items — this matters more than usual for ED. Three separate distortions sit in the reported numbers. Management is stripping out gains, not losses. Take the guidance range of $6.05–$6.15 as the honest earnings power, not the $6.26 TTM GAAP figure.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

An approved three-year rate plan removes the single biggest uncertainty a utility faces: CECONY has a 9.40% authorized ROE on a 48% equity ratio with electric rate base rising from $32.9B to $39.2B by 2028 and scheduled revenue increases of $222M / $473M / $329M. Behind it sits a $37.7B capital plan and management's 6–7% long-term adjusted EPS growth target, funded partly by non-dilutive asset sales (Clean Energy Businesses in 2023, Mountain Valley Pipeline in 2026). Pair that with a 2.93% yield, a 57% payout ratio, 52 consecutive annual dividend increases and a 0.22 beta, and you own an inflation-linked, regulator-guaranteed income stream in the most electricity-hungry city in America — with electrification and data-centre load giving the rate base a reason to keep growing past 2030.

Bear Case

You are paying 19.3× earnings and 1.70× book for a business the state of New York has legally capped at a 9.40% return — and it is currently earning 9.10%, below even that. The regulator cut the ROE request from 10.10%, awarded $222M of year-one electric revenue against a $1,612M ask, and made gas revenue go down in 2026. FCF is −$1,226M over the first nine months of 2026, the capital plan equals about 84% of the market cap, and the funding answer is a $2B ATM equity programme that dilutes the per-share growth being promised. Reported TTM EPS of $6.26 is flattered by a $0.37 one-off gain. And seven of nineteen analysts rate it Sell against three Strong Buys — the average target of $112.32 sits 7.2% below the share price.

01
Regulatory and affordability risk in New York. The 2026–2028 case showed how wide the gap between request and award can be: a $1,612M year-one electric ask became $222M, and a 10.10% ROE request became 9.40%. Every subsequent case will be fought in the same political environment, and the $37.7B capital plan only earns its return if the regulator keeps approving the spend into rate base.
02
Financing and dilution. Negative free cash flow of $1,226M in the first nine months of 2026, $27.99B of debt at 1.06× equity, and $6.6B of planned 2026 capital investment mean Con Edison must continuously access debt and equity markets. The $2B ATM is about 4.5% of the $44.9B market cap. A higher-for-longer rate environment raises both the cost of the debt and the discount rate applied to the stock — hitting earnings and multiple at the same time.
03
Gas stranded-asset exposure and one-off earnings quality. $11.5B of gas rate base faces a state policy trajectory that shrinks its customer base over decades, and the current plan already cuts 2026 gas revenue. Meanwhile the last several years of GAAP earnings have been repeatedly moved by asset sales — the 2023 Clean Energy disposal and the 2026 MVP gain — which makes the headline trailing multiple misleading and leaves the guided $6.05–$6.15 as the only clean number.
Industry Commentary
New York Affordability Politics vs. a $37.7B Build
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Con Edison's capital plan and its customers' bills are the same number viewed from two sides. The company asked for a $1,612M first-year electric increase and a $441M gas increase; the approved outcome was a phased $222M / $473M / $329M on electric and a $46M decrease on gas in year one, with authorized ROE set at 9.40% against the 10.10% requested. That is the regulator visibly rationing. Yet the plan still requires $6,595M of capex in 2026 and $6,759M in 2027, and rate base still has to climb from $32.9B to $39.2B on electric by 2028 for the growth algorithm to work. The constraint is not engineering or demand — it is how much bill increase New York City will tolerate. Every future rate case is a negotiation about that ceiling, and the last one suggests the ceiling is lower than the company's ask.

The Rate Cycle Sets the Multiple
ONGOING HEADWIND

With a 0.22 beta and a 2.93% yield, ED is priced against long-term Treasury yields (what safe government bonds pay), not against the S&P 500. The five-year chart shows the point: the stock's lowest month-end close since December 2021 ($85.53 in September 2023) came in the same fiscal year as its highest reported EPS ($7.21 in FY2023, inflated by the Clean Energy Businesses sale). The headwind is double-barrelled — higher rates lower the price investors will pay per dollar of earnings and raise the cost of the $27.99B of debt that funds the rate base. Utilities are the one sector where the financing cost and the valuation input are literally the same variable.

Electrification and Load Growth — Bull vs. Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND

After two decades of flat demand, electricity load is growing again — building electrification, transport electrification, and interconnection demand. Con Edison's own marker is 28 new substations in service by 2035, and electric revenue grew 8.9% in FY2025 against gas at 16.2% and steam at 21.6% (the latter two on far smaller bases). The bull reading: load growth is the first genuine reason in a generation for a regulator to approve large rate-base expansion, which is exactly the input the 6–7% EPS growth target needs, and it is a multi-decade runway rather than a cycle. The bear reading: load growth does not change the allowed return, so it can only make the company bigger, not better — and it arrives simultaneously with an affordability fight and a gas network that has to be decommissioned while its book value is still being recovered. At 19.3× earnings and 1.70× book, the price already assumes the bull reading wins.
