01 Company Overview

Everest is the house that other insurers go to when they want to lay off their own risk. It began in 1973 as Prudential Reinsurance, a subsidiary of Prudential Financial, listed in October 1995, took the name Everest Re in 1996, joined the S&P 500 on June 19, 2017 (replacing Mead Johnson Nutrition), and on July 10, 2023 renamed itself Everest Group, Ltd. and swapped its NYSE ticker from RE to EG. Same company, same share line — the long-run per-share history below is continuous. It has no stock splits on record.

The story of the last twelve months is a company deciding which table it wants to sit at. For five years Everest tried to build a primary insurance business alongside its reinsurance franchise. In October 2025 it folded that hand: it sold the renewal rights to its global retail commercial insurance book — about $2 billion of gross premium — to AIG, bought a $1.2 billion adverse development cover from Longtail Re on its North American insurance reserves for accident years 2024 and prior, and took $478 million of unfavourable prior-year reserve development in Q3 2025. Canada, Colombia and (announced August 5, 2026) Mexico insurance operations have since been sold or agreed for sale. What is left is a reinsurer with a specialty insurance arm, priced below its own book: $369.42 against a June 30, 2026 book value per share of $398.83, a price-to-book of 0.93x.

For an insurer, two numbers do most of the work. The combined ratio tells you whether the underwriting makes money; book value per share tells you whether the owner is getting richer. Everest's group combined ratio was 98.6% in FY2025 — barely profitable, dragged by a 114.6% insurance segment and $657 million of adverse prior-year development — and 91.5% for the first half of 2026. Book value per share rose from $322.97 at the end of 2024 to $379.83 at the end of 2025 and $398.83 at mid-2026. The central question: is the cleaned-up Everest a durable ~90% combined-ratio reinsurer that deserves to trade at or above book, or does a softening property-catastrophe market and a long casualty-reserve tail keep it at a discount?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

50-year treaty relationships Balance sheet as the product ($15.4B book) Broker-placed global distribution Underwriting data across cycles Bermuda capital structure Willingness to shrink in soft markets

Reinsurance is a trust business with a balance sheet attached. A cedant buys a promise that will be called in the worst year it ever has, so it wants a counterparty with capital, a long record of paying, and a seat on the panel it can count on at the next renewal. That is Everest's moat: scale of capital and continuity of relationship, not a product anyone else cannot copy. The vulnerability is that capital is a commodity when it is plentiful. When alternative capital and rival reinsurers pile in after good years, rates fall — 13% on Everest's property-cat book at April 1, 2026 — and the only defence is discipline.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The soft market is here. Property-catastrophe rates fell 13% on Everest's book at the April 1, 2026 renewals, and at the Florida June 1 renewals Williamson expected "rates are definitely going to be coming off." A reinsurer's combined ratio lags price by a year or more: the 88.5% Treaty ratio of Q2 2026 was earned on business priced in harder markets.
02
The casualty tail. FY2025 carried $657M of adverse prior-year development. The Longtail Re cover caps the North American insurance book for accident years 2024 and prior — but only above $5.4B of reserves, only up to $1.2B, and with Everest keeping $200M of co-participation. Reinsurance casualty reserves are not covered by it.
03
Catastrophe volatility is the business model. FY2025 absorbed $757M of pre-tax catastrophe losses; H1 2026 absorbed $224M. One large hurricane or earthquake can erase a year's underwriting income in a quarter, and Q3 is the peak of the Atlantic season.
04
A shrinking top line. Q2 2026 group premium fell 19.4% and Core premium 5.9%. Shrinking into a soft market is the right underwriting call, but it means earnings growth has to come from margin, investment income and buybacks rather than volume. Consensus revenue for 2026 is forecast to fall, even as consensus EPS rises.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 0.93x book for a reinsurer whose book value per share rose 17.6% in 2025 and another 5.0% in the first half of 2026, which is running a 91.5% group combined ratio this year and a 90.0% Core ratio in Q2, and which has already taken its medicine: the retail book is sold, the old North American insurance reserves carry a $1.2B cover, and H1 2026 prior-year development was favourable. Buybacks at a floor of $300M a quarter below book are accretive by arithmetic. At 7.87x trailing and 6.87x forward earnings, a simpler Everest that merely holds a low-90s combined ratio earns its way back to book — and every analyst target on the board is above the current price.

Bear Case

The market has seen this movie. FY2025 carried $657M of adverse development and a 98.6% combined ratio, and a sub-1x book multiple is the market's way of saying it does not fully trust the reserves behind that $398.83. The property-cat market is softening — 13% down on Everest's book at April 1 — so the margins of 2024–25 business will not repeat on 2026–27 business, and the company is shrinking premium to protect them. Revenue is forecast to fall, 10 of 16 analysts sit at Hold, and 6.89% of the float is short. A heavy hurricane quarter or one more casualty reserve top-up, and the discount to book is justified rather than an opportunity.

01
Casualty reserve inadequacy. $657M of adverse development in FY2025, $478M of it in one quarter. The Longtail Re cover protects only the North American insurance book for accident years 2024 and prior, above $5.4B of reserves; reinsurance casualty reserves remain fully exposed.
02
Soft pricing in property catastrophe. Rate down 13% at April 1, 2026 on Everest's book, with further softening expected at Florida June 1. Combined ratios lag price, so the damage shows in 2027 results, not 2026's.

Industry Commentary
The reinsurance cycle turns soft
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Reinsurance is a cyclical business priced on memory: after big-loss years capital is scarce and rates rise; after quiet years capital floods in and rates fall. 2026 is the second kind. Williamson said property-cat pricing "continued to soften with rate down 13% on our book globally" at April 1, and expected Florida rates at June 1 were "definitely going to be coming off." Everest's stated answer is selectivity — on the Q1 call Williamson said India pricing "was down sharply at 4/1 and we substantially cut our book of business in response" — and after Q2 he said the mid-year renewals showed "outperformance on rate and terms versus the market" (as quoted by Insurance Business). Premium falling while the combined ratio holds in the high 80s is what discipline looks like; premium falling while the ratio drifts toward 95% would be what losing the cycle looks like.

U.S. casualty and the reserve question
ONGOING HEADWIND

The industry-wide problem of the 2020s has been U.S. liability claims costing more than priced — larger jury awards and longer litigation. Everest's $478M Q3 2025 charge was concentrated in excess casualty and U.S. liability for accident years 2022–2024. Since January 2024 it has cut casualty premium by more than $1.2B, and Williamson has said a reversal would need normalisation of the U.S. legal environment. The Legacy segment's expected 110%+ combined ratio for 2026 is the tail of that book still being paid out.

The discount to book — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

EG trades at 0.93x book and 7.87x trailing earnings with a 2.17% dividend, a 4.73% buyback yield and debt at 0.23x equity. The bull framing: the reset is done — the problem book has been sold, the tail covered, the CFO seat refilled from outside, and the remaining business earns a low-90s combined ratio plus about $2B a year of investment income. Buying stock below book compounds book value per share mechanically. The bear framing: book value is only as good as the reserves under it, and insurers that have needed one large reserve charge often need another. The way to adjudicate is not the P/E; it is four consecutive quarters of neutral-to-favourable prior-year development and a Core combined ratio that holds near 90% as prices soften. Q1 and Q2 2026 both passed that test. Two quarters is not four.
