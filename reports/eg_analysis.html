01 Company Overview

Everest is the house that other insurers go to when they want to lay off their own risk. It began in 1973 as Prudential Reinsurance, a subsidiary of Prudential Financial, listed in October 1995, took the name Everest Re in 1996, joined the S&P 500 on June 19, 2017 (replacing Mead Johnson Nutrition), and on July 10, 2023 renamed itself Everest Group, Ltd. and swapped its NYSE ticker from RE to EG. Same company, same share line — the long-run per-share history below is continuous. It has no stock splits on record.

The story of the last twelve months is a company deciding which table it wants to sit at. For five years Everest tried to build a primary insurance business alongside its reinsurance franchise. In October 2025 it folded that hand: it sold the renewal rights to its global retail commercial insurance book — about $2 billion of gross premium — to AIG, bought a $1.2 billion adverse development cover from Longtail Re on its North American insurance reserves for accident years 2024 and prior, and took $478 million of unfavourable prior-year reserve development in Q3 2025. Canada, Colombia and (announced August 5, 2026) Mexico insurance operations have since been sold or agreed for sale. What is left is a reinsurer with a specialty insurance arm, priced at about its own book: $410.97 against a September 30, 2026 book value per share of $381.04, a price-to-book of 1.08x, or 0.99x the $414.00 book value per share that leaves out unrealised losses on its bond portfolio.

For an insurer, two numbers do most of the work. The combined ratio tells you whether the underwriting makes money; book value per share tells you whether the owner is getting richer. Everest's group combined ratio was 98.6% in FY2025 — barely profitable, dragged by a 114.6% insurance segment and $657 million of adverse prior-year development — then 91.5% for the first half of 2026 and 107.1% in Q3 2026, when Everest added about $600 million to its gross reserves for old casualty reinsurance business and absorbed $322 million of catastrophe losses; net of offsets, prior-year reserve development cost the group $268 million in the quarter. Book value per share rose from $322.97 at the end of 2024 to $379.83 at the end of 2025 and $398.83 at mid-2026, then fell to $381.04 at September 30, 2026 as the market value of its bonds dropped; leaving those unrealised losses out, it rose to $414.00. The central question: is the cleaned-up Everest a durable ~90% combined-ratio reinsurer that has now reserved its old casualty business properly, or do a softening property-catastrophe market and a long casualty-reserve tail keep it from earning much more than its book?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

50-year treaty relationships Balance sheet as the product ($14.3B book) Broker-placed global distribution Underwriting data across cycles Bermuda capital structure Willingness to shrink in soft markets

Reinsurance is a trust business with a balance sheet attached. An insurer buying reinsurance buys a promise that will be called in the worst year it ever has, so it wants a counterparty with capital, a long record of paying, and a seat on the panel it can count on at the next renewal. That is Everest's moat: scale of capital and continuity of relationship, not a product anyone else cannot copy. The vulnerability is that capital is a commodity when it is plentiful. When alternative capital and rival reinsurers pile in after good years, rates fall — 13% on Everest's property-cat book at April 1, 2026 — and the only defence is discipline.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The soft market is here. Property-catastrophe rates fell 13% on Everest's book at the April 1, 2026 renewals, and for January 1, 2027 Williamson told analysts "it'll be another competitive renewal," adding: "You're looking at some additional rate decreases." A reinsurer's results lag price by a year or more: the 84.9% Treaty attritional ratio of Q3 2026 was earned on business priced when rates were higher.
02
The casualty tail. FY2025 carried $657M of adverse prior-year development, and Q3 2026 another $253M in reinsurance after about $600M of gross casualty strengthening. The Longtail Re cover caps the North American insurance book for accident years 2024 and prior — but only above $5.4B of reserves, only up to $1.2B, and with Everest keeping $200M of those losses itself. Reinsurance casualty reserves, where the Q3 charge landed, are not covered by it; asked about a similar cover there, Williamson said: "We could, I think if conditions were right."
03
Catastrophe volatility is the business model. FY2025 absorbed $757M of pre-tax catastrophe losses; the first nine months of 2026 absorbed $546M ($224M in H1 and $322M in Q3, led by the Colombia earthquake and events in the Middle East). One large hurricane or earthquake can erase a year's underwriting income in a quarter.
04
A shrinking top line. Q3 2026 group premium fell 14.5% and Core premium 3.2%. Shrinking into a soft market is the right underwriting call, but it means earnings growth has to come from margin, investment income and buybacks rather than volume. The analyst consensus on StockAnalysis has revenue falling again in 2026 and 2027.

Notable One-Off Items

Four items distort the reported figures. First, the Q3 2026 reserve strengthening: $268M of net unfavourable prior-year development for the group ($253M in Reinsurance Treaty, including about $600M of gross casualty strengthening), plus $322M of catastrophe losses. It replaces the Q3 2025 charge ($478M) inside the TTM window. Second, buybacks: $1.2B repurchased in the first nine months of 2026 (3,426,047 shares), $474M of it in Q3; 37.4M shares were outstanding at September 30, 11.0% fewer than the 42.0M a year earlier, and the quarterly floor is $300M. Per-share metrics therefore grow faster than absolute profit. Third, unrealised bond losses: they grew by $891M in Q3 and cut reported book value per share to $381.04, while the measure that leaves them out rose to $414.00. Fourth, revenue is shrinking by design as the Legacy book runs off, so top-line growth rates say little about the Core franchise.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Industry Commentary
The reinsurance cycle turns soft
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Reinsurance is a cyclical business priced on memory: after big-loss years capital is scarce and rates rise; after quiet years capital floods in and rates fall. 2026 is the second kind: rate on Everest's property-cat book fell 13% at April 1, and for the January 1, 2027 renewal Williamson said on the Q3 call "it'll be another competitive renewal" and "You're looking at some additional rate decreases." Everest's stated answer is selectivity: Treaty premium fell 5.3% in Q3, with casualty excess-of-loss down 24.8%. Premium falling while the attritional combined ratio holds in the mid-80s (84.9% for Treaty in Q3) is what discipline looks like; premium falling while that ratio drifts toward 95% would be what losing the cycle looks like.

U.S. casualty and the reserve question
ONGOING HEADWIND

The industry-wide problem of the 2020s has been U.S. liability claims costing more than priced — larger jury awards and longer litigation. Of Everest's $478M Q3 2025 charge, $361M was for U.S. casualty insurance reserves, mainly accident years 2022–2024, driven by excess casualty and U.S. liability lines. In his FY2025 shareholder letter Williamson wrote that Everest had cut casualty premium by more than $1.2B since early 2024, pointing to "legal system abuse" as the cause of rising claim costs in U.S. casualty. The Legacy segment's expected 110%+ combined ratio for 2026 is the tail of that book still being paid out. In Q3 2026 the problem showed up on the reinsurance side: about $600M of gross casualty reserve strengthening, mostly for underwriting years 2022 and prior, of which the CFO said about two-thirds came from North American casualty.

Price against book — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

EG trades at 1.08x book (0.99x leaving out unrealised bond losses) and 8.64x trailing earnings with a 1.95% dividend, a 6.38% buyback yield (StockAnalysis) and debt at 0.25x equity. The bull framing: the reserve work is done twice over — the problem retail book has been sold, the insurance tail covered, casualty reinsurance reserves topped up and, per management, booked more than $850M above the actuarial central estimate — and the remaining business earns a high-80s attritional combined ratio plus a record $634M of quarterly investment income. Buying stock below the $414.00 book value that leaves out bond losses adds to it per share. The bear framing: book value is only as good as the reserves under it, and Everest has needed reserve additions in FY2025, Q2 2026 and Q3 2026. The way to judge it is not the P/E; it is four consecutive quarters of neutral-to-favourable prior-year development and a Core combined ratio that holds near 90% as prices soften. Q1 and Q2 2026 passed that test on a net basis; Q3 did not, so the count starts again.
