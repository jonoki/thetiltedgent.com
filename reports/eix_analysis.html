01 Company Overview

On the evening of January 7, 2025, a fire started in Eaton Canyon above Altadena. By the time it was contained 24 days later it had burned 14,021 acres, destroyed roughly 9,400 structures and killed at least 17 people (sources differ: Edison's newsroom says 17; Wikipedia and the LA Times report 19). Edison International closed December 2024 at $79.84. It closed January 2025 at $54.00. Twenty months later, on Monday September 21, 2026, it closed at $54.95. Everything that has happened to this company since — a higher rate base, a higher dividend, strong core earnings growth — has netted to roughly zero for the share price. This report explains why.

Edison International is the holding company for Southern California Edison (SCE), which delivers power to "15 million people in 50,000 square miles across central, coastal and Southern California." It is a regulated utility: SCE invests in poles, wires and substations, the regulator adds that investment to its rate base, and SCE earns an authorised return on it — 10.03% on CPUC-regulated equity, 10.30% on FERC-regulated transmission. Management projects the rate base growing from $47.6B (2025) to $67.9B (2030), roughly 7% a year, and reaffirmed 2026 core EPS guidance of $5.90–$6.20 on July 30, 2026.

That is the boring half. The other half is a liability with no number on it. SCE's own words: "Absent additional evidence, SCE believes that it is likely that its equipment was associated with the ignition of the Eaton Fire." The company says it is "currently unable to estimate potential losses." California's Wildfire Fund — $21B when the fire started, topped up by $18B of new money in 2025 under SB 254 — stands between the claims and Edison's balance sheet. On August 31, 2026, the legislature's wildfire bill (SB 492) took shape without the protections investors wanted, and EIX fell 23% in a day — its worst session since the 2001 California energy crisis. The next day the bill died without a vote on the session's final day (CalMatters). The stock now trades at 5.7x trailing GAAP earnings and yields 6.39%. The market is not pricing a utility. It is pricing a claim.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Legal monopoly franchise 15M people, 50,000 sq mi Regulated ROE 10.03% / 10.30% ~7% rate base growth to 2030 Electrification load growth Wildfire Fund backstop

A utility's moat is its franchise: no competitor is going to string a second set of wires down the same street. SCE's customers cannot leave, the regulator lets it earn a return on everything it prudently builds, and California's electrification push gives it a long list of things to build. On those terms this is as protected as a business gets. The problem is that California law exposes utilities to inverse condemnation, so the moat that protects revenue does nothing to protect the equity from a fire its equipment starts. The Wildfire Fund was built to fill exactly that gap. August 31 was the market's verdict that it may not be large enough.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
An unquantified liability sits ahead of the equity. Edison's funding stack, per its Q2 2026 slide: the first $1B of Eaton claims comes from customer-funded self-insurance (including a $12.5M shareholder contribution); then reimbursement from the Wildfire Fund up to its remaining capacity; above that capacity, SB 254 gives the ability to securitise, subject to CPUC approval. If the CPUC later finds SCE acted imprudently, it must pay the Fund back. Every layer depends on a regulator or a court not yet heard from.
02
SB 492 died and left the Fund's weaknesses in place. The bill died without a vote on September 1, 2026 (CalMatters). As reported by TIKR and 24/7 Wall St (not confirmed on a primary source), the version on the table had dropped Governor Newsom's proposal to shield utilities from insurer subrogation suits, left out a $6B per-incident cap on Fund withdrawals, left the 2028 sunset in place and added no mechanism to refill the Fund once it runs dry. A single fire could use up the Fund that is meant to protect every California investor-owned utility.
03
Credit ratings at the bottom of investment grade. TIKR reports SCE at BBB- (not confirmed on a rating-agency page), the bottom rung of investment grade, with Moody's and S&P having warned that a weak legislative outcome could trigger downgrades. Pizarro himself said downgrades were a "strong likelihood" if action fell short. A move to junk would raise the cost of the $9–12B of incremental debt in the financing plan, and that cost flows straight into EPS.
04
Affordability. Rate base growth is only earnings growth if the CPUC keeps approving it, and SCE's customers are already paying Wildfire Fund charges, extended through 2035 with continuation charges planned from 2036 to 2045 (PBS SoCal). Every dollar of wildfire cost passed to ratepayers makes the next rate case harder.

Notable One-Off Items

The gap between GAAP and core earnings is the whole story of the income statement. In the first half of 2025 SCE booked $1,343M (pre-tax, $3.49 per share) of "wildfire-related recoveries, net of claims and expenses", tied to cost recovery for earlier fires — management's slides reference the TKM Settlement Agreement and Woolsey recovery. That pushed H1 2025 GAAP EPS to $4.62 against core EPS of $2.34, and it is why FY2025 GAAP EPS ($11.55) and TTM EPS ($9.69) are far above anything the regulated business earns in a normal year. In the other direction, SCE books a recurring non-core "Wildfire Fund expense" of about $0.09 per share a quarter, and Q2 2026 included a $0.08 per share loss on the Trio disposition. Management expects to have recovered $11B+ of historical costs by end-2027, including a planned Woolsey securitisation. None of this includes any Eaton Fire loss accrual, because none has been booked.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 9x management's 2026 core EPS guidance ($5.90–$6.20) for a monopoly with a $50.8B rate base growing ~7% a year, 10.03%/10.30% allowed returns, 2027 and 2028 guidance already on the table, no equity issuance planned through 2030, and a 6.39% dividend yield. The Eaton liability is real, but it sits behind a customer-funded first $1B, a Wildfire Fund recapitalised by SB 254, and a securitisation option for anything above it. The program is settling claims fast: $860M+ offered, 77%+ acceptance, and subrogation settled at around 55 cents on the dollar with two insurers. As TIKR put it: "Sacramento raised Edison's cost of capital. It did not erase the earnings underneath it." If the liability is contained within the Fund, a 9x multiple on a regulated earnings stream is a very large discount.

Bear Case

The bull case is a sum of things a regulator or court has not yet decided. Edison itself says it is likely its equipment was involved and that it cannot estimate losses. Third-party estimates of total losses run from $24B to $45B (UCLA), against a Fund that held $21B at the time of the fire. The legislature just adjourned without passing SB 492, so nothing new caps withdrawals, blocks subrogation or refills the Fund. SCE's rating sits at BBB- (per TIKR), one notch from junk, with the CEO on record that downgrades were likely without action. A downgrade raises the cost of $9–12B of planned new debt, and if the CPUC later finds imprudence, SCE must repay the Fund. Bank of America's $51 target says the discount may not be large enough.

Industry Commentary
California's wildfire liability regime
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Under California's inverse condemnation doctrine a utility can owe for fire damage its equipment causes even without negligence. That rule pushed PG&E into bankruptcy in 2019 and produced AB 1054 and the Wildfire Fund. The Fund was meant to be a shared backstop for all the state's investor-owned utilities. The Eaton Fire tested whether one fire could use it up; state officials warned the claims could "fully exhaust the Fund" (LA Times, July 2025). SB 254 in 2025 added $18B, with ratepayer charges extended through 2035 and continuation charges planned for 2036–2045. SB 492 in August 2026 was meant to finish the job. Instead, per TIKR and 24/7 Wall St, it dropped the subrogation shield, the $6B per-incident withdrawal cap and any refill mechanism, and kept the 2028 sunset. The bill then died without a vote on September 1 (CalMatters).

Subrogation: the insurers' claim on the utility
ONGOING HEADWIND

When a homeowner's insurer pays out, it can sue the utility to get its money back. Those subrogation claims are typically the largest block of wildfire liability. Past utility settlements have run at 50 to 70 cents on the dollar, and a 40-cent cap was proposed in 2025 (LAist, August 2025). Edison has settled with two insurers at about 55 cents. Governor Newsom's proposal to end subrogation suits against utilities failed on August 31, 2026 (Motley Fool). As long as that door stays open, insurers — the best-capitalised claimants — can pursue Edison, or the Fund, for most of what they paid.

Electrification and grid hardening
EMERGING TAILWIND

The long-run case for SCE is load growth. Management expects demand up ~30–40% by 2035 and nearly doubling by 2045 as transport and buildings electrify, supporting a $38–41B five-year capex plan and ~7% rate base growth. Wildfire mitigation is part of that capital: ~90% of high-fire-risk distribution is already hardened, and a risk-prioritised 2029+ plan is in the RAMP filing. The bull framing: every mile hardened lowers the odds of the next Eaton and earns an allowed return. The bear framing: hardening reduces future risk but does nothing about the liability already incurred, and each capex dollar adds to bills the state is already struggling to justify.
