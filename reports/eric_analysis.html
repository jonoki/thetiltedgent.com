01 Company Overview

Ericsson builds the equipment and software that mobile operators use to run their networks: the radios and antennas on cell towers, the "core" software that routes calls and data, and the services to install and run it all. It also licenses its mobile-standard patents to phone makers, and runs an enterprise unit built around Vonage, a cloud communications business. It began in 1876 as Lars Magnus Ericsson's mechanical workshop in central Stockholm. Sales over the four quarters to June 30, 2026 were SEK 227.5B (about $22.7B), and the Networks segment brought in 64% of them. For full-year 2025, sales fell to SEK 236.7B from SEK 247.9B in kronor, though they rose 2% organically; diluted EPS was SEK 8.51, lifted by a SEK 7.6B gain on selling the iconectiv unit.

The 2026 story is a company with rebuilt margins and too little growth. Adjusted gross margin was 48.4% in Q2 2026, the balance sheet carries SEK 59.8B of net cash, and the board is returning money through a SEK 3.00 dividend and a SEK 15B share buyback. The market liked that story until May: the ADS rose from $9.65 at the end of 2025 to a $13.06 May close, up 35.3%. It has since fallen 28.9% to $9.29. The CEO change was announced on June 16. The July 14 Q2 report showed sales down 6% (1% organically) and warned about component cost inflation; the ADS fell 13.5% that day. Morgan Stanley downgraded the stock to Underweight on September 22. On October 1 a new chief executive, Per Narvinger, took over. The central debate: the radio market is flat (Dell'Oro expects it to stay stable in 2026), so is Ericsson now a cash-return story, or can a new CEO find growth in defence, enterprise and the AI-era network he inherits?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Installed base at major operators Patent portfolio (~SEK 13.5B/yr) Standards and R&D depth Net cash SEK 59.8B Managed-services lock-in

The moat is incumbency plus patents. Once an operator has built a region on one vendor's radios and core, swapping vendors means re-engineering the network, so upgrades and expansions usually go to the incumbent. The patent book, built from its work on mobile standards, earns a licensing stream (about SEK 13.5B a year on a recurring basis) that does not depend on selling hardware. What the moat does not protect is volume: when operators finish a 5G build-out, they stop buying, however entrenched the vendor.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A flat market leaves little room to grow. In the Q4 2025 report Ekholm said: "For 2026, we expect the RAN market to be flat." Dell'Oro's May 2026 estimate agrees. Reported sales fell 8% in H1 2026 (SEK 102.0B against SEK 111.2B, with SEK −9.6B of that from currency), and the Infront consensus that Ericsson publishes (July 27, 15 contributors) has full-year 2026 sales of SEK 225.7B, 4.6% below 2025.
02
Component costs are rising. In the Q2 report Ekholm wrote: "In Q2, we took action to mitigate component cost inflation. As the impact builds in the coming quarters, we will continue to pursue internal measures and pricing actions to help offset the effect." He added: "We also expect some pressure on Networks adjusted gross margin in Q3 due to higher volumes of network rollout projects." Gross margin has been the core of the recovery story, so these two sentences matter.
03
Legal overhang from the Iraq investigation. The US Department of Justice is still investigating matters from Ericsson's 2019 internal Iraq investigation, and the company says the scope and duration "remain uncertain." Four civil suits under the US Anti-Terrorism Act (filed 2022, 2024, 2025 and March 2026) allege that payments by the company ultimately aided terrorist groups. In Sweden, 93 claimants are suing over alleged inadequate disclosure. China's market regulator (SAMR) has been investigating Ericsson's patent licensing there since 2019. Ericsson says it will vigorously defend the civil cases.
04
Currency works against it both ways. About half of 2025 sales were in US dollars and over 15% in euros, while Ericsson reports in Swedish kronor. A stronger krona cut H1 2026 reported sales by SEK 9.6B. US holders of the ADS face the same exposure in reverse: over five years the ADS fell 17.1% while the Stockholm B share fell 6.0%, the gap being the krona's fall against the dollar.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the iconectiv sale in Q3 2025 produced a SEK 7.6B capital gain. It sits inside TTM EPS, net margin, ROE and ROCE, and inside the Enterprise segment's rolling margin. Second, restructuring: SEK 4.4B of charges in H1 2026 against SEK 0.9B a year earlier, which is why Q1 2026 net income was only SEK 0.9B and why Ericsson reports "adjusted" figures that exclude them. Third, patent timing: Q2 2025 included non-recurring licensing revenue from a partial settlement of a patent dispute, which makes Q2 2026's −1% organic sales look weaker than the underlying trend. The company says Networks organic sales were broadly stable without IPR. Share count matters too: Ericsson held 108.5 million B shares in treasury at September 25, 2026, against 38.0 million at the end of 2025, as the buyback runs.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay about 7.6 times 2026 consensus EBITA for the whole business once SEK 59.8B of net cash is netted off, for a company with a 48% adjusted gross margin, about SEK 13.5B a year of recurring patent income, and SEK 27.8B of trailing free cash flow. Between the SEK 15B buyback and the SEK 3.00 dividend, roughly 8% of the market value is going back to holders over about a year. Cloud Software and Services has turned from a problem into a 12% EBITA-margin business. The market already priced in the bad Q2, a CEO change and a Morgan Stanley downgrade, which leaves the ADS 28.9% below May. A new CEO who knows both network segments, a weaker krona than the company's Q3 planning rate, and any sign that defence, enterprise or AI traffic can lift a flat RAN market would all work from a low base of expectations.

Bear Case

Ericsson sells into a market that its former CEO calls flat and Dell'Oro calls stable, and reported sales are falling: −8% in H1 2026, with consensus at −4.6% for the year. The margin gains came from cost cuts and mix, and component cost inflation now pushes the other way, which management has said it will work to offset with internal measures and pricing. Trailing earnings are inflated by a one-off SEK 7.6B gain, the Enterprise unit still loses money, and the Iraq-related DOJ investigation and four US civil suits remain open with no date. The US analyst panels range from Hold to Sell, and a new CEO arriving in a cost squeeze can reset targets at his first report. Cheap on cash flow can stay cheap if the cash only comes from shrinking.

Top 3 Risks

01
Gross-margin squeeze from component costs and project mix. Management expects pressure on Networks adjusted gross margin in Q3 and says the component-cost impact builds in coming quarters. Gross margin has been the core of the recovery story, and the stock fell 13.5% on the day this was flagged.
02
Shrinking sales in a flat RAN market. With North American Networks sales declining and Europe finishing modernisation projects, reported sales are falling. Without a new growth driver, buybacks and cost cuts carry per-share earnings on their own.
03
Legal and governance overhang. The DOJ Iraq-related investigation is open with an uncertain scope, four Anti-Terrorism Act suits name the company and its former CEO, Swedish shareholder claims proceed on the merits, and China's SAMR patent-licensing probe could bring remedies. All of this lands on a new CEO in his first year.
Industry Commentary
A flat radio market after the 5G build-out
STRUCTURAL CONSTRAINT

Mobile network equipment sells in waves: a new generation (4G, then 5G) triggers years of heavy spending, then operators sweat the assets. Ericsson's Q4 2025 report said "For 2026, we expect the RAN market to be flat," and Dell'Oro's May 2026 estimate is a stable market. The Q2 2026 report shows that pattern regionally. European Networks sales fell on "the gradual completion of modernization projects," North America declined, and growth came from Japan, South East Asia, India and parts of the Middle East and Africa. In a market like this, share and margin matter more than volume, which is why Ericsson keeps cutting costs and headcount (86,536 people at June 30, down 5.9% in a year).

Component inflation and the margin question
NEAR-TERM HEADWIND

In Q2 2026 the adjusted gross margin was 48.4%, which Ekholm described as "up by 2 percentage points after normalizing for the one-off benefit of the IPR settlement last year." The same report flagged component cost inflation and more low-margin rollout work in Q3. Ericsson's Q3 guide still asks for a 48–50% Networks adjusted gross margin, against 50.4% in Q2. The Q3 print on October 15 tests whether pricing actions and internal measures can hold the margin while input costs rise. The stock nearly tripled from its 2023 low while that margin recovered.

AI-era networks and the new CEO's opening hand
POSSIBLE TAILWIND

Ekholm's last quarterly letter said Ericsson had "expanded into attractive growth areas, positioning Ericsson to capitalize as AI increasingly moves into the physical world." The Q2 report's highlights include AI-enabled drone sensing using existing cell towers, demonstrated at a Texas stadium. In January the company said it planned to increase investment in defence during 2026, and that mission-critical and enterprise markets were expected to grow. None of this is yet large enough to show in the segment numbers: Enterprise is 8% of sales and loss-making. Per Narvinger inherits a business with SEK 59.8B of net cash, a SEK 15B buyback under way and a flat core market. Whether he keeps returning cash or spends some of it on growth is the open question for the next twelve months.
