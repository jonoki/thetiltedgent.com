01 Company Overview

Eversource is New England's wires-and-pipes utility: electric transmission, electric distribution and natural gas distribution for 4.4 million customers across Connecticut, Massachusetts and New Hampshire, over roughly 4,500 miles of transmission line and 59,900 miles of distribution line. It was Northeast Utilities until April 2015. FY2025 revenue was $13.55B, up 13.84%, with GAAP EPS of $4.56 and non-GAAP recurring EPS of $4.76. At $66.89 the market values the equity at $25.2B — against a regulated rate base of about $34B (ex-water, per the Q2 2026 investor slides).

The story of the last five years is a utility that wandered off the reservation and walked back. Eversource took 50% stakes in Ørsted's US offshore wind projects — South Fork, Revolution Wind, Sunrise Wind — then spent 2023–2024 getting out: the South Fork and Revolution Wind stakes went to Global Infrastructure Partners for $745 million, about $375 million below the original $1.12 billion, with an expected $520 million loss on the exit. The tail is still wagging: in Q2 2026 Eversource booked another $164 million after-tax ($0.43/share) for expected future payments to GIP tied to Revolution Wind cost increases. On June 30, 2026 it also closed the $1.7 billion sale of Aquarion, its water utility, completing the move to what management calls a pure-play regulated electric and gas utility. What remains is a simple bet with two dials: how fast the rate base grows, and what return on equity the regulators let it earn. In 2026 the second dial turned the wrong way — FERC cut the New England transmission base ROE from 10.57% to 9.57% in March, and full-year guidance came down from $4.80–$4.95 to $4.57–$4.72.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territories 4.4M captive customers $34B regulated rate base 8.3% rate-base CAGR plan FERC transmission franchise 26 years of dividend growth

A utility has no competitors in the usual sense — nobody strings a second set of wires down a Hartford street. The moat is the franchise itself, and its price is regulation: the company earns what the regulators allow on what it invests, not what the market would bear. That makes Eversource's real counterparty not a rival but three state commissions and FERC. The vulnerability is the same thing seen from the other side. When a regulator trims the allowed ROE, disallows a cost or delays a case, there is no pricing lever to pull — and New England customers pay some of the highest bills in the country, which makes every ask political.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Connecticut regulatory relationship. CL&P had gone nearly a decade without a rate review; the July 2026 filing asks for $451M and ~11% on customers' bills. The storm-cost decision already showed the tone — ~$105M of the request not granted up front and no retroactive carrying charges. A low outcome in mid-2027 would hit the largest distribution utility in the group.
02
Allowed-ROE compression at FERC. The March 2026 order took 100bps off the transmission base ROE and cut 2026 guidance. The Section 205 request for 11.39% is a request; the outcome is expected by November 30, 2026.
03
The offshore wind tail. Two years after selling out, Eversource took a further $164M after-tax charge in Q2 2026 for contingent payments to GIP on Revolution Wind cost increases. The project was ~97% complete with commercial operation expected later in 2026 — until it does, the tail can move again.
04
Leverage and free cash flow. Total debt of $29.81B against $25.2B of market cap, Debt/EBITDA of 6.0x, interest coverage of 2.4x, and TTM free cash flow of just $298M after $4.13B of capex. The dividend and the capex plan are funded by the capital markets, not by surplus cash.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $81.76 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $66.89, ES lost 18.2% of its price. A utility is bought for the dividend, and roughly $14 per share was paid over the period (an estimate from annual dividends per share, not a tallied series), which brings the five-year total return to roughly flat to slightly negative before reinvestment — five years of collecting coupons on a principal that shrank. The path explains it: a 2022 high of $92.32 (May), then a two-year slide as interest rates rose and the offshore wind venture unravelled, bottoming at $53.79 in October 2023, then a slow, uneven climb back into the $60s and $70s.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 17.0 in October 2023 — the most oversold reading in the window — and 73.2 in October 2025, its most overbought. It now reads 41.6, on the soft side of neutral after three down months. The 10-month moving average sits at about $70.18, above the current price: the stock has slipped under its own trend since the FERC order. Note that September's close is the ex-dividend day, so about $0.79 of the month's decline is the dividend leaving the share price, not the market repricing the company.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The distractions are gone. Offshore wind is sold, water is sold, and what is left is a pure regulated utility with a rate base planned to grow at 8.3% a year to $49.3B by 2030 behind a $26.5B capital plan — and only 2% of the funding from new equity. You are paying 14.4x the 2026 guidance midpoint and collecting a 4.71% yield that has grown for 26 straight years. The two regulatory blows of 2026 are already in the numbers: guidance was cut, the refund booked. If FERC lands above the 9.57% assumption in November, or Connecticut delivers a constructive CL&P decision in 2027, the earnings base steps up from a price that has already given back the whole February rally.

Bear Case

Rate base growth only turns into earnings if regulators allow a decent return on it, and the 2026 evidence runs the other way: 100bps off the transmission ROE at FERC, storm costs trimmed and carrying charges denied in Connecticut, and an 11% bill-increase request now sitting in front of that same commission. The balance sheet has little slack — 6.0x debt/EBITDA, 2.4x interest coverage, $298M of TTM free cash flow against a ~$1.2B annual dividend bill (estimate: $3.15 × 376.67M shares) — and the offshore wind exit keeps sending invoices two years after closing. Three Sell ratings and a $52 low target say part of the Street thinks 14x is fair for a utility whose allowed returns are being ratcheted down.

Top 3 Risks

01
Connecticut. The CL&P rate case (decision mid-2027) is the largest single swing factor. The storm-cost decision — ~$105M not granted up front, no retroactive carrying charges — is the most recent read on the commission's stance.
02
Allowed-return compression. Transmission base ROE is now 9.57% with a 10-year Treasury around 4.33% when it was set. Every 100bps of ROE on a $34B rate base is a large share of a ~$1.75B recurring-earnings run-rate (estimate: $4.65 × ~377M shares); if rates stay high while allowed ROEs fall, the equity-risk spread keeps thinning.
03
Funding and credit. FFO/debt of 14.3% (S&P) is above the downgrade threshold but the plan needs $7.0–7.5B of new debt and $0.8–1.1B of equity over five years. A regulatory setback that cut cash flow would push the company toward either more equity (dilution) or a downgrade (higher cost of capital on the whole plan).
Industry Commentary
Affordability politics in New England
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A regulated utility's growth is a request, not a decision. Eversource wants to spend $26.5B over five years on wires, substations, gas pipe and meters, and every dollar of it ends up on a customer's bill once it enters rate base. The CL&P filing alone implies roughly an 11% total bill increase. That is the tension in one sentence: the capital plan is the growth story for shareholders and the affordability problem for regulators, and both are looking at the same number.

Allowed ROE versus the cost of money
ONGOING HEADWIND

Utility equity is priced partly as a bond substitute. When FERC set the New England base ROE at 9.57% the 10-year Treasury stood at 4.33%, per 24/7 Wall St. — a spread of about 5.2 points for owning a leveraged, regulated equity. Eversource's own asks (10.25% in Connecticut, 11.39% at FERC) are the company's view of what that spread should be. The gap between asked and granted is the whole investment case.

Transmission build-out
EMERGING TAILWIND

Regional grid planning is creating new transmission work that did not exist in the old plans. ISO New England's preliminary selection of the ~$2.2B Maine-to-southern-New-England line (Eversource ~$700M, benefit-to-cost ratio 2.2x per the company's slides) is the first concrete example, with about $350M potentially falling within the current five-year window. Transmission is the segment where the ROE fight is happening — which is exactly why the November FERC decision matters more than its size suggests.
