01 Company Overview

Eversource is New England's wires-and-pipes utility: electric transmission, electric distribution and natural gas distribution for 4.4 million customers across Connecticut, Massachusetts and New Hampshire, over roughly 4,500 miles of transmission line and 59,900 miles of distribution line. It was Northeast Utilities until April 2015. FY2025 revenue was $13.55B, up 13.84%, with GAAP EPS of $4.56 and non-GAAP recurring EPS of $4.76. At $69.84 the market values the equity at $26.3B (377.03M shares × $69.84, our calculation) — against a regulated rate base of about $34B (ex-water, at year-end 2025, per the Q3 2026 investor slides).

The story of the last five years is a utility that wandered off the reservation and walked back. Eversource took 50% stakes in Ørsted's US offshore wind projects — South Fork, Revolution Wind, Sunrise Wind — then spent 2023–2024 getting out: the South Fork and Revolution Wind stakes went to Global Infrastructure Partners for $745 million, about $375 million below the original $1.12 billion, with an expected $520 million loss on the exit. The tail is still wagging: in Q2 2026 Eversource booked another $164 million after-tax ($0.43/share) for expected future payments to GIP tied to Revolution Wind cost increases. Revolution Wind then reached commercial operation in October 2026, according to Ørsted’s November 5 report; on the Q3 call management said construction completion reduces the risk of further payments, with the remaining obligations to the buyer to be settled in 2027. On June 30, 2026 it also closed the $1.7 billion sale of Aquarion, its water utility, completing the move to what management calls a pure-play regulated electric and gas utility. What remains is a simple bet with two dials: how fast the rate base grows, and what return on equity the regulators let it earn. In 2026 the second dial turned the wrong way — FERC cut the New England transmission base ROE from 10.57% to 9.57% in March, and full-year guidance came down from $4.80–$4.95 to $4.57–$4.72. With the Q3 results on November 4 it was narrowed to $4.62–$4.70, a midpoint of $4.66.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territories 4.4M captive customers $34B regulated rate base 8.3% a year rate-base plan FERC transmission franchise 26 years of dividend growth

A utility has no competitors in the usual sense — nobody strings a second set of wires down a Hartford street. The moat is the franchise itself, and its price is regulation: the company earns what the regulators allow on what it invests, not what the market would bear. That makes Eversource's real counterparty not a rival but three state commissions and FERC. The vulnerability is the same thing seen from the other side. When a regulator trims the allowed ROE, disallows a cost or delays a case, there is no pricing lever to pull — and New England customers pay some of the highest bills in the country, which makes every ask political.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Connecticut regulatory relationship. CL&P had gone nearly a decade without a rate review; the July 2026 filing asks for $451M and ~11% on customers' bills. The July storm-cost decision already showed the tone — roughly $40M of CL&P storm costs disallowed, per the CFO, and carrying charges denied; CL&P appealed in October. A low outcome in mid-2027 would hit the largest distribution utility in the group.
02
FERC cutting the allowed return. The March 2026 order took one percentage point off the transmission base ROE and cut 2026 guidance. The Section 205 request for 11.39% is a request; FERC’s decision is pending and the new rate takes effect November 30, 2026.
03
The offshore wind tail. Two years after selling out, Eversource took a further $164M after-tax charge in Q2 2026 for contingent payments to GIP on Revolution Wind cost increases. The project reached commercial operation in October 2026 (Ørsted); management says completion significantly reduces the risk of further obligations, with the remaining ones to the buyer settled in 2027 and reserves adequate in its view.
04
Leverage and free cash flow. Long-term debt of $28.90B including the current portion (10-Q, September 30, 2026) against $26.3B of market cap, debt of 5.9 times EBITDA (operating earnings before depreciation), operating profit covering interest 2.3 times, and TTM free cash flow of about −$52M ($3.98B of operating cash flow less $4.04B of capex, per StockAnalysis). The dividend and the capex plan are funded by the capital markets, not by surplus cash.

Notable One-Off Items

GAAP and recurring earnings diverge sharply this year. Nine-month 2026 GAAP EPS was $2.67 against recurring $3.52, the gap made up of the $111.4M ($0.30/share) Aquarion sale loss, the $164.0M ($0.43/share) offshore wind contingent liability and the $43.9M ($0.12/share) FERC ROE refund. Over five years the GAAP line has been whipsawed by the offshore wind venture: FY2023 GAAP EPS was −$1.27 and FY2024 $2.27, the years in which the exit from the Ørsted joint ventures was agreed and closed, before a rebound to $4.56 in FY2025. Recurring EPS, by contrast, grew about 5.4% a year from $2.96 (2016) to $4.76 (2025) (our calculation: ($4.76 ÷ $2.96)^(1/9) − 1, using the company's Q2 2026 slides). Share count rose 2.15% YoY — equity issuance is part of the funding model, not buybacks.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 6, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 close of $82.27 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $69.84, ES lost 15.1% of its price (closing price, dividends not added back). A utility is bought for the dividend — $3.15 a year is 4.51% of today's price — so the dividends collected over the five years offset part of that fall; this report does not tally them. It is five years of collecting coupons on a principal that shrank. The path explains it: a 2022 high of $92.32 (May), then a two-year slide as interest rates rose and the offshore wind venture unravelled, bottoming at $53.79 in October 2023, then a slow, uneven climb back into the $60s and $70s.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 17.3 in October 2023 — the most oversold reading in the window — and 73.2 in October 2025, its most overbought. It now reads 52.7, just above neutral after a $68.55 October close. The 10-month moving average sits at about $70.10, about 26 cents above the current price: the stock has closed each month below that average since August.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The distractions are gone. Offshore wind is sold, water is sold, and what is left is a pure regulated utility with a rate base planned to grow at 8.3% a year through 2030 behind a $26.5B capital plan — and only about 3% of the funding from new equity ($0.8–1.1B; 3.2% on the company's Q2 2026 slides). You are paying 15.0x the 2026 guidance midpoint and collecting a 4.51% yield that has grown for 26 straight years. The two regulatory blows of 2026 are already in the numbers: guidance was cut, the refund booked — and in November the midpoint edged back up to $4.66. If FERC lands above 9.57% for the rate effective November 30, or Connecticut delivers a constructive CL&P decision in 2027, the earnings base steps up from a price that has already given back the whole February rally.

Bear Case

Rate base growth only turns into earnings if regulators allow a decent return on it, and the 2026 evidence runs the other way: one percentage point off the transmission ROE at FERC, storm costs trimmed and carrying charges denied in Connecticut, and an 11% bill-increase request now sitting in front of that same commission. The balance sheet has little slack — 5.9x debt/EBITDA, 2.3x interest coverage, about −$52M of TTM free cash flow against a ~$1.2B annual dividend bill (estimate: $3.15 × 377.03M shares) — and the offshore wind exit sent another invoice in Q2, two years after closing. Three Sell ratings and a $49 low target say some analysts think even 15x is too much for a utility whose allowed returns are being ratcheted down.

Top 3 Risks

01
Connecticut. The CL&P rate case (decision mid-2027) is the largest single swing factor. The July storm-cost decision — roughly $40M of CL&P storm costs disallowed, per the CFO — is the most recent read on the commission's stance.
02
Regulators cutting allowed returns. Transmission base ROE is now 9.57%, set with the 10-year Treasury at 4.25% (FRED). Each percentage point of ROE on a $34B rate base is a large share of about $1.76B of recurring annual earnings (estimate: $4.66 × ~377M shares); if rates stay high while allowed ROEs fall, the extra return for owning the shares keeps thinning.
03
Funding and credit. FFO/debt of 15.4% (S&P) is above the 12% downgrade threshold but the plan needs $0.8–1.1B of new equity through 2030 on top of new debt. A regulatory setback that cut cash flow would push the company toward either more equity (dilution) or a downgrade (higher cost of capital on the whole plan).
Industry Commentary
Affordability politics in New England
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A regulated utility's growth is a request, not a decision. Eversource wants to spend $26.5B over five years on wires, substations, gas pipe and meters, and every dollar of it ends up on a customer's bill once it enters rate base. The CL&P filing alone implies roughly an 11% total bill increase. That is the tension in one sentence: the capital plan is the growth story for shareholders and the affordability problem for regulators, and both are looking at the same number.

Allowed ROE versus the cost of money
ONGOING HEADWIND

Utility equity is priced partly as a bond substitute. When FERC set the New England base ROE at 9.57% the 10-year Treasury stood at 4.25% (FRED) — a spread of about 5.3 points for owning a leveraged, regulated equity. Eversource's own asks (10.25% in Connecticut, 11.39% at FERC) are the company's view of what that spread should be. The gap between asked and granted is the whole investment case.

Transmission build-out
EMERGING TAILWIND

Regional grid planning is creating new transmission work that did not exist in the old plans. ISO New England's selection of the ~$2.2B TIDE line between Maine and southern New Hampshire (Eversource ~$700M, benefit-to-cost ratio 2.2x per the company's Q2 2026 slides) is the first concrete example, with about $400M falling in 2027–2030 (Q3 2026 slides). Transmission is the segment where the ROE fight is happening — which is exactly why FERC's pending decision, for a rate effective November 30, matters more than its size suggests.
