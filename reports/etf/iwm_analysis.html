01 Fund Overview

IWM is a way to own about two thousand smaller U.S. companies through a single share. Its July 31, 2026 summary prospectus states the objective: the fund "seeks to track the investment results of an index composed of small-capitalization U.S. equities." That index is the Russell 2000, which the prospectus describes as "a float-adjusted capitalization-weighted index of equity securities issued by the approximately 1,933 smallest issuers in the Russell 3000 Index." Float-adjusted means each company is weighted by the market value of the shares available to the public, so bigger companies carry more weight. The Russell 3000 is the broad U.S. market; at March 31, 2026 the Russell 2000 made up about 5% of its total market value (prospectus).

Launched May 22, 2000, IWM is one of the series of iShares Trust, managed by BlackRock Fund Advisors. On October 2, 2026 it had $79.03 billion in net assets ($79,026,382,936) and 280,750,000 shares outstanding (issuer). The expense ratio is 0.19%, all of it management fee: under the advisory agreement the adviser pays the fund's other operating costs, apart from items such as interest, taxes, brokerage and extraordinary expenses (prospectus).

Structure
Physical, representative sampling
The prospectus says the adviser "uses a representative sampling indexing strategy" and that the fund "may or may not hold all of the components" of the index. In practice the October 2, 2026 holdings file lists 1,986 stock lines. The fund also holds Russell 2000 futures: 1,842 December 2026 E-mini contracts, $262.6 million of exposure, which the issuer says "may be used to offset cash and receivables." It may lend securities worth up to one-third of its total assets.
Currency
USD, no hedging needed
Every holding is priced in U.S. dollars. By the issuer's location field, U.S. companies are 97.79% of the fund (our sum); the rest are companies based in Canada, Bermuda, Norway, Brazil and a few other countries that are part of the U.S. index. For an investor who spends another currency, the U.S. dollar's moves pass straight through.

03 Structural Strengths & Vulnerabilities

Structural strengths:

1,986 holdings Top-10 share 3.39% $79.03B net assets 30-day median bid/ask 0.00% 5-yr gap to index −0.12 pts/yr Trading since May 2000

The fund spreads its money across close to two thousand companies with no single name above 0.41%, so the failure of any one company barely registers. It is also heavily traded: the issuer reports an average of 23,954,795 shares a day over 30 days and a median bid/ask spread that rounds to 0.00% (October 2, 2026). Its results have stayed close to the index. Over the five years to June 30, 2026 the NAV returned 6.86% a year against the index's 6.98%, a gap of 0.12 percentage points, a little below the 0.19% expense ratio. Over ten years the gap was 0.09 points (11.53% vs 11.62%). On October 2 the market close ($281.52) sat 0.014% above NAV ($281.48), our calculation.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Small companies swing more. The prospectus: "Compared to mid- and large-capitalization companies, small-capitalization companies may be less stable and more susceptible to adverse developments." Over the last five years IWM's monthly returns were 20.90% volatile a year, against 15.64% for the S&P 500 total-return index (our calculation), and the issuer's 3-year beta against the S&P 500 is 1.24 (August 31, 2026).
02
The smallest names are thinly traded. The prospectus also lists micro-capitalization risk: "Micro-capitalization stocks also may be thinly traded, making it difficult for the Fund to buy and sell them." The 986 holdings ranked below 1,000 are 10.73% of the fund.
03
Cost relative to its peers. At 0.19% a year IWM costs more than the other small-company index funds in section 05 (0.03% to 0.06%), including Vanguard's VTWO, which tracks the same index at 0.06%.
04
Sector tilt. Health care (20.17%) and financials (18.92%) are almost two-fifths of the fund. The prospectus names financials, healthcare and industrials as the sectors that made up a significant portion of the index at March 31, 2026, and lists each as a principal risk.
05
Index rules move companies in and out. FTSE Russell decides membership by ranking companies on size. Companies that grow past the dividing line ($5.7 billion at the June 2026 rebuild) can move up to the Russell 1000, and from 2026 this happens twice a year. Successful companies therefore tend to leave the fund, and the fund buys and sells around each reconstitution date.
06
Sampling and lending. Because the fund samples rather than fully replicates, and may lend up to one-third of its assets, its return can drift from the index's. The prospectus lists tracking error and securities lending among its principal risks.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Rising prices for smaller U.S. companies, since the fund is the Russell 2000 less 0.19% a year. Periods in which small companies do better than large ones; FTSE Russell noted that the Russell 2000 outperformed the Russell 1000 in the year to April 30, 2026, and IWM's 1-year NAV return to June 30, 2026 was 40.59%. Strength in health care, financials and industrials, its three largest sectors. A broad spread of companies means that no single failure moves the fund much: the largest holding is 0.41%.

What hurts it

Broad U.S. stock declines, which the fund has taken harder than the S&P 500: its beta to the S&P 500 was 1.13 over five years (our calculation) and the deepest daily fall in the window was −31.9% in 2022. Long stretches in which large companies lead: over these five years the S&P 500's total return was 92.58% against IWM's 36.90%. Weakness in regional banks and health-care companies, given their weights. Thin trading in the smallest names during market stress.
