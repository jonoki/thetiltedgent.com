01 Fund Overview

QQQ is a single share that holds the roughly one hundred largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq. Its summary prospectus (December 22, 2025) says the fund "seeks to track the investment results, before fees and expenses, of the Nasdaq-100 Index®," and that it "employs a 'full replication' methodology": it buys the index's stocks themselves, in the index's proportions, rather than a sample or derivatives that mimic them. Launched on March 10, 1999, it had $505.2 billion of net assets on October 2, 2026 (Invesco). The expense ratio is 0.18%, all of it a management fee; the prospectus lists other expenses as "None."

From unit investment trust to open-end fund

For more than 26 years QQQ was a unit investment trust (UIT), an older legal form for a pooled fund. The fund's August 18, 2025 proxy statement explains what that meant: the Trust was set up under New York law by "a Trust Indenture and Agreement, dated March 4, 1999," and "because the Trust is organized under the Trust Indenture and has no board of directors, it is classified as a UIT under the 1940 Act." A trust document, administered by a trustee (Bank of New York Mellon) for the sponsor (Invesco Capital Management LLC), fixed how the fund operated. Among its rules, the Trust could not lend the stocks it held to other market participants for a fee ("the Trust is unable to engage in securities lending"), and it was "required by its Governing Instruments to hold all income it receives from its investments in uninvested cash until ultimately such cash is distributed to Shareholders."

Shareholders voted on the change at a special meeting that concluded on December 19, 2025 (proxy filing of that day). In the prospectus's words, "effective after market close on December 19, 2025, the Fund was reclassified as an open-end management investment company," the standard form for U.S. ETFs, overseen by a board of trustees. The expense ratio fell from a maximum of 0.20% to 0.18% a year, and as an open-end fund it can reinvest income pending distribution and take part in securities lending (proxy statement). QQQ began trading in the new form on Monday, December 22, 2025. The objective and the index did not change; the prospectus notes that returns before the change "reflect the Fund's operation as a UIT."

Blocks of 50,000
The fund "issues and redeems Shares at NAV only with APs and only in large blocks of 50,000 Shares" (prospectus). 03 Structural Strengths & Vulnerabilities

Structural strengths:

$505.2B net assets Full replication, 101 stocks Expense ratio 0.18% 33.6M shares a day Open-end fund since Dec 2025 Close 0.03% above NAV

The fund does one thing: it owns the Nasdaq-100. Over the five years to September 30, 2026, its NAV returned 16.33% a year against the index's 16.56%, a gap of 0.24 percentage points a year; over ten years the gap was 0.25 points (20.91% vs 21.16%). For almost all of those years the fund was a UIT charging up to 0.20%, which had to hold dividends in cash until paying them out and could not lend securities (proxy statement); both limits ended with the December 2025 conversion. Portfolio turnover was 7.98% in the most recent fiscal year cited in the prospectus. On October 2 the market close ($749.58) was 0.03% above NAV ($749.38), our calculation.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Concentration at the top. The ten largest stock lines are 46.77% of the fund, and NVIDIA, Apple and Microsoft alone are 21.46% (our sum). The index caps no single company below 24%, so a few large companies can move the whole fund.
02
One dominant sector. Technology is 68.0% of the stock holdings under ICB. The prospectus lists Industry Concentration Risk and Information Technology Sector Risk among its principal risks: the index "from time to time may be concentrated to a significant degree in securities of issuers operating in a single industry or industry group."
03
Gaps by design. No banks, insurers or other financial companies, and no companies whose primary listing is on the New York Stock Exchange. The fund is a slice of the U.S. market, not the whole of it; 97.12% of the holdings are U.S. companies.
04
Index rules decide membership. Nasdaq chooses what enters and leaves. Since May 1, 2026 a newly listed company whose market value ranks within the top 40 current members can be added after about 15 trading days, without another company leaving ("Fast Entry" in Nasdaq's methodology). SpaceX joined that way on July 7, 2026 and was the fund's tenth-largest holding at 2.81% on October 2.
05
Non-diversified status. The prospectus describes the fund as "non-diversified," so it may hold larger positions in single companies than a diversified fund under the 1940 Act.
06
Low income. The 30-day SEC yield is 0.39% and the trailing distribution yield 0.41%. Returns come almost entirely from price.
07
Trading at market price. ETF shares trade at the market price, which can differ from NAV. The prospectus lists Market Trading Risk, Authorized Participant Concentration Risk and "Shares May Trade at Prices Different than NAV" among principal risks. On the banner date the gap was 0.03%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Rising prices for the largest U.S. technology and internet companies, which make up most of the fund: technology is 68.0% of the holdings and NVIDIA, Apple and Microsoft alone are 21.46%. Strong demand for semiconductors, since chipmakers fill five of the top 13 lines. Markets that reward growth companies over banks, energy and other sectors the fund holds little or none of. Low costs and full replication mean little is lost between the index and the fund.

What hurts it

Sell-offs in large technology stocks, which hit the fund harder than the broad market: beta 1.22 against the S&P 500 over five years. In calendar 2022 the fund's total return was −32.6% against −18.1% for the S&P 500, and the daily trough was −35.1% (our calculations from Yahoo Finance data). Periods when investors favour other sectors over technology. Trouble at a few companies near the top of the list. A falling U.S. dollar, for investors who measure returns in another currency.

Top 3 Risks

01
Sector concentration. Technology is 68.0% of the holdings (ICB), and the prospectus names industry concentration and the information technology sector as principal risks. The fund is a growth-and-technology portfolio, not a cross-section of the economy.
02
Company concentration. Ten stock lines are 46.77% of the fund. The index's caps (24% per company; 48% for all companies above 4.5% together) still allow a handful of companies to drive most of the return.
03
Larger swings than the market. Five-year volatility is 20.87% a year against 15.64% for the S&P 500, and the worst quarter in the prospectus is −22.33% (June 30, 2022). Gains and losses both run larger than the broad market's.

PRODUCT CATEGORY

QQQ and QQQM hold the same 101 stock lines. Cost: 0.18% for QQQ, 0.15% for QQQM. Size and trading: QQQ had $505.2 billion of net assets and traded 33.6 million shares a day on average over 30 days; QQQM had $111.7 billion and 2.6 million shares a day (Invesco, October 2, 2026). Share price: $749.58 against $308.69, a difference that matters to investors who buy whole shares. Structure: since December 2025 both are open-end funds; QQQ was a unit investment trust before that.

Same companies, different weights
WHAT DIFFERS

Equal-weight versions such as QQEW start from Nasdaq-100 companies but give each holding about the same weight, so the largest companies count for far less: QQEW's largest position was 2.67% on October 2, against 8.43% for NVIDIA in QQQ, and its overlap with QQQ by weight was 40.40% (our calculation). Broad growth funds such as IWF draw from the Russell 1000 and include financial companies and stocks listed on other exchanges. Funds tracking the Nasdaq-100 itself differ mainly in fees, size and trading costs.
