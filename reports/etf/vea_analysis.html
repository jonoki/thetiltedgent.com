01 Fund Overview

VEA is a way to own several thousand companies from the developed stock markets outside the United States through a single U.S.-listed share. In the words of its April 28, 2026 prospectus, the fund "seeks to track the performance of a benchmark index that measures the investment return of stocks issued by companies located in Canada and the major markets of Europe and the Pacific region." That index is the FTSE Developed All Cap ex US Index, which the prospectus describes as "a market-capitalization-weighted index that is made up of common stocks of large-, mid-, and small-cap companies …" The fund "attempts to replicate the Target Index by investing all, or substantially all, of its assets in the stocks that make up the Target Index …"

Launched July 20, 2007, VEA is the ETF share class of Vanguard Developed Markets Index Fund, a series of Vanguard Tax-Managed Funds; the issuer's profile links it to Investor, Admiral, Institutional and Institutional Plus share classes of the same fund. Vanguard reports $238.1 billion for the ETF share class and $323.8 billion for the whole fund at August 31, 2026, so the ETF is about 73.5% of the fund (our calculation). The expense ratio is 0.03%: a 0.02% management fee and 0.01% other expenses.

Structure
Physical, full replication
The fund holds the stocks themselves: the August 31, 2026 holdings file lists 3,891 stock lines worth $322.2 billion in total (our sum), against 3,895 stocks in the issuer's count and 3,808 in the index. The prospectus allows derivatives such as "total return swaps, equity futures, foreign currency exchange forward contracts," for example to gain exposure to a basket of stocks.
Currency
Not hedged
Vanguard reports foreign holdings of 99.5% (August 31, 2026). The stocks trade in yen, pounds, euros, Canadian dollars, won and other currencies, and the prospectus lists currency risk as a principal risk: "Changes in currency exchange rates can affect the value of the Fund's holdings." A weaker U.S. dollar raises the fund's dollar value; a stronger one lowers it.
Distributions
Quarterly
Last four dividends: $1.0400 (record date Dec 19, 2025), $0.1090 (Mar 20, 2026), $0.3772 (Jun 18, 2026) and $0.1775 (Sep 18, 2026). The prospectus says income dividends "generally are distributed quarterly in March, June, September, and December …" No capital-gains distribution appears in the issuer's list since March 2025.
Foreign taxes
Pass-through election
Dividends from foreign companies can be taxed where they are paid. The prospectus explains that if more than 50% of the fund's assets are foreign securities and the fund makes a special election, shareholders include their share of qualifying foreign taxes in their U.S. taxable income, and adds: "There is no assurance that the Fund will make this election for a taxable year …"

03 Structural Strengths & Vulnerabilities

Structural strengths:

Expense ratio 0.03% $238.1B ETF share class 3,895 stocks, 24 countries Top 10 = 12.47% 5-yr gap to index −0.01 pts/yr Portfolio turnover 4.1%

The fund's job is to own the index, and it has stayed close to it. Over the five years to August 31, 2026 its NAV returned 10.20% a year against 10.21% for Vanguard's benchmark series, and over ten years 10.24% against 10.26% (issuer figures; the benchmark returns are adjusted for withholding taxes). Turnover was 4.1% in fiscal 2025 on Vanguard's data, "4% of the average value of its portfolio" in the prospectus. On September 24 the market close ($71.05) sat 0.06% above NAV ($71.01), our calculation.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Currency, unhedged. Virtually all the holdings are priced in foreign currencies (foreign holdings 99.5%). For a U.S.-dollar investor, returns are the local stock returns plus or minus the currency move; a rising dollar can offset gains in local markets.
02
Korean chip exposure. Samsung Electronics (common and preference shares) and SK hynix are 4.92% of the fund (our sum of issuer weights), and South Korea's weight doubled in a year, from 4.4% to 8.7%. Memory-chip prices and Korean market rules now move a visible share of the fund.
03
Japan and Europe carry the fund. Japan alone is 21.1% and Europe 49.2%. The fund has no U.S. companies and no emerging-market sleeve, so it depends on these economies, their currencies and their banks (financials are the largest sector at 23.2%).
04
Index rules decide the map. FTSE Russell decides which countries count as developed. It classes South Korea and Poland as developed, and on April 7, 2026 it gave September 21, 2026 as the scheduled date for Greece's reclassification from Advanced Emerging to Developed. Another provider's index (MSCI, S&P) draws the lines differently, which is why overlap with funds on other indices is 78% to 90% rather than near 100%.
05
Price vs NAV across time zones. The Asian and Pacific markets, 38.7% of the fund, are closed during NYSE Arca trading hours. The prospectus notes that securities may be "priced at fair value" when events after a market's close affect their value, and that ETF shares "may trade at a discount to NAV" if authorized participants step back. The banner-date gap was +0.06%.
06
Uneven, taxable income. Quarterly dividends swing widely: $1.0400 in December 2025 against $0.1090 the next March. Foreign withholding taxes reduce what reaches the fund, and the pass-through of foreign tax credits depends on an annual election the prospectus does not guarantee.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Rising stock markets in Japan, Europe, Canada and the rest of the developed world outside the U.S. A falling U.S. dollar, which raises the dollar value of foreign holdings. Strong results at the large banks and industrial companies that make up 40.6% of the fund (financials 23.2% plus industrials 17.4%). Demand for memory chips, through Samsung Electronics and SK hynix. Low costs and low turnover, which keep the fund within a few hundredths of a point a year of its index.

What hurts it

Broad falls in developed markets: 2022 cost −15.35% over the calendar year and −29.3% at the daily trough; the worst quarter in the prospectus is −24.01% (March 31, 2020). A rising U.S. dollar. Periods in which U.S. companies, which the fund does not hold, lead world markets: from September 2021 to September 2026 the S&P 500's total return was 27.9 points higher. A sell-off in Korean memory-chip stocks, now the fund's two largest holdings.

One category, three maps of the world
PRODUCT CATEGORY

Developed-markets ETFs that exclude the U.S. all promise the same thing, but their index providers draw the map differently. FTSE (VEA) counts Canada and South Korea as developed. MSCI puts Korea in its emerging-market indices (iShares' MSCI emerging-markets fund IEMG holds Samsung Electronics), so IEFA and IDEV hold no Korean stocks, and MSCI EAFE also leaves out Canada. S&P (SPDW) includes both Canada and Korea. Those choices, not cost, explain most of the difference between the funds compared here: VEA overlaps 78.1% with IEFA, 87.4% with IDEV and 87.8% with SPDW (90.4% counting SPDW's Samsung GDR as Samsung; our calculations).

Costs are close; structure and income differ
WHAT DIFFERS

Expense ratios in the group run from 0.03% (VEA, SPDW) to 0.07% (IEFA), a difference of $4 a year on $10,000. VEA pays dividends quarterly; the other three pay semi-annually. VEA is a share class of a larger Vanguard fund that also has mutual-fund share classes. All four are U.S.-listed and priced in U.S. dollars.
