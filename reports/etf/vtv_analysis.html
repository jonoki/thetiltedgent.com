01 Fund Overview

VTV holds the large U.S. companies that an index provider's formula ranks as "value": shares priced low relative to the company's earnings, book value, sales and dividends. Its April 28, 2026 prospectus says the ETF "seeks to track the performance of a benchmark index that measures the investment return of large-capitalization value stocks," and that the fund attempts to replicate the index "holding each stock in approximately the same proportion as its weighting in the Target Index." Vanguard's fact sheet describes "a passively managed, full-replication strategy": the fund owns the index's stocks themselves.

The fund launched on January 26, 2004. It is the ETF share class of Vanguard Morningstar Value Index Fund, which also has mutual-fund share classes, including Admiral Shares. Vanguard reports $192.2 billion for the ETF share class and $262.4 billion for the whole fund at August 31, 2026 (the June 30 fact sheet gives $186,106 million and $254,207 million), so the ETF is about 73% of the fund (our calculation). All share classes own the same stocks. The expense ratio is 0.03%: a 0.02% management fee and 0.01% other expenses.

A new name, the same index. Morningstar, Inc. bought the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP), the index's original provider, in February 2026, and in July 2026 the CRSP indexes were renamed the Morningstar Market Indexes (Morningstar's July 2026 methodology guide). Vanguard's supplement of July 29, 2026 renamed the fund and its target index, from CRSP US Large Cap Value Index to Morningstar US Large Cap Value Index, effective that day, and states that "Each Fund's investment objective, strategies, and polices remain unchanged." The ticker stayed VTV. Among the four large-cap value ETFs compared in section 05, VTV is the largest by net assets: its ETF share class ($192.2 billion, August 31) is more than twice the size of IWD ($81.4 billion, October 2).

How the index decides what counts as value

The rules are in Morningstar's Morningstar Market Indexes Methodology Guide (July 2026), the former CRSP methodology under its new name. The sort happens in four steps, repeated every quarter:

  1. Pick the large companies. Eligible U.S. companies are ranked by total market value. The Large Cap Index holds those within the first 85% of the market's cumulative market capitalisation, with a buffer band from 81% to 89% so that companies near the line do not flip back and forth.
  2. Score each one twice. A value score uses five ratios: book-to-price, forward earnings-to-price, historical earnings-to-price, dividend-to-price and sales-to-price. A separate growth score uses six measures: analysts' forecasts of long-term and short-term earnings-per-share growth, three-year historical growth in earnings per share and in sales per share, the current investment-to-assets ratio and return on assets. Each input is trimmed at the 5th and 95th percentiles and standardised. In the value score, a primary factor built from the earnings and book ratios counts two-thirds and a secondary factor built from the sales and dividend ratios one-third.
  3. Average the two ranks. Each company gets a market-cap-weighted percentile rank on value and an inverted one on growth; the average of the two is its "Average Rank" (AR), from 0 (most growth-like) to 1 (most value-like). In the guide's words, "A company with a high AR, or mean score above 0.5, is categorized as value."
  4. Move slowly. Companies with an AR between 0.3333 and 0.6667 stay where they are. A company that crosses the band moves half its weight (a 50% "packet") to the other style, and the second half only if it is still beyond the band at the next quarterly review. So a company can sit half in value and half in growth.

Two consequences. First, value and growth are scored separately, not as opposites: CRSP's own explanation is that "value and growth styles, as practiced by investors, are not opposites," and the methodology does not force each side to hold half the market. Second, some companies are split. 21 companies appear in both VTV's and its growth sibling VUG's August 31 holdings files, among them Intel (0.69% of VTV, 0.52% of VUG), General Electric (0.64% / 0.49%) and Texas Instruments (0.43% / 0.33%); together they are 4.0% of VTV and 3.3% of VUG (our match of the two files by ticker).

03 Structural Strengths & Vulnerabilities

Structural strengths:

Expense ratio 0.03% $192.2B ETF share class Full replication, 306 stocks 5-yr gap to index 0.00 pts/yr Buffer bands and 50% packets Portfolio turnover 7.8%

The fund owns the index and its results have stayed close to it. Over the five years to September 30, 2026 the fund's NAV returned 12.43% a year, the same as its benchmark (Vanguard's "Spliced Value Index," which has been this index since April 2013); cumulatively 79.61% against 79.64%. Over ten years: 12.28% against 12.29%. The index's buffer bands and half-step moves are designed to limit trading: turnover was 7.8% in fiscal 2025 on Vanguard's data ("8%" in the prospectus). On October 2 the market close ($217.23) sat $0.02 above NAV ($217.21), 0.01% (our calculation).

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Style risk. The prospectus names "Value Investing" as a principal risk: the approach "could cause it to underperform other stock funds that use a different investment style." Over the five years to October 2, 2026 VTV's total return was 80.62% against 92.58% for the S&P 500 and 93.73% for VUG (section 07).
02
Sector tilt. Financials are 22.1% of the fund and Financials, Industrials and Health Care together 52.8% (our sum of Vanguard's ICB weights). Bank, industrial and drug-company cycles weigh more here than in a broad-market fund.
03
Index rules decide membership. A company leaves when its value/growth rank or size changes at a quarterly review, whatever its price does afterwards. Micron Technology, now the largest holding, illustrates how far the formula's definition of value can reach: a memory-chip maker, not a bank or oil company.
04
Index changes by the provider. The prospectus warns that the fund "could be negatively impacted by changes to the Target Index made by the index provider or by errors made by the index provider." The index changed owner in 2026; Vanguard states the methodology did not change with the rename.
05
One country. Foreign holdings are 0.2%. The fund has almost no exposure to companies based outside the U.S.
06
Trading at market price. ETF shares change hands at the market price, which can differ from NAV. The prospectus lists ETF share trading and reliance on authorized participants (the large firms that create and redeem shares) among principal risks. On the banner date the gap was 0.01%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Periods when investors favour cheaper, established companies over fast growers, as in 2022 (−2.05% NAV return against −18.11% for the S&P 500 total-return index). Strength in banks and other financial firms (22.1% of the fund), industrials (15.9%) and health care (14.8%). Rising oil and gas prices, through ExxonMobil, Chevron and the rest of the 8.1% energy weight. Dividend income: a 1.89% SEC yield against 1.00% for VOO (both September 30, 2026, Vanguard). Low costs and turnover, which keep the fund close to its index.

What hurts it

Markets led by the largest technology and internet companies, which sit on the growth side of the index: in 2023 VTV returned about 9% against about 47% for VUG (market-price total return, Yahoo). Broad U.S. bear markets: there is no bond or cash cushion; the worst quarter in the prospectus is −25.03% (March 31, 2020). Stress in banks and credit, given the financials weight. Falling energy prices. A falling U.S. dollar, for investors who measure returns in another currency.

Top 3 Risks

01
Long stretches behind the market. Value can trail growth for years: over the five years to October 2, 2026, VTV's total return was 80.62% against 92.58% for the S&P 500 and 93.73% for VUG. The prospectus lists value investing as a principal risk.
02
Full U.S. equity market risk. A 100% stock portfolio of U.S. companies takes market falls largely in full: −17.04% daily peak to trough in 2022 and −14.52% from late 2024 to April 2025 inside this five-year window. Five-year volatility is 14.00% a year and beta 0.75 (our calculations).
03
A formula decides what "value" is. The fund holds what the Morningstar (formerly CRSP) value score selects, rebalanced quarterly. Its largest holding is Micron Technology, and the other main value indexes define the style differently: VTV overlaps IWD by 62.5% and IVE by 67.0% by weight (our calculation).

"Value" depends on whose index
WHAT DIFFERS

Large-cap value funds track indexes that sort the same companies by different rules. The Morningstar (ex-CRSP) index uses five value and six growth measures with buffer bands and half-step moves; the S&P 500 Value and Russell 1000 Value indexes each apply their own rules, and their October 2 holdings include Apple and Amazon, which VTV's do not. The overlaps in section 05 (62.5% to 67.0% by weight) show how much the definition matters. Over the same five years the peers' total returns were IWD +75.16%, IVE +73.35% and SPYV +74.61%, against VTV's +80.62% (Yahoo dividend-adjusted closes, September 30, 2021 to October 2, 2026, our calculation). Expense ratios run from 0.03% (VTV) to 0.18% (IWD, IVE).
