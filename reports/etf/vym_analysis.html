01 Fund Overview

VYM is a single share that holds about 600 U.S. companies whose dividends, measured against their share price, rank high. Vanguard describes it this way: "Vanguard High Dividend Yield ETF seeks to track the performance of a benchmark index that measures the investment return of common stocks of companies that are characterized by high dividend yields." Its product summary adds that it "Provides a convenient way to track the performance of stocks that are forecasted to have above-average dividend yields" and "Follows a passively managed, full-replication approach." The word forecasted matters: the index ranks companies on forecasts of next year's dividend, not only on what they paid last year (section 02).

Launched November 10, 2006 and listed on NYSE Arca, VYM is the exchange-traded share class of Vanguard High Dividend Yield Index Fund, which also has Admiral Shares for mutual-fund investors. The whole fund held $100.8 billion on August 31, 2026; the ETF shares were $78.9 billion of it on September 30 (Vanguard fund data). Its expense ratio is 0.04% (management fee 0.03% plus other expenses 0.01%), per the summary prospectus of February 27, 2026; Vanguard cut it from 0.06% with effect from February 2, 2026. Unlike an S&P 500 fund, VYM owns only companies that pay a dividend and rank in the higher-yielding part of the market, and it excludes real estate investment trusts (REITs). It owned none of NVIDIA, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta or Tesla on August 31, 2026 (holdings file).

Structure
Physical, holds the index stocks
The prospectus commits at least 80% of net assets to stocks in the index; the fund aims to hold each one in about its index weight. The August 31, 2026 holdings file lists 603 stocks at 99.61% of net assets, cash and money-market lines of about 0.1%, and nine derivative lines (total return swaps on five companies and two stock-index futures) at 0.24% in total. Vanguard's policy text: "The fund will not use derivatives for speculation or for the purpose of leveraging (magnifying) investment returns."

Vanguard puts the fund's foreign holdings at 0.2% of assets on August 31, 2026; the rest are U.S. companies. A U.S.-dollar investor has no currency exposure. For an investor who spends another currency, the dollar's moves pass straight through.

How the index picks its stocks

The rules as FTSE Russell's ground rules for the FTSE High Dividend Yield Index (v2.4, October 2025, the published redacted version) set them out:

  • 1 · Universe
    Constituents of the FTSE US All Cap Index, which covers large, mid and small U.S. companies.
  • 2 · Exclusions
    All real estate investment trusts (equity and mortgage REITs) are removed, as are stocks "not currently paying a dividend" or not forecast to pay one over the next 12 months. A current member with an irregular payment schedule gets a second check over 13 months.
  • 3 · Yield ranking
    The yield used is a 12-month forward figure: one-year dividend-per-share forecasts divided by the share price. The ground rules add: "Additional proprietary screens are also applied." Vanguard's summary of the result: the index "Tracks common stocks of U.S. companies that have paid above-average dividends for the previous 12 months, excluding REITs."
  • 4 · Weighting
    By the value of shares available to the public: FTSE's index formula multiplies each stock's price by its shares in issue and its free-float factor (the share of stock not held by insiders or strategic owners). Larger companies get more weight.
  • 5 · Upkeep
    "The FTSE High Dividend Yield Index will be reviewed annually." Between reviews, "Companies which have cut dividends to zero will be removed on a quarterly basis." There are no additions between reviews; a stock that leaves the FTSE US All Cap Index leaves this index too.

Because the weighting follows company size, one stock can grow large in the fund: Broadcom was 6.92% of net assets on August 31, 2026, more than the next two holdings, JPMorgan Chase (3.83%) and ExxonMobil (2.69%), combined (Vanguard holdings file).

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A style that can lag for years. Dividend investing is one of the principal risks Vanguard lists: "The fund’s emphasis on dividend-paying stocks could cause the fund to underperform other funds that invest without consideration of a company’s track record of paying dividends." Over the five years to October 2, 2026 VYM's total return was +74.84% against +92.58% for the S&P 500 (our calculation, section 07). NVIDIA, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Tesla were not in the fund on August 31, 2026 (holdings file).
02
One stock, one sector. Broadcom was 6.92% of net assets on August 31, 2026; no rule in the published ground rules limits a single stock's weight, so its share moves with its market value. Financials are the largest sector at 21.1% (ICB).
03
Selection rests on forecasts and screens that are not all published. The ranking uses dividend forecasts, which can be wrong, and FTSE's ground rules say "Additional proprietary screens are also applied." LSEG publishes a redacted version of the rules, so an investor cannot rebuild the index from them.
04
Dividends vary. The last four quarterly payments ranged from $0.8617 (March 2026) to $0.9795 (June 2026) per share (Vanguard). Payments depend on what the companies in the index pay, and companies can cut them; under the index rules a company that cuts its dividend to zero is removed at the next quarterly check.
05
Tax on income. In a taxable account part of the return arrives each year as dividends. Over five years to September 30, 2026, tax on distributions lowered the annualised NAV return from 11.63% to 10.82% on Vanguard's after-tax calculation.
06
Trading at market price. Vanguard's risk text on ETF share trading: "there may be times when the market price of an ETF Share and its NAV differ significantly." Shares change hands at the market price, which can differ from the value of the holdings.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Markets that favour established, dividend-paying companies over fast-growing ones, as in 2022, when the fund's market-price total return was −0.43% and the S&P 500's was −18.11%. Strength in banks and other financial companies, the largest sector at 21.1%, and in Broadcom, its largest holding at 6.92%. Steady or rising dividends from the holdings, which feed the 2.35% yield. Low costs: 0.04% a year keeps the fund close to its index.

What hurts it

Markets led by companies the fund does not own, such as NVIDIA, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet: the S&P 500 beat VYM in each calendar year from 2023 to 2025. A setback at Broadcom or across the banks. Dividend cuts at holdings. A broad stock sell-off: the fund's total return fell 15.84% in 2022 and 14.46% in early 2025 on daily data, with no bond cushion. For investors spending another currency, a weaker U.S. dollar.

Top 3 Risks

01
Style risk. The fund owns one kind of stock. Over five years it returned 11.81% a year against 13.99% for the S&P 500 (total return, our calculation), and that kind of gap can last for years when markets reward companies that pay little or no dividend.
02
Concentration inside a broad fund. 603 stocks, but Broadcom is 6.92%, the ten largest holdings are 25.70%, and financial companies are 21.1%. Trouble at one large holding or one sector shows up quickly in the fund.
03
The index decides what the fund owns. Selection depends on dividend forecasts and on screens FTSE Russell does not fully publish, applied once a year. The fund's future holdings depend on data and decisions outside its own control.
Category Commentary
"High dividend" funds yield very different amounts
PRODUCT CATEGORY

Funds sold as high-dividend ETFs share a label, not a yield. Of the four compared here, VYM reports the lowest 30-day SEC yield, 2.35% (September 30), against 3.32% for DVY and 3.34% for HDV (August 31) and 4.71% for SPYD (October 2). It is also by far the broadest, with 603 stocks against 74 to 98 for the others, and the cheapest, at 0.04% a year against 0.07% to 0.38%.

The same names, in different amounts
WHAT DIFFERS

Every stock in HDV and DVY was also in VYM, yet by weight the overlap was only 25.35% and 21.20% (our calculation, section 05), because VYM spreads its money across many more companies and weights them by size. SPYD shares 58 of its 78 stocks with VYM; the other 20 all carry the GICS Real Estate label, and VYM's index excludes REITs. With overlaps this low, the index rules rather than the fees decide what each fund owns and what it pays out.
