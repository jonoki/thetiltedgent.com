01 Fund Overview

XLI holds the industrial companies of the S&P 500 — makers of aircraft, engines and weapons, construction and farm machinery, electrical and power equipment, railroads and truckers, airlines, parcel carriers, building-products makers and business-services firms — in one share. Its January 31, 2026 summary prospectus says the fund "seeks to provide investment results that, before expenses, correspond generally to the price and yield performance of publicly traded equity securities of companies in the Industrial Select Sector Index." State Street describes the index as one that "seeks to provide an effective representation of the industrial sector of the S&P 500 Index." The eleven Select Sector indexes together cover every company in the S&P 500; XLI's is the industrial one of the eleven, a split by sector rather than by size.

The fund started on December 16, 1998; the issuer gives December 22, 1998 as its listing date, and it trades on NYSE Arca. On October 2, 2026 it held $30,591.43 million of net assets and 180.03 million shares outstanding (issuer). The expense ratio is 0.08%: a 0.03% management fee, a 0.02% distribution and service (12b-1) fee and 0.03% of other expenses. Until December 1, 2025 the fund was called The Industrial Select Sector SPDR Fund; a November 14, 2025 prospectus supplement gave it its current name from that date and said the change did not affect its objective, strategy or principal risks.

Of the peers in section 05 with published net assets, XLI is the largest: $30.6 billion against $8.6 billion for Vanguard's VIS (whole fund, August 31, 2026) and $2.0 billion for iShares' IYJ (October 2, 2026). XLI's own share count rose from 155.18 million on October 2, 2025 to 180.03 million a year later (issuer NAV history), a 16.0% increase from net creations (our calculation).

Structure
Physical, replication
The prospectus says the fund "typically invests in substantially all of the securities represented in the Index in approximately the same proportions as the Index" and "invests substantially all, but at least 95%, of its total assets in the securities comprising the Index." The October 2, 2026 holdings file lists 83 stocks (99.87% of net assets, our sum), a money-market fund (0.12%), a small negative U.S. dollar cash line (−0.01%) and a December 2026 futures contract on the industrial sector index. How the index caps weights

A sector index can end up with a few very large companies. Key Vulnerabilities

01
One sector. The prospectus lists industrial sector risk among its principal risks: "the Fund will be more affected by the performance of the industrial sector than a fund that is more diversified." Capital spending, freight volumes, government budgets and fuel and labor costs move many holdings at the same time.
02
The economic cycle. "Industrial companies are affected by supply and demand both for their specific product or service and for industrial sector products and services in general" (prospectus). In 2022 the fund's total-return line fell 21.64% on daily data, from January 4 to September 30 (our calculation).
03
Government spending. Aerospace and defense is the largest industry at 23.64%, and the prospectus warns these companies rely "to a significant extent, on U.S. and foreign government demand for their products and services." Defense budgets and aircraft-certification decisions are set outside the companies.
04
Concentration at the top. The ten largest holdings are 39.65% of the fund; Caterpillar alone is 7.19%, and GE Aerospace and GE Vernova together are 10.80%. The capping rules leave room for more: no company is cut until it passes 24%, and the group above 4.8% can reach 50%.
05
Index rules and corporate actions decide membership. The fund follows the S&P 500's additions and deletions and GICS classification. Spin-offs bring new companies in: FedEx Freight (June 1, 2026) and Honeywell Aerospace (June 29, 2026) are both holdings now, beside FedEx and Honeywell.
06
Non-diversified fund, traded at market price. As a "non-diversified" fund it "may hold a smaller number of portfolio securities than many other funds" (prospectus). ETF shares trade at the market price, which can differ from NAV; the prospectus lists this premium and discount risk among principal risks. On the banner date the gap was 0.01%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Rising capital spending by builders, miners, farmers, utilities and factories, which feeds the machinery and electrical-equipment makers that are 36.58% of the fund (our sum of the issuer's industry weights). Demand for power and electrical gear, the business of GE Vernova, Eaton, Vertiv and Bloom Energy. Steady or rising defense budgets and commercial-aircraft production for the aerospace and defense group (23.64%). Growing freight and travel volumes for the railroads, truckers, parcel carriers and airlines. Lower fuel costs for the transport companies.

What hurts it

Recessions and falling business investment, which cut orders across most holdings at once: in 2022 the total-return line fell 21.64% on daily data. Cuts in government spending on defense. Higher fuel prices, labor disputes and insurance costs for transport companies, the drivers the prospectus names. Trade disputes and tariffs that raise input costs or slow exports. Broad market falls: with a beta of 0.98 the fund has moved almost one for one with the S&P 500.

Top 3 Risks

01
One-sector exposure. All 83 holdings are industrial companies whose earnings share the same drivers: capital spending, freight and travel volumes and government budgets. Five-year volatility is 18.49% a year against 15.64% for the S&P 500, and the five-year total return trailed the index by 5.35 points (our calculations).
02
The economic cycle. The prospectus calls transportation securities "cyclical", and machinery and electrical-equipment orders follow business investment. The fund's sharpest fall in the window, 21.64% on daily data in 2022, came in a year of broad market decline.
03
Concentration in a few names. The ten largest holdings are 39.65% of the fund; Caterpillar, GE Aerospace and GE Vernova alone are 17.99%. The capping rules allow one company up to 24% before any cut.
Category Commentary
Same sector, different definitions
PRODUCT CATEGORY

Industrial-sector ETFs differ mainly in which companies count as "industrial" and how far down in size they go. XLI holds only the S&P 500's industrials: 83 large companies. VIS and FIDU track MSCI's investable-market (IMI) industrials indexes, which reach further down in company size (396 stocks in VIS on August 31, 2026). IYJ tracks a Russell 1000 industrials index; its 215 stocks (October 2, 2026) include the card networks Visa and Mastercard and leave out Uber, the airlines and the waste haulers. Cost: 0.08% (XLI) to 0.37% (IYJ). Age: XLI from December 1998, IYJ June 2000, VIS September 2004.

Different rules, different top weights
WHAT DIFFERS

Each index sets its own rules for which companies count and how they are weighted; XLI's capping rules are set out in section 02. The largest weights differ: Caterpillar is 7.19% of XLI, 4.77% of IYJ (October 2, 2026) and 5.29% of VIS (August 31, 2026). With overlaps of 61.0% (IYJ) and 78.8% (VIS) by weight (our calculations), the funds share much of their money but not all of it.
