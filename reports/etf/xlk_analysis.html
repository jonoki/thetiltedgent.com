01 Fund Overview

XLK is a way to own the technology companies of the S&P 500 through one share. In State Street's words, the fund "seeks to provide investment results that, before expenses, correspond generally to the price and yield performance of the Technology Select Sector Index," and that index "seeks to provide an effective representation of the technology sector of the S&P 500 Index." The summary prospectus (January 31, 2026) describes the method: "the Fund employs a replication strategy, which means that the Fund typically invests in substantially all of the securities represented in the Index in approximately the same proportions as the Index." It holds the stocks themselves, at least 95% of total assets in index securities.

Launched on December 16, 1998 and listed on December 22, 1998, XLK is one of the Select Sector SPDR funds. State Street's page notes that the eleven Select Sector indices on which those funds are based "together comprise all of the companies in the S&P 500." The fund was named The Technology Select Sector SPDR Fund until December 1, 2025, when it became State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF (prospectus supplement of November 14, 2025). Net assets were $126.84 billion on September 24, 2026, with 651.26 million shares outstanding (issuer NAV history). The expense ratio is 0.08%: a 0.03% management fee, a 0.02% distribution (12b-1) fee and 0.03% other expenses.

02 What It Holds

The index takes the S&P 500 companies that the GICS system classifies as Information Technology. The prospectus says it "includes companies that have been identified as Information Technology companies by the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS®)"; State Street lists its industries as "technology hardware, storage, and peripherals; software; communications equipment; semiconductors and semiconductor equipment; IT services; and electronic equipment, instruments and components." The index had 70 stocks on November 30, 2025 (prospectus) and 74 on September 23, 2026 (issuer). Membership therefore moves with the S&P 500: when Everpure joined the S&P 500 on September 21, 2026 as an Information Technology company (S&P DJI release, September 4, 2026), it entered XLK too.

How the index is weighted and capped

Each company starts at its float-adjusted market value. Because a handful of technology companies are very large, S&P DJI caps their weights so that funds tracking the index can meet U.S. diversification rules for regulated funds. The rules below are quoted or paraphrased from S&P DJI's index announcement of September 3, 2024 (Internet Archive copy captured September 26, 2024), which introduced this method before the open on September 23, 2024.

01
Quarterly timetable. The index rebalances "after the close of business on the third Friday of March, June, September, and December." Weights are set with prices from the reference date, "the second Friday" of those months.
02
Single-company cap. "If any company has an FMC weight greater than 24%, cap all companies' weights at 23%, which allows for a 2% buffer" below the 25% limit.
03
Large-company group cap. "The sum of the companies with weights greater than 4.8% cannot exceed 50% of the total index weight." If it does, those companies are cut back so that together they hold 45%, shared in proportion to their weights, with none set below 4.5%. "Set 4.5% and 45% caps to allow for a buffer below the 5% limit."
04
Redistribution. The weight removed is spread "to companies with an initial weight less than 4.8%, setting a 4.5% upper bound on the companies' index weight."
05
Mid-quarter check. If on the second-to-last business day of March, June, September or December a company exceeds 24%, or the companies above 4.8% sum to more than 50%, "the capping breach triggers a secondary reweighting" effective after the close of the last business day of the month.

What changed in 2024. Before September 23, 2024, a breach of the 50% group limit was fixed by taking "the smallest company whose weight is greater than 4.8%" and reducing it to 4.5%, one company at a time, until the limit was met. The current rule scales the whole group down to 45% together, in proportion to each company's weight.

Where the fund stands now. On September 23, 2026, two days after the September rebalance, four companies were above 4.8%: NVIDIA 15.31%, Apple 13.79%, Microsoft 10.42% and AMD 5.09%, together 44.62% (our sum), close to the 45% the rule sets when it binds. Broadcom (4.74%) and Micron (4.50%) sat just below the 4.8% line. Funds on differently capped indices held more NVIDIA: 17.73% of VGT (August 31) and 16.83% of IYW (September 23).

Structural strengths:

The fund does one job: it owns the index. Over the five years to August 31, 2026 its NAV returned 19.50% a year against the index's 19.61%, a gap of 0.11 percentage points a year, a little above the 0.08% expense ratio; over ten years the gap was 0.17 points (24.30% vs 24.47%). Key Vulnerabilities

01
Three companies decide much of the result. NVIDIA, Apple and Microsoft are 39.52% of the fund and the top ten 64.39% (our sums). The prospectus lists non-diversification among the principal risks, and with weights this large one company's news moves the whole fund.
02
One sector, and nearly half of it one industry. The whole fund is technology, and semiconductors and chip equipment are 45.62%. The prospectus names technology sector risk as a principal risk. A downturn in chip demand or prices reaches almost half the portfolio at once.
03
Capping creates trades. Each quarter the index cuts the largest companies back to its limits and hands the weight to smaller ones, and the fund must trade to follow. S&P DJI last changed these rules in September 2024, and they are S&P DJI's to change again.
04
Valuation of what it owns. State Street reports a price/earnings ratio of 35.07 for the index (27.33 on next-year forecast earnings) and a price/book ratio of 12.43 for the fund (September 23, 2026). The fund buys at whatever price the market sets; it has no mechanism to avoid expensive stocks.
05
Larger swings than the market. Over the last five years the fund's monthly returns had an annualised volatility of 24.22% against 15.84% for the S&P 500, and a beta of 1.34 (our calculations, section 04). The daily total-return drawdown in 2022 reached −33.6%.
06
Classification boundaries. Membership depends on GICS labels. Companies many investors think of as technology businesses, such as Alphabet, Meta and Amazon, are outside the fund, and a company reclassified out of Information Technology leaves it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Rising prices for large U.S. technology companies, since the fund is the technology slice of the S&P 500 less 0.08% a year. Strong demand for chips and chip-making equipment, which make up 45.62% of the fund. Gains at NVIDIA, Apple and Microsoft, which together are 39.52%. Periods when investors pay up for earnings growth: State Street's estimated 3–5 year EPS growth for the holdings is 38.83%. Low costs and low turnover mean little is lost between the index and the fund.

What hurts it

Technology sell-offs, which hit it harder than the broad market: a beta of 1.34 and a −33.6% daily drawdown in 2022, against a worst quarter of −19.70% in the prospectus. A turn in the semiconductor cycle. Bad news at one of the three largest companies. A fall in valuation multiples from an index price/earnings ratio of 35.07, which lowers prices even if earnings hold. Rotation into sectors the fund does not own, since it holds nothing outside technology.

Top 3 Risks

01
Single-sector equity risk. A 100% stock portfolio in one sector takes that sector's falls in full: −33.6% daily peak to trough in 2022, −25.6% in early 2025 and −15.9% in both late 2025–early 2026 and mid-2026 inside this five-year window. Five-year volatility is 24.22% a year (our calculation).
02
Concentration in a few companies and one industry. The top three holdings are 39.52% of the fund, the top ten 64.39%, and semiconductors 45.62%. The capping rules limit the largest weights, but on September 23, two days after the latest rebalance, the four companies above 4.8% still held 44.62%.
03
Starting valuation. The index trades at 35.07 times earnings and the fund at 12.43 times book value (State Street, September 23, 2026). The fund cannot move away from expensive stocks; its future return depends on earnings and on the multiple the market pays for them.
Category Commentary
Sector ETFs: one index family's cut of the market
PRODUCT CATEGORY

Sector ETFs hold one part of the market rather than all of it. The Select Sector SPDR funds split the S&P 500 into eleven GICS sectors, and their indices "together comprise all of the companies in the S&P 500" (State Street), so an investor can weight sectors differently from the index. Technology funds from other issuers draw the boundary in other places. Universe: XLK takes only S&P 500 members (74 stocks); the MSCI indices behind VGT and FTEC add mid and small caps (318 and 291 stocks on August 31). Definition: IYW's Russell index and IGM's S&P expanded index include Meta and Alphabet; XLK, VGT and FTEC do not. Capping: XLK's group of companies above 4.8% is cut to 45% when it exceeds 50%; the indices of VGT and FTEC use a 25/50 structure and IYW's a 22.5/45 structure, as their names state. Cost: 0.08% to 0.37% a year.

Returns differ because the weights differ
WHAT DIFFERS

The four peers in section 05 hold many of the same companies, but their overlaps with XLK run from 74.2% to 82.9% by weight (our calculation), so their returns can part ways. The differences come from how much NVIDIA, Apple and Microsoft each fund may hold, whether internet platforms are included, and how many smaller technology companies sit in the tail. Fee differences are smaller by comparison: 0.01 percentage points a year between XLK and VGT, 0.004 points between XLK and FTEC, 0.29 points between XLK and the two iShares funds.
