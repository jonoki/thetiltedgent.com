01 Fund Overview

XLP holds the consumer staples companies of the S&P 500 in one share: the makers and sellers of things people buy week in, week out whatever the economy is doing — groceries, drinks, cleaning and personal care products, tobacco — and the big retailers that stock them. Its January 31, 2026 summary prospectus states the objective as investment results that, before expenses, "correspond generally to the price and yield performance of publicly traded equity securities of companies in the Consumer Staples Select Sector Index". S&P Dow Jones Indices calculates the index. The fund uses a replication strategy, holding substantially all of the index's stocks in about the same proportions, and puts at least 95% of its total assets into them.

The index is one of eleven Select Sector Indexes that together cover every company in the S&P 500. Membership follows the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS, the sector system S&P's indices use): a company joins when it is in the S&P 500 and GICS places it in Consumer Staples. State Street lists six industries: consumer staples distribution and retail, household products, food products, beverages, tobacco and personal care products. On October 2, 2026 the index had 33 stocks, down from 37 at November 30, 2025 (prospectus), as five food and drink companies left the S&P 500 and one retailer joined between December 2025 and September 2026. The index is not weighted purely by market value: S&P Dow Jones Indices applies what the prospectus calls a "modified market capitalization" method that trims the largest weights so that funds tracking it can meet U.S. tax diversification rules (the capping rules are set out in section 02). Launched December 16, 1998 and listed on December 22, 1998, XLP had $13.25 billion of net assets and 164.57 million shares outstanding on October 2, 2026.

How the weights are capped

A U.S. fund has to meet diversification tests to keep its tax status, so the index cannot simply follow market value when a few companies dominate a sector. The fund's Statement of Additional Information (January 31, 2026) sets out S&P DJI's rules. The index is weighted by float-adjusted market value (counting only shares available to the public) and rebalanced at the closing prices of the second Friday of March, June, September and December, effective after the close on the third Friday:

01
Single company. A company whose weight is above 24% is cut to 23%.
02
The big names together. The companies weighing more than 4.8% may not add up to more than 50% of the index. If they do, they are cut back in proportion to a combined 45%; any that this pushes below 4.5% are topped back up to 4.5% from the removed weight.
03
Where the excess goes. Companies between 4.5% and 4.8% are cut to 4.5%. The rest of the removed weight is spread in proportion over the companies below 4.5%, none of which may then exceed 4.5%.
04
Check before quarter-end. If on the second-to-last business day of a rebalance month a company is above 24% or the over-4.8% group is above 50%, a secondary reweighting takes effect after the close at month-end.

On October 2, 2026 five holdings were above 4.8%: Walmart, Costco, Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola and Philip Morris International, together 41.79% of the fund (41.98% of the index on State Street's index top holdings). That is below the 50% limit. The next five — Altria and Target (4.56% each), Colgate-Palmolive (4.40%), Mondelez (4.29%) and PepsiCo (4.24%) — sit close to the 4.5% level the rules use for the companies below the 4.8% line. No company is near the 24% single-company trigger; Walmart, the largest, is 10.59%.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One sector. Every holding is a consumer staples company. The prospectus lists consumer staples sector risk among its principal risks: because the fund's assets are concentrated in the sector, it will be more affected by that sector's performance than a more diversified fund.
02
A few names carry the fund. Walmart is 10.59%, Walmart and Costco together 19.90%, and the top ten 63.84%. The fund is "non-diversified" under U.S. fund rules, which, in the prospectus's words, means it "may hold a smaller number of portfolio securities than many other funds."
03
A shrinking index. The index went from 37 stocks at November 30, 2025 to 33 on October 2, 2026. Kellanova left as Mars was acquiring it; Lamb Weston, Campbell's, Conagra and Molson Coors were dropped from the S&P 500, while Casey's joined. Each removal concentrates the remaining weight further in the largest companies.
04
It has lagged the broad market. From September 30, 2021 to October 2, 2026 XLP's total return was +33.54% against +92.58% for the S&P 500 (our calculation, section 07). The sector's lower volatility came with lower returns in this window.
05
Index membership comes from S&P. S&P DJI decides S&P 500 membership and GICS decides the sector; the fund follows both. A takeover or a market-value review can remove a company at short notice, as with Molson Coors on September 21, 2026.
06
Trading at market price. ETF shares trade at a market price that can differ from NAV. In the third quarter of 2026 the fund's 4 p.m. bid/ask midpoint was above NAV on 43 days and below it on 21 (issuer). On the banner date the issuer's premium was 0.03%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What helps it

Markets that favour steady earners over fast growers: in 2022, when the S&P 500 lost 18.11% with dividends, XLP lost 0.81%. Its beta of 0.47 against the S&P 500 means broad market falls have reached it only in part. Strength at the large retailers and brand owners at the top — Walmart, Costco, Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola and Philip Morris International are 41.79% of the fund. Dividends, which supplied about half of its five-year total return. Low costs and low turnover keep the fund close to its index.

What hurts it

Markets led by technology and other growth sectors, as in 2023 and 2024, when the fund returned −0.82% and +12.20% while the S&P 500 returned more than 25% in each year. Trouble at one of the largest holdings, given 19.90% in Walmart and Costco together and 63.84% in the top ten. Further removals from the index: Lamb Weston, Campbell's, Conagra and Molson Coors left the S&P 500 between March and September 2026 for market capitalization changes.

Top 3 Risks

01
Sector risk. The whole fund is one sector, and the prospectus says it will be more affected by that sector's performance than a more diversified fund. Retailers, drink makers and household-goods companies are 70% of it (our sum of State Street's industry weights).
02
Concentration in a few companies. Five holdings are 41.79% of the fund, and Walmart alone is 10.59%; the index's caps cut that group back to 45% whenever it passes 50% at a rebalance. The index has fallen from 37 to 33 stocks in ten months, and the fund is classed as non-diversified.
03
Opportunity cost against the broad market. Over the five years to October 2, 2026 the fund returned 5.9% a year with dividends against 14.0% for the S&P 500. Lower volatility (13.57% a year) and a beta of 0.47 came with that lower return in this window.
Category Commentary
Sector funds slice up the market
PRODUCT CATEGORY

A sector ETF holds one part of the market instead of all of it. The eleven Select Sector SPDR ETFs split the S&P 500 by GICS sector, so together they hold every company in it; XLP is the consumer staples slice. State Street presents them as a way to "take strategic or tactical positions at a more targeted level than traditional style based investing." Because each fund holds a single sector, it carries that sector's risks in full and owns nothing that offsets them. State Street's 2026 distribution schedule also lists a Consumer Staples Select Sector SPDR Premium Income ETF (XLSI), a separate fund paying monthly.

Same sector, different indices
WHAT DIFFERS

The four consumer staples funds compared here draw from different universes. Breadth: XLP holds the 33 S&P 500 consumer staples companies; IYK holds 54 from the Russell 1000; VDC (103 stocks) and FSTA add mid and small companies. Capping: each index has its own rule for limiting large weights ("25/50", "22.5/45", or S&P's 24%/4.8%/50% steps). Classification: the indices do not draw the sector's borders in the same place; IYK holds none of Walmart, Costco, Target, Dollar General or Dollar Tree, together 27.59% of XLP, and holds drug retailers and distributors that XLP does not. Cost: 0.08% to 0.37%. XLP's overlap is 76.6% by weight with VDC and 58.5% with IYK (our calculation).
