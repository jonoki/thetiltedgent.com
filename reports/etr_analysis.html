01 Company Overview

Entergy is a regulated electric utility holding company serving more than 3 million customers across Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi and Texas — including New Orleans — from about 24,600 megawatts of generating capacity, including natural gas, nuclear and renewable plants. FY2025 revenue was $12.95B, up 8.98% from $11.88B in FY2024, and revenue over the twelve months to September 2026 reached $14.04B, up 10.3%. At the November 3, 2026 close its 477.8 million shares were worth $52.04B.

The central debate on this page is not whether Entergy is a good utility — by the usual regulated-utility tests it is performing, and the third quarter of 2026 beat expectations. It is whether the market has already paid for the load-growth story. Entergy trades at a trailing P/E of 24.92 and a forward P/E of 21.32 on 2027 consensus earnings, while its free cash flow over the last twelve months was −$3.70B: about $9.87B of capital expenditure against $6.17B of operating cash flow. That combination is the whole argument: a utility spending far more than it brings in is either building its rate base into a genuine multi-decade rise in electricity demand, or funding a capital programme whose returns depend on four state commissions and the New Orleans City Council staying friendly. The Q3 call sharpened both sides: management put data-centre interest at 7 to 12 gigawatts and added about $1.5 billion to the capital plan through 2030. Sections 04 and 07 take that apart.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
LEGAL MONOPOLY FRANCHISE 4-STATE RATE BASE ~24,600 MW GENERATION ROUND-THE-CLOCK NUCLEAR GULF INDUSTRIAL CORRIDOR INTERCONNECTION QUEUE POSITION BETA 0.43

The moat is statutory, not commercial. Entergy's five operating utilities hold exclusive retail franchises in their service territories; no rival can sell a kilowatt-hour to a residential customer in Jackson, Mississippi. What that buys is not pricing power in the ordinary sense — regulators set the price — but the right to earn a permitted return on every dollar of prudently incurred capital. The moat therefore scales with the capital programme, which is why a utility with $34.94B of debt and negative free cash flow can nonetheless be a defensive holding: the spending is the product. The secondary, harder-to-replicate asset is location. A hyperscaler choosing where to put a gigawatt-scale campus cares about land, water, tax treatment and — decisively — how fast a utility can interconnect it. Entergy sits on cheap Gulf gas, has nuclear plants that run around the clock already in the ground, and serves states actively competing for that investment. That combination is not something a peer utility in a congested Northeastern market can copy.

01
Five regulators, five separate risks. Earnings depend on the Louisiana Public Service Commission, the Arkansas PSC, the Mississippi PSC, the New Orleans City Council and the PUCT all continuing to authorise recovery on a very large capital programme. Entergy's own "Fair Share Plus" framing — routing data-centre revenue into customer bill relief — is evidence that the political cost of large-load growth is real and being actively managed. A single adverse rate order in Louisiana, the largest jurisdiction, would matter more than any operating result.
02
The financing gap is structural, not cyclical. Operating cash flow over the last twelve months was $6.17B against $9.87B of capital spending — a $3.70B shortfall funded by debt and new shares (our calculation from the Q3 2026 Form 10-Q and the 2025 Form 10-K). On September 2, 2026 Entergy settled earlier forward share sales by delivering about 11.1 million shares for about $913 million (Form 8-K). Debt-to-equity is 1.73, and total debt was 63% of total capital at September 30 (Q3 release). This is normal for the sector, but it makes the equity a leveraged bet on the cost of capital: every percentage point of higher long-term interest rates narrows the gap between the allowed ROE and the cost of the debt that funds the rate base.
03
Hurricane and storm-cost exposure. The service territory is the Gulf Coast. Storm restoration costs run into the billions in a bad year and are recovered only with a regulatory lag, through securitisation or storm-cost riders. The August 2026 announcement of a $270 million grid resilience project in Jefferson Parish, Louisiana is the mitigation — and also a reminder of the magnitude of the underlying problem. In the third quarter Entergy Mississippi applied to securitize its Winter Storm Fern costs (Q3 2026 release).
04
Large-load concentration risk cuts both ways. Building generation and transmission for named hyperscalers concentrates the forward growth in a handful of counterparties whose AI capital plans are themselves subject to revision. If a campus is deferred after the plant is built, the question of who pays for a plant nobody needs (a "stranded asset") is argued in front of a state commission, and existing ratepayers — not the hyperscaler — are the ones asked to absorb it. That is a political risk as much as a financial one.

RSI compares the size of recent up-months to the size of recent down-months on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen sharply relative to its own recent history (traders call this "overbought"); below 30 means the opposite. It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a stock can stay overbought for a long time while the business keeps improving.

Over the five years from the November 2021 month-end close of $50.17 (split-adjusted) to $108.92 on November 3, 2026, ETR returned +117.1% on price alone, before $10.88 a share of dividends with ex-dates in that window (split-adjusted, Yahoo). That is more than a doubling from a regulated utility — an outcome few would have expected when the period began, and almost entirely a story of the last three years. From November 2021 through September 2023 the stock went nowhere: $50.17 to $46.25, a 7.8% loss across twenty-two months, as rising interest rates pulled down the prices of stocks bought for their dividends. Everything ETR has earned as an investment was earned after that.

The turn is datable to the second half of 2024, when the market began to price AI-driven electricity demand into utilities with available generation and cheap gas. ETR moved from $46.25 (Sep 2023) to $78.09 (Nov 2024) — a 69% gain in fourteen months with no comparable change in earnings. That is investors paying more for each dollar of earnings, not earnings growth, and it is the reason the current 24.9× trailing P/E has to be held up to the light. The next leg, from $75.82 (Dec 2024) to the chart's highest month-end close of $117.91 (Apr 2026), added another 55%. The stock then slid to $99.26 at the end of September 2026, recovered to $110.11 at the end of October, and closed at $108.92 on November 3 — 7.6% below the April month-end.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Entergy holds legal monopolies across four states that are actively winning hyperscale data-centre investment, with about 24,600 MW of generation, cheap Gulf gas and nuclear power already in the ground. On the Q3 call management put data-centre interest at 7 to 12 gigawatts and said those projects could become signed agreements within 24 months; Google's Arkansas expansion is paid for by Google and, by Entergy's estimate, adds at least $500 million of customer savings — the template that makes the next approvals easier. Consensus sees adjusted EPS of $4.41 in 2026 (guidance $4.35–$4.45) and $5.11 in 2027, a 14.3% two-year compound rate from FY2025's $3.91; the dividend has risen every year since 2021, the latest raise is 6%, and the new rate is 62% of trailing earnings. At $108.92 against a $120.10 average target, the sell side sees 10.3% upside.

Bear Case

A 24.9× trailing P/E on a utility earning a 4.29% return on invested capital, with free cash flow of −$3.70B over the last twelve months, needs a lot to go right. The company must fund a capital programme running about 1.6× operating cash flow — and about $1.5 billion bigger after the Q3 call — from debt and share sales, while already carrying $34.94B of debt at 1.73× equity; any sustained rise in long-term rates narrows the gap between allowed ROE and funding cost directly. Six of the eight October target changes on MarketBeat's list were cuts, and Morgan Stanley's $101 target sits below the price. The 7 to 12 gigawatts is interest, not contracts. And the load-growth revenue is deliberately shared with customers — Mississippi's avoided rate increase, Google's promised savings in Arkansas — which caps the earnings gain per megawatt of new load.

1
Regulatory recovery risk across five commissions. Earnings are the product of rate base and allowed ROE, both set by regulators in Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, New Orleans and Texas. An adverse order on the Arkansas rate settlement, the Google expansion, cost recovery for new plants such as Ironwood, or the Jefferson Parish resilience spend would hit earnings power directly and permanently, not cyclically.
2
Cost of capital. With negative free cash flow every year since at least 2022 and 1.73× debt-to-equity, Entergy is a perpetual issuer of debt and shares. Higher long-term rates raise the funding cost immediately while allowed returns reset only at the next rate case — a lag that has repeatedly pushed utility shares down, as the −23% drawdown into September 2023 showed.
3
Storm exposure concentrated on the Gulf Coast. One severe hurricane season can generate billions in restoration costs recovered only over years and only with regulatory consent; Entergy Mississippi is already applying to securitize Winter Storm Fern costs. This is the risk shareholders cannot insure away, and the reason ETR's beta understates its true tail risk.
Industry Commentary
Data-centre load is arriving — and the economics are being shared
EMERGING TAILWIND

US electricity demand is growing again, and the Gulf South is a preferred destination: cheap gas, available land, accommodating regulators. Entergy's own numbers now show it: weather-adjusted retail sales rose 4.5% in the third quarter of 2026 and industrial sales 4.9%, which the company put down to data-centre and primary-metals customers. In Mississippi, new data-centre revenue prevented a $4.87 monthly increase in July bills under the "Fair Share Plus" pledge (Entergy, August 27, 2026); in Arkansas, Google is paying the full cost of about $600 million of grid investment for its West Memphis expansion. But read those disclosures for what they say: part of the surplus is routed to customers. That is politically necessary and dilutes the growth story at the same time. The bull assumption that each new gigawatt converts one-for-one into shareholder earnings is not supported by the structure the company is publicly promoting.

The capital cycle has outrun the cash flow
ONGOING HEADWIND

Entergy spent about $9.87B on capital expenditure over the last twelve months against $6.17B of operating cash flow — a gap of $3.70B funded from outside (Entergy's Q3 2026 Form 10-Q and 2025 Form 10-K). That is not an anomaly: free cash flow was negative in every year from 2022 (−$2.48B) to 2025 (−$2.53B). And the plan keeps growing: on the Q3 call CFO Kimberly Fontan said, "In total, we increased capital approximately $1.5 billion from 2026 through 2030". For shareholders the consequence is arithmetic: a ROIC of 4.29% against a rising cost of debt leaves very little margin, and the value created depends almost entirely on regulators granting timely recovery at an adequate allowed return. Investors buying ETR for AI load growth are, whether they frame it this way or not, underwriting five utility regulators.

What would change the verdict
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The Q3 print answered part of the question this page asked in September. Management held data-centre interest at 7 to 12 gigawatts and said those projects could become signed agreements within 24 months, raised the floor of 2026 guidance to $4.35, and signed a Google expansion that Google pays for. It also raised the capital plan again, and free cash flow stays deeply negative. If the next prints turn that interest into signed megawatts and the Arkansas commission approves both the rate settlement and the Google expansion, then 21× forward earnings for a monopoly with a long demand tailwind is defensible and the $120 consensus target is reachable. If instead the pipeline stays a range while capital spending keeps climbing, the market is paying 24.9× trailing earnings for a business earning a thin margin over its cost of capital — and the October run of target cuts says a group of professionals is already leaning that way. The stock at $108.92, 8.0% below its 52-week high and 7.8% above Morgan Stanley's $101 target, is priced roughly between those two outcomes.
