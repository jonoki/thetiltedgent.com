01 Company Overview

Evergy is the regulated electric utility for Kansas and the Kansas City side of Missouri — about 1.7 million customers served through three operating utilities: Evergy Kansas Central, Evergy Metro and Evergy Missouri West. It was born on June 4, 2018 from the merger of equals between Westar Energy and Great Plains Energy (parent of KCP&L), began trading on June 5, 2018 and joined the S&P 500 the same day, replacing Navient. It moved its listing from the NYSE to the Nasdaq Global Select Market on December 28, 2022. It owns generation across coal, gas, wind, solar and nuclear, including a share of the Wolf Creek nuclear station in Burlington, Kansas. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $6.09B and GAAP EPS is $3.92.

A utility is a simple machine with a complicated governor. The company builds poles, wires and power plants; the regulator adds that spend to its rate base; and it is allowed to earn a set return on equity on it. Earnings grow when the rate base grows and the regulator lets the return through. For most of the last decade that meant a slow, flat-load, 5-ish-percent story in two states that are sensitive about bills. Data centers changed the arithmetic. Evergy has now signed five energy service agreements for about 2.5 gigawatts of steady-state data-center load — two with Google, one with Meta, one with Digital Realty and one with Beale — and expects retail load to grow 7–8% a year through 2030. It raised its rate-base growth target to about 12% a year through 2030, on a $21.6 billion capital plan. The stock followed: from a $49.14 month-end close in October 2023 to $86.43 in June 2026. It has since slipped for three straight months to $79.26. The question underneath the price is no longer "will the load show up" — it is whether Kansas and Missouri regulators let a utility spend $21.6B and still keep residential bills where the CEO has promised: "in line with or below inflation."

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchised monopoly territory Regulated return on rate base 2.5 GW signed data-center ESAs LLPS tariff cost protection Owned generation incl. nuclear share Transmission & distribution grid

Nobody competes with Evergy for a household in Topeka; the moat is the law. That changes what the risk is. The competitor is not another utility — it is the regulator's patience. A regulated utility's return is a negotiated number, and Evergy is about to ask two commissions to approve a $21.6B spend while households watch bills. The data-center wave strengthens the moat (more rate base, more fixed costs spread over more kilowatt-hours) only if the LLPS tariff works as designed and the big customers carry their own cost. If they do, residential bills can stay flat while earnings compound. If they don't, the political pushback lands on the allowed return.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Regulatory outcomes are the product. Kansas granted $128M of a $196.4M request in 2025, and one commissioner dissented on the 9.7% transmission ROE as "excessive." Missouri Metro's ~$138–140M request goes to hearing in October. Each rate case that lands below the ask widens the gap between rate-base growth and earnings growth.
02
Concentrated customers behind the growth story. Five agreements with a handful of hyperscalers and developers underpin the 7–8% load CAGR. If one project delays or shrinks, the generation already planned for it still needs a home in rate base — and the question of who pays becomes live.
03
Balance sheet is the throttle. Debt/equity 1.61, debt/EBITDA 5.38, interest coverage 2.44 and negative free cash flow every year FY2021–FY2025. The plan needs $700–900M of equity in 2026 alone ($425M already priced through the at-the-market program by June 30), and projected FFO/debt of 14–15% leaves limited room.
04
Weather and storms. In early June 2026 "back-to-back severe storms that generated straight-line winds of 115 miles per hour and multiple tornadoes" caused extensive damage across the territory. The dollar cost was not quantified on the call; storm cost recovery goes through the same regulators.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Signed contracts with Google, Meta, Digital Realty and Beale for 2.5 GW turn a flat-load utility into one planning 7–8% annual load growth and ~12% rate-base growth through 2030. Management guides 6–8%+ EPS growth, accelerating above 8% from 2028, on top of a 3.51% dividend yield. The LLPS tariff is built to make the big customers pay for their own infrastructure, which is the only way the build is politically durable — and if it works, spreading fixed costs over far more kilowatt-hours can keep household bills near inflation. All 13 analysts' targets sit above today's price, and three down months have taken the stock back to its 200-day average.

Bear Case

The plan is $21.6B of capex against an $18.27B market cap, funded by debt (already 5.38x EBITDA) and new shares ($700–900M in 2026 alone), with free cash flow negative every year on record here. The return on all of it is set by two commissions, and the latest Kansas order included a dissent calling the 9.7% transmission ROE "excessive." At 20x trailing and ~18.6x forward earnings, the stock is priced for the plan to be delivered; load growth year-to-date is 3.3%, not 7–8%. One delayed data center, one stingy rate order or one expensive storm season, and the 250 bp gap between rate-base growth and EPS growth widens.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory lag and allowed returns. Earnings depend on what Kansas and Missouri let into rates and at what return. The October Missouri Metro hearings and the Kansas predetermination filing are the next tests.
02
Execution on concentrated large-load customers. A handful of counterparties underpin the load forecast. Delays shift revenue out while generation commitments remain.
03
Financing and dilution. Negative free cash flow, 1.61x debt/equity and projected 14–15% FFO/debt leave little cushion. Rising rates or a credit-rating action would raise the cost of the whole plan.
Industry Commentary
Data centers and the return of load growth
EMERGING TAILWIND

For years a Midwestern electric utility's demand grew at roughly nothing, and earnings growth came from replacing old assets. Evergy's own numbers show what changes when hyperscale data centers arrive: five agreements for 2.5 GW, a retail load CAGR of 7–8% through 2030, a $1B increase to the capital plan for generation for signed customers, and a rate-base growth target raised to ~12%. The CEO also pitched the region's "deep concentration of EPC firms and highly skilled engineering talent" as a competitive advantage for winning more projects.

Affordability — the regulator's constraint
ONGOING HEADWIND

The Kansas order granted about 65% of the request. The Missouri Metro filing would add about $17.70 a month to an average residential bill. Management's line on the Q2 call — residential increases "in line with or below inflation for the majority" of customers, with Missouri West the exception — is a promise the regulators will hold it to. A utility's growth plan is only as good as the commission's willingness to approve it, and commissions answer to ratepayers, not shareholders.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
DEBATE

The bull framing: ~18.6x forward earnings for a utility guiding to 6–8%+ EPS growth that accelerates above 8% from 2028, with a 3.51% yield, is a fair price for a total-return profile near low double digits if the plan holds. The bear framing: the plan is a projection built on a handful of customers, two commissions and a stretched balance sheet, and the market paid its highest multiple of the five years for it in June. The way to decide is not the P/E. It is the Missouri Metro settlement, the next ESA, and weather-normalized demand moving from 3.3% toward the 7–8% the plan needs. All three are reported in the next two quarters.
