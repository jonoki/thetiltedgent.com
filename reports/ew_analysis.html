01 Company Overview

Edwards Lifesciences makes heart valves. That is close to the whole description. It builds and sells transcatheter aortic valves (delivered by catheter through an artery rather than by open-chest surgery), transcatheter mitral and tricuspid repair and replacement devices, and traditional surgical valves and repair rings. Third-quarter 2026 sales were $1,715.5M, up 10.5% (11.2% in constant currency, which removes exchange-rate effects), and the nine months to September 30 came to $5,105.1M, up 13.5% on the same nine months of 2025 (our arithmetic from the release). FY2025 revenue was $6,068M, up 11.5% from $5,440M in FY2024. At a $58.73B market capitalisation the company ranks #205 in the S&P 500 — small next to the diversified device conglomerates it competes with, and that narrowness is the point.

The central debate on this page is what you are actually paying for. EW trades at 58.0× trailing earnings and 33.9× forward earnings (the price divided by the $3.01 midpoint of 2026 adjusted EPS guidance) on a business growing revenue in the low double digits, with a 78.0% gross margin and little debt. That multiple is a bet that TMTT, 12.2% of third-quarter sales and growing 44% year on year, becomes a second aortic-sized franchise, and that the aortic franchise itself speeds up as treatment reaches earlier-stage patients (the PROGRESS trial in moderate aortic stenosis was presented at the TCT conference and published in the New England Journal of Medicine, per the Q3 release). The reimbursement question the last edition flagged has moved in the company's favour: during the quarter, the U.S. Medicare agency (CMS) released the final updated national coverage decision for TAVR (Section 03). With its Q3 results on November 4 the company raised its 2026 sales, TAVR, TMTT and adjusted EPS guidance, and the shares are up 17.7% since the previous edition. Sections 04 and 07 take the valuation and the price apart.

The SAPIEN platform — a collapsible bioprosthetic aortic valve threaded to the heart through a catheter, usually via the femoral artery, and expanded inside the diseased native valve. It replaced open-chest surgery for a large share of aortic stenosis patients over the last decade and it is the reason Edwards exists in its present form. The economics are as good as medical devices get: a single-use implant, a physician base trained on Edwards' delivery system, and a 78.0% company gross margin (trailing twelve months) that TAVR anchors. Q3 TAVR sales grew 8.0% (8.8% in constant currency), and the company raised its 2026 TAVR growth guidance to 9.0–9.5% from 8–9%. In the quarter the final TAVR coverage decision was released (Section 03) and the PROGRESS trial in moderate aortic stenosis was presented and published (Q3 release). Growth from here depends less on winning share and more on expanding the treated population — earlier-stage aortic stenosis patients who are diagnosed but not yet treated.

Competition: Medtronic (Evolut) and Abbott (Navitor) make competing transcatheter aortic valves. Boston Scientific, once a third rival, said on May 28, 2025 that it would stop selling its Acurate TAVR systems worldwide (Cardiac Interventions Today).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
IMPLANT SWITCHING COST PHYSICIAN TRAINING BASE 20+ YEARS CLINICAL EVIDENCE REGULATORY BARRIER HEART-TEAM INSTALLED BASE 78.0% GROSS MARGIN FORTRESS BALANCE SHEET

The moat is clinical evidence compounded into physician habit. A transcatheter valve is implanted once, permanently, by an interventional cardiologist who has been trained on one manufacturer's delivery system and who is answerable for the outcome. Switching costs here are not contractual — they are the surgeon's own risk tolerance. Every additional year of randomised trial data and registry follow-up that Edwards accumulates on SAPIEN raises the evidentiary bar a challenger must clear, and the regulator requires that bar to be cleared with new trials rather than equivalence arguments. The financial signature of that moat is visible in two places: a 78.0% gross margin and an 18.7% ROIC against a 9.3% ROE (both trailing twelve months, StockAnalysis). The gap between those two returns is not weakness; it is the roughly $4.6B of cash and short-term investments sitting in equity and earning less than the business does. Strip the cash out and the operating business earns far more than 9%.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Reimbursement is still a single point of failure, but this round went Edwards' way. During Q3, the Medicare agency CMS released the final updated national coverage decision for TAVR (Q3 call). As reported by the American College of Cardiology on September 16, 2026, it covers symptomatic severe aortic stenosis without the earlier requirement to collect evidence in studies, removes hospital volume minimums and mandatory registry participation, and lets the heart team decide between one operator and two. Coverage for patients without symptoms still requires CMS-approved evidence-development studies, and the ACC notes none exist yet. When one payer determination governs whether a franchise of roughly $4.8B a year (twelve months to June 30, 2026) is covered, the outcomes are not symmetric: the next change could cut as easily as this one widened.
02
72% of revenue in one product family. After the Critical Care divestiture Edwards is less diversified than at any point in its listed history. TAVR is the company (72.4% of Q3 sales). A safety signal, a competitive trial readout, or a procedural-volume slowdown in aortic stenosis hits nearly three-quarters of revenue at once. July 2024 showed the cost: the stock lost 31.7% in a single month after Edwards cut its full-year TAVR outlook.
03
The valuation leaves little room for disappointment. At 58.0× trailing and 33.9× forward earnings with a PEG of 2.70, the price already assumes TMTT keeps growing fast and TAVR growth picks up. The consensus average target of $111.64 implies 9.3% upside; the low target of $84.00 implies −17.8% (StockAnalysis, S&P Global data, read November 9, 2026). The stock sits near the top of a 52-week range that spans $74.66 to $104.17.
04
Well-capitalised competition in the growth segment. TMTT is the option the multiple is paying for, and it is the segment where Edwards is not the incumbent with twenty years of evidence. Abbott's TriClip competes with PASCAL in tricuspid repair and Abbott also sells MitraClip for the mitral valve, and Abbott and Medtronic make the competing aortic valves (Navitor and Evolut). A new market attracts rivals.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses and scores the result from 0 to 100. Above 70 the stock has risen hard and fast enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 it has fallen hard enough that sellers may be. It says nothing about whether the business is good — only about how stretched the recent price moves are. This is a 6-month RSI, so each point covers half a year of price behaviour.

The five-year trajectory is a round trip that has nearly come back. From the November 2021 month-end close of $107.31 to the November 6, 2026 close of $102.14, EW has lost 4.8% — and because the company pays no dividend, that is the total return. The business is bigger (trailing-twelve-month sales are $6.67B), so what changed was the price investors paid for each dollar of earnings: it fell with the 2022 interest-rate rises and again with a company-specific growth scare in 2024, and it has been rebuilding since.

Late 2021 into late 2022 was a fall in what investors would pay for each dollar of earnings: the December 2021 peak of $129.55 to the October 2022 low of $72.43, a −44.1% drawdown (month-end closes) that coincided with the 2022 rise in interest rates. 2023 into mid-2024 was a partial recovery to $95.56 in March 2024 that broke violently in July 2024. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A 78.0% gross margin, 18.7% ROIC, net cash of roughly $3.9B and no dividend obligation, attached to a franchise where switching costs are the implanting physician's own risk tolerance. The company has just raised its 2026 guidance — sales growth to about 11.5%, TAVR to 9.0–9.5%, TMTT to $780–790M, adjusted EPS to $2.97–$3.05 — and TMTT grew 44.3% in Q3 on a base that has nearly tripled in about nine quarters. The final TAVR coverage decision removed hospital volume minimums and the evidence-study requirement for symptomatic patients, 25 of 30 analysts rate the stock Buy, and the $111.64 average target implies 9.3% upside. If TMTT reaches even half of TAVR's scale, the forward 33.9× multiple is not demanding.

Bear Case

You are paying 58.0× trailing earnings and a PEG of 2.70 for a business where 72% of revenue sits in one product family and the growth narrative rests on a segment that is 12% of revenue. July 2024 demonstrated exactly what that concentration costs: −31.7% in a single month from one segment's deceleration. Reimbursement is still a single point of failure: coverage for patients without symptoms depends on evidence studies that do not yet exist. The stock has gained 17.7% since the previous edition and 18.3% since the September 30 close, so the good news from the results is now in the price. The low analyst target of $84.00 is 17.8% below today's price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Reimbursement / coverage determination. The final TAVR coverage decision widened access, but coverage still sits with one payer. Coverage for patients without symptoms depends on CMS-approved evidence-development studies, and the ACC reports none exist yet. A later restrictive change would constrain centre expansion — the exact mechanism the volume-growth case depends on — without any change in the product, the evidence, or the competitive position.
02
Single-franchise concentration post-divestiture. With Critical Care gone, TAVR is 72.4% of Q3 revenue and the company has fewer offsets than at any point in its listed history. The −31.7% month of July 2024 is the best real-world measure of what a TAVR disappointment costs, and the 0.83 beta understates how bad such a month can be.
03
Price risk on a product line that is 12% of revenue. At 33.9× this year's adjusted earnings, the price already assumes TMTT keeps growing fast. Growth rates above 40% do not usually survive a tripling of the base. A slowdown to 30% would still be an excellent result for the business and could still pull the price down sharply.
Industry Commentary
Reimbursement policy is the sector's real regulator
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

In structural heart devices, regulatory approval is necessary but coverage is sufficient. A valve that is approved and not reimbursed is not a business. The final TAVR national coverage decision, released by CMS during Q3 2026, governs precisely this: which hospitals may bill for the procedure, what operator and quality requirements they must meet, and which patients qualify. As reported by the ACC on September 16, 2026, it removed hospital volume minimums and mandatory registry participation and let the heart team choose one operator or two, which lowers the barriers to opening new TAVR programmes; for patients without symptoms, coverage requires evidence-development studies that do not yet exist. For a company with 72.4% of Q3 revenue in one procedure, coverage terms are more consequential to the earnings path than any competitor's product launch.

Tricuspid treatment is close to a new market
EMERGING TAILWIND

TMTT is closer to creating a new market. The U.S. approval for Abbott's TriClip, for example, covers patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation despite medical therapy who are at intermediate or greater risk for surgery (Alliance for Aging Research). The growth rate has stayed high (+56% in FY2025, +44% in Q3 2026) while the base nearly tripled from $228.9M (trailing twelve months to March 2024) to $674.6M (to June 2026) and the company now guides 2026 sales to $780–790M. This matters for forecasts: growth like this depends on how fast centres can be trained, which makes it slower to start and more durable once running than growth won by taking customers from a rival. The bull case needs that durability; the bear case only needs training to hold growth back for a few quarters.

The business grew while its valuation fell — and what has started to rebuild it
ONGOING HEADWIND

Edwards's business improved through 2021–2026 while the price investors paid per dollar of its earnings did not. Market capitalisation went from $80.88B at the end of 2021 to $58.73B today while trailing sales reached $6.67B. Higher interest rates explain the 2022 leg; the 2024 leg was company-specific. What would end the pattern is arithmetically simple and operationally hard — a second segment large enough that a single-segment miss no longer breaks the stock. Bull framing: TMTT at 12% of sales and growing 44% is visibly on that path, the balance sheet can accelerate it through acquisition, and 25 of 30 analysts rating the stock Buy say the professionals already believe it. Bear framing: that belief is already in the 58.0× trailing multiple, the segment is still only 12% of revenue, and five years of price history say the market has repeatedly paid up for this story and then taken the payment back. The December 4 investor conference, where the company gives 2027 guidance, and the fourth-quarter report are the next places that argument gets settled.
