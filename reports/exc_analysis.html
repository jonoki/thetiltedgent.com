01 Company Overview

Exelon is the largest pure transmission-and-distribution ("T&D" — the poles, wires, substations and gas mains that move energy, as opposed to the plants that make it) utility holding company in the United States. Since spinning off its nuclear and power-marketing arm as Constellation Energy on February 1, 2022, it owns no meaningful generation: it operates six regulated utilities — ComEd (northern Illinois), PECO (Philadelphia), BGE (Baltimore) and Pepco Holdings (Pepco in DC and Maryland, Delmarva Power in Delaware and Maryland, Atlantic City Electric in New Jersey) — serving roughly 10.7 million electric and gas customers across the mid-Atlantic and Chicago. FY2025 revenue was $24.26B, up 5.3%, on GAAP net income of $2.77B ($2.73 per share); trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $25.33B, up 6.6%. At $45.05B it ranks #25 among listed electricity companies globally.

The debate on this page is unusually clean because a wires-only utility has exactly one earnings engine: rate base (the regulator-approved value of the assets a utility has invested in, on which it is allowed to earn a return) multiplied by allowed ROE. Exelon guides to 7.9% annual rate-base growth through 2029 on a $41.7B four-year capital plan, with operating EPS compounding "near the top end" of 5–7% and a 9–10% consolidated earned ROE. Data-center interconnection demand — 11 GW of "high-probability" load, 4 GW of it under signed agreements backed by $1B of collateral — is the new argument for why that growth is durable. Against it: PJM capacity prices have hit their cap in three straight auctions, retail bills are rising fast enough that Exelon withdrew a $510M PECO rate request in April, Maryland's legislature has just banned the reconciliation mechanism Pepco relied on, and the Illinois Commerce Commission rules on ComEd's $15.3B grid plan on December 15. Every dollar of rate base needs a regulator's signature, and regulators answer to ratepayers who are angry about bills. That tension is the whole stock.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EXCLUSIVE FRANCHISE TERRITORIES $41.7B FOUR-YEAR CAPEX PLAN FERC-REGULATED TRANSMISSION (16% CAGR) DATA-CENTER TSA COLLATERAL ($1B) NO GENERATION / COMMODITY EXPOSURE TOP-QUARTILE RELIABILITY SCALE: ~10.7M CUSTOMERS

A regulated wires utility has the most legally airtight moat in the stock market and the least valuable one: nobody can string competing wires down your street, and in exchange you may charge only what a commission says. So the moat is not against competitors, it is against the regulator — and it consists of being so obviously necessary, so reliable and so well-capitalised that commissions keep approving your capital plans. Exelon's version has three real components. First, the Constellation spin removed commodity risk: it does not own the nuclear plants whose capacity revenue is now the political football in PJM, so record capacity prices hurt its customers' bills but not its own earnings directly. Second, an increasing share of the plan is FERC-regulated transmission, where allowed returns are set federally, formula rates update annually, and transmission rate base is guided to grow 16% a year through 2029 — a deliberate shift away from the state commissions that just refused it money. Management sizes an incremental $12–17B transmission opportunity beyond the plan, and two MISO Tranche 2.1 competitive bids with Invenergy ($1.9B) await a Q4 2026 decision. Third, the TSA framework converts speculative hyperscaler demand into collateralised contracts — 4 GW backed by $1B — which is the difference between "data centers will pay for the grid" as a slogan and as a receivable. What the moat cannot do is force a commission to approve a 10.4% ROE in a state where the legislature has just told it not to. Management's own reliability numbers — ~$1B of avoided outage costs in 2025, nearly 2 million fewer interruptions since 2021 — are the argument it will make.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Affordability politics is now the binding constraint. PJM capacity costs of $16.4B for 2028/29 (which would have been $29.7B without the $325/MW-day cap) flow straight onto the bills Exelon delivers, even though Exelon earns nothing on them. Customers do not distinguish the wires charge from the capacity charge. The result: a $510M PECO request withdrawn, a Maryland reconciliation cut by 56%, $0.02 of BGE credit losses in one quarter, and legislation banning the recovery tool that multi-year plans depend on. When the cap expires after the December 2026 auction, ACE customers in New Jersey could see $14.70–$23.64 more per month — and every state commission will be asked to offset it somewhere.
▸
Regulatory concentration in Illinois. ComEd is 43% of Q2 earnings and ~9 of 11 GW of the credible data-center pipeline. One five-member commission decides on December 15 whether a $15.3B plan proceeds as filed. The ICC has already rejected a ComEd grid plan once (2023). A partial approval does not kill the thesis, but it re-bases the 7.9% rate-base CAGR downward immediately.
▸
Data-center demand is shrinking as it gets verified. "High-probability" load fell 40% from 18 GW to 11 GW in six months once TSAs were required. Management frames this as quality over quantity, and it is — but the direction of revision is down, a $20B Joliet campus TSA was cancelled, and PJM's own "connect and manage" proposals could shift interconnection costs and timing. If hyperscalers redirect to states with cheaper capacity, the load thesis moves with them.
▸
Leverage and equity dilution. $52.67B of total debt against $28.77 of book value per share, debt-to-equity of 1.77, interest coverage of 2.35× and negative $1.92B free cash flow (capex exceeds operating cash flow, by design). The plan needs $3.4B of new equity through 2029 plus $16.5B of new debt. Holding-company interest already cost $0.02 of EPS in Q2. A one-notch downgrade or a 100 bp move in rates matters more here than at almost any Nasdaq-100 constituent.

Notable One-Off Items

Two things distort the trailing numbers and both are worth understanding rather than adjusting away.

The Sep 4 price is ex-dividend. Exelon went ex its $0.42 quarterly dividend on September 4, so roughly $0.42 of the day's $0.89 decline is mechanical, not a reaction to news. The underlying move was closer to −1%.

The GAAP-to-adjusted bridge is small and getting smaller. Q2'26 GAAP EPS was $0.39 against $0.43 adjusted — a $0.04 gap, mostly at PECO ($119M vs $130M), BGE ($55M vs $70M) and PHI ($109M vs $126M); ComEd had no adjustments. Q2'25 had no gap ($0.39 both), so the year-over-year GAAP comparison is flat while the adjusted comparison is +10%. Management's Q2 driver bridge: +$0.04 distribution/transmission rates, +$0.04 absence of a prior-year customer relief fund charge, +$0.01 PECO weather, −$0.02 BGE credit losses, −$0.02 holding-company interest. Note that $0.04 of the $0.04 improvement is a non-recurring comparison (the relief fund lapping), so the organic run-rate improvement is essentially rates minus interest minus credit losses — about a penny.

Pre-2022 history is a different company. Every FY2021 and earlier figure — and every chart month before February 2022 — includes Constellation's nuclear fleet. FY2021 revenue of $17.94B and EPS of $1.74 are not comparable to the post-spin series; FY2022 onward is the clean T&D record (revenue $19.08B → $24.26B, EPS $2.20 → $2.73, a 5.5% EPS CAGR over three years).

Storm costs from 16 major ComEd events in 1H 2026 are largely deferred as regulatory assets under Illinois rules and recovered later, so they suppress cash flow now more than earnings.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal. The Feb 2022 spin step shows up as a spurious oversold reading — ignore that one.

Five-year trajectory. The honest starting point is the spin. From the first post-spin monthly close of $42.56 (Feb 2022) to $43.64 today, the price is up +2.5% in four and a half years. Add the roughly $7.2 per share of dividends paid since ($1.35 in 2022, $1.44, $1.52, $1.60, and $1.26 so far in 2026) and the total return is about +19%, or roughly 4% a year — most of it the dividend. Including the pre-spin months, the unadjusted price fell from $53.19 (Oct 2021) to $43.64, −18%, but that figure ignores the Constellation shares handed to holders and is not a return. Inside the flat outcome are two distinct regimes: a grinding −18% decline from $42.56 to the $34.81 low of January 2024 as 10-year yields rose and the ICC rejected ComEd's first grid plan, then a +42% recovery to the $49.47 peak in February 2026 as rates eased and the data-center narrative arrived, followed by a −12% fade as the pipeline was cut and Maryland turned hostile. Relative to the NASDAQ-100 — a tech-weighted index that roughly doubled over the same window — a low-beta wires utility was never going to keep up, and it did not; against the S&P 500 it also lagged badly. Against utility peers (PPL, FE, ES, PEG) the relevant comparison is the yield and the forward multiple, and on both EXC screens cheap: 14.8× forward vs a 16.3× peer average, at a matching 3.85% yield. Specific index total-return figures over the identical window were not fetched and are therefore not stated.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The arithmetic of a wires utility is short. Earnings = rate base × allowed ROE × equity ratio, less holding-company interest. Exelon's plan grows rate base 7.9% a year through 2029 on $41.7B of capex, funded by $21.8B of internal cash flow, $13.1B of utility debt, $3.4B of holdco debt and $3.4B of new equity. Because new shares are issued, EPS grows slower than rate base — hence 5–7% EPS growth, "near the top end." Consensus has bought exactly that: $2.86 for 2026, $3.04 for 2027 (+6.4%). At $43.64 you pay 14.8× forward earnings for ~6% growth and a 3.85% yield, which sums to a plausible 9–10% annual return if — and only if — regulators approve the rate base and the allowed ROE holds near 9.5–10%.

What the market is pricing. A 1.5-turn forward discount to PPL, FE, ES and PEG, a price 13% off the 52-week low, and a Hold consensus with two Strong Sells. That discount is the market's estimate of regulatory risk: 43% of earnings await one Illinois order on December 15; Maryland has just banned a recovery mechanism and cut a reconciliation by more than half; Pennsylvania's rate base is frozen at old rates until PECO dares to refile. The CFO's pivot — $1.1B out of distribution, $1.5B into FERC-regulated transmission growing 16% a year — is the right chess move, because federal formula rates are insulated from state affordability politics. But it also means the "growth" is migrating toward the part of the business where Exelon must win competitive bids rather than simply file.

The data-center thesis, honestly stated. 11 GW of high-probability load is enormous — roughly the peak demand of the entire state of Maryland — and 4 GW of it is backed by $1B of collateral. If it connects, it spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours, lowering everyone else's bill per unit and giving regulators political cover to approve the grid spend. That is the bull case's most important sentence. The bear case's is that the number was 18 GW six months ago, that PJM cannot find generation to serve the load it already has (6.8 GW short, 525 MW cleared), and that a grid built for hyperscalers who then locate elsewhere is stranded cost that ratepayers — and eventually shareholders — eat.

Bull Case

The cheapest large T&D utility on forward earnings, with the lowest beta of its peer group, a 3.85% yield growing ~5% a year, and a fully-funded $41.7B plan whose 2026 equity is already priced. No commodity or generation exposure means PJM's price spike is a customer problem, not an earnings problem. A clean ICC order on December 15 plus MISO transmission wins would re-rate EXC from 14.8× toward the 16–17× peer average — roughly $47–50 on 2027 EPS — while 4 GW of collateralised data-center load makes the grid spend easier to defend. Wells Fargo's $50 and Morgan Stanley's $53 sit in exactly that range.

Bear Case

Affordability has already cost Exelon a $510M rate case, a 56% reconciliation haircut, a legislative ban on its recovery tool, and $0.02 a quarter in credit losses — before the PJM price cap even expires. A second ICC rejection would cut the rate-base CAGR from 7.9% toward 5–6% and the EPS target below the range. The equity need ($3.4B) and 2.35× interest coverage leave no cushion for higher rates; negative $1.92B FCF means the dividend is effectively debt-funded during the build. The two Strong Sells and the $41 low target are pricing a utility that has to keep asking for money from politicians who have stopped saying yes.

  Industry Commentary
PJM Has Run Out of Power and the Cap Runs Out in December
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The July 2026 PJM auction for 2028/29 cleared at the $325/MW-day cap for the third straight time, missed the reliability target by 6.8 GW, and attracted just 525 MW of new supply (down from 774 MW in December). Total cost to consumers: $16.4B, or $29.7B without the cap. A 10 MW industrial customer's monthly capacity charge goes from ~$6,000 in 2024 to ~$70,000 in 2028. Exelon owns no generation and earns none of this — but it sends the bill. PJM's board is filing a backstop-auction and "connect and manage" framework at FERC; the Edison Electric Institute says the status quo "benefits generation owners and fails to attract sufficient new supply." For wires utilities the transmission mechanism is simple: when the generation line on the bill doubles, the commission looks for savings on the wires line.
Multi-Year Rate Plans Are Being Rewritten Mid-Term
ONGOING HEADWIND
Multi-year rate plans (MYPs — where a commission pre-approves several years of rate increases against a capital budget, with a "reconciliation" true-up afterward) were the industry's answer to regulatory lag. Maryland is now the test of what happens when the true-up bill arrives. On March 31, 2026 the Maryland PSC granted Pepco $13.36M of a $30.6M reconciliation, wrote that MYPs do "not allow for unbounded and run-away cost recovery when the utility overruns," and the legislature's Next Generation Energy Act then prohibited bill-increasing reconciliations in future MYPs. In Pennsylvania, PECO withdrew $510M rather than fight. The implication across the sector: utilities are shifting capital toward FERC-jurisdiction transmission — Exelon moved $1.5B — and toward assets with ratepayer-benefit stories (storage, resilience). Growth is not disappearing; it is relocating to where the regulator is less exposed to the ballot box.
Collateralised Load Is the New Currency of Grid Growth
EMERGING TAILWIND
The data-center story is maturing from press-release gigawatts to contracts. Exelon's FERC-approved TSAs — credit obligations, committed revenue contributions and shortfall payments that make the hyperscaler, not the household, bear the cost of grid built for it — cut the "high-probability" pipeline from 18 GW to 11 GW, with 4 GW backed by $1B of collateral, and a $20B Joliet campus was cancelled when it failed the test. PJM's July peak hit a record 168 GW. Bull framing: verified, collateralised load is the strongest possible argument to a commission that grid spend is prudent, and it spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours — the only mechanism that actually lowers per-unit bills while rate base grows. Bear framing: the revision was down 40% in six months, PJM has no generation to serve it, and PJM's "connect and manage" proposal may shift the interconnection queue and cost allocation in ways that either accelerate or strand the wires investment. The stock at 14.8× forward is pricing something closer to the bear framing than the bull.
