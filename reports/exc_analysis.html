01 Company Overview

Exelon is the largest pure transmission-and-distribution ("T&D" — the poles, wires, substations and gas mains that move energy, as opposed to the plants that make it) utility holding company in the United States. Since spinning off its nuclear and power-marketing arm as Constellation Energy on February 1, 2022, it owns no meaningful generation: it operates six regulated utilities — ComEd (northern Illinois), PECO (Philadelphia), BGE (Baltimore) and Pepco Holdings (Pepco in DC and Maryland, Delmarva Power in Delaware and Maryland, Atlantic City Electric in New Jersey) — serving almost 11 million electric and gas customers across the mid-Atlantic and Chicago (Q3 2026 release). FY2025 revenue was $24.26B, up 5.3%, on GAAP net income of $2.77B ($2.73 per share); trailing-twelve-month revenue through September 2026 is $25.94B, up 6.7%. The third quarter, reported on October 30, beat: adjusted EPS of $0.87 against $0.80 Nasdaq consensus, and management narrowed its 2026 range to $2.85–$2.91, the top part of the old $2.81–$2.91.

The debate on this page is unusually clean because a wires-only utility has exactly one earnings engine: rate base (the regulator-approved value of the assets a utility has invested in, on which it is allowed to earn a return) multiplied by allowed ROE. Exelon guides to 7.9% annual rate-base growth through 2029 on a $41.7B four-year capital plan, with operating EPS compounding "near the top end" of 5–7% and a 9–10% consolidated earned ROE. Data-center interconnection demand — about 11 GW of "high-probability" load, 9 GW of it at ComEd, where about 45% is backed by signed transmission security agreements and roughly $1.2B of collateral (Q3 2026 earnings presentation, Form 8-K exhibit 99.2) — is the new argument for why that growth is durable. Against it: PJM capacity prices have hit their cap in three straight auctions, retail bills are rising fast enough that Exelon withdrew a $510M PECO rate request in April, Maryland's legislature banned the reconciliation mechanism Pepco relied on this year, and the Illinois Commerce Commission's order on ComEd's $15.3B grid plan is expected by December 15. Every dollar of rate base needs a regulator's signature, and regulators answer to ratepayers who are angry about bills. That tension is the whole stock.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EXCLUSIVE FRANCHISE TERRITORIES $41.7B FOUR-YEAR CAPEX PLAN FERC-REGULATED TRANSMISSION (16% A YEAR) DATA-CENTER TSA COLLATERAL (~$1.2B) NO GENERATION / COMMODITY EXPOSURE TOP-QUARTILE RELIABILITY SCALE: ~11M CUSTOMERS

A regulated wires utility has the most legally airtight moat in the stock market and the least valuable one: nobody can string competing wires down your street, and in exchange you may charge only what a commission says. So the moat is not against competitors, it is against the regulator — and it consists of being so obviously necessary, so reliable and so well-capitalised that commissions keep approving your capital plans. Exelon's version has three real components. First, the Constellation spin removed commodity risk: it does not own the nuclear plants whose capacity revenue is now the political football in PJM, so record capacity prices hurt its customers' bills but not its own earnings directly. Second, an increasing share of the plan is FERC-regulated transmission, where allowed returns are set federally, formula rates update annually, and transmission rate base is guided to grow 16% a year through 2029 — a deliberate shift away from the state commissions that just refused it money. On the Q3 call the CFO again sized "an additional $12 billion-$17 billion of investment opportunity over the next decade beyond our plan" in transmission. Third, the TSA framework converts speculative hyperscaler demand into collateralised contracts — about 45% of ComEd's high-probability load, backed by roughly $1.2B — which is the difference between "data centers will pay for the grid" as a slogan and as a receivable. What the moat cannot do is force a commission to approve a 10.4% ROE in a state that granted Pepco 9.4% in August. Management's own reliability numbers — ~$1B of avoided outage costs in 2025, nearly 2 million fewer interruptions since 2021 — are the argument it will make.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Affordability politics is now the binding constraint. PJM capacity costs of $16.4B for 2028/29 (which would have been $29.7B without the $325/MW-day cap) flow straight onto the bills Exelon delivers, even though Exelon earns nothing on them. Customers do not distinguish the wires charge from the capacity charge. The result: a $510M PECO request withdrawn, a Maryland reconciliation cut by 56%, $0.02 of BGE credit losses in one quarter, and legislation banning the recovery tool that multi-year plans depend on. If the cap lapses after the December 2026 auction, capacity charges can rise further — and every state commission will be asked to offset them somewhere.
▸
Regulatory concentration in Illinois. ComEd is 40% of Q3 utility earnings and ~9 of 11 GW of the credible data-center pipeline. One five-member commission decides on December 15 whether a $15.3B plan proceeds as filed. The ICC has already rejected a ComEd grid plan once (2023). A partial approval does not kill the thesis, but it cuts the 7.9% yearly rate-base growth target at once.
▸
Data-center demand is shrinking as it gets verified. "High-probability" load fell 40% from 18 GW to 11 GW at the Q2 update once TSAs were required, and on the Q3 call the CFO said the wider cluster-study pipeline went "from 25 to 23 gigawatts." Management frames this as quality over quantity, and it is — but the direction of revision is down, a $20B Joliet campus TSA was cancelled, and PJM's own "connect and manage" proposals could shift interconnection costs and timing. If hyperscalers redirect to states with cheaper capacity, the load thesis moves with them.
▸
Leverage and equity dilution. $53.19B of total debt against $29.12 of book value per share, debt-to-equity of 1.77, interest coverage of 2.26× and negative $2.59B free cash flow over the last twelve months (capex exceeds operating cash flow, by design). The plan needs $3.4B of new equity through 2029 plus $16.5B of new debt. Interest and other costs took $0.05 off Q3 EPS against a year earlier. A one-notch credit downgrade or a one-percentage-point move in rates matters more here than at almost any other Nasdaq-100 company.

Notable One-Off Items

Several things distort the trailing numbers, and each is worth understanding rather than adjusting away.

The GAAP-to-adjusted gap widened in Q3. Q3'26 GAAP EPS was $0.70 against $0.87 adjusted — a $168M gap, where Q3'25 had none ($0.86 both). The excluded items, per the release: $130M for "regulatory matters" (at PHI, $133M, described as "the disallowance of certain capitalized costs"), $26M of project impairments (capital-project write-downs, at BGE), $15M of consulting and severance costs for a cost-management programme, and a $3M change in environmental costs that went the other way. So GAAP earnings fell 17% while adjusted earnings rose 3%. A disallowance means a regulator refused to let customers pay for money already spent: that is a real cost to shareholders, even though management strips it out as a one-off.

What moved adjusted EPS. Management's Q3 bridge from $0.86 to $0.87: +$0.11 other energy delivery, +$0.06 distribution and transmission rates, +$0.02 weather, −$0.01 load, −$0.07 operating and maintenance, −$0.05 depreciation and amortization, −$0.05 interest and other (per the release; net +$0.01). Year to date, adjusted EPS is $2.20 against $2.17; GAAP $1.98 against $2.15.

Pre-2022 history is a different company. Every chart month before February 2022 includes Constellation's nuclear fleet, and so does FY2021 GAAP EPS of $1.74 (Exelon's 2021 10-K), so neither is comparable to the post-spin series; FY2022 onward is the clean T&D record (revenue $19.08B → $24.26B, EPS $2.20 → $2.73, EPS growth of 7.5% a year over three years; stockanalysis.com).

Storm costs from 16 major ComEd weather events in 2026 through July are largely deferred as regulatory assets under Illinois rules and recovered later, so they suppress cash flow now more than earnings. PECO's are different this year: higher storm costs went straight through its Q3 earnings.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The arithmetic of a wires utility is short. Earnings = rate base × allowed ROE × equity ratio, less holding-company interest. Exelon's plan grows rate base 7.9% a year through 2029 on $41.7B of capex, funded by $21.8B of internal cash flow, $13.1B of utility debt, $3.4B of holdco debt and $3.4B of new equity. Because new shares are issued, EPS grows slower than rate base — hence 5–7% EPS growth, "near the top end." Consensus has bought exactly that: $2.87 for 2026, $3.03 for 2027 (+5.8%; non-GAAP, stockanalysis.com). At $46.45 you pay 15.3× FY2027 earnings for ~6% growth and a 3.62% yield, which sums to a plausible 9–10% annual return if — and only if — regulators approve the rate base and the allowed ROE holds near 9.5–10%. The third quarter did not change that arithmetic: the plan, the rate-base growth and the growth target were all reaffirmed, and the 2026 range moved up at the bottom.

What the market is pricing. At the last edition EXC traded about one and a half points of forward earnings below PPL, FE, ES and PEG; after a 15% rebound from the September low it is about half a point below (15.3× vs 15.8×), with a Hold consensus, one Strong Sell left and an average target only 2.8% above the price. Most of the discount has gone, while the regulatory calendar has not moved: ComEd, 40% of Q3 utility earnings, awaits one Illinois order by December 15; Maryland has banned a recovery mechanism, cut a reconciliation by more than half and granted Pepco 9.4% against BGE's 10.40% ask; Pennsylvania's rate base earns returns set in its last case until PECO refiles. The Q3 $133M disallowance at PHI is a reminder that a regulator can also refuse money already spent. The CFO's pivot — $1.1B out of distribution, $1.5B into FERC-regulated transmission growing 16% a year — is the right move, because federal formula rates are insulated from state affordability politics. But it also means the "growth" is migrating toward the part of the business where Exelon must win competitive bids rather than simply file.

The data-center thesis, honestly stated. 11 GW of high-probability load is enormous, and at ComEd about 45% of it is backed by roughly $1.2B of collateral. If it connects, it spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours, lowering everyone else's bill per unit and giving regulators political cover to approve the grid spend. That is the bull case's most important sentence. The bear case's is that the number was 18 GW before the Q2 update, that PJM cannot find generation to serve the load it already has (6.8 GW short at the July auction, 525 MW cleared; about 11.5 GW short by 2030 on Exelon's own estimate), and that a grid built for hyperscalers who then locate elsewhere is stranded cost that ratepayers — and eventually shareholders — eat.

Bull Case

A large T&D utility priced just under its peers on forward earnings (15.3× FY2027 vs 15.8×), with the lowest beta of the group, a 3.62% yield growing ~5% a year, a $41.7B plan with all 2026 debt financing done and about 37% of the $3.4B equity need through 2029 already priced. No commodity or generation exposure means PJM's price spike is a customer problem, not an earnings problem. A clean ICC order by December 15 plus new transmission wins could lift the price to 16–17 times FY2027 earnings — about $48–52 a share on the $3.03 consensus — while collateralised data-center load makes the grid spend easier to defend. Q3 showed the rate-base engine working: ComEd +10.5% and BGE +45% in adjusted earnings on approved rates.

Bear Case

Affordability has already cost Exelon a $510M rate case, a 56% reconciliation haircut, a legislative ban on its recovery tool, $0.02 of BGE credit losses in Q2 and a $133M disallowance at PHI in Q3 — before the PJM price cap even expires. A second ICC rejection would cut yearly rate-base growth from 7.9% toward 5–6% and the EPS target below the range. The equity need ($3.4B) and 2.26× interest coverage leave no cushion for higher rates; negative $2.59B of trailing FCF means the dividend is effectively debt-funded during the build. With the price now within 3% of the average target, the remaining Strong Sell is betting against a utility that has to keep asking for money from politicians who have stopped saying yes.

  Industry Commentary
PJM Has Run Out of Power and the Cap Runs Out in December
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The July 2026 PJM auction for 2028/29 cleared at the $325/MW-day cap for the third straight time, missed the reliability target by 6.8 GW, and attracted just 525 MW of new supply (down from 774 MW in December). Total cost to consumers: $16.4B, or $29.7B without the cap. A 10 MW industrial customer's monthly capacity charge goes from ~$6,000 in 2024 to ~$70,000 in 2028. Exelon owns no generation and earns none of this — but it sends the bill. PJM's board is filing a backstop-auction and "connect and manage" framework at FERC; on its Q3 call Exelon said PJM could be about 11.5 GW short by 2030 and that it supports keeping the cap for now; the Edison Electric Institute says the status quo "benefits generation owners and fails to attract sufficient new supply." For wires utilities the transmission mechanism is simple: when the generation line on the bill doubles, the commission looks for savings on the wires line.
Multi-Year Rate Plans Are Being Rewritten Mid-Term
ONGOING HEADWIND
Multi-year rate plans (MYPs — where a commission pre-approves several years of rate increases against a capital budget, with a "reconciliation" true-up afterward) were the industry's answer to regulatory lag. Maryland is now the test of what happens when the true-up bill arrives. On March 31, 2026 the Maryland PSC granted Pepco $13.36M of a $30.6M reconciliation, wrote that MYPs do "not allow for unbounded and run-away cost recovery when the utility overruns," and the legislature's Next Generation Energy Act then prohibited bill-increasing reconciliations in future MYPs. In Pennsylvania, PECO withdrew $510M rather than fight. The implication across the sector: utilities are shifting capital toward FERC-jurisdiction transmission — Exelon moved $1.5B — and toward assets with ratepayer-benefit stories (storage, resilience). Growth is not disappearing; it is relocating to where the regulator is less exposed to the ballot box.
Collateralised Load Is the New Currency of Grid Growth
EMERGING TAILWIND
The data-center story is maturing from press-release gigawatts to contracts. Exelon's FERC-approved TSAs — credit obligations, committed revenue contributions and shortfall payments that make the hyperscaler, not the household, bear the cost of grid built for it — cut the "high-probability" pipeline from 18 GW to 11 GW; at ComEd about 45% of it is backed by roughly $1.2B of collateral (Q3 2026 earnings presentation, sec.gov), and a $20B Joliet campus was cancelled when it failed the test. PJM's July peak hit a record 168 GW. Bull framing: verified, collateralised load is the strongest possible argument to a commission that grid spend is prudent, and it spreads fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours — the only mechanism that actually lowers per-unit bills while rate base grows. Bear framing: the revision was down 40% in six months, PJM has no generation to serve it, and PJM's "connect and manage" proposal may shift the interconnection queue and cost allocation in ways that either accelerate or strand the wires investment. At 15.3× FY2027 earnings, close to the peer average, the stock now sits between the two framings rather than at the bear end.
