01 Company Overview

Expand Energy is the company that used to be Chesapeake Energy. Chesapeake — the shale pioneer that went bankrupt in 2020 — emerged from Chapter 11 on February 9, 2021 with about $7.8 billion of debt converted into equity and its old shares cancelled; the new stock began trading on NASDAQ as CHK. On October 1, 2024 Chesapeake completed its merger with Southwestern Energy, renamed itself Expand Energy, and began trading as EXE on October 2, 2024. It joined the S&P 500 on March 24, 2025. Today it is, in its own description, the largest domestic natural gas producer: about 7.54 Bcfe/d in Q3 2026, 92% of it natural gas, from the Haynesville in Louisiana/Texas and the Marcellus and Utica in Appalachia.

The story of this stock is a story about one price it does not control. In Q1 2026 Expand realised $5.70/Mcf for gas in Northeast Appalachia; in Q2 and again in Q3 it realised $2.15. Net income went from $1,159M in Q1 to $522M in Q2 and $519M in Q3 on almost identical production (7.44 → 7.48 → 7.54 Bcfe/d). The company's answer is to stop being a pure price-taker: on September 16, 2026 it completed the $1.25B purchase of Twin Eagle, a gas marketing and storage business, and it targets $500M a year of marketing and commercial EBITDA (operating cash earnings) by the end of 2028, against $150M in the first nine months of 2026. It also has a permanent leader again: Danny Brown, who ran Chord Energy, became President and CEO on October 26, 2026, after eight months under interim CEO Mike Wichterich. Q3 beat Nasdaq consensus (adjusted EPS $1.46 against $1.28), yet the stock fell 3.22% the next session. At $90.44 it sits 28.6% below its 52-week high and 8.6% above its 52-week low. The central debate: is Expand a gas-price option wearing a utility's balance sheet, or is it becoming an integrated gas company whose earnings deserve a steadier multiple?

The Haynesville and Bossier shales sit closest to the Gulf Coast, which is where US gas demand is growing fastest: LNG export terminals. That geography is the Haynesville's whole case. The company guides the market toward a structural demand story — 13–15 Bcf/d of LNG feedgas growth by 2030 — and the Haynesville is the part of Expand best placed to feed it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: largest US gas producer Two-basin portfolio Gulf Coast / LNG proximity Low leverage (0.8x) Marketing + storage network Hedge book

A commodity producer's only durable moat is being low on the cost curve — being able to keep pumping, and keep paying a dividend, at a price that forces weaker producers to stop. Expand's case rests on two things: rock quality across two basins that feed different markets, and a balance sheet at 0.8x leverage (net debt against a year of cash earnings) that lets it ride out $2 gas quarters without a crisis. The Twin Eagle purchase, completed in September, is an attempt to add a second, less commodity-like moat: owning the customer relationships, storage and pipe capacity that capture the spread between where gas is produced and where it is needed. The vulnerability is that none of this changes the fact that 92% of production is sold at a price set by weather, LNG demand and everyone else's drilling.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The price is the business. Realised gas prices fell 40–62% by area between Q1 and Q2 2026 and stayed low in Q3 ($2.15–$2.68/Mcf); free cash flow fell 80% from Q1 to Q2 and did not recover in Q3 ($339M). Hedges cover 67% of Q4 2026 and about 55% of 2027, so next year carries less cushion.
02
A new chief executive at a turning point. Danny Brown took over on October 26, 2026, after eight months under an interim CEO, and inherits a just-completed $1.25B acquisition and a headquarters move to Houston. On the Q3 call he said he would consider acquisitions only with strong synergies (Investing.com's transcript); priorities can still shift as he sets his own plan.
03
Marketing target versus run-rate. The $500M-a-year marketing and commercial EBITDA target for end-2028 compares with $150M in the first nine months of 2026 and about $80M guided for Q4. Trading and marketing earnings are also more volatile and harder to value than production — the market may not pay a steadier multiple for them.
04
Northeast basis exposure. Northeast Appalachia's realised price was $2.15/Mcf in Q2 and again in Q3, 80 cents under the NYMEX benchmark — the weakest of the three areas — because of limited takeaway pipes. More than a third of production carries that local-price risk.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the long-run numbers. First, the 2021 bankruptcy: FY2021 net income of $6.33B and EPS of $60.44 include the reorganisation, and per-share history before February 2021 belongs to a different, cancelled equity — this report's price series starts in October 2021, after emergence. Second, the October 2024 Southwestern merger roughly doubled the business, so FY2025 revenue growth of 173% is mostly acquisition, not organic. Third, hedge accounting: GAAP net income includes gains and losses on derivatives, which is why Q3 2026 GAAP net income ($519M) exceeded adjusted net income ($338M). Buybacks also matter: about $900M repurchased in the first nine months of 2026 ($305M of it in Q3), about 4% of the shares, with $1B of authorisation left. Consensus shows FY2025 revenue of $11.64B falling to $9.80B in FY2026 (estimate) even though total revenues for the first nine months of 2026 were already $10.64B in the release — the consensus counts revenue on a different basis (it leaves out items such as derivative gains), so the two are not comparable.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the largest US gas producer at 7.8x trailing earnings, 3.8x EV/EBITDA and a ~12% trailing free-cash-flow yield, with 0.8x leverage and a 2.54% base dividend — at a moment when realised prices sit near the Haynesville breakeven, arguably close to the bottom of what the business can earn. Q3 beat estimates even so. The company-guided demand picture (13–15 Bcf/d of new LNG feedgas by 2030) points toward its Gulf Coast-adjacent Haynesville. Twin Eagle, now owned, can turn a pure price-taker into a company that earns a margin on moving gas; a permanent CEO is in place; and every analyst target, even the lowest at $92, sits above today's price.

Bear Case

Trailing numbers include a cold Q1 and hedge gains; Q2 and Q3 are the run-rate that matters, and free cash flow was $343M and $339M in those quarters, not $1.7B. Forward P/E is 10.3 on 2027 adjusted estimates, and consensus has 2027 adjusted EPS ($8.77) below 2026 ($8.97). The $500M marketing EBITDA target for end-2028 compares with $150M in nine months, and marketing earnings are harder to value, not easier. Spending guidance went up to about $3B, net debt rose to $4.66B with the acquisition, and about 45% of 2027 is unhedged. And the stock fell 3.2% on a quarter that beat estimates and still sits below its 10-month average.

Industry Commentary
The gas price cycle
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Everything in this report comes back to one variable. The five-year chart has three peaks and three troughs, and the company's revenue went $7.30B → $14.12B → $6.05B → $4.26B → $11.64B across FY2021–FY2025 — part merger, mostly price. Management's lever is volume discipline: production guidance of 7.45–7.55 Bcfe/d holds output flat rather than growing into a weak market, the rig count drops to 10 in Q4, and well costs are down about 14% since 2024 (Q2 presentation). That lowers the breakeven, but it does not change the fact that when realised gas fell to $2.15–$2.62 in Q2 2026, free cash flow fell by four-fifths, and it stayed there in Q3 with realised gas at $2.15–$2.68.

Demand growth: LNG and power
MANAGEMENT FORECAST

The company's own view is that US gas demand grows structurally by 19–24 Bcf/d by 2030 and 31–40 Bcf/d by 2040, with LNG feedgas alone adding 13–15 Bcf/d by 2030. In Q1 2026 Mike Wichterich put it plainly: "The world critically needs natural gas supply to meet rapidly rising power demand …" These are management forecasts, not independent data. The Delfin LNG agreement (about 1.15 million tonnes a year from 2031) gives Expand a direct link to that export demand — real, but five years away.

From producer to integrated gas company
EMERGING TAILWIND

The strategic bet is that a gas producer which also owns storage, transport capacity and a customer book can capture value that pure producers leave on the table — the gap between a weak local price in Pennsylvania and a strong price somewhere else. The bull framing: 14 Bcf/d of marketed volume and 49 Bcf of storage after Twin Eagle give Expand the tools to earn that spread every year, not just in cold winters. The bear framing: marketing earnings are thin-margin, trading-like and volatile, and the market has historically paid low multiples for them. The way to judge it is not the P/E; it is the marketing EBITDA line, quarter by quarter: about $80M is guided for Q4, against the $500M a year targeted for the end of 2028.
