01 Company Overview

Expand Energy is the company that used to be Chesapeake Energy. Chesapeake — the shale pioneer that went bankrupt in 2020 — emerged from Chapter 11 on February 9, 2021 with about $7.8 billion of debt converted into equity and its old shares cancelled; the new stock began trading on NASDAQ as CHK. On October 1, 2024 Chesapeake completed its merger with Southwestern Energy, renamed itself Expand Energy, and began trading as EXE on October 2, 2024. It joined the S&P 500 on March 24, 2025. Today it is, in its own description, the largest domestic natural gas producer: about 7.48 Bcfe/d in Q2 2026, 92% of it natural gas, from the Haynesville in Louisiana/Texas and the Marcellus and Utica in Appalachia.

The story of this stock is a story about one price it does not control. In Q1 2026 Expand realised $5.70/Mcf for gas in Northeast Appalachia; in Q2 it realised $2.15. Net income went from $1,159M to $522M in one quarter on almost identical production (7.44 → 7.48 Bcfe/d). The company's answer is to stop being a pure price-taker: it is buying Twin Eagle, a gas marketing and storage business, for $1.25B, and has raised its target for marketing-driven free cash flow to $750M a year. Meanwhile it is doing it without a permanent CEO — Nick Dell'Osso left in February 2026 and Chairman Mike Wichterich is running the company on an interim basis. The stock at $87.65 sits 30.8% below its 52-week high and just 3.1% above its 52-week low. The central debate: is Expand a gas-price option wearing a utility's balance sheet, or is it becoming an integrated gas company whose earnings deserve a steadier multiple?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: largest US gas producer Two-basin portfolio Gulf Coast / LNG proximity Low leverage (~0.5x) Marketing + storage network Hedge book

A commodity producer's only durable moat is being low on the cost curve — being able to keep pumping, and keep paying a dividend, at a price that forces weaker producers to stop. Expand's case rests on two things: rock quality across two basins that feed different markets, and a balance sheet at ~0.5x leverage that lets it ride out a $2 gas quarter without a crisis. The Twin Eagle deal is an attempt to add a second, less commodity-like moat: owning the customer relationships, storage and pipe capacity that capture the spread between where gas is produced and where it is needed. The vulnerability is that none of this changes the fact that 92% of production is sold at a price set by weather, LNG demand and everyone else's drilling.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The price is the business. Realised gas prices fell 40–62% by area between Q1 and Q2 2026, and free cash flow fell 80% with them. Hedges (66% of 2026, 41% of 2027) soften the blow this year; next year there is less cushion.
02
Leaderless at a turning point. The CEO who built the merger left in February 2026. An interim chief executive is closing a $1.25B acquisition and relocating headquarters to Houston while a permanent successor is sought. Strategy can shift with the next appointment.
03
Marketing target versus run-rate. The $750M annual marketing FCF target compares with ~$120M achieved in H1 2026. Trading and marketing earnings are also more volatile and harder to value than production — the market may not pay a steadier multiple for them.
04
Northeast basis exposure. Northeast Appalachia's realised price was $2.15/Mcf in Q2 — the weakest of the three areas — because of limited takeaway pipes. More than a third of production carries that local-price risk.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the long-run numbers. First, the 2021 bankruptcy: FY2021 net income of $6.33B and EPS of $60.44 include the reorganisation, and per-share history before February 2021 belongs to a different, cancelled equity — this report's price series starts in September 2021, after emergence. Second, the October 2024 Southwestern merger roughly doubled the business, so FY2025 revenue growth of 173% is mostly acquisition, not organic. Third, hedge accounting: GAAP net income includes gains and losses on derivatives, which is why Q2 2026 GAAP net income ($522M) exceeded adjusted net income ($317M). Buybacks also matter: about $849M repurchased year-to-date through Q2 2026, retiring roughly 4% of shares. Consensus shows FY2025 revenue of $11.64B falling to $9.78B in FY2026 (estimate) even though the first half of 2026 alone was $7.04B — the consensus revenue basis appears to differ from reported revenue, and we treat it with caution.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the largest US gas producer at 7.6x trailing earnings, 3.5x EV/EBITDA and a ~12% trailing free-cash-flow yield, with ~0.5x leverage, a BBB rating and a 2.62% base dividend — at a moment when realised prices are near the Haynesville breakeven, arguably close to the bottom of what the business can earn. The company-guided demand picture (13–15 Bcf/d of new LNG feedgas by 2030) points toward its Gulf Coast-adjacent Haynesville. Twin Eagle, if it delivers, turns a pure price-taker into a company that earns a margin on moving gas — and every analyst target, even the lowest at $93, sits above today's price.

Bear Case

Trailing numbers include a cold Q1 and hedge gains; Q2 is the run-rate that matters, and Q2 free cash flow was $343M, not $1.7B. Forward P/E of 11.5 is higher than trailing because earnings are expected to fall. The $750M marketing target is about three times the H1 2026 run-rate annualised, it is being pursued by an interim CEO, and marketing earnings are harder to value, not easier. Only 41% of 2027 is hedged. And the stock has told the story itself: down 10.7% in September to just above its 52-week low, below every moving average.

Top 3 Risks

01
Natural gas prices. Q1 to Q2 2026 showed the sensitivity: realised prices down 40–62% by area, free cash flow down 80%. With hedge coverage falling from 66% (2026) to 41% (2027), next year's results depend more on the spot market.
02
Execution on Twin Eagle and the marketing strategy. A $1.25B purchase funded with cash and revolver borrowings, a synergy target running to end-2028 and a free-cash-flow target far above the current run-rate. If the numbers fall short, the company will have added debt and complexity to a commodity business.
03
Leadership transition. No permanent CEO since February 2026, with a headquarters move under way. Strategy, capital allocation and the balance between buybacks, dividends and deals could all change with the permanent appointment.
Industry Commentary
The gas price cycle
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Everything in this report comes back to one variable. The five-year chart has three peaks and three troughs, and the company's revenue went $7.30B → $14.12B → $6.05B → $4.26B → $11.64B across FY2021–FY2025 — part merger, mostly price. Management's lever is volume discipline: production guidance of 7.4–7.6 Bcfe/d holds output flat rather than growing into a weak market, and well costs are down about 14% since 2024. That lowers the breakeven, but it does not change the fact that when realised gas fell to $2.15–$2.62 in Q2 2026, free cash flow fell by four-fifths.

Demand growth: LNG and power
MANAGEMENT FORECAST

The company's own view is that US gas demand grows structurally by 19–24 Bcf/d by 2030 and 31–40 Bcf/d by 2040, with LNG feedgas alone adding 13–15 Bcf/d by 2030. In Q1 2026 Mike Wichterich put it plainly: "The world critically needs natural gas supply to meet rapidly rising power demand." These are management forecasts, not independent data. The Delfin LNG agreement (about 1.15 million tonnes a year from 2031) is Expand's first direct link to that export demand — real, but five years away.

From producer to integrated gas company
EMERGING TAILWIND

The strategic bet is that a gas producer which also owns storage, transport capacity and a customer book can capture value that pure producers leave on the table — the gap between a weak local price in Pennsylvania and a strong price somewhere else. The bull framing: 14 Bcf/d of marketed volume and 49 Bcf of storage after Twin Eagle give Expand the tools to earn that spread every year, not just in cold winters. The bear framing: marketing earnings are thin-margin, trading-like and volatile, and the market has historically paid low multiples for them. The way to judge it is not the P/E; it is the marketing free-cash-flow line, quarter by quarter, against the $750M target.
