01 Company Overview

Fastenal distributes the unglamorous stuff that keeps factories running — threaded fasteners, safety gear, cutting tools, janitorial and MRO (maintenance, repair and operations — the consumable supplies a plant burns through that never end up inside the finished product) — and, increasingly, sells the service of managing that inventory on the customer's own floor through vending machines, smart bins and embedded "Onsite" teams. FY2025 revenue was $8.20B, up 8.7% from $7.55B, and the trailing twelve months through September 2026 reached $9.08B, up 13.5%. At the October 15, 2026 close it was worth $57.80B (1,147.3M shares × $50.38, our calculation). Its closest listed peers are W.W. Grainger and MSC Industrial.

The debate on this page is a single number: 41×. That is the trailing P/E the market is paying for a company that sells nuts and bolts, with a 44.6% trailing gross margin that has slipped every year since 2021, in a business where Grainger trades at 34× and MSC Industrial at 32×. Either Fastenal is doing something structurally different from a distributor — and the FMI, large-site and contract-customer data below are the evidence to weigh — or it is a very good cyclical carrying a very rich multiple. The third quarter of 2026, Jeff Watts' first as CEO, was a clean beat with operating margin up to 21.5%, and the stock ended the two sessions after it 2% lower: the business kept its promises and the multiple drifted down from 42× to 41× as earnings caught up. The report quantifies what 41× actually buys.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
143,878 FMI DEVICES ON CUSTOMER FLOORS ONSITE EMBEDDED STAFF 76.5% CONTRACT REVENUE FASTENER SOURCING DEPTH OWNED TRUCKING & HUB NETWORK 32% ROIC, LITTLE DEBT DECENTRALISED "BLUE TEAM" CULTURE

The moat is physical presence inside the customer's plant, not product. A vending machine or a bank of smart bins bolted to a factory wall, restocked by a Fastenal employee who knows the plant manager by name, converts a purchasing decision into a habit — and it generates consumption data no competitor can see. That is why FMI is now 44.8% of sales and why contract customers, who grew 18.8% in Q3 2026, are 76.5% of revenue. The economics show up in the return line: trailing ROIC is 32.6% (stockanalysis.com), on a balance sheet carrying $452M of total debt including leases ($100M of it borrowings) against $4.15B of book equity. A distributor earning over 30% on capital with a 3.9× current ratio (short-term assets almost four times short-term bills) is not a normal distributor; it is closer to a route-based service business. The weakness of the moat is that it is expensive to extend — every new device and Onsite costs capex (still guided to $310–330M in 2026, up from $230.6M) — and that it is cyclical underneath: 76.6% of sales go to manufacturers. The CFO put the U.S. PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index, a factory survey where above 50 means activity is expanding) at an average of nearly 55 in the quarter; the demand is real, and it is the kind that turns.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Tariffs and price/cost. Fasteners are steel, and much of the steel is imported. Pricing added about 240 bps to Q3 2026 sales growth (the release's figure, against 240–270 bps a year earlier) and price/cost still took about 30 bps off gross margin, improved from about 40 bps in Q2. The release lists tariff refunds among the quarter's gross-margin helps and says about 20 bps of those helps was timing; the CFO said on the call not to expect the refunds in any material amount in Q4. The cost is certain; recovering it is a negotiation with contract customers (76.5% of sales) who have leverage.
▸
Gross margin drift. 46.20% (FY2021) → 46.07% → 45.66% → 45.08% → 45.01% (FY2025) → 44.61% TTM. A decline in each of the last four fiscal years, and the trailing figure is lower still. The company attributes it to customer mix — the shift toward larger customers cost about 50 bps in Q3 alone — which means the drift is the strategy working, not a fixable execution problem. Q3's 44.8% was a sequential improvement from Q2's 44.6%, but still below 45.3% a year earlier. Operating margin rose to 21.5% only because overhead (SG&A, 23.3% of sales from 24.6%) grew more slowly than sales.
▸
Manufacturing cyclicality. Over three-quarters of revenue is manufacturing, 44.6% is heavy manufacturing, and heavy manufacturing grew fastest (+20.2%) in Q3. In FY2024, when the PMI sat below 50, revenue rose only 2.7% and EPS fell 1%. Today's +15.5% arrived with the PMI near 55 by the CFO's account. At 41× earnings the stock is priced as though the current growth rate is structural. The FY2024 print says a meaningful part of it is not.
▸
Softer targets and a slowing September. Jeff Watts became CEO on July 16, 2026. The FMI signings goal he inherited was cut in July (to 27–29k from 28–30k) and was held, not restored, in October; Q3 signings rose 6.2% per day, slower than sales. Watts said September grew 14.4%, below July and August. None of these is alarming alone; together they are the soft edges of a story the multiple assumes keeps accelerating.

Notable One-Off Items

Fastenal's accounts are unusually clean — GAAP only, no adjusted-EBITDA reconciliation, no restructuring charges in the periods reviewed. On the Q3 call the CEO described a technology acquisition that brought coordination the company needed around its AI and technology; no name or price was given, and the CFO said the company sees no need for anything large. Four things distort the trailing numbers and should be held in mind:

The 2-for-1 stock split (May 2025). All per-share figures here are split-adjusted. Any source showing pre-split EPS of ~$2.00 or prices in the $70–80s is stale. The chart in Section 07 uses split-adjusted closes throughout.

Pricing is still inflating growth, but less. About 240 bps of Q3 2026's 15.5% growth was price (versus 240–270 bps a year earlier and roughly 290 bps in Q2 2026), taken largely to recover tariff-driven costs. Volume-and-mix growth is therefore about 13%. Price/cost was still negative at about 30 bps — the company raised prices and still gave up a little margin, which is cost push rather than pricing power.

Q3 gross margin had help that may not repeat. The release credits direct-sourcing savings, supplier rebates and tariff refunds, and says about 20 bps of that help was timing. Strip that 20 bps out and Q3's 44.8% gross margin is closer to 44.6–44.7%, roughly flat on Q2.

Working capital swung back. Q3 operating cash flow of $421.6M was 105.0% of net income, after a weak Q2 (69%); over nine months it is 94.8%. Receivables still stood at $1,615M at September 30, up from $1,245M at December 31 on faster sales. The 2026 capex step-up to $310–330M (from $230.6M) for hub and FMI capacity keeps free-cash-flow conversion below 100% this year.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $28.54 (split-adjusted) to the October 15, 2026 close of $50.38, FAST has returned +76.5% in price (dividends excluded) — a compound annual rate of about 12.1%, plus a dividend that has run near 2% a year. Over the same dates SPY returned +68.1% and QQQ +88.6% in price. EPS grew from $0.80 (FY2021) to $1.23 (TTM), or +54%, so most of the price gain came from earnings and the rest (about 15%) from a higher multiple. The path was orderly by growth-stock standards: the worst monthly-close drawdown was −28% (Dec 2021 $32.03 → Sep 2022 $23.02), and the sharpest single-month drop was −16% in October 2025 after the Q3 2025 print. The qualitative point is that a 0.69-beta industrial gaining three-quarters in five years has leaned partly on its multiple, and multiples run in reverse too. The last year shows both sides: the stock fell 19% from August to December 2025 on monthly closes, then climbed back 25.5% to $50.38 as earnings grew about 16%.

Earnings-led recovery
08 Summary & Key Risks

What 41× buys, itemised. Start with the top line. Sales grew 15.5% in Q3 2026, of which about 2.4 points is price. Strip that out and volume-plus-mix is running about 13% — for a $9.1B distributor, genuinely excellent, and well ahead of Grainger's 7.8% and MSC's 4.4% trailing revenue growth. Next, the mechanism: 44.8% of sales flow through FMI devices (FASTBin / FASTVend / FASTStock — Fastenal-owned vending machines, weight-sensing bins and scanned shelf-stock on the customer's floor), with the installed base at 143,878 MEU (Machine Equivalent Units — a weighting that counts a full vending machine as 1.0 and smaller bins as fractions), growing 7.4%. Then the customer concentration the model is designed to produce: 3,288 sites spending $50k+ a month, +18.7%, generating $1,457M of the quarter's $2,463M, while total active sites fell 8% to 90,759. Fastenal is deliberately trading many small branch walk-ins for fewer large embedded relationships — that is the shift from branches to Onsites, expressed in the numbers the company actually discloses. The result: 15.62% net margin, 32.6% ROIC, 35% ROE, 0.11 debt-to-equity, and in Q3 an operating margin of 21.5%, up from 20.7%, because overhead grew more slowly than sales.

And what it does not buy. It does not buy gross-margin stability: 46.2% → 44.6% over five years, and the company itself frames the decline as a consequence of the strategy (bigger customers cost about 50 bps of gross margin in Q3 alone), not a blip. It does not buy pricing power in the strict sense — about 2.4% of price in Q3 still came with a negative 30 bps price/cost, meaning tariff costs are being passed through late and incompletely, and part of Q3's margin help (tariff refunds, timing) is not expected to repeat. It does not buy immunity from the cycle: FY2024 grew 2.7% with EPS down 1%. And it does not buy an Onsite count: the Q3 release again gave no active Onsite figure, so the large-site count is the best measure the company offers of the shift from branches to Onsites.

Valuation, three ways. (i) 41.0× trailing GAAP EPS of $1.23 — the honest number, and there is no adjusted-EPS fudge here: Fastenal reports GAAP and nothing else. (ii) 39.7× 2026 consensus EPS of $1.27, and 36.6× on stockanalysis.com's forward measure. (iii) 49× trailing free cash flow of $1.18B, held down by the capex step-up. Against Grainger at 34.0× trailing / 27.5× forward and MSC at 31.6× / 24.4×, the premium is 7–12 turns on every measure. The bull says a 32%-ROIC business growing about 13% before price at 15% net margins should trade like a compounder, and that Grainger's 34× is the wrong anchor — Cintas or Old Dominion is the right one. The bear says the PEG of 3.0 is the whole story: paying 41× for mid-teens growth is close to what investors paid in August 2025 (roughly 48×), right before a 19% drawdown on an 11.5% EPS-growth quarter, and September's 14.4% says growth has stopped accelerating.

Bull Case

Fastenal has quietly converted from a branch-based distributor into an embedded-supply-chain service with 143,878 devices and 3,288 large sites generating 59% of its revenue. That model is producing 15%-plus net margins and 32% ROIC with essentially no debt, and it is growing about 13% before price. Q3 showed the operating leverage: sales +15.5%, net income +19.7%, operating margin up to 21.5%, and cash flow back above net income. Reshoring, a PMI the CFO put near 55 and tariff-driven demand for domestic supply chains give the manufacturing base a tailwind. If earnings keep growing faster than sales, the multiple comes down on its own, as it did from roughly 48× in August 2025 to 41× now while the price ended slightly higher. A new CEO from sales, not finance, is the right person to keep the device-signing engine running.

Bear Case

At 41× trailing, a PEG of 3.0 and a Hold consensus with an average target only 1.4% above the price, the stock has already discounted the good news — the market's verdict on a clean beat was −2.0% over two sessions. Gross margin has fallen in each of the last four fiscal years; Q3's improvement leaned on refunds and timing the company does not expect to repeat; price/cost is still negative; the FMI signings goal cut in July was not restored; device signings grew 6.2%, slower than sales; and 77% of revenue comes from manufacturers; in FY2024, the last time the PMI sat below 50, total revenue grew 2.7%. The stock fell 16% in one month in October 2025 on an 11.5% EPS-growth quarter. Same setup, one year later, with a first-time CEO.

Top 3 Risks

01
Multiple compression on deceleration. The Oct 2025 episode is the template: double-digit growth that merely decelerates from the prior quarter can cost 15–20% at this valuation. September's 14.4% was already below July and August; Q4 2026 laps an 11.1% quarter, and pricing's contribution is shrinking as the 2025 price increases drop out of the comparison.
02
Tariff cost push outrunning price. Fasteners are 30.3% of sales and made of steel, much of it imported. A negative 30 bps price/cost on about 2.4% of price means tariff costs are not yet fully recovered; Q3 also had tariff refunds the CFO does not expect in any material amount in Q4. A further round of tariff increases would force either another price push into contract customers (76.5% of sales) or further gross-margin erosion.
03
Manufacturing cycle turning. Heavy manufacturing is 44.6% of sales and grew 20.2% in Q3 — the most cyclical slice growing fastest. If the PMI slips back below 50, FY2024 (revenue +2.7%, EPS −1%) is the base-rate outcome, and at 0.69 beta the market is not pricing that scenario at all.
  Industry Commentary
Tariffs Turn Distributors Into Price-Takers
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Industrial distribution's economics assume the distributor can re-price faster than its costs move. In 2025–26 that assumption is being tested: Fastenal's realised price rose from 140–170 bps (Q2 2025) to about 290 bps (Q2 2026), and gross margin still fell, with price/cost costing about 40 bps in Q2 and about 30 bps in Q3. Costs are certain, recovery is negotiated, and the customers that matter most (large contract accounts, 76.5% of sales) have the most negotiating leverage. Q3 brought some relief — tariff refunds and supplier rebates — that the company itself treats as partly one-time. Every broadline peer faces the same squeeze; Fastenal's fastener weighting makes it the most steel-exposed of the three.
Consolidation Toward the Embedded Model
ONGOING HEADWIND
The $50k+/month site count rising 18.7% while total active sites fall 8% is the industry in one line: the large customer is consolidating vendors, the small customer is going to Amazon Business or a regional house, and the middle is disappearing. Fastenal's response — FMI devices and Onsite staff that make it structurally hard to unbundle — is working (contract mix 73.8% → 76.5% in a year) but it costs money and margin: 2026 capex of $310–330M is up by more than a third, the shift to big customers cost about 50 bps of gross margin in Q3, and the company trimmed its 2026 device-signing goal in July. Grainger is running the same play with its Keepstock program; MSC is a smaller, more metalworking-centric version. The winners will be the ones with the density to restock a bin cheaply; the losers are anyone still relying on counter sales.
Reshoring and a PMI Above 50
EMERGING TAILWIND
The CFO put the U.S. manufacturing PMI at an average of nearly 55 in Q3 2026, and heavy manufacturing (+20.2%) was Fastenal's fastest end market; the CEO also described mobile service units deployed to give Onsite-style support on data-center projects. Tariff-driven reshoring, if it persists, adds domestic factory floors that need bins and vending machines. Bull framing: Fastenal is the cleanest listed way to own U.S. factory-floor activity, with 77% manufacturing exposure and a device base that scales with new plants; 41× is a fair price for that scarcity. Bear framing: a strong PMI is a cyclical high, not a permanent state; the FY2024 numbers (revenue +2.7%, EPS −1%) show exactly what this business does below 50, and heavy manufacturing, the slice growing fastest, is also the most cyclical.
