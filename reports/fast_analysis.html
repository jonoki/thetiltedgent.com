01 Company Overview

Fastenal distributes the unglamorous stuff that keeps factories running — threaded fasteners, safety gear, cutting tools, janitorial and MRO (maintenance, repair and operations — the consumable supplies a plant burns through that never end up inside the finished product) — and, increasingly, sells the service of managing that inventory on the customer's own floor through vending machines, smart bins and embedded "Onsite" teams. FY2025 revenue was $8.20B, up 8.7% from $7.55B, and the trailing twelve months through June 2026 reached $8.75B, up 12.5%. It is the world's roughly #450 company by market capitalisation at $56.92B, and the second-largest listed U.S. industrial distributor behind W.W. Grainger.

The debate on this page is a single number: 42×. That is the trailing P/E the market is paying for a company that sells nuts and bolts, with a 44.7% gross margin that has slipped every year since 2021, in a business Grainger trades at 33× and MSC Industrial at 30×. Either Fastenal is doing something structurally different from a distributor — and the FMI, Onsite and contract-customer data below are the evidence to weigh — or it is a very good cyclical carrying a very rich multiple into a leadership change. The report quantifies what 42× actually buys.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
140,789 FMI DEVICES ON CUSTOMER FLOORS ONSITE EMBEDDED STAFF 75.8% CONTRACT REVENUE FASTENER SOURCING DEPTH OWNED TRUCKING & HUB NETWORK 32% ROIC, NET-CASH BALANCE SHEET DECENTRALISED "BLUE TEAM" CULTURE

The moat is physical presence inside the customer's plant, not product. A vending machine or a bank of smart bins bolted to a factory wall, restocked by a Fastenal employee who knows the plant manager by name, converts a purchasing decision into a habit — and it generates consumption data no competitor can see. That is why FMI is now 44.6% of sales and why contract customers, who grew 17.6% in Q2, are 75.8% of revenue. The economics show up in the return line: trailing ROIC is 32%, back above 30% for the first time in more than a decade according to management, on a balance sheet carrying $441M of total debt (largely leases) against $4.07B of book equity. A distributor earning 32% on capital with 4.18× current-ratio liquidity is not a normal distributor; it is closer to a route-based service business. The weakness of the moat is that it is expensive to extend — every new device and Onsite costs capex (guided to $310–330M in 2026, up from $231M) and headcount — and that it is cyclical underneath: 75.9% of sales go to manufacturers, and the U.S. PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index — a survey above 50 means factory activity is expanding) has only been above 50 for six months.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Tariffs and price/cost. Fasteners are steel, and a large share are imported. Fastenal pushed roughly 2.9% of price in Q2 2026 (4.5% on a two-year stack) and still ran a negative 40 bps net price/cost, driving gross margin to 44.6% from 45.3%. Management said tariff uncertainty shows up "through cost planning and pricing discussions rather than demand" — which is exactly the problem: the cost is certain, the ability to pass it on is a negotiation with 75.8%-contract customers who have leverage.
▸
Gross margin drift. 46.20% (FY2021) → 46.07% → 45.66% → 45.08% → 45.01% (FY2025) → 44.70% TTM. Five consecutive years of decline, and Q2 2026 (44.56%) was the lowest quarter in the eight shown. The company attributes it to customer and product mix — larger contract customers and non-fastener products carry lower margins — which means the drift is the strategy working, not a fixable execution problem. Operating margin has held at ~20–21% only because SG&A leverage offsets it.
▸
Manufacturing cyclicality. Three-quarters of revenue is manufacturing, 44% is heavy manufacturing. FY2024 revenue grew 2.7% and EPS fell 1% when the PMI sat below 50; today's +14.7% daily sales growth arrived with a PMI averaging 53. At 42× earnings the stock is priced as though the current growth rate is structural. The FY2024 print says a meaningful part of it is not.
▸
Leadership transition and softened targets. Jeff Watts became CEO on July 16, 2026 after 30 years of Florness/Kierlin continuity. In the same quarter the company cut its 2026 FMI signings goal (to 27–29k from 28–30k) and its Digital Footprint target (to 63–64% from 66%), and Q2's 20.5% June growth was flagged as partly "one-off orders". None of these is alarming alone; together they are the first soft data points in a story the multiple assumes is accelerating.

Notable One-Off Items

Fastenal's accounts are unusually clean — no acquisitions, no adjusted-EBITDA reconciliation, no restructuring charges in the periods reviewed — but three things distort the trailing numbers and should be held in mind:

The 2-for-1 stock split (May 22, 2025). All per-share figures here are split-adjusted. Any source showing pre-split EPS of ~$2.00 or prices in the $70–80s is stale. The chart in Section 07 halves every pre-May-2025 close.

Pricing is inflating growth. Roughly 290 bps of Q2 2026's 14.7% daily-sales growth was price (versus 140–170 bps a year earlier), taken largely to recover tariff-driven fastener costs. Volume-and-mix growth is therefore closer to 11–12%. The price came with a negative 40 bps price/cost — the company raised prices and still lost margin, which is the definition of cost push rather than pricing power.

Working capital absorbed cash. Q2 operating cash flow of $265.7M was 69.4% of net income, with receivables up 17.6% on faster sales; accounts payable stretched 25.2% partly offsets it. The 2026 capex step-up to $310–330M (from $230.6M) for hub and FMI capacity will hold FCF conversion below 100% this year. June's 20.5% daily-sales growth included "one-off orders" per management — so the Q2 exit rate overstates the run rate, though August's +16.6% suggests not by much.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the September 2021 close of $25.81 (split-adjusted; $51.61 at the time) to the September 4, 2026 close of $49.60, FAST has returned +92.2% in price — a compound annual rate of about 13.9%, plus a dividend that has run near 2% a year. For a company whose EPS grew from $0.80 (FY2021) to $1.17 (TTM), or +46%, that means roughly half the return came from the multiple expanding from the low-30s to 42×. The path was orderly by growth-stock standards: the worst monthly-close drawdown was −28% (Dec 2021 $32.03 → Sep 2022 $23.02), and the sharpest single-month drop was −16% in October 2025 after the Q3 2025 print. A verified NASDAQ-100 or S&P 500 total-return figure for the identical window was not fetched and is deliberately not stated; the qualitative point is that a 0.71-beta industrial nearly doubling in five years is a re-rating story, and re-ratings run in reverse too. The 52-week change of +0.28% says that reverse has quietly been under way for a year: earnings grew ~13%, the stock did not.

08 Summary & Key Risks

What 42× buys, itemised. Start with the top line. Daily sales grew 14.7% in Q2 2026 and 16.6% in August, of which roughly 3 points is price. Strip that out and volume-plus-mix is running 11–13% — for a $8.7B distributor in a PMI-53 economy, genuinely excellent, and roughly double Grainger's 7.8% and triple MSC's 4.4%. Next, the mechanism: 44.6% of sales flow through FMI devices (FASTBin / FASTVend / FASTStock — Fastenal-owned vending machines, weight-sensing bins and scanned shelf-stock on the customer's floor), up 60 bps of mix in a year, with the installed base at 140,789 MEU (Machine Equivalent Units — a weighting that counts a full vending machine as 1.0 and smaller bins as fractions), growing 6.5%. Then the customer concentration the model is designed to produce: 3,125 sites spending $50k+ a month, +16.5%, generating ~$1.38B of the quarter's $2.39B, while total active sites fell 8% to 93,283. Fastenal is deliberately trading many small branch walk-ins for fewer large embedded relationships — that is the "branches to Onsites" shift in the task brief, expressed in the numbers the company actually discloses. The result: 15.45% net margin, 32% ROIC, 34% ROE, 0.11 debt-to-equity.

And what it does not buy. It does not buy gross-margin stability: 46.2% → 44.7% over five years, and the company itself frames the decline as a consequence of the strategy (bigger customers, more non-fastener product), not a blip. It does not buy pricing power in the strict sense — 2.9% of price in Q2 still produced a negative 40 bps price/cost, meaning tariffs on imported fasteners are being passed through late and incompletely. It does not buy immunity from the cycle: FY2024 grew 2.7% with EPS down 1%. And it does not buy the Onsite disclosure this report went looking for — the active Onsite count was not in the Q2 release or call as fetched, so the single cleanest measure of the branch-to-Onsite shift remains an open item for the Q3 release.

Valuation, three ways. (i) 42.1× trailing GAAP EPS of $1.17 — the honest number, and there is no adjusted-EPS fudge here: Fastenal reports GAAP and nothing else. (ii) 39× 2026 consensus EPS of $1.26, 35× 2027's $1.40. (iii) 49× trailing free cash flow of $1.16B, flattered downward by the working-capital build and the capex step-up. Against Grainger at 33.5× trailing / 27.1× forward and MSC at 29.6× / 23.2×, the premium is roughly 10 turns on every measure. The bull says a 32%-ROIC business growing 12% organically at 15% net margins should trade like a compounder, and that Grainger's 33× is the wrong anchor — Cintas or Old Dominion is the right one. The bear says the PEG of 3.2 is the whole story: consensus EPS growth decelerates to 10.9% in 2027, and paying 42× for 11% growth is what investors did in August 2025, right before a 19% drawdown on an 11.5% EPS-growth quarter.

Bull Case

Fastenal has quietly converted from a branch-based distributor into an embedded-supply-chain service with 140,789 devices and 3,125 large sites generating over half its revenue on contract. That model is producing 15%-plus net margins and 32% ROIC with essentially no debt, and it is growing 12% organically before price. Reshoring, a PMI back above 50 for six straight months and tariff-driven demand for domestic supply chains give the manufacturing base a multi-year tailwind. If the $1.40 2027 EPS is beaten the way 2026 has been so far, 35× forward turns into 30× and the stock re-rates on earnings alone. A new CEO from sales, not finance, is the right person to keep the device-signing engine running.

Bear Case

At 42× trailing, 3.2× PEG and a Hold consensus with an average target below the price, the stock has already discounted the good news. Gross margin has fallen for five straight years and Q2's 44.6% was the lowest quarter on the page; price/cost is negative despite 2.9% of price; the company just cut both its FMI signings and Digital Footprint targets for 2026; and 76% of revenue is manufacturing that grew 2.7% the last time the PMI sat below 50. The stock fell 16% in one month in October 2025 on an 11.5% EPS-growth quarter. Consensus has 2027 EPS growth at 10.9%. Same setup, one year later, with a first-time CEO.

Top 3 Risks

01
Multiple compression on deceleration. The Oct 2025 episode is the template: double-digit growth that merely decelerates from the prior quarter can cost 15–20% at this valuation. The Q3 2026 print on October 14 will lap a +11.7% quarter with an August run-rate of +16.6% — a high bar to keep clearing into 2027, when consensus growth steps down to 10.9%.
02
Tariff cost push outrunning price. Fasteners are 30.7% of sales and disproportionately imported steel. A negative 40 bps price/cost on 2.9% of price means the current tariff regime is not yet fully in the P&L; a further round of Section 232 or reciprocal tariff increases would force either another price push into 75.8%-contract customers or further gross-margin erosion below 44.5%.
03
Manufacturing cycle turning. Heavy manufacturing is 44% of sales and grew 18% in Q2 — the most cyclical slice growing fastest. If the PMI slips back below 50, FY2024 (revenue +2.7%, EPS −1%) is the base-rate outcome, and at 0.71 beta the market is not pricing that scenario at all.
  Industry Commentary
Tariffs Turn Distributors Into Price-Takers
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Industrial distribution's economics assume the distributor can re-price faster than its costs move. In 2025–26 that assumption is being tested: Fastenal's realised price rose from 140–170 bps (Q2 2025) to 290 bps (Q2 2026) — a 4.5% two-year stack — and gross margin still fell 75 bps with 40 bps of that explicitly price/cost. The company's own language is that tariff uncertainty is showing up "through cost planning and pricing discussions rather than demand." Read plainly: costs are certain, recovery is negotiated, and the customer base most exposed (large contract manufacturers, 75.8% of sales) has the most negotiating leverage. Every broadline peer faces the same squeeze; Fastenal's fastener weighting makes it the most steel-exposed of the three.
Consolidation Toward the Embedded Model
ONGOING HEADWIND
The $50k+/month site count rising 16.5% while total active sites fall 8% is the industry in one line: the large customer is consolidating vendors, the small customer is going to Amazon Business or a regional house, and the middle is disappearing. Fastenal's response — FMI devices and Onsite staff that make it structurally hard to unbundle — is working (contract mix 73.2% → 75.8% in a year) but it costs money: 2026 capex of $310–330M is up 40%, headcount is up 423, and the company just trimmed its 2026 device and digital targets. Grainger is running the same play with its Keepstock program; MSC is a smaller, more metalworking-centric version. The winners will be the ones with the density to restock a bin cheaply; the losers are anyone still relying on counter sales.
Reshoring and a PMI Back Above 50
EMERGING TAILWIND
Six consecutive months of a U.S. PMI above 50 — averaging 53 in Q2 2026 — is the first sustained manufacturing expansion since 2022, and heavy manufacturing (+18.1%) and non-residential construction (+17.0%) are Fastenal's two fastest end markets as a result. Tariff-driven reshoring, if it persists, adds domestic factory floors that need bins and vending machines. Bull framing: Fastenal is the cleanest listed way to own U.S. factory-floor activity, with 76% manufacturing exposure and a device base that scales with new plants; 42× is a fair price for that scarcity. Bear framing: the PMI has been above 50 for six months, not six years; the FY2024 numbers (revenue +2.7%, EPS −1%) show exactly what this business does below 50, and the stock's flat 52-week return says the market already knows it.
