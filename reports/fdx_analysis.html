01 Company Overview

FedEx moves parcels and freight through a global air-and-ground network built around a hub-and-spoke architecture the company essentially invented in 1971. Fiscal 2026, which ended May 31, 2026, produced revenue of $94.72B, up 7.73% — the first genuinely strong top-line year after three consecutive years of decline (FY2023 −3.59%, FY2024 −2.73%, FY2025 +0.27%). GAAP net income was $4.43B and diluted EPS $18.55; on the company's adjusted basis, operating income was $6.61B (7.0% margin) and adjusted EPS $20.24. It is the world's ~330th largest listed company by market value.

Then the company changed shape. On June 1, 2026 FedEx completed the spin-off of FedEx Freight — its less-than-truckload trucking arm — as a separate NYSE-listed company (FDXF), distributing one FDXF share for every two FDX shares held on the May 15, 2026 record date, and retaining 19.9% of the new company to be disposed of within 24 months against FedEx debt. Everything in this report therefore sits on a fault line: the FY2026 figures above still include a business FedEx no longer owns. Freight contributed $8.80B of revenue and $1.108B of adjusted operating income in FY2026. Strip it out and continuing FedEx is roughly an $86B business with about $5.5B of adjusted operating income — and, critically, a lower margin than the old consolidated entity, because the trucking unit was the higher-margin half.

The central debate: what remains is Federal Express — a $82.3B express and ground parcel network running a 7.7% adjusted operating margin against a competitor, UPS, that has historically earned more per dollar of revenue. Management's answer is self-help: transformation savings (the DRIVE programme and its successor initiatives), Network 2.0 station consolidation, and a deliberate shift from chasing volume to pricing for yield. The bear answer is that the gap is structural — FedEx runs a costlier, air-heavy, more internationally exposed network with a contractor-model ground arm bolted onto it, and no amount of cost-out changes that. This report tries to put numbers on both sides rather than pick one.

One more structural change readers should not miss: effective June 1, 2026 FedEx also moved its fiscal year end from May 31 to December 31. There is now a seven-month June–December 2026 transition period, and guidance is being issued on a calendar-2026 basis. Any brief describing FedEx as a "May fiscal year end" company is describing the past, not the present.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
GLOBAL AIR FLEET & SLOTS MEMPHIS SUPERHUB DENSITY TIME-DEFINITE SERVICE GUARANTEE CUSTOMS & TRADE EXPERTISE B2B ENTERPRISE CONTRACTS DUOPOLY PRICING DISCIPLINE 55-YEAR BRAND TRUST

The moat is the air network, and the air network is also the problem. An intercontinental time-definite express operation — aircraft, landing slots, customs brokerage, bonded facilities, sortation capacity sized for peak — cannot be assembled incrementally. That is why the express business is effectively a three-player world (FedEx, UPS, DHL) rather than a fragmented one, and why FedEx could raise yield 6% in U.S. domestic and 6% in international export in a single year without losing volume: domestic volume still grew 5% alongside that 6% price increase. Duopoly-adjacent pricing power is real and it showed up in FY2026.

But fixed-cost networks cut both ways. The same asset base that repels entrants makes FedEx's margin exquisitely sensitive to volume density, which is why the whole equity story now rests on self-help — putting more boxes through fewer buildings. Management's disclosure on that is specific: 45% of eligible volume was flowing through optimised (Network 2.0) stations by May 2026, rising toward 65% before peak season, and the company exceeded its $1B transformation cost-savings goal for FY2026, with cumulative savings guided toward ~$2B by the end of calendar 2027. That is the entire earnings story in two numbers, and it is worth stating plainly what they are and are not: $1B delivered against a $1B annual target, and a $2B cumulative ambition two years out — meaningful against ~$5.5B of continuing adjusted operating income, but not transformational on its own.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The customer that became a competitor. Amazon built its own last-mile delivery arm and now handles a large share of its own parcels; UPS has spent 2025–26 deliberately shrinking its Amazon volume (its glide-down completed in June 2026) precisely because that volume was dilutive. For FedEx the risk is second-order but real: as Amazon's network absorbs the easy, dense, urban volume, what is left for the integrators skews toward lower-density, higher-cost-to-serve routes. Amazon Logistics' parcel volumes are not separately disclosed, so no share figure is asserted here.
▸
Loss of the U.S. Postal Service air-cargo contract. FedEx's long-running contract to fly USPS air mail ended and the work moved to UPS. That removed a large block of committed, schedule-filling belly volume from the FedEx air network — the kind of base load that makes the marginal cost of every other package cheaper. The date, contract value and volume impact could not be verified from the sources fetched for this report, so no figures are stated. The analytical point stands regardless: an air network run at lower utilisation has structurally worse unit economics, and the fix is either replacement volume or fewer aircraft.
▸
De minimis repeal and tariff policy. The elimination of duty-free treatment for low-value cross-border parcels struck directly at China-to-US e-commerce shipping — the fastest-growing volume pool in international express for several years. The FY2026 numbers carry its fingerprint: international export volume +1% against export yield +6%, and international priority yield per package up 12.7% to $66.34. That is a mix effect, not a win. FedEx also held ~$800M of customer tariff refunds on its balance sheet at year-end, and named "global trade policy changes" as a headwind in its own release. Specific de minimis rule dates and the volume tonnage lost were not verified from the pages fetched.
▸
Leverage and a vacant CFO seat. Total debt of $42.94B against $13.55B of cash leaves ~$29.4B net debt and an enterprise value of $105.72B — meaning debt is roughly 28% of the capital structure at current prices, and debt-to-equity sits at 1.36×. Against that, capital spending is guided at $3.9B and pension contributions at $475M for calendar 2026. The company is executing a spin-off, a fiscal-year change, a multi-year cost programme and a 19.9% retained-stake disposal — with an interim CFO. That is a lot of moving parts and no permanent hand on the finance function.
▸
Cyclicality with no defensive ballast left. Freight was the higher-margin, less air-intensive unit, and it is gone. Continuing FedEx is more concentrated in exactly the parcel and express business that is most exposed to industrial production, e-commerce policy and fuel. Beta of 1.36 already reflected that before the spin narrowed the mix further.

Notable One-Off Items

FY2026 is one of the least clean reporting years in FedEx's history, and every distortion needs naming.

The gap between GAAP and adjusted is $1.15B. FY2026 GAAP operating income was $5.46B (5.8% margin); adjusted was $6.61B (7.0%). In the fourth quarter alone the gap was $540M — GAAP $1.55B (6.2%) versus adjusted $2.09B (8.4%). Those adjustments are principally business-optimisation and separation costs. Note the direction of travel: on an adjusted basis FY2026 margin was 7.0%, identical to FY2025's 7.0%. A full year of transformation savings produced flat adjusted consolidated margin. That is the strongest single piece of evidence for the "structural, not fixable" side of the debate, and it is not obscure — it is in the company's own headline table.

Where the improvement actually was. The Federal Express segment's GAAP margin rose from 6.5% to 7.2% year over year. The consolidated number stayed flat because Freight collapsed: adjusted margin 16.7% → 12.6%, GAAP 1.49B → $616M. The company spun off the deteriorating half of the improvement — an uncomfortable framing, but the numbers support it.

The $4.1B pre-spin dividend. FedEx Freight paid its parent roughly $4.1B in cash immediately before separation, funded by $3.7B of Freight's own senior notes and delayed-draw term loan. Cash into FDX, debt onto FDXF. Any comparison of FedEx's pre- and post-spin balance sheet must account for this, and it is not a recurring source of cash.

~$800M of customer tariff refunds in cash. FedEx is holding duty refunds owed to shippers. This inflates the cash line and will reverse. It is also a neat illustration of how much administrative weight the 2025–26 tariff regime has pushed onto carriers.

The fiscal-year change itself. Moving from a May 31 to a December 31 year-end creates a seven-month June–December 2026 transition period, with its own professional-services costs, and makes every year-over-year comparison for the next 18 months a manual exercise. Guidance is now framed on a calendar-2026 basis.

A like-for-like bridge, clearly labelled as our arithmetic, not a company disclosure: FY2026 adjusted EPS was $20.24. Freight's $1.108B of adjusted operating income, taxed at the guided ~23% rate and divided across ~237M shares, is worth roughly $3.50–3.60 per share. That puts FY2026 adjusted EPS excluding Freight at approximately $16.70. Calendar-2026 adjusted guidance of $16.90–$18.10 therefore represents growth of roughly 1% to 8% on a comparable base — not the ~13% decline a naïve $20.24 → $17.50 comparison implies. This is the single most important adjustment in the whole report, and it is exactly the comparison the market appeared to fumble on the day of the print.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and here it is computed on unadjusted prices, so the June 2026 spin-off step-down feeds into it as if it were a real decline. Read the mid-2026 dip with that caveat in mind.

The five-year trajectory. FDX closed October 2021 at $235.53 and September 4, 2026 at $322.52 — a price-only gain of +36.9%. But that understates what a holder earned, because it ignores the FDXF shares distributed in June. A holder of one FDX share on the record date also received half an FDXF share, worth $64.73 at FDXF's September 4 close of $129.45. Add that back and the five-year total price return is approximately +64.4%, before dividends. Note also that stockanalysis.com reports a 52-week price change of +43.13%, which does not reconcile to the unadjusted September 2025 close of $235.81 (+36.8% price-only, +64.2% with the stub) — a partial-adjustment artefact of how the site handles the distribution. The path was anything but linear: a brutal 2022 (−37% from October 2021 to the September 2022 low of $148.47), a 2023–24 recovery to $302 on the first DRIVE cost wave, a 2025 relapse to $210.33 in April as tariff policy hit, and then a violent nine-month re-rating from $210 to $411.75 between April 2025 and May 2026 as the market priced the spin-off, the cost programme and improving yields all at once. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A cleaner, simpler FedEx trading at 17.4× trailing earnings and a 6.7% free-cash-flow yield, with the Federal Express segment already showing the improvement management promised — adjusted margin 7.7%, up from 6.5% GAAP a year earlier, on U.S. domestic volume +5% and yield +6%. The cost programme beat its $1B FY2026 target and is guided toward ~$2B cumulative by end-2027, with Network 2.0 volume penetration going from 45% to 65% into peak. On a like-for-like base excluding Freight, calendar-2026 adjusted EPS guidance of $16.90–$18.10 is growth, not decline — a point the June sell-off appeared to miss. And there is $3.9B of retained FDXF stock earmarked against $42.94B of debt.

Bear Case

A full year of transformation delivered flat adjusted consolidated operating margin: 7.0% in FY2026, 7.0% in FY2025. The segment improvement was offset by Freight's collapse from 16.7% to 12.6% — and FedEx has now spun off the higher-margin half, leaving a more cyclical, more air-intensive, more trade-policy-exposed company behind. ROIC of 9.18% is at best at the cost of capital. International export volume grew 1%; the yield gains flattering the numbers are mix from lost low-value China e-commerce, not pricing power. The company is running a spin-off, a fiscal-year change and a cost programme with an interim CFO, and the most recent rating action on the tape is a downgrade.

Top 3 Risks

01
The margin gap proves structural, not fixable. This is the report's central question and the honest answer from the numbers is "unproven". Evidence for closable: Federal Express segment GAAP margin 6.5% → 7.2% in one year, with volume and yield both up. Evidence for structural: consolidated adjusted margin flat at 7.0% despite $1B of savings, and FedEx's net margin of 4.68% still trails UPS's 5.08%. The $2B cumulative savings target by end-2027 is roughly 2% of continuing revenue — enough to matter, not enough to settle the argument. A second consecutive year of flat adjusted margin would settle it the other way.
02
Network volume erosion from lost base load. The USPS air-cargo work went to UPS, and Amazon continues to internalise its own parcels. Both remove the dense, committed, schedule-filling volume that makes every other package in a fixed-cost air network cheaper. Neither the USPS contract's value nor Amazon Logistics' volumes were verifiable from the sources fetched, so no figure is claimed — but the mechanism is not in dispute, and it is the reason Network 2.0 consolidation is a necessity rather than an optimisation.
03
Trade policy as a permanent operating variable. FedEx names "global trade policy changes" as a headwind in its own release and is holding ~$800M of customer tariff refunds. De minimis repeal removed a growth engine that had been running for years, and international export volume growth of 1% in FY2026 is the scar. Tariff regimes can move again in either direction with no notice and no ability for FedEx to hedge, which puts a permanent volatility premium on the international franchise — roughly the part of the business that carries the highest revenue per package ($66.34 international priority versus $14.75 U.S. composite).
Industry Commentary
De minimis repeal and the tariff regime
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For most of the last decade, the fastest-growing pool of international express volume was low-value cross-border e-commerce — small parcels shipped direct from Chinese sellers to U.S. consumers under duty-free treatment. The withdrawal of that treatment removed the economic basis of the trade almost overnight, and FedEx's FY2026 disclosures carry the signature: international export volume +1%, export yield +6%, international priority yield per package up 12.7% to $66.34. A yield number that rises while volume stalls is a mix story — the cheap boxes stopped, the expensive ones kept coming. FedEx also holds ~$800M of tariff refunds owed to customers, a reminder that carriers have become de facto customs intermediaries with real working-capital consequences. The specific de minimis rule changes and their dates were not verified from the sources fetched for this report; the volume and yield figures above were.

Insourcing: when your largest customers build their own network
ONGOING HEADWIND

The parcel industry's defining structural shift of the 2020s is that its biggest customers stopped being customers. Amazon built a delivery arm that now handles the bulk of its own volume; UPS spent 2025–26 deliberately shrinking its remaining Amazon business, completing that glide-down in June 2026, and its revenue went backwards (−0.4% TTM) and net income fell 20.2% as a result. The U.S. Postal Service air contract that FedEx flew for decades moved to UPS. Each of these removes committed base-load volume from someone's network, and in a business where the marginal cost of the last package is the whole game, base load is not just revenue — it is the thing that makes everything else profitable. The strategic response, on both sides of the duopoly, is the same: fewer buildings, fewer planes, higher price per package. That is what "Network 2.0" and "45% → 65% of volume through optimised stations" actually mean.

Conglomerate breakup as the sector's dominant trade — and what it implies
EMERGING TAILWIND

The market rewarded the FedEx separation before it happened: from the April 2025 low of $210.33 to the May 2026 close of $411.75, the stock nearly doubled in thirteen months, and the largest single monthly move in the whole five-year series was February 2026's +20.1% as the spin approached. Post-distribution, the two pieces are valued very differently — FDX at 17.4× trailing earnings, FDXF at 29.6× — which is precisely the multiple arbitrage the transaction was designed to unlock, and it argues the market now views the parcel network and the trucking asset as genuinely different businesses. For the bull, that re-rating logic is not exhausted: FDX still trades below its peer on trailing earnings, holds $3.9B of FDXF stock earmarked against debt, and has a cost programme with two more years to run. For the bear, the easy value has been released, what remains is the harder, more cyclical half, and the flat FY2026 adjusted margin says the operational thesis has not yet been demonstrated on the business that is left. The October 28 report and the December peak season are the next two places that argument gets settled with evidence rather than narrative.
