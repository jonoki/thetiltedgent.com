01 Company Overview

FedEx moves parcels and freight through a global air-and-ground network built around a hub-and-spoke system the company pioneered after it began flying in 1973. It is now a different-shaped company from a year ago. On June 1, 2026 it spun off FedEx Freight, its less-than-truckload trucking arm, as a separate NYSE-listed company (FDXF), handing shareholders one FDXF share for every two FDX shares and keeping 19.9% of the new company, which it must sell within 24 months. On the same date it moved its fiscal year end from May 31 to December 31, so June–December 2026 is a seven-month transition period and guidance is now given for calendar 2026. On October 1 it also sold FedEx Supply Chain, its warehousing unit, to CMA CGM for about $1.4B.

What remains is the parcel and express network, reported since July in two segments: Express U.S. Domestic and Express International. On FedEx's recast calendar-quarter figures, continuing operations took in $89.83B of revenue in the twelve months to September 30, 2026, up 11.6% on the twelve months before. Adjusted EPS over those four quarters was $17.85; GAAP EPS was $35.10, almost doubled by one-off, mostly non-cash gains from the spin-off (Section 04). It ranks #175 in the S&P 500 by market value.

The latest quarter (July–September 2026, released October 28): revenue rose 11.9% to $22.86B and adjusted operating income 16% to $1.20B, a 5.3% margin. International did the heavy lifting: export packages rose about 8% and adjusted operating income rose 52%. The U.S. business grew revenue 9.8% but its adjusted margin slipped to 6.6% from 6.8%, which interim CFO Claude Russ put down to fuel, about $100M of cost from the new pilots' agreement and higher incentive pay (call as summarised by BigGo Finance). FedEx raised its calendar-2026 adjusted EPS outlook to $18.00–$18.60 and bought back $2.0B of stock between June and September.

The central debate: can FedEx close its profit gap with UPS by cutting cost rather than chasing volume? Management's answer is self-help: Network 2.0, which merges the old Express and Ground delivery networks into one set of stations and routes; the wider cost programme it calls DRIVE; and pricing for revenue per package rather than package count. The bear answer is that the gap is built in: FedEx runs a costlier, air-heavy, more international network, and the quarter's slipping U.S. margin shows how quickly fuel and trucking costs eat the savings. This report puts numbers on both sides rather than pick one.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
GLOBAL AIR FLEET & SLOTS MEMPHIS SUPERHUB DENSITY TIME-DEFINITE SERVICE GUARANTEE CUSTOMS & TRADE EXPERTISE B2B ENTERPRISE CONTRACTS DUOPOLY PRICING DISCIPLINE 55-YEAR BRAND TRUST

The moat is the air network, and the air network is also the problem. An intercontinental time-definite express operation — aircraft, landing slots, customs brokerage, bonded facilities, sortation capacity sized for peak — cannot be assembled incrementally. That is why the express business is effectively a three-player world (FedEx, UPS, DHL) rather than a fragmented one, and why FedEx can keep raising its yield without losing volume: in the September quarter U.S. revenue per package rose 7% while daily packages still grew 2%, and international priority revenue per package rose 16% while export packages grew about 8%. Its next list-price rise, an average 5.9% on parcel rates, takes effect January 4, 2027 (October 28 release).

But fixed-cost networks cut both ways. The same planes and buildings that keep rivals out make FedEx's margin very sensitive to how full they are, which is why the case for the shares rests on self-help: putting more boxes through fewer buildings. On the October 28 call, as summarised by BigGo Finance, Subramaniam said about 65% of eligible off-peak volume now runs on the Network 2.0 model going into peak season, and management repeated the goal of about $2B of cumulative savings by the end of 2027. The test is whether those savings reach the profit line: in the September quarter the U.S. adjusted margin still fell 0.2 points, to 6.6%, while company-wide adjusted operating income ($1.20B) was 5.3% of revenue.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The customer that became a competitor. Amazon built its own last-mile delivery arm and now handles a large share of its own parcels; UPS spent 2025–26 deliberately shrinking its Amazon volume, and raised its forecast in July 2026 after completing that transition (Reuters, July 28, 2026). For FedEx the risk is second-order but real: as Amazon's network absorbs the easy, dense, urban volume, what is left for FedEx and UPS skews toward lower-density, higher-cost-to-serve routes. Amazon Logistics' parcel volumes are not separately disclosed, so no share figure is asserted here.
▸
Loss of the U.S. Postal Service air-cargo contract. FedEx's long-running contract to fly USPS air mail expired on September 29, 2024 (FedEx 8-K) and the work moved to UPS (USPS statement, April 1, 2024). That removed a large block of steady, contracted volume that filled FedEx's planes on a fixed schedule — the kind of guaranteed business that makes every other package cheaper to carry. The point: planes that fly emptier cost more per package, and the fix is either new volume or fewer aircraft.
▸
De minimis repeal and tariff policy. The elimination of duty-free treatment for low-value cross-border parcels struck directly at China-to-US e-commerce shipping — the fastest-growing volume pool in international express for several years. Export volume has since come back (up about 8% in the September quarter), but the September quarter's release still names "the financial impact of global trade policy changes" as a drag, and FedEx was holding about $2.7B of IEEPA tariff refunds for customers within its $7.96B of cash at September 30: money it collected as a customs broker and now has to pass back.
▸
Leverage, fuel and a finance chief who starts in December. At September 30 FedEx had $19.36B of debt plus $14.77B of operating-lease obligations against $7.96B of cash and $34.57B of shareholders' equity (10-Q): debt-to-equity of 0.99 counting leases. It retired about $4.9B of bonds in July but issued €2.0B and $1.1B of new notes on September 14, and spent $2.0B on buybacks. Capital spending is guided at $3.9B for calendar 2026. The shares fell 12.9% in September, the steepest day being September 24 (−4.1% close to close). Fuel alone took 0.3 percentage points off the U.S. adjusted margin in the September quarter (call as summarised by BigGo Finance). The permanent CFO, Jamere Jackson, starts on November 30, in the middle of peak season and with the FDXF stake still to sell.
▸
Cyclicality with no defensive ballast left. Freight was a separate, less air-dependent business, and it is gone. Continuing FedEx is concentrated in the parcel and express business that is most exposed to industrial production, e-commerce policy and fuel. Its beta over five years is 1.33 (stockanalysis.com).

Notable One-Off Items

2026 is one of the least clean reporting years in FedEx's history, and every distortion needs naming.

The retained FedEx Freight stake. FedEx kept 19.9% of FDXF and marks it to FDXF's share price every quarter. In the September quarter that produced a $1.2B loss, which is most of the gap between GAAP EPS of $1.21 and adjusted EPS of $3.74. The stake's carrying value was $3.3B at September 30 (release). For calendar 2026 as a whole, FedEx's guidance reconciliation shows a $3.25B net gain on the stake, a $788M gain from remeasuring pension plans, a $627M gain on retiring debt and $579M of restructuring costs: that is why GAAP EPS guidance is $35.30–$35.90 while adjusted guidance is $18.00–$18.60.

The bond buyback. Before the spin FedEx Freight paid FedEx a cash dividend of about $4.1B. FedEx used it to repurchase about $4.9B of its own debt through tender offers in July, booking a $627M pre-tax gain that is in GAAP profit and excluded from adjusted profit. It then raised €2.0B and $1.1B of new notes on September 14 (8-K).

About $2.7B of customer tariff refunds in cash. FedEx is holding IEEPA tariff refunds owed to shippers for whom it acted as customs broker (release). They swell the $7.96B cash line and will flow back out; at the last edition the figure was about $800M.

The fiscal-year change. Moving from a May 31 to a December 31 year-end creates a seven-month June–December 2026 transition period and forced FedEx to recast its history into calendar quarters (8-Ks of July 21 and October 28, 2026). The September quarter is labelled the "third quarter" of calendar 2026, and every year-over-year figure in this report compares recast calendar quarters.

Buybacks lift per-share figures a little. The $2.0B repurchased between June and September retired about 6.3M shares and added $0.02 to September-quarter EPS, by the release's own count.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and here it is computed on unadjusted prices, so the June 2026 spin-off step-down feeds into it as if it were a real decline. Read the mid-2026 dip with that caveat in mind.

The five-year trajectory. FDX closed October 2021 at $235.53 and October 30, 2026 at $313.28, a price-only gain of +33.0% on the chart's unadjusted closes. That understates what a holder earned, because it ignores the FDXF shares handed out in June: a holder of one FDX share on the record date also received half an FDXF share, worth $63.02 at FDXF's October 30 close of $126.04. Add that back and the gain is about +59.8%, before dividends. Yahoo's spin-adjusted series, which instead scales every pre-spin price down by the value of the FDXF shares, gives +65.1% from October 29, 2021 to October 30, 2026. Over the same dates the S&P 500 ETF SPY rose 64.6% and the Nasdaq-100 ETF QQQ 78.8%, both price only.

The path was anything but linear: a brutal 2022 (−37% from October 2021 to the September 2022 low of $148.47), a 2023–24 recovery to $302 on the first DRIVE cost wave, a 2025 relapse to $210.33 in April as tariff policy hit, and then a near-doubling to $411.75 between April 2025 and May 2026 as the market priced in the spin-off, the cost programme and rising prices per package. About a fifth of the pre-spin share price was then handed back to shareholders as FDXF stock (Yahoo's spin adjustment scales earlier prices by 0.806). Since the spin the shares have swung between $285 and $327 at month-ends.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A simpler FedEx that just beat and raised: adjusted EPS up 28% in the September quarter, calendar-2026 guidance lifted to $18.00–$18.60, and international profit finally moving, with export packages up about 8% and the segment's adjusted margin up to 3.9% from 3.1%. At 17.55× trailing adjusted earnings and 17.1× the midpoint of this year's guidance, it is cheaper than UPS (20.86× GAAP). Management is buying back stock ($2.0B since June), will raise list prices 5.9% in January, has about 65% of eligible volume on the merged Network 2.0 model going into peak, and still holds an FDXF stake worth about $3.75B to sell. A permanent CFO arrives November 30.

Bear Case

The core of the company went backwards: the U.S. adjusted margin slipped to 6.6% from 6.8% even with revenue per package up 7%, as fuel, a new pilot contract and outside-trucking costs ate the price gains. Company-wide adjusted operating margin is 5.3%, thin for a business this capital-hungry. Half of the international yield gain was fuel surcharges, by management's own account. GAAP earnings will swing with FDXF's share price until the stake is sold, and the cash pile includes about $2.7B owed back to customers. The stock fell 12.9% in September with no change in guidance, and over five years it has only matched the S&P 500 ETF once the FDXF shares are counted (+65.1% spin-adjusted against SPY's +64.6%, price only; +33.0% on unadjusted closes), for a lot more volatility.

Top 3 Risks

01
The margin gap proves built-in, not fixable. This is the report's central question and the honest answer from the numbers is still "unproven". Evidence for fixable: international adjusted margin up 0.8 points in a year, a raised outlook, and savings guided toward ~$2B by end-2027. Evidence against: the U.S. adjusted margin fell 0.2 points in the quarter that Network 2.0 reached 65% of eligible volume, and FedEx's adjusted net margin of about 4.8% over four quarters is no better than UPS's 4.84% GAAP. Subramaniam said he is convinced domestic margins will improve in 2027 (call as summarised by BigGo Finance); 2027 is where that gets tested.
02
Losing the steady volume that fills the network. The USPS air-cargo work went to UPS in 2024, and Amazon continues to deliver more of its own parcels. Both remove the dense, committed volume that makes every other package in a fixed-cost air network cheaper to carry. U.S. daily packages grew just 2% in the September quarter, which is why merging stations under Network 2.0 is a necessity rather than an option.
03
Trade policy and fuel as permanent variables. FedEx names "global trade policy changes" as a drag in its own release and holds about $2.7B of IEEPA tariff refunds owed to customers. Tariff rules can change again in either direction with no notice, and fuel spikes hit before surcharges catch up. Both land hardest on the international business, which carries the highest revenue per package ($74.27 for international priority against $15.32 for U.S. domestic) and the thinnest margin.
Industry Commentary
De minimis repeal and the tariff regime
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For most of the last decade, the fastest-growing pool of international express volume was low-value cross-border e-commerce: small parcels shipped direct from Chinese sellers to U.S. consumers without duty. The withdrawal of that duty-free treatment removed the economics of the trade almost overnight, and FedEx's FY2026 figures (year to May 2026) showed it: export volume up just 1%. The September quarter suggests FedEx has refilled some of the gap with higher-value freight, with export packages up about 8% and international priority revenue per package up 16% to $74.27, though management said about half of the export yield gain was fuel (call as summarised by BigGo Finance). FedEx also holds about $2.7B of IEEPA tariff refunds owed to customers, a reminder that carriers have become customs middlemen with real cash consequences.

Insourcing: when your largest customers build their own network
ONGOING HEADWIND

The parcel industry's defining shift of the 2020s is that its biggest customers stopped being customers. Amazon built a delivery arm that now carries much of its own volume; UPS spent 2025–26 deliberately shrinking its remaining Amazon business and completed that transition by July 2026 (Reuters, July 28, 2026). UPS's revenue over its last four quarters rose just 2.1% and its profit fell 19.6% (stockanalysis.com). The U.S. Postal Service air contract that FedEx flew for decades moved to UPS in 2024. Each of these takes steady, contracted volume out of someone's network, and in a business of fixed planes and buildings that steady volume is not just revenue: it is what makes everything else profitable. The response on both sides is the same: fewer buildings, fewer planes, higher price per package. That is what Network 2.0, and the 65% of eligible volume now running on it, actually mean.

Breaking up the conglomerate — and what it implies
EMERGING TAILWIND

The market rewarded the FedEx separation before it happened: from the April 2025 low of $210.33 to the May 2026 close of $411.75 the stock nearly doubled, and the largest single monthly gain in the five-year series was February 2026's +20.1% as the spin approached. Since then the two pieces have been priced very differently: FDX at 17.55× trailing adjusted earnings, FDXF at 28.8× the year to May 2026, a sign the market sees the parcel network and the trucking business as different animals. FedEx has kept simplifying, selling FedEx Supply Chain on October 1. For the bull, there is more to come: the FDXF stake to sell, buybacks, a new CFO and two more years of the cost programme. For the bear, the easy value has been released, and the September quarter's slipping U.S. margin says the operating case is still unproven on the business that is left. Peak season and the 2027 margin are the next two places that argument gets settled with evidence rather than narrative.
