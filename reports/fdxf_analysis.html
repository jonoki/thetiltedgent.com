01 Company Overview

FedEx Freight describes itself as North America's largest less-than-truckload (LTL) carrier: over 365 locations, nearly 30,000 vehicles (nearly 17,000 of them tractors) and 40,000 team members, selling Priority, Economy and Direct service plus the expedited FedEx Custom Critical subsidiary. Until May 31, 2026 it was part of FedEx Corporation. On June 1, 2026 FedEx spun it off — one FDXF share for every two FDX shares held on the May 15 record date, with FedEx keeping about 19.9% — and S&P Dow Jones Indices added it to the S&P 500 on day one.

It started life with debt. Before the separation it borrowed $4.3B ($3.7B of bonds and a $0.6B term loan) and paid FedEx about $4.1B in cash. At September 30, 2026 it had $4.42B of debt (after repaying $100M of the term loan early), $1.84B of lease obligations, $388M of cash and negative book equity (−$252M). It has also moved its year-end from May to December, so on October 29 it reported a one-off four-month period (June–September) and now publishes calendar quarters. In the July–September quarter revenue rose 8.1% to $2.44B and adjusted EPS was $1.49, ahead of estimates; shipments per day slipped 0.7%. The catch is in the pricing: revenue per hundredweight rose 8.8%, but only 0.4% excluding fuel surcharges (the extra charge carriers bill when diesel prices rise). The stock had fallen to $107.95 by October 21, jumped 14.9% the day after the report, and closed November 3 at $130.26, still 12.9% below its first-day close of $149.53.

The terminal network is the product. A shipper's few pallets are picked up, sorted at a service center, driven overnight between hubs and delivered; Priority is the faster service, Economy trades speed for price. Terminals, doors, tractors and drivers cost roughly the same whether trailers leave full or half-empty, so density (weight per trailer, shipments per door) drives margin. In July–September 2026 average daily shipments were 89,105, down 0.7% (Priority 62,247, +0.8%; Economy 26,858, −3.9%), weight per shipment rose to 932 lb (+0.6%), revenue per hundredweight rose 8.8% to $44.20 and revenue per shipment 9.5% to $412.05. Strip out fuel surcharges and revenue per hundredweight was $33.98, +0.4%, and revenue per shipment $316.78, +1.1%: higher diesel prices did most of the work. The volume decline has narrowed sharply from −5.9% in the March–May quarter, the last one the previous edition covered.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

365+ location terminal network Largest North American LTL carrier ~30,000 vehicles Priority & Economy service tiers FedEx brand & customer relationships Record-low claims ratio, two quarters running

LTL's moat is real estate and density. A national network of service centers with enough doors, in the right metro areas, takes decades and zoning battles to replicate, and scale lets a large carrier fill more trailers per lane than a small one. The vulnerability is that density cuts both ways: every lost shipment leaves a fixed cost uncovered. The market's comparison is in plain sight. FedEx Freight's $9.07B of trailing revenue is larger than Old Dominion's $5.79B, yet its $19.48B market value is about half of Old Dominion's $36.84B. Investors pay about 2.1x sales for FDXF, 6.4x for ODFL and 2.5x for XPO (market value at the November 3 close ÷ trailing revenue; peers per StockAnalysis). Old Dominion's trailing operating margin is 26.6%, against FedEx Freight's 5.7% GAAP and 12.2% adjusted: the gap is a verdict on margins and the balance sheet, not on size.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Born with debt. The ~$4.1B payment to FedEx left $4.42B of debt at September 30 (the $4.2B of spin-related borrowing carries a 4.82% average rate, per the 10-Q), plus $1.84B of lease obligations, against $388M of cash and negative book equity. Interest is guided at about $130M for June–December. Net debt including leases (debt plus lease obligations minus cash) of $5.87B is about 5.3x trailing adjusted operating income of $1.105B (computed). S&P rates the bonds BBB- and Moody's Baa3, the lowest investment-grade grade at each (10-Q).
02
Base pricing has stalled. Revenue per hundredweight excluding fuel rose just 0.4% in July–September, and Economy's fell 1.5%. The headline yield gain depends on diesel prices; if fuel falls, so does the surcharge, and the CFO named “fuel tailwinds” as one reason for the raised outlook.
03
A 19.9% stake still to come to market. FedEx has said it will dispose of its retained stake — roughly 30M shares — within 24 months of June 1, 2026. Whether that happens through debt-for-equity exchanges or distributions to FDX holders, it is a known future supply of stock that buyers can wait for.
04
Spin-off costs keep coming. $589M over the last twelve months, $105M in July–September, and about $230M expected for June–December as a whole, including $105M in October–December (company outlook). GAAP earnings will trail adjusted earnings by a wide margin until they stop. History before June 2026 is a carve-out from FedEx's accounts, earned with no debt of its own.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

North America's largest LTL network trades at about 2.1x sales against 6.4x for Old Dominion. The first standalone quarter beat on profit, the adjusted profit forecast went up, and volume has nearly stopped shrinking (−0.7%, from −5.9%). Strip out spin-off costs and the July–September margin was 14.7%, up from 14.2%, with a record-low claims ratio for a second quarter. Management expects 2027 to deliver 4–6% revenue growth and a 15% adjusted margin, and a dividend and buyback plan is due. A network with mostly fixed costs keeps much of the revenue from each extra shipment once volume comes back, and the spin-off costs, $589M over the last year, are one-offs.

Bear Case

The yield gain is mostly fuel: excluding surcharges, revenue per hundredweight rose 0.4% and Economy's fell. If diesel eases, so does the headline. The company carries $4.42B of debt plus $1.84B of leases with negative book equity, and expects about $105M more of spin-off costs in October–December, a quarter it guides to an 8.0–8.6% adjusted margin. GAAP EPS over the last year is $3.25, so the stock trades at 40 times trailing earnings. FedEx's 19.9% stake has to come to market by June 2028. The average analyst target fell from $164 to $143 between editions, and short interest rose to 6.3M shares.

Top 3 Risks

01
Debt in a soft freight market. Net debt including leases of $5.87B against $1.105B of trailing adjusted operating income (about 5.3x, computed) leaves limited room if the freight recovery stalls. Both credit ratings sit one grade above junk (BBB-/Baa3).
02
Pricing that depends on fuel. Base yield excluding fuel barely grew in July–September. A fall in diesel prices would lower revenue per shipment even if volume holds, and the raised outlook leans partly on “fuel tailwinds”.
03
The FedEx stake and standalone execution. FedEx's 19.9% has to come to market by June 2028, and the company still buys services from FedEx under transition service agreements it has to exit. Spin-off costs ran $589M over the last year; more than expected would hit the margin targets directly.
Industry Commentary
Diesel up, volumes flat
ONGOING HEADWIND

Reuters, reporting the results on October 29, cited the U.S. Bank Freight Payment Index: shipper spending in the third quarter was up 32.1% from a year earlier on widespread cost pressure, and Reuters said a surge in diesel prices and fewer available truck drivers had pushed rates higher. FedEx Freight's own quarter fits that picture — revenue per hundredweight up 8.8%, but only 0.4% once fuel surcharges are removed, and tonnage per day flat. For a network business the order matters: price protects margin today; volume later is what would deliver the 15% margin target.

Industry pricing discipline
EMERGING TAILWIND

LTL is a concentrated market with high barriers to entry: terminals cannot be conjured, which is why carriers can pass fuel costs on to customers even in a weak market. The bull framing: rational pricing plus a cyclical volume recovery is the best combination in trucking, and the largest network benefits most. The bear framing: the fuel pass-through hides flat base pricing, and a newly independent carrier with debt has less room than its peers to wait out the cycle.

Spin-off mechanics
STRUCTURAL

Spin-offs hand stock to shareholders who never chose it. FDX holders received one FDXF share per two FDX shares, and many parcel-focused holders had reason to sell. S&P 500 inclusion on day one absorbed some of that — index funds had to buy — but the slide from June 9 to October 21 is consistent with continued selling by holders who did not want the stock. The first standalone results gave the market its own set of numbers to price, and the stock moved more that day than on any other. The retained 19.9% stake is the next chapter of the same process: none of it changes the business, but it affects the price until the shareholder base settles.
