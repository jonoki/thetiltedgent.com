01 Company Overview

FirstEnergy owns the wires. Seven regulated distribution utilities carry power to more than six million customers in Ohio, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, West Virginia, Maryland and New York — a service area of about 65,000 square miles and 14 million people — plus more than 24,000 miles of high-voltage transmission line inside the PJM grid. Its old competitive generation arm, FirstEnergy Solutions, filed for bankruptcy in 2018 and later became Energy Harbor. The only generation left is 3,610 MW of regulated capacity owned or contracted by Mon Power, mostly in West Virginia. FY2025 revenue was $14.90B, up 12.2%, and GAAP diluted EPS was $1.76. The company's own non-GAAP "Core" EPS was $2.55, up 7.6%.

The third quarter of 2026, reported October 27, kept the plan on track. Core EPS was $0.98 against $0.83 a year earlier and GAAP EPS was $0.96 against $0.76; revenue was $4.56B, up 9.9%. Management reaffirmed 2026 Core EPS guidance of $2.62 to $2.82 and expects results at or slightly above the midpoint. Contracted demand from large-load customers (the heavy power users, such as data centers) reached 7.9 GW, nearly double the level at the start of 2026, with about 4 GW more in active negotiations. The company will roll its capital plan forward in February 2027, with transmission investment up at least 25% from the current $19 billion, and is seeking West Virginia approval for a 1.2 GW gas plant, the Maidsville Energy Center. The Q3 presentation's plan-update slide shows up to $2 billion of equity or equity-like financing (new shares, or hybrid securities that credit-rating agencies count partly as equity) and says up to 35% of new investment beyond the current plan would be funded that way; the Q2 presentation gave no amount and spoke only of "modest equity".

A regulated utility makes money in one way. It invests capital in poles, wires and substations, the regulator adds that capital to the rate base, and customer rates are set so the company earns an allowed return on equity on the equity-financed share of that base. Earnings growth is roughly rate-base growth times the ROE the company actually earns. FirstEnergy's plan is a $36 billion "Energize365" capital program for 2026–2030, which it says produces 10% compound annual rate-base growth and Core EPS growth near the top end of 6% to 8% a year. The question the stock is really asking is whether regulators let it earn a fair return on that money. The last big Ohio case, decided November 19, 2025, cut the allowed distribution ROE to 9.63% from 10.5% and forced a $352 million impairment for disallowed costs.

History matters here. In July 2020 federal prosecutors charged Ohio's House Speaker over roughly $60 million in bribes from FirstEnergy tied to Ohio's House Bill 6. The company fired its CEO in October 2020, signed a deferred prosecution agreement in July 2021 and paid a $230 million penalty. The legal costs still show up: 2025 "investigation and related costs" were $0.50 a share of the $0.78 gap between GAAP and Core EPS. Management since 2023 has been rebuilding FirstEnergy as a plain transmission-and-distribution utility.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territories 6M+ customers, 6 states 24,000+ transmission miles in PJM FERC formula-rate transmission $29.8B rate base (Sep 2026) Large-load demand in WV & NJ

No one else can string wires down a street in Akron. A regulated utility's moat is legal, not competitive, and FirstEnergy's is as deep as any in the industry. The customer has no alternative for delivery, and the regulator guarantees the opportunity to earn a return on prudent investment. The limit on that moat is the same regulator. The franchise protects the volume of the business; the commissions in Columbus, Harrisburg, Trenton, Charleston and Annapolis, and FERC, set the price. FirstEnergy's recent history in Ohio shows how much that price can move.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Regulatory relationship in Ohio. The November 2025 order cut the Ohio distribution ROE to 9.63% from 10.5% and disallowed enough cost to require a $352M impairment. A separate $275M of Ohio settlement charges covered customer refunds and restitution. Ohio regulators are still working through the House Bill 6 legacy. The new Three-Year Rate Plan is the next test of whether the relationship has reset.
02
Funding a plan bigger than cash flow. Trailing free cash flow is −$2.16B (operating cash $3.19B minus capex $5.35B, per StockAnalysis). Total debt is $30.05B against $48M of cash, debt-to-equity is 2.07 and interest coverage is 2.73×. The Q3 presentation now puts equity or equity-like financing at up to $2B under the updated plan (the Q2 presentation said only "modest equity"); $2B is about 7% of the $27.16B market cap (computed). Every dollar of the plan not covered by operating cash must come from debt, equity or asset sales. Each option either raises interest cost or dilutes the per-share growth the plan promises.
03
Affordability and politics. Rate base growing at 10% a year means customer bills rise unless load grows as fast. Data-center demand helps share the fixed cost, but it also makes bills a political issue in every state FirstEnergy serves. Allowed ROEs are set by people who answer to voters.
04
Interest-rate sensitivity. Utility shares trade partly as bond substitutes: a 3.97% yield competes with Treasuries. With a 0.42 beta the stock is insulated from equity-market swings, but not from rates. The worst stretch in the five-year chart, the September 2023 low of $34.18, came as interest rates climbed, with no company event in the sources to explain it.

Notable One-Off Items

FirstEnergy's GAAP numbers carry heavy distortions, and the gap between GAAP and Core earnings is the first thing to check. In FY2025 the company reported GAAP diluted EPS of $1.76 ($1.77 basic, as quoted in the release) against Core EPS of $2.55. The largest reconciling items were $0.50 of investigation and related costs (the HB6 legacy), $0.58 of regulatory charges (the Ohio order's $352M impairment plus $275M of settlement charges, both pre-tax), partly offset by $0.35 of pension and retiree-benefit accounting credits and a $0.06 asset-retirement-obligation benefit. Those four items net to $0.67 of the $0.78 gap. In 2026 the gap has narrowed: third-quarter GAAP EPS was $0.96 against Core $0.98 ($0.03 of investigation costs and $0.01 of reorganization costs, offset by $0.02 of pension credits), and nine-month GAAP EPS was $2.16 against Core $2.20, with investigation costs of $0.11. If that holds, GAAP EPS should converge toward Core, and the 22.7× trailing P/E would fall toward the high teens without any change in price.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay about 17.1× forward earnings and collect a 3.97% yield for a $29.8B rate base that management plans to grow 10% a year, with more than half the $36B plan in FERC formula-rate transmission, where capital earns with little lag. Contracted large-load demand of 7.9 GW, nearly double the start of the year, gives the plan real load behind it, and the transmission plan is due to grow by at least 25% in February. GAAP EPS is converging on Core as HB6 costs fade (nine months: $2.16 vs $2.20). If that continues, the trailing P/E falls from 22.7× toward the high teens with no change in price. Most targets sit above $46.91, from $46 to $56 with an average of $50.64.

Bear Case

FirstEnergy's biggest state has just shown what it thinks of the company: an ROE cut to 9.63% from 10.5%, a $352M impairment and $275M of customer refunds. Rate base growing 10% a year is worth nothing if the returns on it are squeezed. The plan needs outside money: trailing free cash flow is −$2.16B, debt is $30.05B, debt-to-equity is 2.07 and interest cover is 2.73×. The company now names up to $2B of equity or equity-like financing, which cuts into per-share growth if it is issued below the price the plan assumes. Transmission ROE is under FERC review, and in a 10%-rate-base-growth plan customer bills become a political issue. The stock is below its ten-month average ($47.50), and the lowest analyst target ($46) is under the current price.

Industry Commentary
Data-center load growth and the transmission build-out
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

For two decades US electricity demand was roughly flat and utilities grew by replacing old assets. Data centres changed that in the PJM footprint where FirstEnergy operates. Its contracted load from large customers rose to 7.9 GW at the Q3 print, nearly double the level at the start of 2026, with about 4 GW more in negotiation. For a wires company, new load means new transmission and substations, which is new rate base. It also spreads fixed network cost over more kilowatt-hours, which can help affordability. That is why FirstEnergy's planned transmission capital rose nearly 35% versus the prior plan, to more than $19B, which the company expects to raise by at least 25% in February 2027, and why FET joined Dominion and Transource in the Valley Link venture in February 2025.

Allowed returns under pressure
ONGOING HEADWIND

Regulators must fund a capex wave and keep bills affordable at the same time, and the allowed ROE is where they balance the two. FirstEnergy's own table shows the pressure. Ohio distribution fell from 10.5% to 9.63%. ATSI's FERC ROE lost its 0.5-point bonus for regional grid membership after the Sixth Circuit ruling, taking it to 9.88%, and FERC's methodology review continues. The spread between the lowest (Maryland distribution, 9.5%) and highest (TrAIL, 12.7%) allowed ROE across FirstEnergy's own jurisdictions is 3.2 percentage points. Where each new dollar of capital is spent is therefore as important as how much is spent.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

FE trades at 17.2× trailing Core EPS and 17.1× forward consensus, with a 3.97% yield, 10% guided rate-base growth and EPS growth guided near 8%. The bull framing: the market is still charging an HB6-era governance discount on a company that has changed its management, sold a 49.9% stake in FirstEnergy Transmission to Brookfield, and put more than half its capital into FERC transmission. As GAAP converges with Core and the Ohio case resolves, the discount closes. The bear framing: the discount is rational. Regulators remember, the capital plan cannot be funded internally, and in a regulated business growth that needs outside money is only worth what regulators allow it to earn. The deciding evidence will come from the Ohio rate-plan order (hearings begin March 1, 2027) and the funding mix of the next two years of capex. Neither is available yet.
