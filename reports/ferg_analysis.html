01 Company Overview

Ferguson is the plumber's and the HVAC contractor's warehouse: a distributor of pipe, valves, fittings, water heaters, heating and cooling equipment, waterworks infrastructure and industrial supplies to trade professionals across the United States and Canada. It does not make much of what it sells — it makes the part show up at the job site, on time, in the right quantity, with credit terms attached. Quarter ended June 30, 2026 net sales were $8.751B, up 4.6% (3.8% organic, 1.0% from acquisitions), and the United States was 95.3% of that. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $31.45B with net income of $1.99B and diluted EPS of $10.24.

The story of the last five years is a change of address. The group was founded in 1887 and spent most of its listed life in London; over the last five years it has walked west: an additional NYSE listing in 2021, the primary listing moved from London to New York in 2022, a new Delaware holding company — Ferguson Enterprises Inc. — slotted on top on August 1, 2024 in a one-for-one share exchange, the fiscal year moved from a July to a December year-end (with a five-month transition period ended December 31, 2025), and the London secondary listing cancelled effective July 20, 2026. Eleven days later S&P Dow Jones Indices announced that Ferguson would join the S&P 500 on August 5, 2026, replacing Electronic Arts. The US index membership was, in effect, the prize at the end of that walk. The stock rose from a $234.33 July close to $250.08 on the next trading day — and then gave all of it back and more: at $214.43 it is 21.1% below its 52-week high of $271.64. The question for a new index member is whether it bought the stock a buyer, or only a date.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Branch density (1,700+ locations) Purchasing scale ($31B sales) Contractor credit & relationships Breadth: plumbing + HVAC + waterworks Bolt-on M&A machine Distribution-centre logistics

A distributor's moat is availability. The contractor on a job site does not shop for the cheapest ball valve; he shops for the one he can have in forty minutes, on account, from a counter person who knows what he needed last time. That is a density game: more branches, more inventory, more trucks, spread across more sales — and $31B of trailing revenue buys purchasing terms smaller rivals cannot match. The same scale funds the roll-up: Ferguson buys regional distributors, plugs them into its supply chain and earns a better margin on their volume than they could. Management calls this its "scale-advantaged business model." The vulnerability is that none of it creates demand. When housing turnover stalls, the densest branch network in the country sells fewer water heaters.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Half the business is a housing bet. Residential is roughly 50% of US revenue and grew 2% in Q2 2026, with Residential Trade Plumbing flat and Ferguson Home down 1%. The non-residential half is doing the work; if project activity cools before housing recovers, there is no second engine.
02
Cash conversion slipped. First-half 2026 operating cash flow was $716M versus $1,123M a year earlier. Trailing free cash flow of $1.12B is a 3.55% FCF margin, against a 6.32% net margin — earnings are running ahead of cash, typically as working capital (inventory and receivables) builds.
03
Leverage is rising into a soft tape. FloWorks takes net debt/EBITDA from 1.3x to ~1.8x by management's estimate. Comfortable for an investment-grade distributor, but it narrows the room for buybacks and further deals if demand weakens.
04
Index inclusion already priced — and reversed. The S&P 500 announcement lifted the stock from $234.33 to $250.08 in a day; it closed August at $224.51 and has since fallen to $214.43. Index demand is a one-time purchase. It is gone from here.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $139.52 to the September 18, 2026 settled close of $214.43, FERG gained 53.7% on price, before dividends. The whole window is NYSE trading in US dollars — the 2024 redomicile swapped Ferguson plc shares one-for-one into Ferguson Enterprises Inc. shares under the same ticker, so the series has no per-share break. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A 17.58% ROIC distributor with gross margins that have not left a 30.3%–30.7% band in four fiscal years, now fully American, fully in the S&P 500, and 21% off its high for no reason visible in its own numbers — Q2 beat, guidance went up, non-residential grew 8% and Industrial 18%. At 18.96x consensus 2026 EPS and with every one of the 22 covering analysts' targets above the price, the sell-off reads as index-flow round-tripping. FloWorks adds ~$1B of revenue in exactly the data-centre, power and process markets that are growing, and when housing turnover eventually recovers the residential half has operating leverage to spare.

Bear Case

Half the company is flat housing — Residential Trade Plumbing 0%, Ferguson Home −1% — and the other half is carrying a cycle that may already be late. Operating cash flow fell from $1,123M to $716M in the first half, trailing FCF is only $1.12B (a ~2.7% yield), leverage is rising to ~1.8x on the FloWorks deal, and the stock still trades at nearly 21x trailing earnings for a business whose trailing revenue grew 2.2%. The S&P 500 bid has been paid in full. The market has been selling a beat-and-raise for six weeks — and the price is usually the first to know.

Top 3 Risks

01
Residential stagnation. About half of US revenue, growing 2% in aggregate with its two named customer groups at 0% and −1% in Q2 2026. Without a housing-turnover or remodel recovery, group growth depends on non-residential alone.
02
Non-residential cycle and project timing. Industrial +18% and Commercial/Mechanical +15% are strong prints that can reverse as large projects complete; Fire & Fabrication is already −13%. FloWorks increases exposure to the same project-driven end-markets.
03
Cash conversion and leverage. H1 2026 operating cash flow down 36% ($716M vs $1,123M) while net debt/EBITDA heads to ~1.8x after FloWorks, with $1.2B of new notes priced in August. If working capital does not unwind in H2, buybacks and bolt-on M&A — two of the three legs of the equity story — slow.
Industry Commentary
Two halves, two cycles
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Ferguson's Q2 2026 is a clean picture of a split construction economy. Non-residential — commercial buildings, industrial plants, large mechanical projects — grew 8%, with Industrial at +18% and Commercial/Mechanical at +15%. Residential grew 2%, with price inflation "in the low single digits" meaning volumes were roughly flat. A distributor sells into both, so its reported growth is an average of a boom and a stall. The investment question is which cycle turns first: if the residential half recovers while non-residential holds, growth accelerates; if the project cycle cools while housing stays stuck, it does the opposite.

Index inclusion — the round trip
ONGOING HEADWIND

The path to the S&P 500 took five years and four structural steps — NYSE listing (2021), primary listing to New York (2022), US holdco (August 1, 2024), London delisting (July 20, 2026). The payoff was a one-day move from $234.33 to $250.08 and a peak close of $263.78 on results day. By September 18 the stock was at $214.43, below where it stood before the announcement. Index buying is a single, price-insensitive purchase; once it is done, the stock trades on fundamentals again, and the post-inclusion drift has been one-way.

Roll-up economics — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

Seven bolt-ons in H1 2026 and ~$1.4B of annualised acquired revenue year to date, then FloWorks at ~$1.6B. The bull framing: a scaled distributor earns more on an acquired branch's volume than the seller could — better purchasing, better logistics, cross-selling HVAC into plumbing accounts — and a 17.58% ROIC says that the machine works. The bear framing: acquired growth flatters a revenue line whose organic rate is 3.8% with low-single-digit price inflation inside it, and it is being funded while cash conversion is weakening. The way to adjudicate is not the P/E; it is organic growth and free cash flow over the next four quarters. Both are reported. Neither has yet given a clean signal.
