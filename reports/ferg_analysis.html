01 Company Overview

Ferguson is the plumber's and the HVAC contractor's warehouse: a distributor of pipe, valves, fittings, water heaters, heating and cooling equipment, waterworks infrastructure and industrial supplies to trade professionals across the United States and Canada. It does not make much of what it sells — it makes the part show up at the job site, on time, in the right quantity, with credit terms attached. Net sales for the quarter ended September 30, 2026 were $9.03B, up 9.5% (7.0% organic, 2.7% from acquisitions), and the United States was 95.5% of that. Over the twelve months to September 30, 2026, revenue was $32.74B, net income $2.07B and diluted EPS $10.62 (computed from the company's releases).

The story of the last five years is a change of address. The group was founded in 1887 and spent most of its listed life in London; over the last five years it has walked west: an additional NYSE listing in 2021, the primary listing moved from London to New York in 2022, a new Delaware holding company — Ferguson Enterprises Inc. — slotted on top on August 1, 2024 in a one-for-one share exchange, the fiscal year moved from a July to a December year-end (with a five-month transition period ended December 31, 2025), and the London secondary listing cancelled effective July 20, 2026. Eleven days later S&P Dow Jones Indices announced that Ferguson would join the S&P 500 on August 5, 2026, replacing Electronic Arts. The US index membership was, in effect, the prize at the end of that walk. The stock rose from a $234.33 July close to $250.08 on the next trading day, then gave all of it back and more, touching a 52-week low of $212.13 during the session on September 21. The third-quarter report on November 9 turned it: sales up 9.5%, full-year guidance raised, and the stock up 11.2% that day. At $256.47 it sits 5.6% below its 52-week high of $271.64 and 9.4% above the July close that came before the index news. The index news moved the stock for a day; the quarter's numbers moved it further.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Branch density (1,800+ locations) Purchasing scale ($33B sales) Contractor credit & relationships Breadth: plumbing + HVAC + waterworks Buying smaller rivals Distribution-centre logistics

A distributor's moat is availability. The contractor on a job site does not shop for the cheapest ball valve; he shops for the one he can have in forty minutes, on account, from a counter person who knows what he needed last time. That is a density game: more branches, more inventory, more trucks, spread across more sales — and $33B of trailing revenue buys purchasing terms smaller rivals cannot match. The same scale funds the roll-up: Ferguson buys regional distributors, plugs them into its supply chain and earns a better margin on their volume than they could. On the Q3 2026 call the CEO called it a "scale advantage business model." The vulnerability is that none of it creates demand. When housing turnover stalls, the densest branch network in the country sells fewer water heaters.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Half the business is a housing bet. Residential is roughly 50% of US revenue. It grew about 6% in Q3 2026 but only 2% over the first nine months, while the CFO put price inflation across the business at low-to-mid single digits. The non-residential half (+15% in Q3) is still doing most of the work; if project activity cools before housing recovers, there is no second engine.
02
Cash conversion slipped. Operating cash flow for the first nine months of 2026 was $1,472M versus $1,884M a year earlier (−21.9%). Trailing free cash flow of $1.32B is a 4.0% FCF margin, against a 6.3% net margin — earnings are running ahead of cash, typically as working capital (inventory and receivables) builds.
03
Debt is up. After FloWorks, net debt rose to $5.695B, 1.7x adjusted EBITDA, against $3.297B a year earlier; buybacks are paused and 2026 interest expense guidance went up to about $235M from about $200M. That is inside the company's 1–2x target, but it narrows the room for buybacks and further deals if demand weakens.
04
Index buying was a one-off. The S&P 500 announcement lifted the stock from $234.33 to $250.08 in a day; it closed August at $224.51 and touched $212.13 during the session on September 21. The Q3 report took it to $259.93 on November 9 and $256.47 at this edition. Index funds bought once; from here the quarterly numbers move the stock.

Bull Case

A distributor earning a 15.58% return on invested capital (StockAnalysis), with gross margin the CFO puts in a 30%–31% range, now fully American and in the S&P 500, coming off a quarter in which sales grew 9.5%, residential grew again (+6%), non-residential grew 15%, Industrial 55%, and guidance went up. Residential growth still leans on price: about 6% in Q3 with low-to-mid-single-digit inflation, 2% over nine months, and the CFO expects weak new-home building to weigh a little more in 2027. Fire & Fabrication fell 8% and Canada 4.5%. At 24.2x trailing GAAP earnings that grew 6.7%, and up 19.6% since September 18, the stock already prices in much of the good quarter; UBS's $270 target is only 5.3% above it.

CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Ferguson's Q3 2026 is a picture of a split construction economy that has started to narrow. Non-residential — commercial buildings, industrial plants, large mechanical projects — grew about 15%, with Industrial at +55% (32% organic) and Commercial/Mechanical at +17%. Residential grew about 6%, led by HVAC (+15%), with plumbing and Ferguson Home at +3% each and price inflation in the low-to-mid single digits. A distributor sells into both, so its reported growth is an average of a boom and a slow recovery. The investment question is which cycle turns first: if the residential half recovers while non-residential holds, growth accelerates; if the project cycle cools while housing stays stuck, it does the opposite.

Buying smaller distributors — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

Eleven acquisitions so far in 2026, with about $1.5B of combined annual revenue, FloWorks ($1,616M) the largest. The bull framing: a scaled distributor earns more on an acquired branch's volume than the seller could — better purchasing, better logistics, cross-selling HVAC into plumbing accounts — and a 15.58% return on invested capital (StockAnalysis) says that the machine works. The bear framing: acquired growth flatters a revenue line whose organic rate is 7.0% with low-to-mid-single-digit price inflation inside it, and it is being funded with debt while cash conversion is weakening. The way to judge it is not the P/E; it is organic growth and free cash flow over the next four quarters. Both are reported. Q3 gave a clear signal on the first; the second has not turned.
