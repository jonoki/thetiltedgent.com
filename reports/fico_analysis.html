01 Company Overview

Every American who has applied for a mortgage, a car loan or a credit card has been priced, in part, by a three-digit number that Fair Isaac invented. FICO sells two things: the Scores — the FICO Score licensed to lenders through the credit bureaus, plus consumer products via myFICO.com — and Software, an analytics and decisioning platform used by banks for originations, fraud and customer management. The Scores business is the money machine: in the quarter to June 30, 2026 it was 68% of revenue, and the company as a whole ran an 85.1% gross margin and a 52.1% operating margin on trailing revenue of $2.39B, up 24.1%. Trailing EPS is $34.47.

The story of the last fourteen months is a toll-booth owner discovering that the road belongs to someone else. FICO's recent growth came overwhelmingly from price: B2B Scores revenue rose 49% in the June quarter, "primarily reflecting a higher mortgage-origination score unit price," in the CFO's words, while mortgage-origination volumes rose only low single digits. The road in question — the mortgages bought by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — is run by the FHFA. In July 2025 its director, Bill Pulte, said lenders could use VantageScore 4.0 instead of FICO; on September 4, 2026 the FHFA gave VantageScore 4.0 full approval for all lenders selling to the two agencies, and the stock fell 16.68% in a day, from $1,118.93 to $932.26. TransUnion, one of VantageScore's three bureau owners, cut its VantageScore 4.0 mortgage price to $0.99 a score; FICO's fee is $10 a score for 2026 (HousingWire). At $924.52 the stock is 53.7% below its 52-week high of $1,998.01 and 61% below its November 2024 month-end peak. The question for the next year is simple to state: how much of FICO's pricing was product, and how much was monopoly?

FICO does not collect credit data; the three bureaus do. FICO licenses the algorithm that turns a credit file into a score, and gets paid a royalty each time a score is pulled. B2B revenue (lenders) rose 49% in Q3 FY2026; B2C (myFICO.com and scores sold to consumers via the bureaus) rose 5%. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Industry-standard score Lender models built around FICO Mortgage pricing power 85.1% gross margin FICO Score 10T validation Platform net retention 148% Consumer brand (myFICO)

The real moat was never the algorithm alone — it was that everyone downstream of a loan speaks FICO. Underwriting cut-offs, mortgage-insurance grids, securitization disclosures and rating-agency models are written in FICO points, which is why the company could lift mortgage score prices and watch revenue compound with volumes flat. The vulnerability is that the single largest buyer of that utility — the government-controlled mortgage agencies — can change the rulebook by directive, and on September 4, 2026 it did. A standard protected by habit is weaker than one protected by rule.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A regulator that wants the price lower. The FHFA opened the door to VantageScore 4.0 in July 2025 and granted full approval on September 4, 2026, letting every lender selling to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac choose between Classic FICO and VantageScore 4.0. By the end of August, during a pilot that began May 1, 2026, VantageScore already accounted for more than 9% of GSE mortgage securitizations (per Parameter's report).
02
A competitor priced at a tenth. VantageScore's owners advertise $0.99 a score against FICO's $10 or more. VantageScore was created by Equifax, Experian and TransUnion — the same bureaus that distribute FICO's score, which is exactly why FICO launched a direct-license route around them.
03
Growth is price, and price is the target. Mortgage-origination revenue up 97% on low-single-digit volume growth means the recent earnings trajectory depends on unit price holding. Any forced price concession falls almost entirely to the bottom line, in reverse.
04
Leverage built for a monopoly. $5.60B of debt, $248M of cash, and shareholders' equity of −$4.10B after years of buybacks. Debt/EBITDA is 4.39x and interest coverage 6.69x — comfortable for a toll road with pricing power, less so if the toll gets cut.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF SEP 21, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over five years, from the September 2021 month-end close of $397.93 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $924.52, FICO is still up 132.3% on price (it pays no dividend, so that is also the total return). But the shape is a round trip: a three-year climb that multiplied the price nearly six-fold to a $2,375.03 month-end peak in November 2024, then a 22-month descent that has taken 61.1% off that peak. The climb was the pricing story — mortgage score prices rising on flat volume. The descent has been punctuated, almost month by month, by the FHFA.

The 6-period monthly RSI captures the regime change. It sat above 70 in all but two months of 2023–2024 and peaked at 95.4 in November 2024 — about as one-sided as a chart gets. It now reads 26.5, oversold, after a low of 22.8 in April 2026. The 10-month moving average, at about $1,229.58, sits 33% above the price: the long-term trend is firmly down, and nothing on the chart yet says otherwise.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 26.8x trailing GAAP earnings and roughly 17–21x consensus, you are buying an 85% gross-margin, 65% ROIC business after a 61% fall from its peak month-end, with a $1B-a-year free-cash-flow stream. Management says it sees no mortgage volume losses to VantageScore so far, and lenders who must satisfy investors, insurers and rating agencies calibrated in FICO points will not switch overnight — "score shopping" may mean both scores are pulled, not one replaced. FICO Score 10T has 70 lenders in its adopter program and an independent study behind it; the direct-license route lets FICO cut the price at the tri-merge while keeping a fee on each closed loan. Meanwhile platform software — $413M ARR growing 62% — is a quality asset that barely registers in a Scores-dominated valuation. A 55.8% gap to the average target says the Street thinks the reaction overshot.

Bear Case

The last three years of growth were price, and the biggest customer's regulator has now handed every lender a $0.99 alternative. Mortgage origination is 62% of Scores revenue and grew 97% on low-single-digit volume; even a partial price concession falls almost straight to the bottom line. The competitor is owned by the bureaus that distribute FICO's own score. The balance sheet was built for a toll road that could only raise tolls: $5.6B of debt, negative equity of $4.1B, and $1.96B of buybacks in a single quarter at an average price about 24% above today's. Consensus estimates and most price targets pre-date September 4. An 8.58% short float suggests a meaningful part of the market thinks the earnings, not just the multiple, have to come down.

Top 3 Risks

01
Mortgage score pricing under regulatory pressure. The FHFA's September 4, 2026 full approval of VantageScore 4.0 removes FICO's exclusive position in GSE mortgages. The risk is less lost volume in the near term than forced price cuts to keep it.
02
Direct-license program stuck at certification. FICO's answer to bureau mark-ups and to Vantage pricing needs GSE certification it does not yet have. The longer it waits, the longer lenders compare a $10 score with a $0.99 one.
03
Leverage and capital allocation. Net debt of $5.35B at 4.39x EBITDA and negative book equity leave less room if Scores earnings reset. Buybacks at ~$1,149 average now look expensive against a $924.52 share price.
Industry Commentary
The FHFA and the end of the single-score mortgage
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For decades a GSE mortgage meant a FICO score. That changed in two steps: in July 2025 FHFA Director Bill Pulte said lenders could use VantageScore 4.0 as an alternative, and on September 4, 2026 — after a pilot starting May 1 in which VantageScore reached more than 9% of GSE securitizations by the end of August — the agency made the choice available to every lender, effective immediately. The Motley Fool's account quotes the directive: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are "to accept mortgage loans for purchase from banks when those loans were issued in reliance on a Vantage credit score rather than the more traditional FICO score." Pulte had earlier criticised FICO's price increases. The industry question is no longer whether lenders can switch but whether the downstream chain — mortgage insurers, investors, rating agencies — will re-price risk in VantageScore terms fast enough to make switching worthwhile.

Fighting price with structure: the direct-license model
ONGOING HEADWIND

FICO's October 2025 program lets tri-merge resellers compute the FICO Score themselves, which FICO says halves the average per-score fee by removing bureau mark-ups — $4.95 a score plus a $33 per-borrower, per-score fee only when a FICO-scored loan closes. CEO Will Lansing said it "brings transparency, competition, and cost-efficiency"; the Mortgage Bankers Association's Bob Broeksmit called it "a step in the right direction"; Pulte himself said he appreciated FICO "taking Constructive Criticism." Nearly a year on, the program is still waiting for GSE certification. The logic is sound — shift the charge from every inquiry to the loans that actually close — but it only works if it launches.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

FICO trades at 20.04x EV/EBITDA and 20.05x price-to-free-cash-flow after a 61% fall from its peak month-end. The bull framing: this is a quality compounder de-rated on a headline; lender inertia, the downstream calibration to FICO points and a strong platform-software business mean earnings fall less than the price did, and a 29% three-year EPS growth forecast at a 0.67 PEG is cheap. The bear framing: the multiple was always a bet on pricing power, and pricing power is what the regulator just removed; forecasts made before September 4 are the wrong denominator. The way to decide is not the P/E but two numbers FICO reports every quarter: mortgage-origination unit price and mortgage score volume. The November 4 print is the first with the new rules in force.
