01 Company Overview

FIS is the plumbing under a large slice of the banking system: core banking platforms, digital banking, payment processing, card issuing and capital-markets software sold to financial institutions worldwide. The lineage runs from Systematics, founded in Little Rock in 1968, through ALLTEL, to Fidelity National Financial, which bought the business in 2003 and renamed it Fidelity Information Services. It then grew by acquisition — Certegy (2006), eFunds (2007), Metavante (2009), SunGard ($9.1B, 2015) and, at the top of the cycle, Worldpay for $43 billion in 2019.

The Worldpay bet is the story of the last five years. FIS sold 55% of Worldpay to GTCR at an $18.5 billion valuation, closing February 1, 2024. Then, in April 2025, it agreed a swap: sell its remaining 45% stake to Global Payments and buy Global Payments' Issuer Solutions business (formerly TSYS) for a $13.5 billion enterprise value — $12 billion net after $1.5 billion of tax assets — paying with the Worldpay stake (valued at $6.6 billion pre-tax in the April 2025 announcement) plus about $7.7 billion of cash (Q3 2026 10-Q), funded with new debt — the April 2025 release planned $8 billion of new borrowing. That closed ahead of schedule, announced January 12, 2026. The card-issuing business now goes to market as FIS Total Issuing Solutions.

So FIS swapped a stake in a business that handles card payments for merchants for one that processes cards for the banks that issue them, and paid the difference in debt. On paper, it worked: TTM revenue is $12.94B, up 23.6%, Q3 2026 GAAP revenue rose 27%, and trailing EPS is $6.70, which puts the stock at 4.3x trailing earnings. That multiple is an illusion. About $2.2 billion of the $3.46 billion TTM net income is the one-off gain on the Worldpay sale in Q1 2026. On November 4, 2026, FIS cut its 2026 guidance for the second quarter running, sketched a 2027 in which revenue barely grows and adjusted EPS falls, and said its CFO was leaving. The stock lost 23.7% that day. It closed at $28.73 on November 6, 2026, down 57% from the $66.46 close at the end of 2025 and 72.5% below the November 2021 month-end. The question is whether a heavily indebted processor that still produces about $2.2 billion of free cash a year is cheap for good reason, or is a steady stream of cash the market has marked down too far. The 2027 outlook is where the answer starts.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Core-banking switching costs ~$25.5B contracted backlog Issuer processing scale (40B+ txns/yr) Multi-year processing contracts Regulated-client compliance depth 47–54% segment EBITDA margins

The moat is the cost of leaving. A bank's core system holds every account and posts every transaction, and a card issuer's processor authorises every swipe. Changing either is a multi-year conversion with regulators watching and customers ready to leave if one payment fails. That is why this revenue is recurring and why segment margins run 47–54%. The weakness is on the other side of the balance sheet. The business is steady, but the capital structure is not. FIS has spent two decades buying growth. The biggest purchase, Worldpay, cost $43B. A majority was sold at an $18.5B valuation, and the rest went to Global Payments in a deal that valued the whole of Worldpay at $24.25B (Electronic Payments International, April 2025). The latest swap was paid for with new debt.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage. $20.77B of debt, $740M of cash, a 0.55 current ratio and an Altman Z-score of 0.31, a bankruptcy-risk score on which lower is worse (StockAnalysis). Buybacks have been cut back until leverage comes down toward ~2.8x, and small acquisitions are paused. Until then, free cash goes mainly to lenders and the 6.1% dividend.
02
Execution credibility. Two guidance cuts in two quarters. In August 2026 the company took adjusted EPS guidance to $6.15–6.24 from $6.22–6.32. In November it went to $5.82–5.89, and revenue to $13.445–13.505B from $13.63–13.70B (FIS Q2 and Q3 2026 releases). In August the CEO said the shortfall was self-inflicted; in November the CFO left the day after the print. Trust takes several clean quarters to earn back, and the preliminary 2027 outlook gives little room for them.
03
Integration of a $13.5B acquisition. Issuer Solutions is about ten months into its first year inside FIS. Client conversions, the moves onto FIS's systems, are running late, and the $500M incremental FCF target for 2026 is the test.
04
A sector out of favour, and FIS now worse than the sector. Two days after FIS's August cut, Fiserv cut its 2026 adjusted EPS forecast to $7.20–7.40 from $8.00–8.30 (Fiserv Q2 2026 release, August 6, 2026). But on November 4, the day FIS fell 23.7%, Fiserv rose about 6% and Jack Henry about 11% (Yahoo daily closes). The latest fall belongs to FIS alone.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the Worldpay gain: FIS reported Q1 2026 GAAP EPS of $4.58 and net income of $2,366M, which includes a preliminary estimated $2.2 billion pre-tax gain on the Worldpay minority-stake sale (Q3 2026 10-Q). Proceeds were about $5.8 billion before tax, net of transaction fees and other costs (Q3 2026 10-Q). Second, acquisition accounting: goodwill rose to $25.00B after the Issuer Solutions purchase, and adjusted EPS ($1.61 in Q3) is more than twice GAAP EPS ($0.69). Third, the year-ago base: the first nine months of 2025 were a GAAP net loss of $128M. That makes year-over-year GAAP comparisons for 2025–26 unreliable.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $104.50 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $28.73, FIS lost 72.5% of its price (our calculation from the monthly series, dividends excluded). The fall came in two legs with a recovery in between. The first leg ran from 2021 to October 2023 (a $49.11 month-end). The market gave up on the Worldpay merger, and the stock traded at less than half its 2021 level before the GTCR sale closed. Then came a clean rebuild to $89.73 in October 2024, once the Worldpay separation closed and the story became a simpler bank-processor story. The second leg has run from mid-2025 to now, through the Issuer Solutions swap, two guidance cuts and the CFO's exit.

The 6-period monthly RSI reached 10.99 at the June 2026 close, the most oversold reading in the five-year series. The +15.1% July rebound lifted it to 31.8, September took it to 19.4, October's +17.2% bounce to 34.8, and it stands at 24.9 on the November 6 close. The 10-month moving average is about $41.20, 43% above the current price. The long-term trend is down and has shown no sign of turning. Two bounces off oversold readings have failed this year.

FIS's discount now comes from its debt, its repeated guidance cuts and a 2027 outlook that points to falling adjusted EPS.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay about 5x adjusted earnings for 2026 or 2027 and get a ~15% free-cash-flow yield before one-off Worldpay taxes and a 6.1% dividend. The business is recurring bank processing with 47–54% segment EBITDA margins and ~$25.5B of contracted backlog, and the FCF guide survived the November cut. The analyst average of $36.32 sits 26% above the price. The debt math is simple. About $2.2B of annual FCF against ~$21B of debt closes the leverage gap within a few years, and then the buyback comes back at a depressed share price. If the one-time revenue drag eases in 2028, as management said on the Q3 2026 call it expects, and conversions catch up, today's price will look like peak pessimism.

Bear Case

The headline P/E is fiction: ~11.8x once the Worldpay gain is removed. The adjusted multiple leaves out costs that GAAP counts, on assets FIS paid for with real debt. Net debt of $20.0B is 1.4x the equity, and $5.64B of debt falls due within a year. The current ratio is 0.55 and the Altman Z-score 0.31. Management has cut guidance twice in two quarters and the CFO has left. Adjusted EPS is expected to fall in 2027. FIS has a long record of buying growth and writing it down, and Worldpay was bought for $43B and a majority sold at an $18.5B valuation. The new $13.5B acquisition put goodwill up to $25.0B, more than the $14.81B the market now pays for the whole company. Two bounces off oversold readings have failed this year.

Top 3 Risks

01
Leverage meets execution. $20.77B of debt leaves little room for another miss, and FIS has now cut its own guidance twice this year. A third cut in February would push out the debt timeline, keep buybacks small and raise questions about the 6.1% dividend, which is the main shareholder return.
02
Integration and goodwill. Issuer Solutions is a $13.5B bet in its first year. If the $500M/$700M FCF targets slip, the $25.0B goodwill balance becomes an impairment risk, and a market cap of $14.81B makes that test harder to pass. FIS has taken large write-downs before (FY2023 net loss $6.655B).
03
Slower growth in both segments. Capital Markets, the highest-margin segment (54.0%), drove the August cut and was cut again in November to 2–2.5% growth; Banking went to 3.5–4% from 5–5.5%. For 2027, the preliminary outlook is roughly no revenue growth at all. If it keeps slowing, the debt paydown takes longer.
Industry Commentary
Bank processors, marked down
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

In August 2026 two of the biggest US bank-technology processors cut their outlooks within days of each other. FIS cut on August 4. Fiserv cut its adjusted EPS forecast to $7.20–7.40 from $8.00–8.30 two days later (Fiserv Q2 2026 release). Then the two paths split. Fiserv held its reduced full-year guidance at its Q3 report, while FIS cut again on November 4. In August the FIS CEO said "We don't see any trends in market that are changing here." Three months later the reasons FIS gave were client conversion delays and bank mergers. Investors are treating the stability of FIS's base business as unproven.

Consolidation by swap
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

The January 2026 three-way transaction reshaped the industry. Global Payments bought Worldpay, combining the stakes of GTCR and FIS. FIS took Global Payments' issuer processing. Each company narrowed its focus: Global Payments toward handling card payments for merchants, FIS toward the bank and card-issuer side. For FIS that means more exposure to bank IT budgets and card volumes, less to merchant payments, and a lot more debt.

The cash-yield argument
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $28.73, FIS's $14.81B market cap is about 6.5x trailing FCF of $2.30B before one-off Worldpay taxes. On its Q2 call the company pointed to more than $3.0B by 2028. The bull framing: this is a low point in sentiment applied to long-contract bank revenue, and cash will pay down the debt whatever the share price does. The bear framing: an enterprise value of $34.84B, about 2.4x the market cap, means shareholders only own what is left after lenders are paid. A small miss in FCF becomes a large miss in equity value. The next two prints on FCF and leverage will decide which framing is right.
