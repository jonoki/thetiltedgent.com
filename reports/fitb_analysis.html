00 Structural Event — The Comerica Merger Is Done
Status: Completed — not pending

Fifth Third's acquisition of Comerica Incorporated closed effective February 1, 2026, announced complete on February 2, 2026. Comerica shareholders received 1.8663 Fifth Third common shares per Comerica share in an all-stock exchange; Comerica's 6.875% Series B perpetual preferred was converted into a newly created Fifth Third preferred series with terms "not materially less favorable," and depositary shares converted 1:1. Fifth Third's board expanded to 16 directors, adding three former Comerica directors (Michael G. Van de Ven, Barbara R. Smith, Derek J. Kerr). Tim Spence remains Chairman, CEO and President. Aggregate deal value was not stated in the Form 8-K, and this report does not estimate it.

This is no longer a deal-spread stock. There is no arbitrage, no completion probability to handicap and no regulatory gate left — the merger is closed and the remaining question is purely one of execution. The combined bank crossed $300.1 billion in total assets, making it the 9th largest U.S. bank and, importantly, a Category III institution for regulatory purposes (a tier that brings tougher capital, liquidity and resolution-planning requirements than Fifth Third faced as a ~$215bn bank). Systems and brand conversion was scheduled for Labor Day weekend 2026 — i.e. the weekend immediately after this report's data date — which management called "the final step to unlocking the full run-rate" of cost synergies. Merger-related charges added $203 million pre-tax to Q2 noninterest expense, and year-to-date merger charges are roughly 65% of the expected full-year total.

Everything downstream in this report — the segment mix, the charge-off rates, the deposit base, the capital ratios — reflects a bank that is roughly 45% larger by loans than it was twelve months ago. Year-over-year comparisons are not organic and should not be read as such.

01 Company Overview

Fifth Third Bancorp is the holding company for Fifth Third Bank, N.A., a Cincinnati-headquartered regional bank operating through three reportable segments: Commercial Banking, Consumer & Small Business Banking, and Wealth & Asset Management. Following the Comerica merger it operates 1,500 full-service banking centers and holds $300.1 billion of assets, $234.1 billion of deposits and $178.5 billion of loans and leases as of June 30, 2026 — the 9th largest U.S. bank. Full-year 2025 revenue (the last complete pre-merger year) was $8,355 million, up 5.11% YoY; trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $10,095 million, up 26.31%, with the step-up driven almost entirely by five months of Comerica. Analyst consensus models $12.85 billion of 2026 revenue (+42.16%).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
$234B DEPOSIT BASE 27.3% NON-INTEREST-BEARING 1,500 BANKING CENTERS TWO $1B FEE FRANCHISES MIDDLE-MARKET RELATIONSHIPS CATEGORY III SCALE SWITCHING COSTS ON TREASURY

A regional bank's moat is its deposit franchise, and Fifth Third's has just been enlarged rather than diluted. $63.9 billion of the $234.1 billion deposit base is noninterest-bearing demand money — 27.3% — which is the single most valuable liability a bank can hold, because it funds earning assets at zero coupon. That mix is what allowed NIM to expand 24 basis points in two quarters, from 3.12% in Q2 2025 to 3.36% in Q2 2026, while interest-bearing deposit costs actually fell to 2.13%. Layered on top is genuine switching friction: treasury-management and commercial-payments mandates are operationally embedded in a corporate client's accounts-payable and receivable plumbing, and middle-market relationships are relationship-manager-led rather than price-led. The Comerica deal adds two things a Midwest bank could not build organically at speed — a Texas/California/Arizona commercial footprint and a second $1bn fee business — while pushing the bank past $300 billion of assets into Category III, which is simultaneously a scale advantage and a regulatory cost.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
Integration execution risk is concentrated in one weekend. The Comerica systems and brand conversion was scheduled for Labor Day weekend 2026 — days after this report's data date. Core-banking conversions are the classic point of failure in bank M&A: outages, misposted balances or a service degradation event drives depositor attrition in exactly the acquired Southwest markets the deal was bought for. Management called it "the final step to unlocking the full run-rate" of synergies, which is another way of saying the cost saves are not banked until it works.
B
Consumer credit is the soft underbelly. Consumer net charge-offs ran 0.53% in Q2 2026 versus 0.21% commercial, and consumer has been remarkably stable at 0.52–0.59% across all five quarters shown — it never improved. In a genuine consumer downturn this is the line that moves first and the reserve build that follows hits earnings immediately.
C
Capital is thinner than it was. CET1 fell from 10.81% at Q4 2025 to 9.89% at Q1 2026 as the Comerica balance sheet consolidated, recovering only to 9.93% by Q2. Ninety basis points of capital were spent on the deal, and the bank simultaneously stepped into a stricter regulatory tier. That constrains buybacks until earnings rebuild it.
D
Geographic strategy is unproven on profitability, not on volume. The disclosed evidence of Southeast/Southwest success is volume: 7% household growth, $2.5bn of campaign deposits. The bank has not disclosed the cost of those deposits or the branch-level returns. Promotional deposit gathering into a de novo branch network is expensive, and volume growth in new markets can destroy value if the money is bought at rate.

Notable One-Off Items

Trailing earnings are heavily distorted and should not be annualised. Q2 2026 carried $203 million of pre-tax merger-related charges, exactly reconciling reported diluted EPS of $0.83 to adjusted EPS of $1.02 — a $0.19 gap. Management stated year-to-date merger charges are approximately 65% of the expected full-year total, so roughly a third of the cost still lands in the second half. Q1 2026 was the extreme quarter: efficiency ratio 84.5% and ROTCE 3.5%, versus 55.8% and 19.0% in Q4 2025 — the merger closed mid-quarter and the initial provision on acquired loans plus deal costs landed at once. Separately, the bank executed a $4 billion securities repositioning in the quarter out of a roughly $68 billion portfolio, which is a discretionary realisation of losses to reinvest at higher yields, not an operating result. Finally, Q3 2025 contained a commercial charge-off spike — total NCO 1.09%, commercial 1.46%, versus 0.45%/0.38% the prior quarter — which appears idiosyncratic rather than a trend, since commercial NCOs fell straight back to 0.27% the next quarter. Any comparison of TTM EPS, ROE or net margin against peers without these adjustments is misleading.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The hard part is done: the deal closed in under four months, NIM has expanded 24bps in a year to 3.36%, charge-offs are at their lowest since Q2 2023 at 0.30%, and tangible book value per share has risen every single quarter from $20.98 to $23.15. On adjusted numbers the bank is already earning a 15.6% ROTCE with a 57.1% efficiency ratio before the systems conversion releases the remaining cost synergies. At 13.3× consensus 2026 EPS of $4.09 with no sell ratings on the street and a 2.93% yield, the market is paying for a merger that has largely already worked.

Bear Case

You are paying 2.36× tangible book for a bank whose CET1 fell 90bps to 9.93% to fund the deal, whose reserve ratio has been cut from 2.09% to 1.76% over five quarters — releasing reserves into earnings — and whose consumer charge-off rate has not improved at all (0.52–0.59% for five straight quarters) even in a benign environment. A $3.35 billion AOCI hole still sits against tangible book. The entire remaining synergy case rests on a core-systems conversion that had not yet happened as of this data date, and the stock already trades at the street's lowest published target of $55.

Top 3 Risks

1
Conversion failure. The Labor Day 2026 core-systems and brand conversion is the last gate to full run-rate synergies. Bank conversions fail publicly — misposted balances, outages, call-centre collapse — and the customers most likely to leave are exactly the acquired Southwest commercial and consumer relationships the deal was bought for.
2
Consumer credit deterioration with a thinner reserve. Consumer NCOs at 0.53% have been flat through a good economy while the allowance has been cut from 2.09% to 1.76% of loans. If consumer losses normalise upward, the bank rebuilds reserves out of earnings from a lower starting point — a double hit to EPS at a moment when CET1 is already 90bps below its pre-merger level.
3
Rate reversal against a large securities book. A roughly $68 billion securities portfolio already carries a $3.35 billion accumulated unrealised loss. A renewed rise in long rates deepens that mark, further suppresses tangible book value per share, and — if it coincides with deposit outflow — converts a paper loss into a realised one.
  Industry Commentary
AOCI and the securities book — the SVB mechanism
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

AOCI — accumulated other comprehensive income — is the bucket where unrealised gains and losses on available-for-sale securities are parked. When rates rose, the bonds banks bought at 2021 yields fell in price. Those losses are real economic losses, but under GAAP they never touch reported earnings; they sit in equity, invisible to the income statement, until the bank is forced to sell. That is precisely the mechanism that killed Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023: depositors withdrew, SVB had to sell securities to fund the outflow, the paper loss became a realised loss, and the equity base evaporated in days.

Fifth Third's number is an accumulated other comprehensive loss of $3,345 million against a roughly $68 billion securities portfolio. The effect on the balance sheet is precise and disclosed: tangible book value per share is $23.15 including AOCI versus $26.84 excluding it — the hole costs $3.69 per share, or 13.7% of tangible book. At $54.54 that is the difference between paying 2.36× tangible book and 2.03×. The bank executed a $4 billion repositioning during Q2 2026, which is a deliberate, funded realisation of some of that loss in order to reinvest at current yields — the opposite of a forced sale, and a sign of a bank managing the problem from a position of liquidity rather than one of desperation. The distinction that matters is entirely about funding: with $234 billion of deposits, 27.3% of it noninterest-bearing, Fifth Third is not remotely in SVB's position. But the mark is real, it suppresses tangible book, and if long rates rise again it gets worse.

ONGOING HEADWIND

That is the number to watch: it is structurally the higher-loss book, it is the book the Southeast branch expansion is growing, and it has no cushion of improvement to give back.

Meanwhile the reserve is being released, not built: the allowance for credit losses has fallen from 2.09% to 1.76% of loans across the five quarters, and a falling reserve ratio adds to reported earnings. Loss rates near cycle lows plus a shrinking reserve is the most flattering possible combination for EPS — and the most fragile.

Geographic mix shift and deposit beta — growth story or expensive money?
EMERGING TAILWIND

The strategic thesis is simple: a Midwest-rooted bank redeploying capital into faster-growing Southeast and, now, Southwest markets. The verified evidence of traction is real but entirely volume-based. Banking centers went from 1,089 to 1,500 in twelve months (+37.7%), almost all of it acquired rather than built. The target is ~1,750 by 2030, with over half the network in the Southeast, Texas, Arizona and California, including 150 de novo branches in Texas. On the customer side, legacy Fifth Third consumer household growth in the Southeast ran 7% against 3% company-wide — the new markets are compounding households at more than double the legacy rate. The Comerica Southwest deposit campaign delivered $2.5 billion of consumer deposits in one quarter, within total period-end consumer deposit growth of $4.6 billion.

The question the disclosure does not answer is whether those are profitable deposits. A "deposit campaign" is by definition promotional pricing, and de novo branches carry occupancy and staffing cost for years before they break even. What we can say is that at the consolidated level the funding is not getting more expensive: interest-bearing deposit costs fell to 2.13%, down 2bps sequentially, and the all-in rate paid on interest-bearing liabilities improved 34bps year over year to 2.44%. That is a favourable deposit beta — the share of a change in market rates that a bank has to pass through to its depositors. A low beta on the way down means deposit costs fall faster than asset yields, which is exactly why NIM expanded from 3.12% to 3.36%. And 27.3% of deposits ($63.9bn of $234.1bn) pay nothing at all, which is the structural anchor holding that beta down.

Bull versus bear framing. The bull reads the household and deposit growth as evidence that Fifth Third has genuinely bought its way into demographic tailwinds while its funding costs went down — growth without margin sacrifice, and 2.36× tangible book is a fair price for a 15.6% ROTCE franchise with a structurally cheap deposit base. The bear reads the same disclosure and notes that every profitability metric offered is a company-wide aggregate: no branch-level return, no cost of the campaign deposits, no separate P&L for the new markets. Volume in new geographies is the easy part; earning a spread on it after paying for the branch and the promotional rate is the part that has not been shown. Until Fifth Third discloses new-market economics rather than new-market activity, the geographic thesis remains asserted rather than demonstrated — and it is being underwritten at a premium multiple.
