00 Structural Event — The Comerica Merger Is Done
Status: Completed — not pending

Fifth Third's acquisition of Comerica Incorporated closed effective February 1, 2026, announced complete on February 2, 2026. Comerica shareholders received 1.8663 Fifth Third common shares per Comerica share in an all-stock exchange; Comerica's 6.875% Series B perpetual preferred was converted into a newly created Fifth Third preferred series with terms "not materially less favorable," and depositary shares converted 1:1. Fifth Third's board expanded to 16 directors, adding three former Comerica directors (Michael G. Van de Ven, Barbara R. Smith, Derek J. Kerr). Tim Spence remains Chairman, CEO and President. Aggregate deal value was not stated in the Form 8-K, and this report does not estimate it.

This is no longer a deal-spread stock. There is no arbitrage, no completion probability to handicap and no regulatory gate left — the merger is closed and the remaining question is purely one of execution. Fifth Third described the combined bank as the 9th largest U.S. bank when the merger closed. It crossed $300 billion in total assets in the second quarter and, in the company's words, formally became a Category III institution for regulatory purposes (a tier that brings tougher capital, liquidity and resolution-planning requirements than Fifth Third faced as a ~$215bn bank); assets were $301.2 billion at September 30, 2026. The systems and brand conversion ran over Labor Day weekend: on September 8, 2026 Fifth Third said it had moved about 600,000 accounts and 293 banking centers in Arizona, California, Florida, Michigan and Texas onto its own platform. In the Q3 release management said the conversion was completed with strong client and banker retention. Merger-related charges added $215 million pre-tax to Q3 noninterest expense and about $1.04 billion year to date.

Everything downstream in this report — the segment mix, the charge-off rates, the deposit base, the capital ratios — reflects a bank that is roughly 45% larger by loans than it was twelve months ago. Year-over-year comparisons are not organic and should not be read as such.

01 Company Overview

Fifth Third Bancorp is the holding company for Fifth Third Bank, N.A., a Cincinnati-headquartered regional bank operating through three reportable segments: Commercial Banking, Consumer & Small Business Banking, and Wealth & Asset Management. Following the Comerica merger it operates 1,429 banking centers (1,500 at June 30) and holds $301.2 billion of assets, $239.5 billion of deposits and $180.3 billion of portfolio loans and leases as of September 30, 2026 — the 9th largest U.S. bank (the company's own description). Full-year 2025 revenue (the last complete pre-merger year) was $8,355 million, up 5.11% YoY; trailing-twelve-month revenue through September 2026 is $11,148 million, up 37.2% (our calculation from StockAnalysis quarterly revenue), with the step-up driven almost entirely by eight months of Comerica. Analyst consensus models $12.90 billion of 2026 revenue (+42.69%) (StockAnalysis, read October 22).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
$239.5B DEPOSIT BASE 27.0% NON-INTEREST-BEARING 1,429 BANKING CENTERS TWO ~$1B FEE BUSINESSES MIDDLE-MARKET RELATIONSHIPS CATEGORY III SCALE SWITCHING COSTS ON TREASURY

A regional bank's moat is its deposit franchise, and Fifth Third's has just been enlarged rather than diluted. $64.7 billion of the $239.5 billion deposit base (September 30, 2026) is noninterest-bearing demand money — 27.0% — which is the single most valuable liability a bank can hold, because it funds earning assets at zero coupon. That mix is what allowed NIM to widen 21 basis points in a year, from 3.13% in Q3 2025 to 3.34% in Q3 2026 (it was 3.36% in Q2), while interest-bearing deposit costs fell to 2.24% in Q3 2026 from 2.41% a year earlier (they rose from 2.13% in Q2). Layered on top is real switching cost: a company that runs its payments and cash management through Fifth Third has its bill-paying and collections wired into the bank's systems, and mid-sized corporate clients tend to stay with their relationship banker rather than move for a better price. The Comerica deal adds two things a Midwest bank could not build quickly on its own — a Texas/California/Arizona commercial footprint and a bigger wealth and asset management business — while pushing the bank past $300 billion of assets into Category III, which is simultaneously a scale advantage and a regulatory cost.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
Integration risk has eased, not ended. The Comerica systems and brand conversion ran over Labor Day weekend and Fifth Third announced on September 8 that it was complete. Core-banking conversions are the classic point of failure in bank M&A: outages, misposted balances or a service degradation event drive depositor attrition in exactly the acquired Southwest markets the deal was bought for. The Q3 release reports strong client and banker retention and core deposits up 3% from Q2 to $235.6 billion, but the cost saves are only proven when expenses fall: Q3 noninterest expense was $2,049 million, down 3% from Q2, and still included $215 million of merger charges.
B
Consumer credit is the soft underbelly. Consumer net charge-offs ran 0.52% in Q3 2026 versus 0.22% commercial, and consumer has been remarkably stable at 0.52–0.59% across all five quarters shown — it never improved. In a genuine consumer downturn this is the line that moves first and the reserve build that follows hits earnings immediately.
C
Capital is thinner than it was. CET1 fell from 10.81% at Q4 2025 to 9.89% at Q1 2026 as the Comerica balance sheet consolidated, and has recovered to 9.93% in Q2 and 10.02% in Q3 — still 79 basis points below its Q4 2025 level, with the bank now in a stricter regulatory tier. The Q3 release shows no share repurchases in the quarter or year to date.
D
Geographic strategy is unproven on profitability, not on volume. The disclosed evidence of Southeast/Southwest success is volume: 7% household growth, $2.5bn of campaign deposits. The bank has not disclosed the cost of those deposits or the branch-level returns. Promotional deposit gathering into a de novo branch network is expensive, and volume growth in new markets can destroy value if the money is bought at rate.

Notable One-Off Items

Trailing earnings are heavily distorted and should not be annualised. Q3 2026 carried $215 million of pre-tax merger-related charges (about $1.04 billion year to date), which is why reported diluted EPS was $0.90 against adjusted EPS of $1.13 — a $0.23 gap from certain items. Q1 2026 was the extreme quarter: efficiency ratio 84.5% and ROTCE 3.5%, versus 55.8% and 19.0% in Q4 2025 — the merger closed mid-quarter and the initial provision on acquired loans plus deal costs landed at once. Separately, Q3 2025 contained a commercial charge-off spike — total NCO 1.09%, commercial 1.46%, versus 0.45%/0.38% the prior quarter — which appears idiosyncratic rather than a trend, since commercial NCOs fell straight back to 0.27% the next quarter. Any comparison of TTM EPS, ROE or net margin against peers without these adjustments is misleading.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The hard part is done: the deal closed in under four months, the systems conversion is finished with client and banker retention reported, NIM is up 21bps in a year to 3.34%, charge-offs are stable at 0.31%, and CET1 has rebuilt to 10.02%. Adjusted ROTCE was 20.6% in Q3, and the reported efficiency ratio improved to 62.4% from 64.3% in Q2 with $215 million of merger charges still inside it. At 12.7× consensus 2026 EPS of $4.16 with no sell ratings on the street and a 3.18% yield, the market is paying for a merger that has largely already worked.

Bear Case

You are paying 2.40× tangible book for a bank whose tangible book value per share fell about 5% in the quarter to $22.01 as the accumulated other comprehensive loss widened to $4.92 billion, whose CET1 is still 79bps below its pre-merger level, whose reserve ratio has slipped from 1.96% to 1.74% over five quarters — releasing reserves into earnings — and whose consumer charge-off rate has not improved at all (0.52–0.59% for five straight quarters) even in a benign environment. Excluding merger charges the efficiency ratio was 54.6%, still above the 54.1% the standalone bank ran on the same basis in Q3 2025, so the promised cost savings have yet to show up in the numbers.

AOCI and the securities book — the SVB mechanism
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

AOCI — accumulated other comprehensive income — is the bucket where unrealised gains and losses on available-for-sale securities are parked. When rates rose, the bonds banks bought at 2021 yields fell in price. Those losses are real economic losses, but under GAAP they never touch reported earnings; they sit in equity, invisible to the income statement, until the bank is forced to sell. That is precisely the mechanism that killed Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023: depositors withdrew, SVB had to sell securities to fund the outflow, the paper loss became a realised loss, and the equity base evaporated in days.

Fifth Third's number is an accumulated other comprehensive loss of $4,919 million at September 30, 2026 (it was $3,286 million at June 30, as shown in the Q3 release) against a securities portfolio of about $71 billion at amortized cost. The effect on the balance sheet is precise: tangible book value per share is $22.01 including AOCI versus about $27.43 excluding it — the hole costs about $5.42 per share, or 19.8% of tangible book excluding AOCI (the release gives $27.43 excluding AOCI). At $52.81 that is the difference between paying 2.40× tangible book and 1.93×. The loss widened by $1.63 billion in one quarter, worth about $1.80 per share. The distinction that matters is entirely about funding: with $239.5 billion of deposits, 27.0% of it noninterest-bearing, Fifth Third is not remotely in SVB's position. But the mark is real, it suppresses tangible book, and if long rates rise again it gets worse.

Commercial has improved sharply — from 1.46% in Q3 2025 to 0.22% in Q3 2026, with the Q3 2025 spike looking idiosyncratic since it reversed completely the following quarter. Meanwhile the reserve is being released, not built: the allowance for credit losses has fallen from 1.96% to 1.74% of loans across the five quarters, and a falling reserve ratio adds to reported earnings. Loss rates near cycle lows plus a shrinking reserve is the most flattering possible combination for EPS — and the most fragile.

Pre-2020 comparison. The only historical anchor the company itself provides is that the Q2 2026 total NCO rate of 0.30% was the lowest since Q2 2023 (Q3 2026: 0.31%). Anyone underwriting this stock on normalised credit should pull the 2017–2019 10-Ks and set the baseline properly — the five-quarter view above is not long enough to establish what "normal" looks like.

Geographic mix shift and deposit beta — growth story or expensive money?
EMERGING TAILWIND

The strategic thesis is simple: a Midwest-rooted bank redeploying capital into faster-growing Southeast and, now, Southwest markets. The verified evidence of traction is real but entirely volume-based. Banking centers fell from 1,500 at June 30 to 1,429 at September 30; the Q3 release lists $74 million of former branch property no longer used for banking, mostly from the Comerica acquisition. The target is ~1,750 by 2030, with over half the network in the Southeast, Texas, Arizona and California, including 150 de novo branches in Texas. On the customer side, in Q2 legacy Fifth Third consumer household growth in the Southeast ran 7% against 3% company-wide, more than double the company rate; in Q3 the company reported 7% in the Southeast and 8% in the Southwest. The Comerica Southwest deposit campaign delivered $2.5 billion of consumer deposits in the second quarter, when consumer deposits grew $4.6 billion in total.

The question the disclosure does not answer is whether those are profitable deposits. A "deposit campaign" is by definition promotional pricing, and de novo branches carry occupancy and staffing cost for years before they break even. At the consolidated level, funding is cheaper than a year ago but got dearer in Q3: interest-bearing deposit costs were 2.24%, up from 2.13% in Q2 and down from 2.41% in Q3 2025. The release puts the quarter's rise down to higher market rates and to money that Comerica clients had kept in off-balance-sheet investment sweep accounts moving into interest-bearing deposits. Over the year the rate paid on all interest-bearing liabilities fell 27bps to 2.50% while the yield on earning assets slipped only 3bps to 5.15%, which is how NIM widened from 3.13% to 3.34%. The share of a change in market rates that a bank has to pass on to its depositors is called its deposit beta; Q3 shows it working in the other direction, with deposit costs up 11bps as market rates rose. And 27.0% of deposits ($64.7bn of $239.5bn at September 30) pay nothing at all, which is the structural anchor holding that beta down.

Bull versus bear framing. The bull reads the household and deposit growth as evidence that Fifth Third has genuinely bought its way into demographic tailwinds while its funding costs fell over the year — growth without margin sacrifice, and 2.40× tangible book is a fair price for a franchise earning a 16.6% reported ROTCE with a structurally cheap deposit base. The bear reads the same disclosure and notes that every profitability metric offered is a company-wide aggregate: no branch-level return, no cost of the campaign deposits, no separate P&L for the new markets. Volume in new geographies is the easy part; earning a spread on it after paying for the branch and the promotional rate is the part that has not been shown. Until Fifth Third discloses new-market economics rather than new-market activity, the geographic thesis remains asserted rather than demonstrated — and it is being underwritten at a premium multiple.
