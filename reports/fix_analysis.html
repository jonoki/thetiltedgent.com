01 Company Overview

Comfort Systems USA is an MEP contractor — mechanical, electrical and plumbing: the company that installs the heating, cooling, piping, power distribution, fire protection and controls inside a building. It is not a household name and it does not want to be. FY2025 revenue was $9.10B, up 29.5% from $7.03B, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $12.54B, up 50.7%. At a $43.53B market value (34.99M shares on its latest 10-Q cover × the October 26 close) it is larger than most of the industrial names investors can name, and it was added to the S&P 500 effective December 22, 2025 — an index entry that arrived roughly a decade into one of the strongest runs any large US stock has had.

The reason to look closely is a single number: backlog of $17.81B at September 30, 2026, up 89.9% year over year and up $3.8B in the third quarter alone, while 58.8% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026 came from technology customers — overwhelmingly data centres. This has stopped being a diversified building-services company and become, in practice, a business whose growth rides on how much the largest cloud and AI companies spend on new data centres. The central debate on this page is not whether the backlog is real (it is contracted and disclosed) but whether the market is paying a P/E of 26.9 for a lasting change in a contracting business, or for the top of a building boom that has historically ended badly for the people holding the shovels. In October the market leaned toward the second answer: the shares fell 24.8% between the September 30 close and October 26, through a quarter that beat forecasts.

This is the answer to "why does this contractor earn a 25.6% gross margin when contracting is a 15–18% business?" Instead of sending electricians and pipefitters to assemble systems in the mud on a customer's site, Comfort Systems builds them as modules in its own factories — skidded chiller plants, pre-piped cooling modules, pre-wired electrical rooms — then ships and sets them. The consequences are all in the same direction: factory labour is cheaper and more productive than field labour; quality control is repeatable; weather and site congestion stop being schedule risks; and one skilled tradesman in a plant can produce several times what he could on a jobsite. On the Q3 call management said it remains on track for over 4 million square feet of modular space by the end of 2026 and expects about 6 million by the end of 2027. The company does not report modular revenue or modular margin in its filings, so the size of the mix benefit cannot be isolated from the outside — but the direction is plain: gross margin went from 18.3% in FY2021 to 25.6% in the first nine months of 2026 while revenue roughly quadrupled.

Why it matters competitively: a factory is a capital asset with lead time. A regional contractor cannot decide next quarter to have 4 million square feet of modular capacity. This is the closest thing to a moat in a fragmented trade business.

Schools, hospitals, government buildings, food and pharmaceutical plants, and the maintenance-and-replacement service business attached to an installed base built over three decades. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
MODULAR FACTORY CAPACITY SKILLED-LABOUR ROSTER HYPERSCALER PREQUALIFICATION SCHEDULE CERTAINTY MULTI-TRADE SELF-PERFORM BONDING & BALANCE SHEET SERIAL ACQUIRER PLAYBOOK

The moat is not technology and not brand — it is the ability to show up with enough qualified people, on a date, at a price. A hyperscaler (one of the largest cloud companies) building a $2B campus is not optimising the MEP bid to the last basis point; it is optimising for the building being energised on the promised day, because every month of delay is a month of GPUs not earning. That inverts the traditional contracting dynamic. In an ordinary commercial build, the general contractor squeezes the mechanical sub and the sub eats it. In a data-centre build with a scarce pool of qualified subs, the sub has pricing power, and the customer pays for certainty. Comfort Systems supplies certainty three ways: modular prefabrication (build it in a factory where the schedule is controllable), self-performance (do the mechanical and the electrical with its own crews rather than coordinating third parties), and a workforce of over 25,000 (Q3 2026 call) assembled over three decades of acquisitions. The financial evidence is the margin: gross margin 18.32% in FY2021 → 24.1% in FY2025 → 25.6% in the first nine months of 2026, and operating margin 6.08% → 16.9% over the same span. A contractor whose operating margin nearly triples while revenue roughly quadruples is being paid for something other than volume.

One structural nuance an investor must hold onto: Comfort Systems is a percentage-of-completion contractor. Revenue is recognised as work is performed — cost incurred against total estimated cost — not when a contract is signed and not when the building opens. That has two consequences. First, backlog is a leading indicator and revenue is a lagging one: the $17.81B booked at September 30, 2026 is future revenue, and on the Q3 call Brian Lane said the backlog now stretches over roughly 18 to 24 months, against about nine months a decade ago. Second, reported profit depends on management's estimate of total contract cost. If a job's cost estimate is revised, the correction lands in the current quarter's margin, retroactively. Percentage-of-completion accounting is legitimate and universal in this industry, but it means margins carry an estimation judgement that a product company's do not.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer and end-market concentration. Technology is 58.8% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, up from 42.4% a year earlier. The buyers of data-centre capacity are a handful of hyperscalers and their developer partners. This is not diversified industrial exposure with a data-centre kicker; it is a bet on hyperscaler spending with an industrial tail. If AI infrastructure spending is deferred rather than cancelled, backlog conversion slows and revenue stalls at exactly the moment the share price assumes acceleration.
▸
Skilled labour is the binding constraint — not demand. The limit on how fast Comfort Systems can grow is the number of licensed electricians, pipefitters and sheet-metal workers it can recruit, train and retain. Demand is currently abundant; qualified crews are not. Scarcity is helping today (it is a principal reason pricing and therefore margin have improved) and it caps the upside tomorrow: the company cannot simply convert a bigger backlog into proportionally bigger revenue if it cannot staff it. Wage inflation is the transmission mechanism by which today's tailwind becomes tomorrow's margin pressure.
▸
Fixed-price risk on very large jobs. Large new-construction contracts concentrate estimation risk. Work on new buildings is now about 75% of revenue (Q3 call), and the average job is far bigger than it was three years ago. Under percentage-of-completion, a single badly-estimated mega-project can move a quarter's margin. Q3 2026 showed how sensitive the margin is to mix: gross margin slipped to 24.8% from 25.9% in Q2 because, management said on the call, equipment and materials were a larger share of costs, and equipment does not earn the margin the company's own labour does. The execution record has been excellent — which is why the market had stopped discounting for the risk.
▸
Acquisition integration and the same-store gap. Comfort Systems has grown by buying regional contractors since its 1997 roll-up IPO, and continues to: on the Q3 call management announced two purchases closed August 1 (Boys Electrical and NeoPod, a healthcare module maker) that it expects to add over $100M of annual revenue. That is why it reports total and same-store backlog separately: at September 30, 2026 the gap was $17.81B total versus $17.39B same-store, so roughly $420M of the backlog was acquired rather than won. The disclosure lets an outsider check that growth is organic (it overwhelmingly is). The risk is the flip side: a roll-up that pays up in a hot market inherits other people's estimating culture — and management said on the call that acquisition prices have been high.

Notable One-Off Items

The Q3 2026 results release shows no material non-recurring gains, impairments, restructuring charges or litigation settlements. The reported figures are clean GAAP operating results, which is itself worth stating: unlike most large growth stories, the earnings on this page need no add-backs to look good — the company's own non-GAAP measure (adjusted EBITDA of $667.9M in Q3 2026, 17.7% of revenue) sits close to the GAAP operating margin of 16.6%.

Three items nonetheless distort period-to-period comparison and deserve flagging. (1) The acquired-backlog wedge. Total backlog $17.81B versus same-store $17.39B means roughly $420M came in through acquisition. Small next to the growth, but the distinction compounds if deal pace picks up. (2) The cash-flow surge. Operating cash flow was $1.01B in Q3 2026 against $553.3M a year earlier, and $2.54B for the first nine months against $717.8M — customers prepaying and progress-billing against a backlog that grew 90%. It is real cash, but it is not a repeatable run rate; it will normalise or reverse when backlog growth flattens. (3) Mix, both ways. The margin expansion through 2025 and early 2026 was mix (modular, industrial, new construction) rather than a windfall, which is the bull case's strongest point. Q3 showed the other side: a heavier share of equipment and materials held gross margin at 24.8%, flat on the year and down from Q2. Mix shifts persist in a way one-offs do not — until the mix shifts back.

The shape matters as much as the magnitude. There was no drawdown worth the name in 2022 — the October 2022 close of $123.28 was above the December 2021 close of $98.94, while most growth stocks were deep in a bear market. The vertical phase began in February 2024 (+40.6% in one month, $217.47 → $305.73), when the market first connected AI data-centre construction to the contractors who build it, and accelerated through 2025 as the technology end market grew toward 59% of revenue. The first serious interruption was January–March 2025 (−26.2%, $436.75 → $322.33), a scare about AI spending rather than a company event — the stock made it all back and more by May.

What is happening right now is more interesting than the run. The stock peaked on a monthly-close basis at $1,981.95 in June 2026 (52-week intraday high $2,073.99 on May 14), fell 12.7% in July and 10.6% in August, recovered 7.0% in September to $1,654.09 and closed at $1,817.96 on October 6. The trailing P/E has fallen from 39.6× at the last edition to 26.9×, on a lower price and higher earnings. The bear reading is that the market prices cyclical peaks before they appear in results, and a stalled margin is the first crack. The bull reading is that 26.9× trailing for 50.7% revenue growth, backlog up 90% and returns on capital above 100% is not a bubble price.

Where the debate stands today
08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the backlog, because everything else is downstream of it. At September 30, 2026 Comfort Systems held $17.81B of total backlog, up 89.9% from $9.38B a year earlier, and $17.39B on a same-store basis — meaning about 95% of the growth was won, not bought. For scale: the backlog alone is 1.42× the entire trailing-twelve-month revenue of $12.54B, and it has nearly tripled from $5.99B at December 2024 in twenty-one months. It also runs longer than it used to: on the Q3 call Brian Lane put its time frame at roughly 18 to 24 months. Spread evenly over that span, $17.81B is about $8.9–11.9B a year of revenue from existing backlog alone (our arithmetic, not company guidance), before a single new order is booked. Management's guide of mid-20% same-store growth for 2027 lines up with consensus: $17.05B of FY2027 revenue against $13.72B for FY2026 (stockanalysis.com), a 24.3% step-up.

Why backlog leads and revenue lags — and why that distinction is the whole game here. Comfort Systems accounts for its contracts on a percentage-of-completion basis: as costs are incurred against the total estimated cost of a job, a proportionate share of the contract value is recognised as revenue. Signing a $400M data-centre contract therefore produces zero revenue on the day it is signed; it produces backlog. Revenue appears over the following quarters as crews and modules are deployed. The practical consequences are three. (i) Backlog turns before revenue does, in both directions — a booking slowdown shows up in backlog quarters before it shows up in the income statement, which is why the market reacts to the backlog line and not the revenue line. (ii) Revenue growth of 50.7% is describing bookings that were made largely in 2025; the stock is being priced off bookings being made now. (iii) Reported margin embeds a management estimate of total job cost, so a re-estimate corrects the current quarter retroactively. None of this is exotic — it is standard construction accounting — but an investor who reads the revenue line as the news is reading a report on the past.

The margin expansion is the second act, and it is genuinely unusual. Mechanical contracting is a commodity trade with commodity economics: bid the job, win on price, earn a thin spread. Comfort Systems' gross margin has gone 18.32% (FY2021) → 21.0% (FY2024) → 24.1% (FY2025) → 25.6% (first nine months of 2026). Operating margin went from 6.08% to 16.9% and net margin from 4.66% to 13.1% over the same period. Three mechanisms explain it, and they are all mix or scarcity rather than magic. Modular prefabrication: systems assembled in the company's own factories (capacity heading past 4 million square feet by year-end 2026) rather than in the field, where labour is more productive, quality repeatable and weather irrelevant. Mix shift to industrial and new construction: technology plus manufacturing is now 76.0% of revenue and new buildings about 75%, both better-priced than commercial tenant work. Labour scarcity as pricing power: when there are more data-centre projects than there are qualified MEP contractors to staff them, the contractor sets the price. Q3 2026 was the first pause: gross margin of 24.8%, flat on the year and down from 25.9% in Q2, because more of the quarter's cost was equipment and materials, which earn less than the company's own labour (Q3 call). Management expects 2027 margins to stay in the strong ranges of recent quarters. The scarcity mechanism is the one to watch, because it is a market condition, not a capability.

The labour constraint is the real ceiling. It is worth being blunt: demand is not what limits this company — supply of skilled trades is. A journeyman electrician or pipefitter takes four to five years of apprenticeship to produce, and the retirement wave in the trades is running ahead of new entrants. Comfort Systems employs over 25,000 people (Q3 call) and its ability to convert $17.81B of backlog into revenue is essentially a function of how many of them it can put on jobsites and in module factories. This cuts both ways with unusual sharpness. Scarcity is why margins expanded — you cannot bid a job below cost if the alternative bidder cannot staff it either. And scarcity is why the growth rate has a hard ceiling — a backlog growing 90% cannot convert into revenue growing 90% if headcount grows far more slowly. Modular capacity is management's direct answer to this: a module factory is, precisely, a way to get more output per tradesman. Watch employee count against backlog conversion; that ratio is the operational tell.

Valuation, stated three ways. (i) On trailing GAAP earnings of $46.30, FIX trades at 26.9× — above EMCOR's 20.7× and IES Holdings' 19.3×, well below Quanta's 64.5×. (ii) On consensus FY2026 EPS of $51.10 (+77.0%) it trades at 24.3×, and at 18.8× FY2027's $66.21; stockanalysis.com's forward multiple is 20.15×. (iii) On trailing free cash flow of $2.44B against an enterprise value of about $41.6B (market value plus debt, less cash; stockanalysis.com), it trades at roughly 17× EV/FCF — but much of that cash is customer prepayments on a booming backlog and will not keep growing at this rate. The PEG of 0.42 is the bull's headline number and also the most fragile, because its denominator is a forecast growth rate that depends on other companies' capital budgets. The honest framing: at 26.9× trailing with returns on capital above 100%, net cash, 50.7% revenue growth and a backlog covering 1.42 years of revenue, the price is a third lower than at the last edition while the delivered numbers improved. It still requires the growth to continue, and the companies deciding whether it continues are Comfort Systems' customers.

Bull Case

Backlog of $17.81B (+89.9%, with about 95% of the growth same-store) covers 1.42 years of revenue, and the company says it now runs over 18 to 24 months, so the next several quarters are largely contracted. Gross margin has expanded about 730 basis points since FY2021 on a mix shift toward modular prefabrication, industrial end markets and new construction — not on a windfall — and modular capacity heading to about 6 million square feet by end-2027 extends that shift. Q3's flat margin had a named, mechanical cause (more equipment in the cost mix), and management expects 2027 margins to stay strong. ROIC above 100% on a net-cash balance sheet ($2.16B cash, $49.6M of borrowings) means growth is self-funded and acquisitions are free options. At 26.9× trailing and a 0.42 PEG, with the shares 40.0% below the 52-week high and every target in the consensus at least 45% higher, the sell-off has priced in a slowdown that has not appeared in any reported number.

Bear Case

58.8% of nine-month revenue comes from technology customers and about 75% from new buildings: this is a bet on hyperscaler spending with a beta of 1.66, and it fell 29.6% from its October 6 close before reporting a single number — which is what a cyclical peak looks like from the inside. Q3 gross margin was flat on the year and down for a second straight quarter, the first stall in the margin story. Percentage-of-completion accounting means today's margins embed management cost estimates on the largest, most complex jobs the company has ever taken, and the CFO who has overseen those numbers since 2005 is retiring. The 25.6% gross margin is partly a scarcity rent that competes away when EMCOR, Quanta, IES and every private regional contractor expand capacity into the same demand. And the growth ceiling is not demand but licensed tradesmen, who take five years to make. Pay 26.9× trailing for a contractor at a cyclical high in its dominant end market and the downside is not just a lower P/E — it is a lower P/E on lower earnings.

Top 3 Risks

01
Hyperscaler spending concentration. With technology at 58.8% of revenue (from 42.4% a year earlier) and backlog growth of 90% driven by the same customers, a deferral — not even a cancellation — of AI data-centre programmes would slow backlog conversion first and revenue second. The stock's October 2026 fall alongside other data-centre builders, before its own results, suggests the market is already handicapping this. FIX does not control the variable that determines its outcome.
02
Skilled-labour scarcity — the constraint that is also the moat. Growth is capped by electricians and pipefitters, not by orders. If wage inflation accelerates faster than the company can reprice fixed-price contracts already in backlog, the 25.6% gross margin compresses on work already sold. Conversely, if the labour shortage eases because demand cools, the pricing power that produced the margin expansion eases with it. There is no version of this risk that is comfortable.
03
Percentage-of-completion estimation risk on record-size jobs. New buildings are about 75% of revenue and average project size has grown enormously. Under percentage-of-completion, a single re-estimate of total cost on a large data-centre contract flows through the current quarter's margin retroactively. Q3's margin pause came from cost mix, not from a re-estimate, and the company's execution record has been excellent — which means the market applies little discount for a risk that is structurally present and would be a genuine surprise if it materialised, in a year when the finance chief changes.
  Industry Commentary
Skilled Trades Are the Sector's Hard Ceiling
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every MEP contractor in America is bidding into the same shortage of licensed electricians, pipefitters and sheet-metal workers, and no amount of capital fixes it inside five years — that is the apprenticeship cycle. The visible symptom is margin: Comfort Systems' gross margin rose from 18.32% in FY2021 to 25.6% in the first nine months of 2026. Scarcity rent is real income, but it is rent, not productivity, and it is the first thing to disappear if demand cools. The genuinely structural response is to move labour off the jobsite and into a factory, which is exactly what modular prefabrication does — and why capacity heading past 4 million square feet by year-end 2026, and about 6 million by end-2027, is a competitive statement, not a logistics detail.
The Whole Group Now Trades on AI Spending
ONGOING HEADWIND
Not long ago these were four different businesses. Today FIX ($43.53B, +26.7% on price over the year), Quanta ($84.78B, +27.9%), IES Holdings ($8.64B, −0.7%) and EMCOR ($29.37B, −11.0%, 20.7× trailing) move on the same headlines about cloud capital-spending budgets. The diversification benefit an investor might expect from owning "industrials" has largely evaporated within this group. The headwind is not competitive — all four have more work than they can staff — it is correlation: when the AI-spending story wobbles, as it did in Q1 2025, through July and August 2026 and again in October 2026 (companies building and equipping AI data centres were down a median 23% from October 6, Yahoo Finance reported on October 23), everything in the group falls together regardless of individual execution. FIX's trailing multiple (26.9×) is second in the group only to Quanta's, and its beta (1.66) second only to IES Holdings' (1.77).
Modular Construction Is the Real Industrial Shift
EMERGING TAILWIND
Construction is one of the few large industries where measured productivity has barely moved in fifty years, because the work happens once, outdoors, in a unique configuration. Prefabrication attacks that directly: build the chiller plant, the electrical room and the cooling module in a factory, ship them, set them. Data centres are the ideal application because they are highly repeatable — the same hall, replicated across a campus and across the country — which is why the shift is happening here first. Bull framing: if modular is the reason a contractor can hold a 25.6% gross margin, the rise in FIX's P/E from a building-services level to 26.9× trailing even after October is a lasting recognition that this is a manufacturing business wearing a contractor's uniform. Bear framing: factories can be built by competitors too, and the company does not report modular revenue or modular margin in its filings — so the most important claim in the bull case is precisely the one an outside investor cannot check.
