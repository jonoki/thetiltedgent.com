01 Company Overview

Comfort Systems USA is an MEP contractor — mechanical, electrical and plumbing: the company that installs the heating, cooling, piping, power distribution, fire protection and controls inside a building. It is not a household name and it does not want to be. FY2025 revenue was $9.10B, up 29.5% from $7.03B, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $11.23B, up 46.1%. At a $56.58B market capitalisation it is now larger than most of the industrial names investors can name, and it was added to the S&P 500 effective December 22, 2025 — an index entry that arrived roughly a decade into one of the best runs any large-cap US stock has had.

The reason to look closely is a single number: backlog of $14.06B at June 30, 2026, up 73.2% year over year, of which 57.6% of year-to-date revenue comes from technology customers — overwhelmingly data centres. This has stopped being a diversified building-services company and become, in practice, a leveraged pure play on hyperscaler capital expenditure. The central debate on this page is not whether the backlog is real (it is contracted and disclosed) but whether the market is paying P/E 39.6 for a durable structural change in a contracting business, or for the top of a capital-spending cycle that has historically ended badly for the people holding the shovels.

This is the answer to "why does this contractor earn a 25.7% gross margin when contracting is a 15–18% business?" Instead of sending electricians and pipefitters to assemble systems in the mud on a customer's site, Comfort Systems builds them as modules in its own factories — skidded chiller plants, pre-piped cooling modules, pre-wired electrical rooms — then ships and sets them. The consequences are all in the same direction: factory labour is cheaper and more productive than field labour; quality control is repeatable; weather and site congestion stop being schedule risks; and one skilled tradesman in a plant can produce several times what he could on a jobsite. Modular capacity is being expanded to over 4 million square feet by the end of 2026 and roughly 5 million by late summer 2027. The company does not disclose modular revenue or modular margin separately, so the magnitude of the mix benefit cannot be isolated from the outside — but the direction is not in doubt: gross margin went from 18.3% in FY2021 to 25.7% today while revenue tripled.

Why it matters competitively: a factory is a capital asset with lead time. A regional contractor cannot decide next quarter to have 4 million square feet of modular capacity. This is the closest thing to a moat in a fragmented trade business.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
MODULAR FACTORY CAPACITY SKILLED-LABOUR ROSTER HYPERSCALER PREQUALIFICATION SCHEDULE CERTAINTY MULTI-TRADE SELF-PERFORM BONDING & BALANCE SHEET SERIAL ACQUIRER PLAYBOOK

The moat is not technology and not brand — it is the ability to show up with enough qualified people, on a date, at a price. A hyperscaler building a $2B campus is not optimising the MEP bid to the last basis point; it is optimising for the building being energised on the promised day, because every month of delay is a month of GPUs not earning. That inverts the traditional contracting dynamic. In an ordinary commercial build, the general contractor squeezes the mechanical sub and the sub eats it. In a data-centre build with a scarce pool of qualified subs, the sub has pricing power, and the customer pays for certainty. Comfort Systems supplies certainty three ways: modular prefabrication (build it in a factory where the schedule is controllable), self-performance (do the mechanical and the electrical with its own crews rather than coordinating third parties), and a labour roster of 22,700 assembled over three decades of acquisitions. The financial evidence is the margin: gross margin 18.32% in FY2021 → 24.1% in FY2025 → 25.66% TTM, and operating margin 6.08% → 16.45% over the same span. A contractor whose operating margin nearly triples while revenue also triples is being paid for something other than volume.

One structural nuance an investor must hold onto: Comfort Systems is a percentage-of-completion contractor. Revenue is recognised as work is performed — cost incurred against total estimated cost — not when a contract is signed and not when the building opens. That has two consequences. First, backlog is a leading indicator and revenue is a lagging one: the $14.06B booked at June 30, 2026 is tomorrow's revenue, of which the company expects 65–75% to convert over the next twelve months. Second, reported profit depends on management's estimate of total contract cost. If a job's cost estimate is revised, the correction lands in the current quarter's margin, retroactively. Percentage-of-completion accounting is legitimate and universal in this industry, but it means margins carry an estimation judgement that a product company's do not.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer and end-market concentration. Technology is 57.6% of year-to-date revenue, up from roughly 40% a year ago. The buyers of data-centre capacity are a handful of hyperscalers and their developer partners. This is not diversified industrial exposure with a data-centre kicker; it is a hyperscaler-capex derivative with an industrial tail. If AI infrastructure spending is deferred rather than cancelled, backlog conversion slows and revenue stalls at exactly the moment the multiple assumes acceleration.
▸
Skilled labour is the binding constraint — not demand. The limit on how fast Comfort Systems can grow is the number of licensed electricians, pipefitters and sheet-metal workers it can recruit, train and retain. Demand is currently abundant; qualified crews are not. Scarcity is helping today (it is a principal reason pricing and therefore margin have improved) and it caps the upside tomorrow: the company cannot simply convert a bigger backlog into proportionally bigger revenue if it cannot staff it. Wage inflation is the transmission mechanism by which today's tailwind becomes tomorrow's margin pressure.
▸
Fixed-price risk on very large jobs. Large new-construction contracts concentrate estimation risk. New construction is now 74.8% of revenue versus 63.2% in FY2025, and the average job is far bigger than it was three years ago. Under percentage-of-completion, a single badly-estimated mega-project can move a quarter's margin. The company's track record here has been excellent — that is precisely why the market has stopped discounting for the risk.
▸
Acquisition integration and the same-store gap. Comfort Systems has grown by buying regional contractors since its 1997 roll-up IPO, and continues to. That is why it reports total and same-store backlog separately: at June 30, 2026 the gap was $14.06B total versus $13.70B same-store, so roughly $360M of the backlog was acquired rather than won. The disclosure is a credit to management — it lets an outsider check that growth is organic (it overwhelmingly is). The risk is the flip side: a roll-up that pays up in a hot market inherits other people's estimating culture.

Notable One-Off Items

No material non-recurring gains, impairments, restructuring charges or litigation settlements were identified in the FY2025 or Q2 2026 results releases fetched. The reported figures appear to be clean GAAP operating results, which is itself worth stating: unlike most large-cap growth stories, the earnings on this page require no add-backs to look good — the company's own non-GAAP measure (adjusted EBITDA of $600.5M in Q2 2026, 18.4% of revenue) sits close to the GAAP operating margin of 17.1%.

Three items nonetheless distort period-to-period comparison and deserve flagging. (1) The acquired-backlog wedge. Total backlog $14.06B versus same-store $13.70B means roughly $360M came in through acquisition. Immaterial to the growth story this quarter, but the distinction compounds if deal pace picks up. (2) The cash-flow spike. Q2 2026 operating cash flow of $1.14B against $252.5M a year earlier is a working-capital phenomenon — customers prepaying and progress-billing against a backlog that grew 73%. It is real cash, but it is not a repeatable run rate; it will normalise or reverse when backlog growth flattens. (3) Mix, not one-off. The 240bp gross margin expansion is a mix shift (modular, industrial, new construction) rather than a windfall, which is the bull case's strongest point — mix shifts persist in a way one-offs do not, until the mix shifts back.

On the exact five-year window, from the September 2021 month-end close of $71.32 to the September 4, 2026 close of $1,610.34, FIX returned +2,158% — a 22.6× multiple of capital, or a compound annual growth rate of roughly 86.5% before the (negligible) dividend. A dollar put into FIX in September 2021 is $22.58 today; the same dollar needed to compound at 86.5% a year for five consecutive years, which almost nothing does.

The shape matters as much as the magnitude. There was no drawdown worth the name in 2022 — the October 2022 close of $123.28 was above the December 2021 close of $98.94, while most growth equities were down 40–70%. The vertical phase began in February 2024 (+40.6% in one month, $217.47 → $305.73), when the market first connected AI data-centre construction to the contractors who build it, and accelerated through 2025 as the technology end market went from roughly 40% of revenue toward 57.6%. The only meaningful interruption was January–March 2025 (−26.2%, $436.75 → $322.33), an AI-capex confidence scare rather than a company event — the stock made it all back and more by July.

What is happening right now is more interesting than the run. The stock peaked on a monthly-close basis at $1,981.95 in June 2026 (52-week intraday high $2,073.99), then fell 12.7% in July and 10.6% in August — through and after a Q2 print that beat EPS consensus by 20.7% and grew backlog 73%. That is the market de-rating the multiple, not the earnings: monthly RSI has rolled over from deeply overbought without any deterioration in the reported numbers. The bear reading is that the market prices cyclical peaks before they appear in results. The bull reading is that a 39.6× trailing multiple on 46% revenue growth and 77% ROIC is not obviously a bubble.

Relative performance. Over the trailing twelve months FIX is +129.82%. Comparable same-window total returns for the S&P 500 (the benchmark index for this NYSE listing) and the NASDAQ-100 were not verified from the sources fetched and are therefore not stated — but no broad US index returned anything close to 130% in that period, so the relative outperformance is not in question even if the precise gap is not computed here. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the backlog, because everything else is downstream of it. At June 30, 2026 Comfort Systems held $14.06B of total backlog, up 73.2% from $8.12B a year earlier, and $13.70B on a same-store basis — meaning roughly 97.4% of the growth was won, not bought. For scale: the backlog alone is 1.25× the entire trailing-twelve-month revenue of $11.23B, and it has more than doubled from $5.99B at December 2024 in eighteen months. The company expects 65–75% of it to convert to revenue within twelve months. That conversion range is the single most useful forward number on this page: applied to $14.06B it implies roughly $9.1–10.5B of revenue from existing backlog alone over the coming year, before a single new order is booked.

Why backlog leads and revenue lags — and why that distinction is the whole game here. Comfort Systems accounts for its contracts on a percentage-of-completion basis: as costs are incurred against the total estimated cost of a job, a proportionate share of the contract value is recognised as revenue. Signing a $400M data-centre contract therefore produces zero revenue on the day it is signed; it produces backlog. Revenue appears over the following four to eight quarters as crews and modules are deployed. The practical consequences are three. (i) Backlog turns before revenue does, in both directions — a booking slowdown shows up in backlog two to three quarters before it shows up in the income statement, which is why the market reacts to the backlog line and not the revenue line. (ii) Revenue growth of 46.1% is describing bookings that were made largely in 2025; the stock is being priced off bookings being made now. (iii) Reported margin embeds a management estimate of total job cost, so a re-estimate corrects the current quarter retroactively. None of this is exotic — it is standard construction accounting — but an investor who reads the revenue line as the news is reading a report on the past.

The margin expansion is the second act, and it is genuinely unusual. Mechanical contracting is a commodity trade with commodity economics: bid the job, win on price, earn a thin spread. Comfort Systems' gross margin has gone 18.32% (FY2021) → 21.0% (FY2024) → 24.1% (FY2025) → 25.66% (TTM), with Q2 2026 at 25.9%, up 240 basis points year over year. Operating margin went from 6.08% to 16.45% and net margin from 4.66% to 12.78% over the same period. Three mechanisms explain it, and they are all mix or scarcity rather than magic. Modular prefabrication: systems assembled in the company's own factories (capacity heading past 4 million square feet by year-end 2026) rather than in the field, where labour is more productive, quality repeatable and weather irrelevant. Mix shift to industrial and new construction: technology plus manufacturing is now 75.1% of revenue and new construction 74.8%, both structurally better-priced than commercial tenant work. Labour scarcity as pricing power: when there are more data-centre projects than there are qualified MEP contractors to staff them, the contractor sets the price. That last mechanism is the one to watch, because it is a market condition, not a capability.

The labour constraint is the real ceiling. It is worth being blunt: demand is not what limits this company — supply of skilled trades is. A journeyman electrician or pipefitter takes four to five years of apprenticeship to produce; the US pipeline has been under-filled for two decades while the retirement wave accelerated. Comfort Systems employs 22,700 people and its ability to convert $14.06B of backlog into revenue is essentially a function of how many of them it can put on jobsites and in module factories. This cuts both ways with unusual sharpness. Scarcity is why margins expanded — you cannot bid a job below cost if the alternative bidder cannot staff it either. And scarcity is why the growth rate has a hard ceiling — a backlog growing 73% cannot convert into revenue growing 73% indefinitely if headcount grows at 10–15%. Modular capacity is management's direct answer to this: a module factory is, precisely, a way to get more output per tradesman. Watch employee count against backlog conversion; that ratio is the operational tell.

Valuation, stated three ways. (i) On trailing GAAP earnings of $40.64, FIX trades at 39.6× — expensive against EMCOR's 23.5× and IES Holdings' 28.7×, cheap against Quanta's 71.5×. (ii) On consensus FY2026 EPS of $49.01 (+69.7%), it trades at 32.9×; the reported forward multiple of 30.27× implies a slightly later earnings base. (iii) On trailing free cash flow of $2.16B against a $55.04B enterprise value, it trades at roughly 25× EV/FCF — but the Q2 cash-flow surge ($999.3M in a quarter versus $222.2M) is working capital released by a booming backlog and will not repeat at that rate. The PEG of 0.67 is the bull's headline number and it is also the most fragile, because its denominator is a forecast growth rate that depends on other companies' capital budgets. The honest framing: at 39.6× trailing with 76.7% ROIC, a net-cash balance sheet, 46% revenue growth and a backlog covering 1.25 years of revenue, this is not a bubble multiple for what has been delivered. It is a multiple that requires the delivered growth to continue, and the entity deciding whether it continues is not Comfort Systems.

Bull Case

Backlog of $14.06B (+73.2%, and 97.4% of the growth same-store) covers 1.25 years of revenue with 65–75% converting within twelve months, so the next four quarters are already largely contracted. Gross margin has expanded 730 basis points since FY2021 on a structural mix shift toward modular prefabrication, industrial end markets and new construction — not on a windfall — and modular capacity heading past 4 million square feet extends that shift. ROIC of 76.7% on a net-cash balance sheet ($1.91B cash, $369.9M debt) means growth is self-funded and acquisitions are free options. At 39.6× trailing and a 0.67 PEG, with the shares 22.4% below the 52-week high and every fetched analyst target at least 18.6% higher, the de-rating since June has already priced in a slowdown that has not appeared in any reported number.

Bear Case

57.6% of year-to-date revenue comes from technology customers and 74.8% from new construction: this is a leveraged derivative on hyperscaler capex with a beta of 1.68, and it fell 22.4% from its June high on no company-specific news — which is what a cyclical peak looks like from the inside. Percentage-of-completion accounting means today's margins embed management cost estimates on the largest, most complex jobs the company has ever taken. The 25.7% gross margin is partly a scarcity rent that competes away when EMCOR, Quanta, IES and every private regional contractor expand capacity into the same demand. And the growth ceiling is not demand but licensed tradesmen, who take five years to make. Pay 39.6× trailing for a contractor at a cyclical high in its dominant end market and the downside is not a de-rating to 30× — it is a de-rating on lower earnings.

Top 3 Risks

01
Hyperscaler capex concentration. With technology at 57.6% of revenue (from ~40% a year ago) and backlog growth of 73% driven by the same customers, a deferral — not even a cancellation — of AI data-centre programmes would slow backlog conversion first and revenue second. The stock's July–August 2026 decline through a record quarter suggests the market is already handicapping this. FIX does not control the variable that determines its outcome.
02
Skilled-labour scarcity — the constraint that is also the moat. Growth is capped by electricians and pipefitters, not by orders. If wage inflation accelerates faster than the company can reprice fixed-price contracts already in backlog, the 25.7% gross margin compresses on work already sold. Conversely, if the labour shortage eases because demand cools, the pricing power that produced the margin expansion eases with it. There is no version of this risk that is comfortable.
03
Percentage-of-completion estimation risk on record-size jobs. New construction is 74.8% of revenue and average project size has grown enormously. Under percentage-of-completion, a single re-estimate of total cost on a large data-centre contract flows through the current quarter's margin retroactively. The company's execution record has been excellent — which means the market is currently applying no discount for a risk that is structurally present and would be a genuine surprise if it materialised.
  Industry Commentary
Skilled Trades Are the Sector's Hard Ceiling
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every MEP contractor in America is bidding into the same shortage of licensed electricians, pipefitters and sheet-metal workers, and no amount of capital fixes it inside five years — that is the apprenticeship cycle. The visible symptom is margin: Comfort Systems' gross margin rose from 18.32% in FY2021 to 25.66% today, and EMCOR, Quanta and IES Holdings have all seen the same directional expansion. Scarcity rent is real income, but it is rent, not productivity, and it is the first thing to disappear if demand cools. The genuinely structural response is to move labour off the jobsite and into a factory, which is exactly what modular prefabrication does — and why capacity heading past 4 million square feet by year-end 2026 is a competitive statement, not a logistics detail.
The Whole Complex Now Trades as One AI Derivative
ONGOING HEADWIND
Twelve months ago these were four different businesses. Today FIX (+129.8% on the year, $56.58B), Quanta (market cap +68.3%, $93.88B, $53B backlog), IES Holdings (market cap +89.0%, $12.85B) and EMCOR ($33.27B, 23.5× trailing) move on the same headlines about cloud capital-expenditure budgets. The diversification benefit an investor might expect from owning "industrials" has largely evaporated within this group. The headwind is not competitive — all four have more work than they can staff — it is correlation: when the AI-capex narrative wobbles, as it did in Q1 2025 and again through July and August 2026, everything in the complex de-rates together regardless of individual execution. FIX carries the highest multiple of the three peers on trailing earnings after Quanta, and the highest beta exposure to the theme.
Modular Construction Is the Real Industrial Shift
EMERGING TAILWIND
Construction is one of the few large industries where measured productivity has barely moved in fifty years, because the work happens once, outdoors, in a unique configuration. Prefabrication attacks that directly: build the chiller plant, the electrical room and the cooling module in a factory, ship them, set them. Data centres are the ideal application because they are highly repeatable — the same hall, replicated across a campus and across the country — which is why the shift is happening here first. Bull framing: if modular is the reason a contractor can hold a 25.7% gross margin, the re-rating of FIX from a 15× building-services multiple to 39.6× is a permanent recognition that this is a manufacturing business wearing a contractor's uniform. Bear framing: factories can be built by competitors too, and the company does not disclose modular revenue or modular margin — so the most important claim in the bull case is precisely the one an outside investor cannot verify.
