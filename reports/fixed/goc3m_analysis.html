01 Overview

A Government of Canada treasury bill is a short loan to the federal government in Canadian dollars, with no interest payments. Finance Canada: "Treasury bills are priced at a discount. The return to the investor is the difference between the purchase price and the par value." At the October 20, 2026 auction buyers paid on average $993.26 for $1,000 due January 27, 2027.

The header yield, 2.54%, is the Bank of Canada's 3-month mid-market yield, "an average of sample secondary market yields taken throughout the business day." It was 2.39% at the end of September and 2.22% a year earlier.

One yield, on the price paid

Canadian bills quote a single yield. Finance Canada: "The rate of return is calculated by dividing this difference by the purchase price and expressing the result as an annual percentage rate, using a 365-day year." On the U.S. discount-rate basis (face value, 360-day year) the October 20 average price would read 2.478%, not 2.529% (our calculation). Non-competitive bids are filled at the average yield; competitive bids are accepted "in rising order of yield" (Standard Terms).

08 Summary & Key Risks

When it does well

When the policy rate holds or rises: each new bill is bought at the latest rate, and a 1-point rise costs only about 0.27% of price.

When it does poorly

When rates fall: each auction pays less, with no long duration to gain from lower yields. With inflation above the yield (3.1% vs 2.54%), the real return is negative before tax.

Top 3 Risks

01
Lower rates on reinvestment. The yield is locked for only about three months; the 2024–25 cuts took 2.56 pp off the month-end yield in seventeen months.
02
Inflation. The October MPR sees CPI averaging 2.9% in 2026, above today's 2.54% yield.
03
Currency. Paid in Canadian dollars; for other-currency holders, exchange-rate moves add to or subtract from the yield.
