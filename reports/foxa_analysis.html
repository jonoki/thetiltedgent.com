01 Company Overview

Fox Corporation is what was left on the table when The Walt Disney Company bought 21st Century Fox's film studio, TV production and cable entertainment businesses. The Murdochs kept the live stuff — FOX News, FOX Sports, the FOX broadcast network and its owned stations — and the new company began trading on March 19, 2019, going straight into the S&P 500. It has two listed share classes: Class A (FOXA), which has no vote and is the line this report is priced on, and Class B (FOX), which carries the votes. On October 28, 2026 there were 201,552,650 Class A and 220,248,764 Class B shares outstanding (cover of the Q1 FY2027 10-Q). Both classes are in the S&P 500; neither is in the NASDAQ-100.

For seven years the story was simple: a cash machine built on live news and live sports, the two things people still watch as they happen. It is paid for by distribution fees from pay-TV bundles and by advertising, and the cash goes back to shareholders mostly through buybacks. FY2026 (the year to June 30, 2026) revenue was $17.13B, up 5%, and adjusted EBITDA was $3.91B, up 8%, capped by the FIFA Men's World Cup in the June quarter. The first quarter of FY2027 (July to September 2026) set first-quarter records: revenue $4.57B, up 22%, and adjusted EBITDA $1.64B, up 54%, as the World Cup broadcast, more political advertising and Tubi lifted advertising revenue 47%. Then there is the deal. On June 14, 2026, Fox signed to buy Roku for about $22B in enterprise value (the value of its shares plus debt, minus cash), paying $96.00 in cash plus 0.9693 FOXA shares for each Roku share. That turns a story about milking a mature business for cash into a bet on remaking it. Roku's shareholders would own about 27% of the combined company. Both companies' shareholders approved it in October; it still needs US antitrust clearance. Fox has $11.0B of bridge-loan commitments available and a committed $1.0B term loan (10-Q), and expects pro forma net leverage (net debt as a multiple of EBITDA, as if the deal had already closed) of about 2.8x. The question for FOXA is no longer just how long the bundle lasts. It's whether owning the TV operating system that more than 100 million streaming households start from is worth giving up a clean balance sheet — and whether the Justice Department lets it happen.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Live news audience Live sports rights 29 owned stations Carriage-fee bargaining power Tubi scale (13B+ hours) Controlled share structure

Fox's edge is that it owns programming nobody wants to watch later. News and sports are the last shows that reliably gather a big audience at the same moment. Advertisers pay a premium for that, and pay-TV distributors can't afford to drop it. That's why Cable Network Programming earns a 42% segment margin inside a shrinking bundle. The weak spot is that the bundle is shrinking. Distribution fees are paid per subscriber, and Fox has never owned the screen its audience watches on. The Roku deal is Fox trying to buy that screen.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Cord-cutting eats the base. Distribution was $8.06B, 47% of FY2026 revenue. Fee increases have kept ahead of subscriber losses, and in the September 2026 quarter distribution revenue still grew 9%, but that can't go on forever. FOX One also shifts some viewers from a bundle fee to a direct subscription that Fox has to win one household at a time.
02
Sports rights keep getting pricier. In Q2 FY2026 (the December quarter), adjusted EBITDA fell to $692M from $781M, which Fox put down mainly to higher sports-rights amortization and production costs and higher digital marketing costs. In the September 2026 quarter the CFO said expenses rose 10% at Cable and 8% at Television, again mostly on sports rights and production. Live sports hold the audience, but they don't come cheap.
03
The Roku deal changes the balance sheet. Fox had $4.54B of cash and $6.61B of borrowings on September 30, 2026. The bridge financing and ~2.8x pro forma net leverage (already counting half of synergies it hasn't achieved yet) trade a clean balance sheet for execution risk. The DOJ issued a second request, a demand for more documents and data that extends its review, on September 8, 2026, and has not yet cleared the deal.
04
Governance is a family matter. Class A holders have no vote. Court records reported by Reuters in August 2026 showed Rupert Murdoch had explored re-merging Fox with News Corp in 2022; a lawyer for Lachlan Murdoch argued the material should stay sealed because a merger was "something that still could happen in the future." Both companies declined to comment.

Notable One-Off Items

In the September 2026 quarter GAAP diluted EPS was $2.20 and adjusted EPS $2.54. The gap is mostly a $143M "non-operating other, net" charge ($0.33 a share; $125M a year earlier) and $17M of restructuring, impairment and other corporate matters, less $18M of related tax. The fiscal year before tells the same story on a bigger scale. GAAP EPS fell from $4.91 to $3.84 in FY2026 while adjusted EPS rose from $4.78 to $5.42. The reconciliation explains the gap. FY2026 had a $773M "non-operating other, net" charge, where FY2025 had a $438M benefit on the same line. It also had $151M of restructuring, impairment and other corporate matters (FY2025: $350M), partly offset by a $229M tax adjustment. The release doesn't break out what's in the non-operating line. The other one-off is the sports calendar: the June 2026 quarter had the FIFA Men's World Cup (quarterly advertising of $1.92B vs $1.08B), and Fox credits the World Cup first for the September quarter too (advertising $2.08B vs $1.41B), while FY2026 as a whole had no Super Bowl and less political advertising. Neither event comes around every year, so read year-over-year comparisons with that in mind.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 11.1x forward earnings and 10.4x the last four quarters' adjusted EPS for FOX News, a sports portfolio, and a free streamer that clocked 13 billion viewing hours last year and grew revenue 35% last quarter. Cable Network Programming earns a 42% segment margin, the company generated $2.21B of free cash flow over the last twelve months, and it has cut its share count 5.78% in a year. The first quarter of FY2027 was a record, and both shareholder votes on Roku have passed. If Roku closes, Fox owns the home screen that more than 100 million households start from, gets about $400M of cost synergies, and can sell ads against its own content and everyone else's. The highest target is $125; the average implies 19.0% upside.

Bear Case

Take out the World Cup and the election and the last two quarters look much less special: sports costs keep rising, and FY2026 had a falling December-quarter EBITDA. Consensus sees revenue down about 1% and adjusted EPS down 3.9% in the year to June 2028 (StockAnalysis). The core earns carriage fees from a bundle that loses subscribers every year. Management's answer is to borrow up to $12.0B, give Roku holders about a quarter of the company and pay $22B for a platform, taking pro forma leverage to ~2.8x even after counting half of synergies it hasn't delivered. The DOJ still has to clear it, a failed deal can cost Fox a fee of about $1.2B, 14.7% of the float is sold short, and Class A holders have no vote on any of it.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deal execution and regulation. Shareholders approved on October 14, 2026; the DOJ second request (September 8, 2026) keeps the antitrust review open. A reported consent decree would likely come with conditions. A block costs Fox time, its deal expenses and, in certain circumstances, a fee of about $1.2B to Roku; a close hands it a $22B integration at ~2.8x leverage.
02
Cord-cutting against distribution revenue. $8.06B, 47% of revenue, depends on pay-TV bundles and retransmission fees. So far fee increases have outrun subscriber losses. When they stop doing that, the highest-margin segment stops growing.

Sports-rights costs and event timing. Rights costs pulled the December 2025 quarter's adjusted EBITDA down 11% ($692M vs $781M), and they drove most of the expense growth again in the September 2026 quarter. Big events like the World Cup and the Super Bowl come and go, so year-on-year numbers will be noisy. The underlying cost trend is the one to watch.
Industry Commentary
The bundle is shrinking; live is what's left
STRUCTURAL HEADWIND

Every traditional TV company is managing the same decline: fewer pay-TV homes, each paying more. Fox is better placed than most because its scripted entertainment and film businesses went to Disney in 2019 and it kept news and sports, the programming people still watch live. The segment margins show it. But the money still flows through the same shrinking pipe, and FOX One (a direct subscription) and Tubi (free, ad-supported) are Fox's own hedges against it.

Owning the front door
DEAL RISK

The Roku bid is a bet that in streaming, the money goes to whoever controls discovery: the home screen, the remote, the ad data. The DOJ's questions are the flip side of that bet. Competitors worry that a content owner controlling the screen will favour its own services, and Roku's free, ad-supported channel competes with Tubi. A consent decree, if that is how the review ends, would write rules for how the two businesses deal with rivals. The tighter those rules, the less of the strategic value is left; the looser they are, the more the competitors' complaints matter.

Valuation — two ways to read it
OPEN QUESTION

The bull reading: 11.1x forward earnings and ~7.1x EV/EBITDA (enterprise value over EBITDA; StockAnalysis) is cheap for a company with 42% margins in its core and a streaming option attached, and the market is over-discounting the deal. The bear reading: the multiple is low because nobody knows what the core is worth in ten years, and the deal swaps a simple buyback story for a leveraged integration. The DOJ outcome and the first quarters without a World Cup will settle more of this than any multiple will.
