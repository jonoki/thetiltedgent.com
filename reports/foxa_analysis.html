01 Company Overview

Fox Corporation is what was left on the table when The Walt Disney Company bought 21st Century Fox's film studio, TV production and cable entertainment businesses. The Murdochs kept the live stuff — FOX News, FOX Sports, the FOX broadcast network and its owned stations — and the new company began trading on March 19, 2019, going straight into the S&P 500. It has two listed share classes: Class A (FOXA), which has no vote and is the line this report is priced on, and Class B (FOX), which carries the votes. On August 4, 2026 there were 199,401,385 Class A and 220,248,764 Class B shares outstanding. Both classes are in the S&P 500; neither is in the NASDAQ-100.

For seven years the story was simple: a cash machine built on live news and live sports, the two things people still watch as they happen. It is paid for by distribution fees from pay-TV bundles and by advertising, and the cash goes back to shareholders mostly through buybacks. FY2026 (the year to June 30, 2026) revenue was $17.13B, up 5%, and adjusted EBITDA was $3.91B, up 8%, capped by the FIFA Men's World Cup in the June quarter. Then, on June 14, 2026, Fox signed a deal to buy Roku for about $22B in enterprise value, paying $96.00 in cash plus 0.9693 FOXA shares for each Roku share. That turns a harvest story into a transformation bet. Roku's shareholders would own about 27% of the combined company. Fox has a $12.0B bridge loan lined up and expects pro forma net leverage of about 2.8x. The question for FOXA is no longer just how long the bundle lasts. It's whether owning the TV operating system that more than 100 million streaming households start from is worth giving up a clean balance sheet — and whether the Justice Department lets it happen.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Live news audience Live sports rights 29 owned stations Carriage-fee bargaining power Tubi scale (13B+ hours) Controlled share structure

Fox's edge is that it owns programming nobody wants to watch later. News and sports are the last shows that reliably gather a big audience at the same moment. Advertisers pay a premium for that, and pay-TV distributors can't afford to drop it. That's why Cable Network Programming earns a 42% segment margin inside a shrinking bundle. The weak spot is that the bundle is shrinking. Distribution fees are paid per subscriber, and Fox has never owned the screen its audience watches on. The Roku deal is Fox trying to buy that screen.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Cord-cutting eats the base. Distribution was $8.06B, 47% of FY2026 revenue. Fee increases have kept ahead of subscriber losses, but that can't go on forever. FOX One also shifts some viewers from a bundle fee to a direct subscription that Fox has to win one household at a time.
02
Sports rights keep getting pricier. In Q2 FY2026 (the December quarter), adjusted EBITDA fell to $692M from $781M on "higher sports programming rights amortization and production costs, and higher digital marketing costs." Live sports hold the audience, but they don't come cheap.
03
The Roku deal changes the balance sheet. Fox ended FY2026 with $4.21B of cash and $6.61B of borrowings. The $12.0B bridge and ~2.8x pro forma net leverage (already counting half of synergies it hasn't achieved yet) trade a clean balance sheet for execution risk. The DOJ issued a second request on September 8, 2026.
04
Governance is a family matter. Class A holders have no vote. Court records made public in August 2026 showed Rupert Murdoch had explored re-merging Fox with News Corp in 2022; a lawyer for Lachlan Murdoch argued the material should stay sealed because a merger was "something that still could happen in the future." Both companies declined to comment.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $40.11 to the September 18, 2026 close of $64.45, FOXA gained 60.7% on price, before dividends. The route mattered more than the endpoints. For two years it went nowhere: every month-end from April 2022 to April 2024 closed between $28.87 and $35.84, as the market priced it like any cable company facing cord-cutting. Then, from June 2024 to December 2025, it more than doubled, from $34.37 to $73.07. 2026 has been the volatile year.

The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 95.0 in February 2025 and was above 80 in seven of 2025's twelve months. Then it broke: 40.4 in February 2026 and 35.0 in June 2026, the lowest reading since November 2023. It's now back at 55.2, which is neutral. The 10-month moving average is about $63.02, just below the price, so the long-run trend is flat rather than falling. The stock now trades on the Roku deal, not on the old cash-cow model.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 10.9x forward earnings and 11.9x FY2026 adjusted EPS for FOX News, a sports portfolio, and a free streamer that clocked 13 billion viewing hours last year. Cable Network Programming earns a 42% segment margin, the company generated $1.47B of free cash flow, and it has cut its share count 4.77% in a year. If Roku closes, Fox owns the home screen that more than 100 million households start from, gets about $400M of cost synergies, and can sell ads against its own content and everyone else's. The highest target is $112; the average implies 18.6% upside.

Bear Case

Take out the World Cup and FY2026 was a year of rising sports costs and a falling December-quarter EBITDA. Consensus sees revenue flat in the year to June 2028. The core earns carriage fees from a bundle that loses subscribers every year. Management's answer is to borrow up to $12.0B, give Roku holders about a quarter of the company and pay $22B for a platform, taking pro forma leverage to ~2.8x even after counting half of synergies it hasn't delivered. The DOJ has questions, 13.8% of the float is sold short, and Class A holders have no vote on any of it.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deal execution and regulation. The DOJ second request (September 8, 2026) extends the review, and both sets of shareholders still have to approve. A block costs Fox a year and its deal expenses; a close hands it a $22B integration at ~2.8x leverage.
02
Cord-cutting against distribution revenue. $8.06B, 47% of revenue, depends on pay-TV bundles and retransmission fees. So far fee increases have outrun subscriber losses. When they stop doing that, the highest-margin segment stops growing.
03
Sports-rights costs and event timing. Rights costs already pulled December-quarter adjusted EBITDA down 11% ($692M vs $781M). Big events like the World Cup and the Super Bowl come and go, so year-on-year numbers will be noisy. The underlying cost trend is the one to watch.
Industry Commentary
The bundle is shrinking; live is what's left
STRUCTURAL HEADWIND

Every traditional TV company is managing the same decline: fewer pay-TV homes, each paying more. Fox is better placed than most because its scripted entertainment and film businesses went to Disney in 2019 and it kept news and sports, the programming people still watch live. The segment margins show it. But the money still flows through the same shrinking pipe, and FOX One (a direct subscription) and Tubi (free, ad-supported) are Fox's own hedges against it.

Owning the front door
DEAL RISK

The Roku bid is a bet that in streaming, the money goes to whoever controls discovery: the home screen, the remote, the ad data. The DOJ's questions are the flip side of that bet. Competitors worry that a content owner controlling the screen will favour its own services. Lachlan Murdoch says he expects the two businesses to "operate separately." If they really do, some of the strategic value disappears; if they don't, the regulatory risk goes up.

Valuation — two ways to read it
OPEN QUESTION

The bull reading: 10.9x forward earnings and ~7.9x EV/EBITDA (StockAnalysis) is cheap for a company with 42% margins in its core and a streaming option attached, and the market is over-discounting the deal. The bear reading: the multiple is low because nobody knows what the core is worth in ten years, and the deal swaps a simple buyback story for a leveraged integration. The DOJ outcome and the first post-World Cup quarter on October 29 will settle more of this than any multiple will.
