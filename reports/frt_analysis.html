01 Company Overview

Federal Realty is the landlord you visit without noticing: open-air shopping centers and mixed-use neighbourhoods — Santana Row in San Jose, Pike & Rose in North Bethesda, Assembly Square in Somerville, Massachusetts — located, in the company's words, "primarily in major coastal markets and select underserved regions." The company's Q3 2026 release describes 102 properties with about 3,700 tenants in 28.8 million square feet of commercial space, plus roughly 2,700 residential units. Q3 revenue was $339.0M, up 5.2%; trailing-twelve-month revenue is about $1.35B (our calculation: FY2025 $1,279.0M − nine months 2025 $942.9M + nine months 2026 $1,015.8M).

For a REIT, the income statement's bottom line is the wrong scoreboard. GAAP earnings swing with depreciation and with gains on property sales; FFO strips both out. On that measure the story is steady: Core FFO of $7.06 per share in 2025 (+4.3%), $1.87 in Q3 2026 (+5.6%), and 2026 guidance raised for the third time this year, to $7.54–$7.58 from $7.48–$7.56 (it started the year at $7.42–$7.52), or 6.8%–7.4% growth over 2025 (company table). At the $112.58 close the stock trades at 15.1x trailing FFO and 14.9x the 2026 guidance midpoint — the same shares show 22.5x on GAAP earnings.

Then there is the streak. In the Q2 2026 release the board raised the quarterly dividend 3% to $1.16 (the Q3 release declares the same $1.16, payable January 15, 2027), and the company stated: "This increase represents the 59th consecutive year that Federal Realty has increased its common dividend, the longest record of consecutive annual dividend increases in the REIT sector." (The February 2026 release counted 58 years, so the count ticks up once a year as expected.) The poker way to read a 59-year streak: it is not a promise, it is a very expensive reputation that management will do almost anything to protect — which is both the comfort and the constraint. The market spent the first seven months of 2026 paying up for the story (the stock rose from $100.80 at year-end to a $124.09 July close) and by the November 6 close had given back 9.3%. Q3 results on October 30 did not change that: the stock fell 1.12% that day.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Infill coastal locations Mixed-use destinations Land already owned for adding apartments ~3,500 entitled / pending resi units $1.4B credit line (undrawn at Sep 30) 59-year dividend record

The moat is dirt. Retail land in dense, high-income suburbs is hard to replicate because zoning, not capital, is the constraint — nobody gets permission to build a new Santana Row down the road. That scarcity shows up in the numbers the company reports: new leases at cash rents 14% above the old ones in Q3 2026, after a record leasing quarter at 15% in Q2, mean tenants are paying up to get in. The second layer is room to build: a landlord that already owns the parking lot can add apartments at roughly a 7% yield on cost without paying for land again. The vulnerability is the one every REIT shares — the rent arrives over many years, so the share price depends as much on interest rates and on the return investors demand as on leasing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage is real. Total debt of $4.88B at September 30 (Q3 release), about 5.75x trailing EBITDA (our calculation, on StockAnalysis's $850M of EBITDA) and debt-to-equity of 1.42 (StockAnalysis) — before the $508.0M Summit purchase closed on October 1. In August 2026 the company issued $460.0M of 3.50% exchangeable senior notes due 2031, bonds that holders can swap for shares (initially at about $138.54 a share; net proceeds about $451M, Q3 release). The interest rate is low, but a swap would mean new shares; the company also paid $21.7M for a capped call, a hedge that offsets that dilution up to about $165.07 a share, and on November 2 it registered up to 3,901,260 shares issuable on exchange (8-K). As older debt comes due and is replaced at current rates, higher costs start to show up in FFO.
02
Shares issued high, bought back lower. In the second quarter the company sold 493,374 shares through its at-the-market program (small sales at the going price) for $61.1M gross at an average $123.92 (Q2 release; the Q3 release shows the same nine-month total). It then repurchased 869,748 shares at a weighted average $108.82 through October 9. Selling near $124 was well-timed; the risk is that growth still needs outside capital, and per-share FFO growth is what the dividend streak rides on.
03
The streak is a constraint as well as a signal. The indicated $4.64 dividend is ~62% of trailing Core FFO — comfortably covered — but a 59-year record means management will protect the raise even in a year when retaining cash would be the better economic choice.
04
Development and geographic drift. The resi pipeline's ~7% yield is a company estimate on projects mostly delivering 2027–2028, and recent acquisitions in Omaha, Kansas City and Birmingham (The Summit, $508.0M) take the portfolio away from the coastal markets that justify the higher price investors pay for it.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items make headline numbers misleading. First, GAAP EPS vs FFO: GAAP profit swings with property sales, which FFO removes. Q2 2026 EPS was $0.97 against $1.78 a year earlier while Nareit FFO was $1.88 against $1.91; Q3 2026 EPS was $0.74 against $0.69 and Nareit FFO $1.87 against $1.77. Second, Nareit vs Core FFO in 2025: the year's Nareit FFO of $7.22 exceeded Core FFO of $7.06, and Q2 2025 alone included "a one-time $13.0 million, or $0.15 per share, of new market tax credit transaction income". That is why nine-month 2026 Nareit FFO rose 4.5% ($5.62 against $5.38) while Core FFO rose 7.5% ($5.62 against $5.23). In Q3 2026 the two were equal at $1.87.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 14.9x this year's guided Core FFO for a landlord that grew Core FFO 5.6% in Q3 on 14% cash spreads, raised guidance for the third time this year, and has a 96.5% leased rate with a gap of about 2.7 percentage points between leased and occupied space still to convert into rent. A 4.12% yield covered at ~62% of Core FFO, a 59-year raise record, a $1.4B bank credit line (undrawn at September 30, the day before the Summit purchase), and a ~$400M apartment pipeline at ~7% yields on land it already owns give it a way to grow per-share FFO without depending on acquisitions. The pullback has left the stock 12.2% below its high while every analyst target on the board stays above the price.

Bear Case

The good news is already known and was priced in July, when RSI hit 83 and the stock closed at $124.09, about 10% above the November 6 price. Debt of $4.88B at about 5.75x EBITDA (before the $508.0M Summit purchase) will be refinanced at whatever rates prevail, and refinancing at higher rates dilutes FFO growth; the August exchangeable notes could add new shares above $138.54. Shares issued near $124 in Q2 were followed by buybacks near $109, the development yields are estimates on 2027–2028 deliveries, and the push into Omaha, Kansas City and Birmingham dilutes the coastal-scarcity story that justifies trading at a premium to Kimco. And a 59-year dividend streak means management cannot easily choose to retain cash if conditions turn.

Top 3 Risks

01
Interest rates and refinancing. Total debt $4.88B, debt/EBITDA about 5.75x, and loans that reset to current rates as they come due. Higher refinancing costs come straight out of FFO per share, and higher bond yields lower the price investors will pay for each dollar of REIT income.
02
Execution on development and property sales. The ~7% blended yield on $385–411M of resi-over-retail projects and the gap of 1.5 to 2 percentage points between the cap rates it sells and buys at are management targets. Cost overruns, slower lease-up or a narrowing spread would slow the growth the current multiple assumes.
03
Tenant health. Occupancy of 93.8% and a small-shop leased rate of 94.1% are strong, but small-shop tenants are the most vulnerable to a consumer slowdown, and the 14% rent spreads depend on retailers' willingness to pay up.
Industry Commentary
Open-air retail: the landlord's market
EMERGING TAILWIND

Federal Realty's own numbers describe a market where tenants compete for space: a record 2.5 million square feet leased in 2025, an all-time quarterly volume record in Q2 2026, and cash re-leasing spreads of 15% in both periods and 14% in Q3 2026. CFO Dan Guglielmone told the BofA conference the core portfolio should produce "3% to 4%" same-store NOI growth (net operating income from buildings owned in both years), translating into "contribution to FFO growth of 4% to 5%" (Investing.com transcript, Sep 16, 2026). Peer valuation data show where the market ranks it: FRT at 15.1x FFO beside Regency at ≈15.5x and Kimco at ≈14.0x.

Cost of capital sets the price
ONGOING HEADWIND

A REIT pays out most of its earnings, so growth needs outside capital — debt, new shares, or the proceeds of selling older buildings. Federal Realty uses all three: a $1.4B bank credit line priced at SOFR plus 0.725 percentage points, $61.1M of shares sold at $123.92 in Q2, $460.0M of exchangeable notes, $94.7M of buybacks near $109 and ongoing dispositions. When the share price is high, issuing equity to buy or build at a 7% yield adds to per-share FFO; when it falls, the same maths gets harder. That feedback loop is why REIT prices move with interest rates even when leasing is strong.

Apartments on land it already owns
EMERGING TAILWIND

The resi-over-retail pitch turns a parking lot into an apartment building: land already owned, zoning slowly won, about 3,500 further units entitled or near approval. The bull framing: this is a decade of internal growth that competitors without mixed-use expertise cannot copy. The bear framing: it turns a retail landlord into a part-time developer, with construction risk and multi-year capital lock-up, just as refinancing costs rise. The test is simple and reported quarterly: delivered projects, on budget, at the ~7% yield promised.
