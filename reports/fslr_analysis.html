01 Company Overview

First Solar is a US-headquartered maker of utility-scale solar panels, and it builds them differently from its import competition. Instead of crystalline-silicon wafers, First Solar deposits a thin film of cadmium telluride (CdTe) onto glass in a single, continuous factory process. It sells utility-scale modules to developers, independent power producers and utilities; 96% of 2025 net sales came from the United States. FY2025 revenue was $5.22B (FY2024: $4.2B), GAAP diluted EPS $14.21, on a record 17.5 GW of modules sold.

Here is the hand the market is actually playing. In 2025 First Solar recognised $1.6B of Section 45X manufacturing tax credits — a federal payment for every watt of solar hardware made and sold in America, created by the Inflation Reduction Act. Its operating income that year was $1.60B. Put the two side by side and the uncomfortable arithmetic is that, before the credit, the business earned roughly nothing at the operating line; the CFO put 2025 gross margin excluding 45X at about 7% against 41% reported. For 2026 management guides $2.10–$2.19B of credits against $2.4–$2.6B of gross profit. So the stock at $199.84 and 12.3x trailing earnings is really a price on two things: how long Washington keeps paying, and whether tariffs on imported panels let First Solar charge enough to stand on its own when it stops. The share price has swung from a $306.79 May 2026 month-end (all-time closing high $318.25 on June 3) back below $200 in under four months without the income statement getting worse — it has been getting better. That gap between the P&L and the tape is the whole report.

Statutory step-down
100% → 0% by 2033
Credit value falls to 75% in 2030, 50% in 2031, 25% in 2032 and nothing from 2033 (26 U.S.C. §45X(b)(3); Crux 45X guide). From January 1, 2026 the One Big Beautiful Bill Act also layers on prohibited-foreign-entity sourcing thresholds. The backlog runs "through 2030" — the first step-down year.

Illustrative math (estimate, not guidance): credit-years left from 2026 = 4 full years (2026–2029) + 0.75 + 0.50 + 0.25 = 5.5. At the 2026 guide midpoint of $2.145B a year and flat qualifying volume, that is ≈ 5.5 × $2.145B ≈ $11.8B undiscounted — against an enterprise value of $19.94B. Roughly 60% of what the market pays for the whole company could be accounted for by remaining credits before any discounting, compliance risk or volume change. The other ~40% is the bet that a US-made, non-silicon panel can earn a living after 2032.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

No polysilicon in the supply chain US manufacturing scale → 45X Domestic-content demand (~41 GW of backlog) Backlog through 2030 Tariff & import-floor shelter Net cash $1.53B · D/E 0.02

The real moat is where the atoms come from. Because CdTe needs no polysilicon, First Solar does not buy the polysilicon, wafers and silicon cells that trade actions target — so the August 2026 Section 232 proclamation (15% tariff plus minimum import prices of $21/kg for polysilicon, $0.22/W for cells and $0.38/W for modules, effective December 4, 2026) raises competitors' floor and leaves First Solar's cost base alone. The weakness is the mirror image: a moat built from policy can be drained by policy. Tariffs, domestic-content bonuses and 45X are all acts of government, and so is their repeal.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The earnings are mostly a tax credit. $1.6B of 45X against $1.60B of 2025 operating income; a 2026 guide in which credits are 81–91% of gross profit. Any change to eligibility — including the prohibited-foreign-entity sourcing tests in force since January 1, 2026 — hits the income statement almost dollar for dollar.
02
The backlog is shrinking. 50.1 GW at year-end 2025, 45.1 GW at June 30, 2026. Management says it is choosing price over volume; if that choice persists into 2027, volume — and with it 45X, which is paid per watt — flattens.
03
Stand-alone economics are thin. ~7% gross margin ex-45X in 2025. The step-down starts in 2030; the business has roughly four full-credit years to prove that tariff-protected US pricing (US bookings at ~$0.36/W versus a $0.38/W import floor) can replace the subsidy.
04
Litigation and insider flow. An ITC Section 337 investigation into TOPCon solar products (337-TA-1494) had procedural updates in late May 2026 and was cited as an overhang on June 5, 2026, when the stock fell 11.4% (Yahoo Finance daily closes). Insiders made zero purchases against 135 sales over the prior six months.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, Q2 2026 included an estimated $89M net tariff-related gain reflecting "expected recoveries related to commercial obligations" — without it, Q2 operating income of $450M would have been closer to $361M. Second, selling credits costs money: in 2025 First Solar sold $701.9M of credits for $668.1M and a further $699.7M for $668.2M — roughly 4.5–4.8 cents lost per dollar (our arithmetic) — and a $15.6M transfer loss hit Q2 2025 cost of sales. The 2026 guide assumes no credit sales, which flatters 2026 margins relative to 2025 and delays the cash. Third, the net cash balance fell from $2.4B at December 31, 2025 to $1.7B at June 30, 2026 as capex ran and credits sat on the balance sheet as receivables.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 12.3x trailing and ~11.4x FY2026 consensus earnings for the only scaled US module maker with no exposure to Chinese polysilicon, $1.53B of net cash, a 45.1 GW / $13.6B backlog through 2030 and a government that has just put a $0.38/W floor under imported modules. The credits are not a rumour — they are statute through 2029, and the illustrative math says remaining credits alone could cover roughly 60% of the enterprise value before discounting. Consensus sees EPS climbing to $23.25 in 2027. If US pricing firms behind the new tariff wall, the business earns its way toward standing without the subsidy before the step-down begins.

Bear Case

Strip out 45X and 2025 gross margin was ~7%; the 2026 credit guide is 81–91% of gross profit. That is not a manufacturer on a low multiple — it is a subsidy stream with a factory attached, and the stream steps down from 2030 and ends in 2033. The backlog is shrinking (50.1 → 45.1 GW in six months), revenue fell 4% in Q2, insiders sold without a single purchase, and 9.27% of the float is short. Every leg of the thesis — credits, tariffs, domestic-content demand — can be changed by the next Congress or the next proclamation.

Top 3 Risks

01
Policy concentration. 45X ($2.10–2.19B guided for 2026), tariffs and domestic-content incentives together underpin most of the profit. The FEOC sourcing tests in force since January 1, 2026 add a compliance layer on top of the statutory step-down.
02
Backlog attrition. Two consecutive quarterly declines in contracted backlog (50.1 → 47.9 → 45.1 GW). Because 45X is paid per watt sold, lower volume cuts both revenue and credits.
03
Credit monetisation and cash timing. Credits sold in 2025 cleared at ~95 cents on the dollar; 2026 guidance assumes none are sold, leaving $1.26B of grants receivable on the balance sheet. Net cash fell from $2.4B to $1.7B in six months.
Industry Commentary
Section 45X: the credit that is the earnings
CRITICAL DEPENDENCY

Section 45X was added to the tax code on August 16, 2022, by the Inflation Reduction Act, for components produced and sold after December 31, 2022. It pays per unit of US-made hardware — $0.07 per watt for a finished solar module, with separate component credits — and can be sold to other taxpayers, which is why First Solar can turn it into cash (in 2025, $1.4B monetised at roughly 95 cents on the dollar). Its growth in First Solar's P&L — $1.0B (2024), $1.6B (2025), $2.10–2.19B guided (2026) — tracks US factory output. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act kept the solar credit but added prohibited-foreign-entity sourcing thresholds from January 1, 2026 and left the 2030–2032 phase-down in place. For a CdTe producer with no polysilicon, those sourcing rules are less of a burden than for silicon rivals — but they are one more thing an auditor has to sign.

Trade walls: Section 232 and the import floor
POLICY TAILWIND

The August 2026 Section 232 proclamation imposes a 15% ad valorem tariff and minimum import prices — $21/kg polysilicon, $100/kg ingots and wafers, $0.22/W cells, $0.38/W modules — effective December 4, 2026. Mark Widmar: "This action closes that loophole, and it is built to be enforced, with a minimum import price, an ad valorem tariff behind it, and real consequences for violators." The 2026 guide assumes a net tariff impact of $60–80M including Section 301 tariffs in the second half. The market's response was muted — August closed down 4.3% — because a tariff floor raises what competitors must charge but does not change First Solar's 2026 volume, which is largely contracted.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

The bull framing: 12.3x trailing earnings, 8.3x EV/EBITDA, a PEG of 0.43 and net cash — for a company whose policy moat just got a wall built around it. The credit runs at full value through 2029; that is four more years of cash to fund capacity and technology (the CuRe program) that could lower cost enough to compete after the step-down. The bear framing: a low multiple on subsidised earnings is not cheap, it is correct. The honest way to value FSLR is in two pieces — a declining annuity of credits (≈$11.8B undiscounted at flat volume, our estimate) plus a stand-alone module business that earned ~7% gross margin in 2025. How you size the second piece is the whole trade. Watch US booking prices against the $0.38/W import floor in 2027 contracts: that is where the answer shows up first.
