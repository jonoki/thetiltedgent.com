01 Company Overview

General Dynamics is a major American defence contractor, yet its largest source of operating profit is a civilian business: Gulfstream business jets. The company runs four reported segments — Aerospace (Gulfstream business jets and Jet Aviation services), Marine Systems (Electric Boat, Bath Iron Works and NASSCO — nuclear submarines and surface combatants), Combat Systems (Abrams tanks, Stryker and Piranha wheeled vehicles, artillery, ammunition) and Technologies (GDIT government IT services and Mission Systems electronics). FY2025 revenue was $52.55B, up 10.1% from $47.72B, with net earnings of $4.21B and diluted EPS of $15.45. At a $95.53B market capitalisation (our calculation: $354.06 × 269.82M shares) it ranks #123 in the S&P 500. Its third quarter, reported on October 30, 2026, brought $98.8B of new orders and lifted the backlog of signed work to $220.3B, about double a year earlier, after the Navy's July 29 awards for nine Virginia-class and five Columbia-class submarines.

The central debate on this page is a mix problem dressed up as a defence story. Marine Systems is the largest segment by revenue — $16.7B, 31.8% of FY2025 sales — and the most strategically protected: nuclear submarines are a duopoly with Huntington Ingalls and the Navy has no alternative supplier. But Marine earns a 7.0% operating margin, roughly half what Aerospace and Combat Systems earn. Aerospace generated 24.9% of revenue but about 31.6% of segment operating profit in FY2025 at a 13.3% margin, and management has guided Aerospace to 14.7% for 2026. Every percentage point (100 basis points) of Gulfstream margin is worth roughly $138M of operating profit on the ~$13.8B of revenue management guided for 2026. So the earnings line is set in Savannah, Georgia, while the backlog and the political narrative are set in Groton, Connecticut. Those two facts pull investors' view of what the earnings are worth in opposite directions. Marine's third-quarter margin rose to 7.9% from 7.1% a year earlier, helped by a one-time catch-up tied to the last Virginia Block IV boats; President Danny Deep said the baseline to think about is the mid-7% range.

This is the earnings engine. Gulfstream delivered 158 aircraft in 2025, 22 more than 2024, and guides to about 160 in 2026. In January Novakovic said 160 "with a little upside"; on the Q2 call she said "about 160," with "some potential upside delivering large-cabin aircraft and some risk on the 280 deliveries." In Q2 2026 the segment did $3.5B of revenue, up 15.1%, with operating earnings of $510M, up 26.6%, and margin of 14.5% — up 130 basis points year over year, on 41 deliveries (35 large-cabin, 6 mid-cabin). The margin story is the G700 and G800 learning curve: management said on the Q2 call that they are "still coming down the curve" on completion work for both models and that there remains "more opportunity" on each. Early units of a new large-cabin aircraft carry heavy completion labour; as the completion centres get repetitions, unit cost falls and the margin on an already-contracted backlog rises without a single extra sale. That is why the 2026 guide steps to 14.7% with "about the same as this quarter" in Q3 and "a better fourth quarter." The offsetting item: G280 deliveries finish around the middle of 2027, leaving a gap before the G300, which Deep said on the Q3 call should be certified toward the end of 2027 with deliveries after that, partly dependent on the Middle East. He said additional large-cabin aircraft should more than offset the lost G280 units, with a more favourable mix. Aerospace book-to-bill was 1.5x in Q2 2026 and 1.3x for the first half, which the CFO called the strongest first half for orders since 2022. In the third quarter (ended October 4, 2026) revenue was $3.54B, up 9.3%, operating earnings $513M, up 19.3%, at a 14.5% margin (13.3% a year earlier), on 40 Gulfstream deliveries (35 large-cabin, 5 mid-cabin). Book-to-bill was 1.4x, and CFO Kim Kuryea said new aircraft orders in the first nine months were up more than 30% from last year. CEO Phebe Novakovic said customers in the Middle East remain in the pipeline but are slow to place orders given the geopolitical environment, and Deep said the intent is to deliver 200 aircraft in 2030.

Electric Boat is prime on both the Columbia-class ballistic-missile submarine — the single highest-priority acquisition programme in the US Navy — and the Virginia-class attack submarine, which it builds in a teaming arrangement with Huntington Ingalls' Newport News yard. The stated destination is two Virginia-class and one Columbia-class delivered per year by the early 2030s; the programme is not there yet, and management's own framing on the Q2 2026 call was that GD is "on that path" and "where we expect to be at this point" — an execution claim, not a schedule claim. The direction of travel: Columbia hours earned rose 37% in H1 2026 versus H1 2025, sequence-critical material deliveries rose 65% year over year in Q2, and Electric Boat submarine tonnage output rose 13% in 2025. On the Q2 call Deep said 2026 Marine sales would be almost $1.3B above 2025 (the July guide was about $18B), and Novakovic said GD had been told to expect the Block VI Virginia-class contract "soon." The Navy executed it on July 29, 2026: $42.1B to Electric Boat for nine Block VI Virginia-class boats and $29.5B for five Columbia-class Build II boats, which with $5B for shipyard enhancements totals $76.6B (statement by Rep. Joe Courtney, July 29, 2026). Both awards, together $71.6B, were booked in the third quarter. On labour, executives said they have been "really pleased with our ability to attract and retain the necessary number of workers," with Navy support in workforce development. Q3 2026 revenue was $4.96B, up 21.1% and operating earnings $391M, up 34.4%, a 7.9% margin (7.1% a year earlier) that included a one-time catch-up on Virginia Block IV; Deep put the baseline in the mid-7% range and said output and throughput on Columbia and Virginia are up 30% on the year. That margin tells you the honest state of play: these are long programmes still climbing a productivity curve, and Navy money for supplier capacity buys output, not a higher profit margin.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Nuclear sub duopoly Sovereign-critical assets $220.3B backlog Gulfstream brand Certification barriers Skilled-trade workforce Aftermarket service revenue

The moat is not one thing, it is two unrelated things bolted together. On the defence side it is irreplaceability: there are exactly two yards in the United States capable of building nuclear submarines. That leaves a customer with no alternative supplier, funding for supplier capacity paid by the buyer, and a backlog that reached $220.3B at the end of Q3 2026 (October 4), about double a year earlier, on a company-wide book-to-bill of 6.8x as the Navy's submarine awards were booked. On the aerospace side it is brand plus certification: a new large-cabin business jet costs billions and years of flight test to certify, the buyer pool is small and reputation-driven, and Gulfstream's installed base feeds steady service and completions work through Jet Aviation. Neither moat is under near-term attack. What differs is who controls the profit — the Pentagon negotiates the defence margin, while Gulfstream's margin is a function of GD's own manufacturing execution.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Marine Systems is a margin anchor with political armour. Marine is nearly a third of revenue at a 7.0% FY2025 margin and only ~21% of segment operating earnings. It was guided to just 7.4% for 2026 in July (segment guidance was not updated on the Q3 call); Q3's 7.9% included a one-time catch-up, and Deep put the baseline in the mid-7% range. The segment absorbs capital — capex is guided at 3.5–4% of sales with shipyard investment set to "grow significantly in the back half" of 2026 — and returns single-digit margins. If the mix keeps shifting toward Marine as the submarine ramp accelerates, the company's overall margin faces a lasting drag even as revenue and backlog look excellent.

2. Concentration in one customer and one budget process. Three of four segments sell almost exclusively to the US government and its allies. Continuing resolutions, reconciliation-bill timing and appropriations delays move revenue recognition and contract awards. Novakovic's own comment that there are "a lot of moving parts" in the budget process is the honest version of this risk.

3. Business-jet demand rises and falls with the economy. Aerospace is the largest source of operating profit, and large-cabin jet orders depend on the wealth of rich buyers, corporate confidence and credit. Book-to-bill of 1.3x in H1 2026 and 1.4x in Q3 is strong, but orders like these can fall quickly when credit tightens. The 2027 gap between the G280 and the G300 adds a dip of GD's own making to a business that already moves with the economy.

4. Skilled-trade labour is the binding constraint, not orders. The submarine ramp is limited by welders, pipefitters and machinists, not by funded demand. Management says retention has been good; that is a claim that has to be re-verified every quarter, because a single bad year of attrition at Electric Boat would show up as schedule slips and cost growth.

5. A change at the top. Phebe Novakovic, CEO since 2013, hands the role to Danny Deep on January 1, 2027 and becomes Executive Chairman. Deep is a 25-year company veteran and continuity is the stated plan, but it is the first change of CEO since 2013 and it arrives while the submarine ramp and the Gulfstream margin plan are both mid-flight. Less than a week before the board's decision, it was announced that Amy Gilliland, president of the GDIT IT-services unit, had left the company (Washington Technology, October 7, 2026).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A record $220.3B backlog — about double a year earlier, after the Navy's $76.6B of submarine awards — gives multi-year revenue visibility: it equals about 3.9 years of 2026 revenue (the $56.65B midpoint of the guide), and sits mostly in programmes — Columbia, Virginia, 155mm shells, European land systems — that governments have backed for years ($111.8B of it was funded at October 4). On top of that, GD can lift margins through its own execution: Gulfstream is still moving down the G700/G800 learning curve, with segment margin guided from 13.3% in FY2025 to 14.7% in FY2026 (14.5% in both Q2 and Q3), and every percentage point there is worth roughly $138M of operating profit. Free cash flow targeted at about 105% of net earnings funds a well-covered 1.80% dividend at a 37.4% payout plus buybacks that cover dilution, with net debt down to $2.6B. At 18.8 times FY2027 consensus earnings with a 0.30 beta, you are paying for low volatility and long visibility.

Bear Case

The backlog everyone celebrates is disproportionately in the lowest-margin segment. Marine Systems was 31.8% of FY2025 revenue at a 7.0% margin; Q3's 7.9% included a one-time catch-up, management's baseline is the mid-7% range, and the $71.6B of submarine orders just booked all sit there. Growth in Marine therefore pulls down the company's overall margin, an argument for a lower price-to-earnings ratio, while capex is stepping up (Q3's $481M was more than the first half combined). Meanwhile the largest share of profit sits in a luxury business that moves with the economy: Aerospace is 24.9% of revenue but roughly 31.6% of segment profit, and business-jet demand depends on credit, with a G280-to-G300 production gap in 2027. A PEG of 2.16, nine Holds among 24 analysts, a CEO handover on January 1, and a TTM free-cash-flow figure lifted by working capital to 46% above the FY2025 level all suggest the good news is largely in the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Submarine execution and schedule. The path to two Virginia-class and one Columbia-class per year by the early 2030s is a labour and supplier-throughput problem, not a funding problem. Management's evidence — Columbia hours earned +37% in H1 2026, sequence-critical material +65%, and Deep's statement that Electric Boat has 6,000 new shipbuilders and expects 8,000 by year-end — is real progress. Slippage means cost growth and margin pressure in the segment least able to absorb it.
02
Aerospace margin fails to reach 14.7%. The FY2026 EPS guide of $17.20–$17.30 leans on G700/G800 completion costs continuing to fall. Q2 and Q3 both came in at 14.5%; management said in July to expect "a better fourth quarter" to reach the 14.7% full-year figure, and on October 30 did not update segment guidance. A back-end-weighted margin plan is exactly the kind that disappoints. Targets in the ratings table above run from $386 (Bernstein, Hold) to $480 (Morgan Stanley, Buy).
03
Budget process and single-customer concentration. Three of four segments depend on US government appropriations timing. Continuing resolutions delay awards and shift revenue recognition; the Technologies segment, which grew 2.6% in FY2025 and 10.8% in Q3 2026 but with margin down to 9.4% from 9.8%, is the most exposed to procurement scrutiny of federal services spending.
Industry Commentary
Shipyard labour and the submarine industrial base
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The binding constraint on US submarine production is not appropriations — it is welders, pipefitters, machinists and the tier-two and tier-three suppliers who forge and machine the components. This is why the Navy funds supplier capacity directly rather than simply ordering more boats, and why GD management's phrasing on the Q2 2026 call was about the customer and the industrial base being "aligned around the need for getting these contracts out," particularly "to stabilize the industrial base." The measurable progress is on the input side: sequence-critical material deliveries up 65% year over year in Q2 2026, Columbia hours earned up 37% in H1, Electric Boat tonnage output up 13% in 2025. The measurable cost is on the margin side: 7.0% in FY2025, guided to 7.4% in FY2026 — the price of building capacity while delivering on contracts written before the ramp. GD said retention has been strong. On the Q3 call Deep said Electric Boat has brought on 6,000 new shipbuilders and expects 8,000 by the end of the year, and that output and throughput on Columbia and Virginia are up 30% on the year.

The defence budget: base funding versus reconciliation money
ONGOING HEADWIND

Novakovic drew a distinction on the Q2 2026 call that matters for how to read GD's revenue durability: "a lot of our programs are funded in the base budget." Base-budget funding is stable and recurring; supplemental and reconciliation money is lumpy and politically contingent. Her argument for why the extra money matters was production-rate economics — "weapons production has been on very low-rate production or fairly minimally sustaining production for quite some time," and moving from low-rate to sustained-rate output is where unit costs fall and margins improve. She also acknowledged "a lot of moving parts" in the budget process. The practical read: the backlog is well-protected, but the timing of new awards sits inside a process GD does not control: Congress authorized Block VI in 2023 and the Navy executed the contract on July 29, 2026. Combat Systems' 2.1x book-to-bill shows what happens when that money does flow.

Large-cabin business jets: the cycle that pays GD's bills
EMERGING TAILWIND

Aerospace book-to-bill of 1.3x in H1 2026 (1.5x in Q2), the strongest first half for orders since 2022, is one of the most encouraging operating figures in the company, and it comes from the business least like a defence contractor. Deliveries went 136 → 158 → ~160 across 2024, 2025 and guided 2026, while Aerospace margin went from 13.3% in 2025 to a guided 14.7% in 2026. The bull-versus-bear framing for the whole stock reduces to this: the bull says GD is a defence company with a hidden, high-margin luxury-jet business that the market prices like the rest of a defence company, and that as G700/G800 costs keep falling and the G300 follows (certification is expected toward the end of 2027), Aerospace margin has years of room to rise. The bear says GD is a defence company with a cyclical luxury business that will be valued lower the moment demand from wealthy buyers falls, and that the segment carrying a third of the profit on a quarter of the revenue is a concentration risk, not a hidden asset. Both readings fit the same numbers. The Q3 results on October 30 showed Aerospace margin of 14.5%, level with Q2 and up from 13.3% a year earlier; the 2027 outlook due in January is the next test.
