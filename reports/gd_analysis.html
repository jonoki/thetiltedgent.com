01 Company Overview

General Dynamics is one of the five American defence primes, but it is the only one whose single most profitable business builds civilian aeroplanes. The company runs four reported segments — Aerospace (Gulfstream business jets and Jet Aviation services), Marine Systems (Electric Boat, Bath Iron Works and NASSCO — nuclear submarines and surface combatants), Combat Systems (Abrams tanks, Stryker and Piranha wheeled vehicles, artillery, ammunition) and Technologies (GDIT government IT services and Mission Systems electronics). FY2025 revenue was $52.55B, up 10.1% from $47.72B, with net earnings of $4.21B and diluted EPS of $15.45. At a $97.09B market capitalisation it is roughly the #256 most valuable listed company in the world.

The central debate on this page is a mix problem dressed up as a defence story. Marine Systems is the largest segment by revenue — $16.7B, 31.8% of FY2025 sales — and the most strategically protected: nuclear submarines are a duopoly with Huntington Ingalls and the Navy has no alternative supplier. But Marine earns a 7.1% operating margin, roughly half what Aerospace and Combat Systems earn. Aerospace generated 24.9% of revenue but about 31.7% of segment operating profit in FY2025 at a 13.4% margin, and management has guided Aerospace to 14.7% for 2026. Every 100 basis points of Gulfstream margin is worth roughly $138M of operating profit on a ~$13.8B revenue base — more than a full year's worth of growth in any single defence programme. So the earnings line is set in Savannah, Georgia, while the backlog and the political narrative are set in Groton, Connecticut. Those two facts pull the multiple in opposite directions.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Nuclear sub duopoly Sovereign-critical assets $136.5B backlog Gulfstream brand Certification barriers Skilled-trade workforce Aftermarket annuity

The moat is not one thing, it is two unrelated things bolted together. On the defence side it is irreplaceability: there are exactly two yards in the United States capable of building nuclear submarines, and building a third would take a decade and a workforce that does not exist. That produces a customer with no exit option, funding for supplier capacity paid by the buyer, and a backlog that reached $136.5B in Q2 2026, up 32% year over year, on a company-wide book-to-bill of 1.4x. On the aerospace side it is brand plus certification: a new large-cabin business jet costs billions and years of flight test to certify, the buyer pool is small and reputation-driven, and Gulfstream's installed base feeds a service and completions annuity through Jet Aviation. Neither moat is under near-term attack. What differs is who controls the profit — the Pentagon negotiates the defence margin, while Gulfstream's margin is a function of GD's own manufacturing execution.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Marine Systems is a margin anchor with political armour. Marine is nearly a third of revenue at a 7.1% FY2025 margin and only ~21% of segment operating earnings. It is guided to just 7.4% in 2026. The segment absorbs capital — capex is guided at 3.5–4% of sales with shipyard investment set to "grow significantly in the back half" of 2026 — and returns single-digit margins. If the mix keeps shifting toward Marine as the submarine ramp accelerates, consolidated margin faces a structural headwind even as revenue and backlog look excellent.

2. Concentration in one customer and one budget process. Three of four segments sell almost exclusively to the US government and its allies. Continuing resolutions, reconciliation-bill timing and appropriations delays move revenue recognition and contract awards. Novakovic's own comment that there are "a lot of moving parts" in the budget process is the honest version of this risk.

3. Business-jet demand is late-cycle and cyclical. Aerospace carries the earnings leverage, and large-cabin jet orders are a function of high-net-worth wealth, corporate confidence and credit. A book-to-bill of 1.5x in H1 2026 is excellent; it is also the kind of number that has historically inflected hard when financial conditions tighten. The 2027 G280-to-G300 production break adds a self-inflicted air pocket to a cyclical business.

4. Skilled-trade labour is the binding constraint, not orders. The submarine ramp is limited by welders, pipefitters and machinists, not by funded demand. Management says retention has been good; that is a claim that has to be re-verified every quarter, because a single bad year of attrition at Electric Boat would show up as schedule slip and cost growth on cost-type contracts — the worst combination.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A record $136.5B backlog growing 32% year over year at a 1.4x book-to-bill gives multi-year revenue visibility that almost no industrial can match, and it is concentrated in programmes — Columbia, Virginia, 155mm, European land systems — the customer cannot cancel. Layered on top is a genuine self-help margin story: Gulfstream is still descending the G700/G800 learning curve, with segment margin guided from 13.4% in FY2025 to 14.7% in FY2026, and every 100bp there is worth roughly $138M of operating profit. Free cash flow conversion targeted at ~105% of net income funds a well-covered 1.77% dividend at a 38.8% payout plus buybacks, with net debt down to $3.2B. At 20.5x forward earnings with a 0.32 beta, you are paying a market multiple for below-market volatility and above-market visibility.

Bear Case

The backlog everyone celebrates is disproportionately in the worst-margin segment. Marine Systems is 31.8% of revenue at a 7.1% margin and is guided to only 7.4% in 2026, while consuming rising capex that management says will "grow significantly" in H2. Growth in Marine therefore dilutes consolidated margin — the mix works against the multiple. Meanwhile the earnings leverage sits in a cyclical luxury business: Aerospace is 24.9% of revenue but roughly 31.7% of segment profit, and business-jet demand is a late-cycle, credit-sensitive market with a self-inflicted G280-to-G300 production break in 2027. A PEG of 2.17, ten Holds against ten Strong Buys, and a TTM free-cash-flow figure inflated by working-capital timing to 63% above the FY2025 level all suggest the good news is largely in the price.

Industry Commentary
Shipyard labour and the submarine industrial base
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The binding constraint on US submarine production is not appropriations — it is welders, pipefitters, machinists and the tier-two and tier-three suppliers who forge and machine the components. This is why the Navy funds supplier capacity directly rather than simply ordering more boats, and why GD management's phrasing on the Q2 2026 call was about the customer and the industrial base being "aligned around the need for getting these contracts out, particularly to stabilise the industrial base." The measurable progress is on the input side: sequence-critical material deliveries up 65% year over year in Q2 2026, Columbia hours earned up 37% in H1, Electric Boat tonnage output up 13% in 2025. The measurable cost is on the margin side: 7.1% in FY2025, guided to 7.4% in FY2026 — the price of building capacity while delivering against fixed-price and incentive contracts written before the ramp. GD said retention has been strong; no hiring or attrition figures were disclosed on the sources fetched, which is itself worth noting.

The defence budget: base funding versus reconciliation money
ONGOING HEADWIND

Novakovic drew a distinction on the Q2 2026 call that matters for how to read GD's revenue durability: "a lot of our programmes are funded in the base budget." Base-budget funding is stable and recurring; supplemental and reconciliation money is lumpy and politically contingent. Her argument for why the extra money matters was production-rate economics — "weapons production has been on very low rate production for quite some time," and moving from low-rate to sustained-rate output is where unit costs fall and margins improve. She also acknowledged "a lot of moving parts" in the budget process. The practical read: the backlog is well-protected, but the timing of new awards, including Virginia Block VI, sits inside a process GD does not control. Combat Systems' 2.1x book-to-bill shows what happens when that money does flow.

Large-cabin business jets: the cycle that pays GD's bills
EMERGING TAILWIND

Aerospace book-to-bill of 1.5x in H1 2026 — the strongest first half since 2022 — is the single most bullish operating datapoint in the company right now, and it comes from the business least like a defence contractor. Deliveries went 136 → 158 → ~160 across 2024, 2025 and guided 2026, while margin went 13.4% → 14.7%. The bull-versus-bear framing for the whole stock reduces to this: the bull says GD is a defence company with a hidden luxury-manufacturing margin engine that the market values at a defence multiple, and that as the G700/G800 curve flattens and G300 arrives in 2028, Aerospace margin has years of runway. The bear says GD is a defence company with a cyclical consumer-discretionary exposure that will de-rate the moment high-net-worth demand rolls over, and that the segment carrying a third of the profit on a quarter of the revenue is a concentration risk, not a hidden asset. Both readings fit the same verified numbers. The Q3 print on October 23 is the next place they get tested.
