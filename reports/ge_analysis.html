01 Company Overview

GE Aerospace designs, manufactures and — far more profitably — services jet engines. Following the completion of the three-way breakup of the old General Electric conglomerate (GE HealthCare spun off January 2023, GE Vernova April 2024), ticker GE is now a pure-play propulsion company: commercial and military engines, plus the aftermarket parts and shop visits that flow from an installed base of roughly 80,000 engines that have logged more than 2.3 billion flight hours. FY2025 revenue was $45.9B, up 18% YoY on a GAAP basis ($42.3B adjusted, +21%), with total orders of $66.2B (+32%). In the third quarter of 2026 orders rose 49% to $19.0B, GAAP revenue 16% to $14.1B (adjusted $13.3B, +18%) and adjusted EPS 36% to $2.25. The contracted backlog (remaining performance obligation, work signed but not yet delivered) stood at $212.1B on September 30, 2026, about four times trailing revenue of $52.6B.

At a market capitalization of about $306B on October 21, 2026, GE Aerospace is one of the larger S&P 500 companies (its rank is in the header). The economic engine is not equipment: engines are frequently sold at low or negative margin, and the returns arrive over a 25–30 year tail of spare parts and shop visits. Commercial services backlog is now over $170B. On September 8, 2026 GE agreed to buy Consolidated Precision Products (CPP), a Cleveland maker of engine castings and one of its suppliers for more than 15 years, for $11.75B: $7B in cash and the rest in new debt, with closing expected in the second half of 2027. It is a bet that the limit on growth is parts supply, not demand.

The jet engine business is one of the most durable oligopolies in industry, and GE sits at the center of it. Three structural facts do the work. First, engine selection is effectively irreversible: once an airline picks an engine for an airframe, that decision locks in 25–30 years of parts and overhaul revenue, because re-engining is economically absurd and the airframe/engine pairing is certified as a unit. Second, certification is a moat measured in decades and billions — a new commercial turbofan takes roughly 10 years and $2–3B to develop and certify, with no revenue until it flies; the last genuinely new entrant to the Western large-turbofan market was decades ago. Third, and most importantly, GE controls the parts: the design authority for a proprietary engine defines the manuals, sets the parts prices, and captures the majority of the maintenance value even when the physical work is performed by a third-party shop. That is why GE's installed base of ~80,000 engines is the asset, not the factory.

The CFM joint venture amplifies this. By splitting development cost and risk 50/50 with Safran while retaining full aftermarket participation, GE gets scale economics on the highest-volume engine program in aviation history without carrying the balance sheet alone — and the JV structure makes CFM extremely difficult to attack, because a competitor must out-spend two of the world's largest aerospace companies simultaneously. The evidence shows up in the returns: TTM ROE of 50.2% and ROIC of 31.8% are roughly 2–2.7x the aerospace industry estimates in Section 04, and free cash conversion was 113% in FY2025.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Supply chain is the binding constraint, not demand. Management keeps saying the limit is supply, not demand. Services orders grew about 34% in the first half of 2026, while spare-parts delinquencies — orders GE could not ship on time for lack of material — rose 20% from the first quarter to the second. On the Q3 call CFO Rahul Ghai said delinquencies rose again from the second quarter, even though CEO Larry Culp said material from suppliers had grown quarter on quarter for ten straight quarters, and from priority suppliers by double digits year on year in Q3. GE's answer is to buy capacity: the $11.75B CPP castings deal, expected to close in the second half of 2027. Every quarter of unmet demand is deferred, not destroyed, but it caps near-term revenue and gives customers a reason to look elsewhere.

2. Aftermarket margin is contestable at the edges. The high-margin parts annuity attracts attackers: PMA parts approved by the FAA, USM harvested from teardowns, and independent MRO shops all chip at GE's share of maintenance value. Airlines and lessors actively fund these alternatives precisely because engine maintenance costs have risen so fast. GE's counter — proprietary repairs, data, and time-on-wing guarantees — has worked so far, but the incentive to break the parts monopoly grows with every price increase.

3. Concentration on two airframers and their rate ramps. Commercial equipment volume is functionally a derivative of Boeing 737 MAX / 787 and Airbus A320neo / A350 production rates, over which GE has no control. Boeing's certification and rate history since 2019 has repeatedly pushed engine deliveries to the right. The offset is real — delayed deliveries mean older aircraft fly longer, which helps the aftermarket — but it makes equipment revenue and near-term guidance hostage to third parties.

4. Competitive re-entry and legacy liabilities. Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan powder-metal problem grounded a large share of the A320neo fleet and handed GE both share and pricing power; as P&W works through inspections and its GTF Advantage variant matures, some of that windfall reverses. Separately, the ERAC long-term-care run-off and the tariff regime on imported aircraft parts (a ~$100M refund landed in Q2 2026) are non-operating items that can move reported results in ways that have nothing to do with engines. Closer to home, the GE9X ramp for Boeing's 777X is still loss-making: Ghai expects about $300M more GE9X losses in 2027 than in 2026.

Notable One-Off Items. Several distortions materially affect comparability and should be stripped before valuing the business:

· GAAP vs adjusted divergence. FY2025 GAAP EPS was $8.14 ($8.05 from continuing operations), against adjusted operating profit of $9.1B and adjusted EPS of $6.37. The ~$1.77/share gap comes principally from non-operating pension income, gains on retained equity stakes in the spun-off entities, and insurance results. The same pattern repeated in Q2 2026 (GAAP EPS $2.30 vs adjusted $2.02) and in Q3 2026: GAAP continuing EPS $2.32 vs adjusted $2.25, and GAAP revenue $14.1B vs adjusted $13.3B, the gap being mostly $0.8B of insurance revenue. On trailing adjusted EPS of $7.70 the P/E is 38.4x, against the 34.1x GAAP headline.

· CPP acquisition. Signed September 8, 2026 for $11.75B, which GE puts at about 26x CPP's expected 2027 EBITDA (operating earnings before depreciation and amortization) before synergies (the gains GE expects from combining the two) and about 18x after. GE expects it to add to adjusted EPS and free cash flow in the first year after closing. Until then it is a financing item: $7B of cash and about $4.75B of new debt.

· Spin-related accounting. FY2024 GAAP net income of $6.56B looks like a decline from FY2023's $9.19B, but the two are not comparable: FY2023 included GE Vernova, and FY2024 straddles the April 2, 2024 separation with discontinued-operations treatment and separation costs. Any multi-year GAAP earnings series for GE that crosses 2023–2024 should be treated as broken. Separation costs have now largely run off.

· Tariffs. GE received approximately $100M in tariff refunds in Q2 2026, which management characterized as "not extremely material." Tariff treatment of imported aircraft parts and engines remains an open policy variable that flows through working capital and cost of goods sold rather than a discrete line item — which makes it hard to model and easy to under-appreciate.

· Legacy insurance run-off. ERAC's annual statutory cash-flow test can require incremental reserve contributions. No material charge in the most recent period, but this is a recurring tail item, not a resolved one.

Five-year trajectory. On a split- and spin-adjusted basis, GE closed October 2021 at the equivalent of $65.34 and October 21, 2026 at $295.88 — a price return of about +353% (dividends not included), or roughly 35% a year. Over the same window (October 29, 2021 to October 21, 2026) SPY, the S&P 500 fund, returned +68.3% on price and QQQ, the Nasdaq-100 fund, +87.8%. GE beat the index by about 285 percentage points. Most of the gain came from the end of 2022 to the end of 2025, the years the breakup was carried out: the month-end close rose from $52.20 to $308.03.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

GE owns the largest installed base in commercial aviation — ~80,000 engines — and the aftermarket annuity attached to it is entering its steepest growth phase in decades. Management guides to a ~25% LEAP shop-visit CAGR through 2030 with the external channel doubling its share, on top of a CFM56 wave still running at 2,300–2,400 shop visits a year. With a $212B contracted backlog (about 4x revenue), commercial services backlog over $170B, 31.8% ROIC, 113% free cash conversion in 2025 and a payout ratio of about 22%, the earnings and cash trajectory is unusually visible for a cyclical. Guidance was raised again in October across revenue, profit, EPS and free cash flow, management already expects low-double-digit services growth in 2027, and the CPP deal buys the castings capacity that limits output. The constraint is supply, not demand — the better problem to have — and the shares are 22% below their August 5 closing high of $381.22.

Bear Case

At ~34x trailing GAAP earnings, 38x trailing adjusted earnings and ~37x the midpoint of 2026 adjusted EPS guidance, much of the aftermarket supercycle is still in the price even after the fall. Two quarters in a row proved how demanding that is: beats on every line with raised guidance produced a 4.1% one-day decline in July and a 5.0% two-day decline in October. Meanwhile spare-parts delinquencies rose again, Q3 free cash flow fell 14% from a year earlier, GE9X losses add about $300M of headwind in 2027, the CPP deal adds about $4.75B of new debt at ~26x CPP's 2027 EBITDA before synergies, the current ratio sits below 1.0, beta is 1.37, and the lowest analyst target ($280) is below the current price. Add Pratt & Whitney's GTF recovery reclaiming share, PMA/USM erosion of the parts monopoly, unresolved tariff policy and a legacy long-term-care run-off, and the risk/reward is asymmetric to the downside on any execution stumble.

01
Valuation leaves little room for error. About 37x the midpoint of 2026 adjusted EPS guidance, on a business with 1.37 beta, prices in smooth execution of a five-year shop-visit ramp. History says aerospace cycles do not run smoothly, and the July and October 2026 reactions to beat-and-raise quarters showed how the stock behaves when results merely meet high expectations.
02
Supply chain caps the upside GE has already sold. Services orders grew about 34% in H1 2026, while spare-parts delinquencies rose 20% in Q2 alone and again in Q3 — GE is turning away revenue it has already booked. If castings, forgings and specialty-alloy capacity does not scale, the shop-visit CAGR guidance becomes a promise the supply base cannot keep, and the deferred revenue eventually invites PMA and USM substitution.
03
Aftermarket share erosion and competitive normalization. GE's model depends on parts and service for engines it already sold (Section 02). FAA-approved PMA parts, used serviceable material and independent MRO shops all attack that pool, and airlines have never had more incentive to fund them. Simultaneously, Pratt & Whitney's GTF fleet returning to service reverses a share and pricing windfall GE has been enjoying since 2023.
  Industry Commentary
The Aftermarket Supercycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
The defining dynamic in commercial aerospace right now is not aircraft production — it is maintenance. Airframe delivery delays since 2019 have kept older aircraft flying far past their planned retirement, and the global fleet is simultaneously aging into first and second heavy overhauls on the CFM56 while the LEAP fleet reaches its first shop visits. For GE this compounds: CFM56 shop visits are running in a 2,300–2,400 annual range, which the CFO expects to stay roughly flat, while internal LEAP shop visits grew over 50% in Q2 2026 alone, with management guiding to a ~25% CAGR through 2030. Commercial services backlog is now over $170B. Aftermarket work carries roughly 2–3x the margin of new equipment, which is why CES held a 27.9% operating margin in Q3 2026 even with equipment revenue up 38%. The catch: this is a known story. Every analyst model contains it, which is why the stock still trades at about 37x 2026 adjusted EPS guidance after its fall.
Aerospace Supply Chain & MRO Capacity
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The industry cannot make parts fast enough. Material from GE's suppliers has grown quarter on quarter for ten consecutive quarters (CEO Larry Culp, Q3 call), and still spare-parts delinquencies climbed 20% from the first quarter to the second, with first-half services orders up about 34%, then rose again in Q3. The bottleneck sits upstream in castings, forgings, single-crystal turbine blades and specialty alloys — capacity that takes years and heavy capital to add, run by suppliers who were financially damaged by the 2020–2021 demand collapse and remain understandably reluctant to over-invest. GE's response has been operational rather than financial: FLIGHT DECK lean deployment, AI-assisted inspection (a GKN collaboration cut inspection time ~90% via 3D scanning), and physical MRO capacity additions such as the new Fort Worth facility with MTU Aero Engines. In September it went further and agreed to buy CPP, a castings maker, for $11.75B. Culp said in October that turnaround times are down by over a week since the end of 2025, two weeks for LEAP. Progress is real and measurable — but management still describes this as a supply problem rather than a demand problem, and that is a constraint on realized revenue, not merely on sentiment.
Tariffs, Trade Policy, and What It All Means
ONGOING HEADWIND
Civil aircraft and parts have traded duty-free under the WTO Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft since 1980, and the aerospace supply chain was engineered on that assumption. GE is unusually exposed to any unwinding: its flagship commercial engine is built through a 50/50 joint venture with a French company, Avio Aero manufactures in Italy, and components cross borders repeatedly before final assembly. The ~$100M tariff refund booked in Q2 2026 — which management called "not extremely material" — is best read as evidence that the policy environment is fluid rather than resolved. Costs flow through cost of goods sold and working capital, making the exposure hard to isolate and easy to underestimate.

Framing the two sides. The bull synthesis: supply constraints are a timing problem inside a demand environment that is structurally strong for a decade, tariffs have so far been a net refund rather than a net cost, and a business earning 32% ROIC with 113% cash conversion and $212B of backlog deserves a premium multiple — the debate is how large a premium, not whether it is a good business. The bear synthesis: at about 37x 2026 adjusted EPS guidance the stock is priced as if the supply chain resolves on schedule, tariffs stay benign, Pratt & Whitney does not recover share, and PMA/USM never meaningfully penetrates the parts pool. Four assumptions, each individually reasonable, all of which must hold. The July and October 2026 beat-and-raise quarters that each produced declines are the market telling you which risk it is currently more worried about.
