01 Company Overview

GE Aerospace designs, manufactures and — far more profitably — services jet engines. Following the completion of the three-way breakup of the old General Electric conglomerate (GE HealthCare spun off January 2023, GE Vernova April 2024), ticker GE is now a pure-play propulsion company: commercial and military engines, plus the aftermarket parts and shop visits that flow from an installed base of roughly 80,000 engines that have logged more than 2.3 billion flight hours. FY2025 revenue was $45.9B, up 18.5% YoY on a GAAP basis ($42.3B adjusted, +21%), with total orders of $66.2B (+32%) and backlog now above $210B — roughly 4.6x trailing revenue.

At a ~$380B market capitalization GE Aerospace is the 39th most valuable listed company in the world, roughly #28 in the US and #20 on the NYSE, and is the single largest holding in the iShares US Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA) at ~21.7% weight. The economic engine is not equipment: engines are frequently sold at low or negative margin, and the returns arrive over a 25–30 year tail of spare parts and shop visits. Commercial services backlog alone reached ~$170B in Q2 2026, up nearly $30B since the end of 2024.

The jet engine business is one of the most durable oligopolies in industry, and GE sits at the center of it. Three structural facts do the work. First, engine selection is effectively irreversible: once an airline picks an engine for an airframe, that decision locks in 25–30 years of parts and overhaul revenue, because re-engining is economically absurd and the airframe/engine pairing is certified as a unit. Second, certification is a moat measured in decades and billions — a new commercial turbofan takes roughly 10 years and $2–3B to develop and certify, with no revenue until it flies; the last genuinely new entrant to the Western large-turbofan market was decades ago. Third, and most importantly, GE controls the parts: the design authority for a proprietary engine defines the manuals, sets the parts prices, and captures the majority of the maintenance value even when the physical work is performed by a third-party shop. That is why GE's installed base of ~80,000 engines is the asset, not the factory.

The CFM joint venture amplifies this. By splitting development cost and risk 50/50 with Safran while retaining full aftermarket participation, GE gets scale economics on the highest-volume engine program in aviation history without carrying the balance sheet alone — and the JV structure makes CFM extremely difficult to attack, because a competitor must out-spend two of the world's largest aerospace companies simultaneously. The evidence shows up in the returns: TTM ROE of 48.2% and ROIC of 31.0% are roughly 2.5–3x aerospace peer averages, and free cash conversion exceeded 110% in FY2025.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Supply chain is the binding constraint, not demand. CEO Larry Culp has been explicit that the problem is "much more a supply-side challenge than it is a demand" one. Spare-parts orders grew 34% in H1 2026 while delinquencies — orders GE could not ship on time for lack of material — rose 20%. Nine consecutive quarters of double-digit output increases from critical suppliers still has not closed the gap on castings, forgings and specialty alloys. Every quarter of unmet demand is deferred, not destroyed, but it caps near-term revenue and gives customers a reason to look elsewhere.

2. Aftermarket margin is contestable at the edges. The high-margin parts annuity attracts attackers: PMA parts approved by the FAA, USM harvested from teardowns, and independent MRO shops all chip at GE's share of maintenance value. Airlines and lessors actively fund these alternatives precisely because engine maintenance costs have risen so fast. GE's counter — proprietary repairs, data, and time-on-wing guarantees — has worked so far, but the incentive to break the parts monopoly grows with every price increase.

3. Concentration on two airframers and their rate ramps. Commercial equipment volume is functionally a derivative of Boeing 737 MAX / 787 and Airbus A320neo / A350 production rates, over which GE has no control. Boeing's certification and rate history since 2019 has repeatedly pushed engine deliveries to the right. The offset is real — delayed deliveries mean older aircraft fly longer, which helps the aftermarket — but it makes equipment revenue and near-term guidance hostage to third parties.

4. Competitive re-entry and legacy liabilities. Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan powder-metal problem grounded a large share of the A320neo fleet and handed GE both share and pricing power; as P&W works through inspections and its GTF Advantage variant matures, some of that windfall reverses. Separately, the ERAC long-term-care run-off and the tariff regime on imported aircraft parts (a ~$100M refund landed in Q2 2026) are non-operating items that can move reported results in ways that have nothing to do with engines.

Notable One-Off Items. Several distortions materially affect comparability and should be stripped before valuing the business:

· GAAP vs adjusted divergence. FY2025 GAAP net profit was $10.0B and GAAP EPS $8.14, against adjusted operating profit of $9.1B and adjusted EPS of $6.37. The ~$1.77/share gap comes principally from non-operating pension income, gains on retained equity stakes in the spun-off entities, and insurance results. The same pattern repeated in Q2 2026: GAAP EPS $2.30 vs adjusted $2.02, and GAAP revenue $13.3B vs adjusted $12.6B. Trailing P/E on adjusted earnings is meaningfully higher than the 42.9x headline.

· Spin-related accounting. FY2024 GAAP net income of $6.56B looks like a decline from FY2023's $9.19B, but the two are not comparable: FY2023 included GE Vernova, and FY2024 straddles the April 2, 2024 separation with discontinued-operations treatment and separation costs. Any multi-year GAAP earnings series for GE that crosses 2023–2024 should be treated as broken. Separation costs have now largely run off.

· Tariffs. GE received approximately $100M in tariff refunds in Q2 2026, which management characterized as "not extremely material." Tariff treatment of imported aircraft parts and engines remains an open policy variable that flows through working capital and cost of goods sold rather than a discrete line item — which makes it hard to model and easy to under-appreciate.

· Legacy insurance run-off. ERAC's annual statutory cash-flow test can require incremental reserve contributions. No material charge in the most recent period, but this is a recurring tail item, not a resolved one.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

GE owns the largest installed base in commercial aviation — ~80,000 engines — and the aftermarket annuity attached to it is entering its steepest growth phase in decades. Management guides to a ~25% LEAP shop-visit CAGR through 2030 with the external channel doubling its share, on top of a CFM56 wave still running at 2,300–2,400 shop visits a year. With backlog above $210B (4.6x revenue), commercial services backlog at ~$170B, 31% ROIC, 113% free cash conversion and a payout ratio of only ~20%, the earnings and cash trajectory is unusually visible for a cyclical. Guidance has been raised twice this year and the constraint is supply, not demand — which is the better problem to have.

Bear Case

At ~43x trailing and ~44x forward GAAP earnings — and materially higher on adjusted earnings, since FY25 GAAP EPS of $8.14 flatters adjusted EPS of $6.37 — essentially the entire aftermarket supercycle is already in the price. The Q2 reaction proved it: a beat on every line with raised guidance still produced a 4% decline. Meanwhile CES margins contracted 160 bps, spare-parts delinquencies rose 20%, the current ratio sits below 1.0, beta is 1.37, and the average analyst target implies only ~11% upside with the lowest target below the current price. Add Pratt & Whitney's GTF recovery reclaiming share, PMA/USM erosion of the parts monopoly, unresolved tariff policy and a legacy long-term-care run-off, and the risk/reward is asymmetric to the downside on any execution stumble.

01
Valuation leaves no room for error. A ~44x forward multiple on a business with 1.37 beta prices in flawless execution of a five-year shop-visit ramp. History says aerospace cycles do not run flawlessly, and the July 2026 reaction to a beat-and-raise showed exactly how the stock behaves when reality merely meets expectations.
02
Supply chain caps the upside GE has already sold. Spare-parts orders grew 34% in H1 2026 while delinquencies rose 20% — GE is turning away revenue it has already booked. If castings, forgings and specialty-alloy capacity does not scale, the shop-visit CAGR guidance becomes a promise the supply base cannot keep, and the deferred revenue eventually invites PMA and USM substitution.
03
Aftermarket share erosion and competitive normalization. Roughly 70% of GE's economics come from parts and service on engines it already sold. FAA-approved PMA parts, used serviceable material and independent MRO shops all attack that pool, and airlines have never had more incentive to fund them. Simultaneously, Pratt & Whitney's GTF fleet returning to service reverses a share and pricing windfall GE has been enjoying since 2023.
  Industry Commentary
The Aftermarket Supercycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
The defining dynamic in commercial aerospace right now is not aircraft production — it is maintenance. Airframe delivery delays since 2019 have kept older aircraft flying far past their planned retirement, and the global fleet is simultaneously aging into first and second heavy overhauls on the CFM56 while the LEAP fleet reaches its first shop visits. For GE this compounds: CFM56 shop visits are running at the top of a 2,300–2,400 annual range while internal LEAP shop visits grew over 50% in Q2 2026 alone, with management guiding to a ~25% CAGR through 2030. Commercial services backlog reached ~$170B, up nearly $30B in eighteen months. Aftermarket work carries roughly 2–3x the margin of new equipment, which is why CES delivered a ~27.3% operating margin while equipment mix was actively diluting it. The catch: this is a known story. Every analyst model contains it, which is precisely why the stock trades at 44x.
Aerospace Supply Chain & MRO Capacity
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The industry cannot make parts fast enough. GE has posted nine consecutive quarters of double-digit output increases from critical suppliers and still saw spare-parts delinquencies climb 20% in Q2 2026 against orders up 34%. The bottleneck sits upstream in castings, forgings, single-crystal turbine blades and specialty alloys — capacity that takes years and heavy capital to add, run by suppliers who were financially damaged by the 2020–2021 demand collapse and remain understandably reluctant to over-invest. GE's response has been operational rather than financial: FLIGHT DECK lean deployment, AI-assisted inspection (a GKN collaboration cut inspection time ~90% via 3D scanning), and physical MRO capacity additions such as the new Fort Worth facility with MTU Aero Engines. LEAP turnaround time has fallen to ~100 days, two weeks better than a year ago. Progress is real and measurable — but as Larry Culp put it, this remains "much more a supply-side challenge than it is a demand" challenge, and that is a constraint on realized revenue, not merely on sentiment.
Tariffs, Trade Policy, and What It All Means
ONGOING HEADWIND
Civil aircraft and parts have traded duty-free under the WTO Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft since 1980, and the aerospace supply chain was engineered on that assumption. GE is unusually exposed to any unwinding: its flagship commercial engine is built through a 50/50 joint venture with a French company, Avio Aero manufactures in Italy, and components cross borders repeatedly before final assembly. The ~$100M tariff refund booked in Q2 2026 — which management called "not extremely material" — is best read as evidence that the policy environment is fluid rather than resolved. Costs flow through cost of goods sold and working capital, making the exposure hard to isolate and easy to underestimate.

Framing the two sides. The bull synthesis: supply constraints are a timing problem inside a demand environment that is structurally strong for a decade, tariffs have so far been a net refund rather than a net cost, and a business earning 31% ROIC with 113% cash conversion and $210B of backlog deserves a premium multiple — the debate is whether it deserves 44x or 34x, not whether it is a good business. The bear synthesis: at 44x forward earnings the stock is priced as if the supply chain resolves on schedule, tariffs stay benign, Pratt & Whitney does not recover share, and PMA/USM never meaningfully penetrates the parts pool. Four assumptions, each individually reasonable, all of which must hold. The July 2026 beat-and-raise that produced a 4% decline is the market telling you which risk it is currently more worried about.
