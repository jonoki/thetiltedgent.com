01 Company Overview

GE HealthCare designs, sells and services diagnostic imaging hardware — MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), CT (computed tomography), X-ray, molecular imaging and ultrasound — together with patient monitoring and anaesthesia equipment and a portfolio of injectable contrast agents and radiopharmaceuticals. It has been an independent public company only since January 4, 2023, when General Electric completed the separation. FY2025 revenue was $20.63B, up 4.84% from $19.67B in FY2024; the trailing twelve months through Q3 2026 come to $21.64B, and the first nine months of 2026 ran 6.8% ahead of the same months of 2025 ($15.94B against $14.93B, company release). Market capitalisation is $34.55B (452.14M shares at the October 29, 2026 close, our calculation), which places it in the middle of the large-cap medtech pack rather than at the top of it.

The central debate here is a valuation one. GEHC trades at 16.16× trailing and 15.22× forward earnings (on analysts' FY2026 adjusted EPS) — a visible discount to the multiples large-cap medical device companies usually command. The bull argument is that the discount is a leftover from the spin and from a China slump that is now in the base. The bear argument is that a business growing organically at about 4%, running a 39.3% gross margin and carrying $10.0B of debt against $2.9B of cash simply is not a premium asset. The third quarter, reported October 28, 2026, helped the bull side: adjusted EPS of $1.34 rose 25.1%, all three segments grew, and full-year guidance went up. This page tests the debate by segment: one very good business (Pharmaceutical Diagnostics), one large adequate one (Advanced Imaging Solutions), and one that is mending while it sits under a strategic review (Patient Care Solutions).

The merged imaging franchise: MRI, CT, molecular imaging, X-ray, interventional and image-guided systems, plus the ultrasound business formerly reported as Advanced Visualization Solutions, plus the imaging software and AI layer. FY2025 revenue of $14,599M against $13,986M in FY2024 is +4.4% reported; segment EBIT was $2,067M, a 14.2% margin, versus $2,080M on a smaller base in FY2024 — meaning margin went slightly backwards while revenue grew. In Q3 2026 the segment did $3,873M, +6.1% reported and +4.5% organic, with segment EBIT of $593M — a 15.3% margin, up 1.3 points on the year. Since March 18, 2026 it has included Intelerad, a maker of software for storing and reading medical images, bought for a base price of $2.3B in cash (AuntMinnie, March 19, 2026). This is a capital-equipment business: hospitals buy scanners on multi-year budget cycles, the systems are installed and serviced for a decade, and a meaningful share of the economics sits in years of service fees rather than the box. Orders cooled after a record quarter: company-wide organic orders grew 4.0% in Q3 2026, after 11.1% in Q2, with a 1.03× book-to-bill (orders booked for every $1 of revenue shipped) and a $23.7B backlog, against $23.9B at the end of June. CFO Bill Grogan reminded the call that orders had grown 5.7% in the same quarter a year earlier, a tougher comparison. The newer software is being sold on subscription — the True Definition DL deep-learning reconstruction tool, which the company says roughly doubles image resolution, is explicitly a recurring-revenue product rather than a one-time licence.

Main competitors: Siemens Healthineers, Philips, Canon Medical, and the Chinese makers Mindray and United Imaging. The Chinese makers are one reason prices in China have hardened.

Injectable contrast media and radiopharmaceutical tracers — the consumables that make the scanners produce a usable image. This is a completely different business model from the hardware: high-volume, recurring, manufactured at scale, and sold into the same installed base GEHC already owns. FY2025 revenue was $2,900M with $872M of segment EBIT — a 30.1% margin, more than double the 14.2% AIS margin. It is also the fastest-growing piece: Q3 2026 revenue of $846M grew 13.0% reported and 13.8% organic at a 29.9% segment EBIT margin, and CEO Peter Arduini told the October 28 call that "PDX has delivered at least high single-digits growth for five straight quarters." On October 5, 2026 the company agreed to buy SOFIE Biosciences, a US contract maker of PET tracers, for $945M in cash; it joins PDx when the deal closes, expected in the first half of 2027, and gives GE HealthCare a hand in the "final mile" of US tracer supply (Kirkland & Ellis, adviser to SOFIE, October 5, 2026). Grogan said on the call that SOFIE will add about 1% to company revenue a year. The growth driver is Flyrcado, the cardiac PET (positron emission tomography) perfusion tracer — see the ramp numbers below. Structurally, PDx is why the "it's just a scanner company" framing understates GEHC: the scanners are the razor and PDx sells the blades, doses hospitals buy again and again, with pharmaceutical-like margins and a regulatory moat that hardware does not have.

Flyrcado ramp (company-stated): Arduini said on the Q3 call: "We delivered approximately 680 doses for the week ending October 23rd" — up about 25% from 545 doses in the week ending July 24, 2026. On the target he said: "we feel very good about the $0.5 billion exiting 2028." In July Arduini tied that $500M to about 10% of PET perfusion studies, which implies a category of about $5B.

Main competitors: Bayer, Bracco and Guerbet in contrast media; Lantheus in cardiac PET tracers.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
INSTALLED BASE SERVICE CONTRACTS $23.7B BACKLOG FDA / REGULATORY BARRIERS RADIOPHARMA SUPPLY CHAIN CLINICAL SWITCHING COSTS GLOBAL SERVICE FOOTPRINT

The durable advantage is not the scanner — it is what happens after the scanner is bolted to the floor. An MRI or CT system is a decade-long relationship: service contracts, software upgrades, consumables, trained radiographers and a hospital IT integration nobody wants to redo. That produces a revenue stream far more stable than the equipment order book implies, and it is why a $23.7B backlog — more than a year of revenue — matters more than any single quarter's orders, which grew 11.1% in Q2 2026 and 4.0% in Q3. Layered on top, PDx is a genuinely different moat: manufacturing and distributing radioactive tracers with short half-lives requires a licensed production and logistics network that cannot be assembled quickly, which is why a new product like Flyrcado can be pushed through the existing channel at 30% margins rather than requiring a new commercial build. The pending SOFIE purchase would deepen that moat: SOFIE runs 15 contract manufacturing sites with 21 cyclotrons, the machines that make short-lived PET tracers (Applied Radiology, October 6, 2026), bringing more of the production chain in-house.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
China is a structural drag, not a one-quarter event. China revenue has fallen for two consecutive years — $2.56B in FY2023 (13.1% of revenue) → $2.14B in FY2024 (10.9%) → $2.03B in FY2025 (9.9%), a −20.7% decline over two years while the rest of the company grew. On the Q2 2026 call management said it expected a year-over-year decline in China in 2026. On the Q3 call the tone softened: Arduini said "we had 7% in the growth in the quarter. Albeit a majority of that was still driven by FX," and "The market is definitely recovering" — without saying whether the 7% referred to orders or revenue, and declining to give a full-year forecast. By his own account, most of that growth was currency movement, not more equipment sold. The company's response to the slump has been to stand up a provincial government-affairs group and to lean into volume-based procurement (VBP: bulk tenders where hospitals buy at a negotiated low price), which it argues favours transparency. That is a strategy for competing in a smaller pool, not yet proof the pool is growing again.
02
Input cost and tariff exposure on a globally manufactured product. Imaging systems are assembled from components sourced across borders, so trade policy hits the cost line directly. In July GEHC put a $250M full-year 2026 headwind on memory chips, oil and freight, and described inflation as roughly a 1.2-point margin drag in Q2 2026. In October CFO Bill Grogan said Q3 margin was again "negatively impacted by inflationary costs for memory chips, as well as the impact of higher oil prices across our supply chain," and that "between tariffs and inflation, I think both are going to be persistent." Working the other way, refunds of tariffs collected under the IEEPA emergency-powers law have flattered 2026: $115M of pre-tax refunds in the first nine months ($9M in Q3), and $55M of refund cash inside Q3 free cash flow (Q3 release). A chunk of 2026 earnings is a refund of a tax, which is not a repeatable source of profit.
03
Growth is structurally low single digit. Full-year 2026 guidance, raised in October, is about 4% organic revenue growth (from 3.0–4.0%). Reported revenue has compounded from $18.34B (FY2022) to $20.63B (FY2025) — about 4.0% a year. At that rate the entire equity story depends on wider margins and a shift in mix toward PDx, not on the top line. The PEG of 1.61 says the market is already giving credit for growth GEHC has not historically delivered.
04
Leverage inherited from the separation, now spent on deals. At September 30, 2026 the company carried $9.97B of borrowings against $2.85B of cash — roughly $7.1B of net debt — and a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.86 (borrowings over $11.63B of equity, Q3 release, our calculation). Enterprise value of about $41.7B (market cap plus net debt) is 21% above the $34.55B market cap for exactly that reason: the debt is a real claim ahead of equity holders. The balance sheet is now funding acquisitions — $2.29B spent buying businesses in the first nine months (mostly Intelerad) and $945M committed to SOFIE — while a new CFO, in the seat since September 14, learns the business.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Where the debate stands now
08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 15.2× this year's expected earnings for a business earning a 12.1% return on invested capital, with a $23.7B backlog — more than a year of revenue. Q3 2026 showed the plan working: adjusted EPS up 25.1%, a 16.6% adjusted EBIT margin, every segment growing, and a raised guide. Pharmaceutical Diagnostics grew 13.8% organically at a 29.9% EBIT margin, Flyrcado is heading for $500M of annual revenue exiting 2028, and SOFIE adds its own tracer factories. PCS bounced back to 8.8% organic growth, so the Q2 problem looks like the timing management said it was; if PCS is sold, the company-wide margin rises simply because its lowest-margin unit is gone, and the proceeds could cut net debt. China, guided down in July, now has the CEO saying the market "is definitely recovering", which the guide did not assume.

Bear Case

The discount looks deserved. Organic growth is guided to about 4%, gross margin is 39.3% — capital-equipment economics, not device economics — and orders slowed from 11.1% organic growth in Q2 to 4.0% in Q3, with the backlog slipping to $23.7B. China shrank 20.7% in two years, and the Q3 growth there was mostly currency. Roughly $7.1B of net debt, with $2.3B spent on Intelerad and $945M promised for SOFIE, means enterprise value exceeds market cap by about 21%. Part of 2026's earnings is a tariff refund — $115M so far, a one-time recovery of a tax — while the CFO calls memory-chip and tariff costs "persistent". And after a 16.9% October the stock trades within $10 of most post-print targets.

Top 3 Risks

01
Orders cool after the record. Organic orders grew 11.1% in Q2 2026 and 4.0% in Q3, with book-to-bill down to 1.03× and the backlog slipping from $23.9B to $23.7B. For an equipment maker, orders are next year's revenue: if growth in orders keeps fading while costs from memory chips and tariffs persist, the 2027 guide due in February is where it shows, and a low-growth company's share price is most sensitive to that.
02
China does not stabilise. Revenue there went $2.56B → $2.14B → $2.03B, and the Q3 growth Arduini described was mostly currency. Domestic Chinese imaging makers have an edge in VBP tenders. If China settles at a permanently smaller, lower-priced share of the business, the roughly 4% organic growth guide becomes the ceiling rather than the floor — and there is no obvious replacement growth pool of that size.
03
A lot of change at once. A new CFO since September 14, a new head of PCS from November 16 in the middle of that segment's strategic review, the Intelerad integration, the pending SOFIE deal and persistent chip and tariff costs are all running together. Any one is manageable; together they raise the odds of a stumble, and this stock has already shown it can fall 28% in two months when something goes wrong.
  Industry Commentary
China Is No Longer a Growth Market for Western Imaging
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The numbers are unambiguous: GEHC's China revenue fell from $2.56B (13.1% of total) in FY2023 to $2.14B (10.9%) in FY2024 to $2.03B (9.85%) in FY2025 — down 20.7% in two years while total revenue grew 5.5%. Two forces did it. The anti-corruption campaign in Chinese healthcare procurement froze tender activity and made hospital purchasing decisions slower and more scrutinised; the equipment-renewal stimulus programmes that were supposed to offset it have not restored the prior run rate. Meanwhile volume-based procurement is spreading from drugs into devices, and it is designed to push prices down. GEHC's response — a provincial government-affairs function and a stated preference for VBP transparency — is a competent adaptation to a smaller market. The first sign of a turn came on the Q3 2026 call, where Arduini said "The market is definitely recovering" but put most of the quarter's China growth down to currency. Until the growth shows up without the currency help, this remains the single largest reason for the discount to peers, and this portion of the discount is deserved.
Tariffs and Input Costs Meet a Global Assembly Line
ONGOING HEADWIND
An MRI system is not made in one country. That makes imaging unusually exposed to trade policy relative to, say, a software company or a domestic services business, and 2025–2026 has been a live demonstration: an 80-cent EPS tariff impact forced a 2025 guidance cut, and in July management put a $250M full-year 2026 headwind on memory chips, oil and freight, worth about 1.2 points of margin in Q2 alone. In October the new CFO said of tariffs and inflation, "I think both are going to be persistent." The $115M of tariff refunds recognised in the first nine months of 2026 shows the policy can also run the other way, which is precisely the problem: the cost line now depends on court cases and policy timing rather than on manufacturing efficiency. Against a 13.75% operating margin, a 1.2-point swing is close to a tenth of operating profit.
The Value Is Migrating from the Scanner to the Molecule and the Model
EMERGING TAILWIND
The industry's profit pool is shifting away from hardware. GEHC's own numbers make the case: in Q3 2026 PDx earned a 29.9% EBIT margin versus 15.3% for imaging systems, and grew about three times as fast (13.8% organic against 4.5%). The same logic applies to software — deep-learning reconstruction tools like True Definition DL are sold on subscription, converting a one-time feature into recurring revenue against an installed base the company already owns, and Arduini's "D3" framing (devices, disease-state focus, digital/AI) is explicitly a strategy to sell the drug and the algorithm alongside the machine. Bull framing: if PDx and software keep growing at mid-teens rates, the shift in mix alone should earn the company a higher valuation — the consolidated 39.3% gross margin is an average of a 30%-margin business and a slower, thinner one, and averages move when weights move. Bear framing: PDx is 14.1% of revenue. At 15% growth against a 71%-of-revenue segment growing 4–5%, mix shift is a decade-long process, not a catalyst — and the market is being asked to pay for 2030's mix with 2026's cash flow.
