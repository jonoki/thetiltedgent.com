01 Company Overview

GE Vernova is the energy half of the old General Electric conglomerate, separated on April 2, 2024 and now the largest pure-play Western supplier of the equipment that generates and moves electricity: heavy-duty gas turbines, nuclear reactors, hydro, wind turbines, and the transformers, switchgear and HVDC systems that make up the grid. Customers using GE Vernova's installed base generate roughly 25% of the world's electricity. FY2025 revenue was $38.07B, up 9.0% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue has since accelerated to $41.37B (+13.0%). Total backlog reached $176B at the end of Q2 2026 — about 4.3x trailing revenue — with services accounting for more than 55% of it.

At a ~$277B market capitalization GEV is roughly the 54th most valuable listed company in the world (approximately #39 in the US and #25 on the NYSE), having compounded from about $40B at the spin. The re-rating is not a story about renewables: it is a story about gas turbines and grid equipment being the physical bottleneck of the AI build-out. Q2 2026 orders were $24.2B, up 88% organically, gas equipment backlog reached 53 GW with a further 63 GW under slot reservation agreements, and management now targets at least 125 GW contracted by year-end 2026. Data-center-linked orders alone exceeded $5B year-to-date, more than double the full-year 2025 total.

This is the profit engine and the entire bull case. GE Vernova is one of three Western-plus-Japanese suppliers of heavy-duty gas turbines and holds roughly 30% of the global installed base — but a much larger share in the United States, where about 55% of installed gas-fired capacity runs on GE technology. The factories are effectively sold out. Gas equipment backlog rose to 53 GW in Q2 2026 from 44 GW a quarter earlier, and customers have paid to reserve a further 63 GW of manufacturing slots (up from 56 GW) under slot reservation agreements — non-cancellable-deposit contracts that convert into firm orders later. Management signed 20 GW of new gas equipment contracts in the quarter, including 18 GW of slot reservations, and targets at least 125 GW contracted by year-end 2026. Output is being ramped from a ~20 GW annualized run-rate in Q3 2026 toward 24 GW in 2028 and 30 GW in 2030. Because delivery slots for 2028–29 are already spoken for, GEV is pricing new bookings against scarcity rather than against competitors — the single most important fact in the equity story. Alongside gas sit two smaller but strategically loud businesses: nuclear (GE Vernova Hitachi's BWRX-300 small modular reactor, in construction at Ontario Power Generation's Darlington site and selected by TVA at Clinch River) and hydro, plus a steam business now largely limited to servicing an existing fleet. Roughly 70%+ of Power's profit comes from services on that installed base, not from selling new machines.

The problem child, and the reason the stock still has a bear case. GE Vernova's moat is best understood as a physical capacity constraint that the company controls and cannot quickly be replicated. Heavy-duty gas turbines are built in a handful of plants worldwide by three credible suppliers; the forgings, castings and single-crystal hot-section components sit behind a supply base that takes half a decade and heavy capital to expand, and a new entrant would need a decade plus a reference fleet before a utility would underwrite thirty years of dispatch on its machine. That scarcity has been converted into contractual form through slot reservation agreements — customers now pay deposits simply for the right to a future manufacturing slot, which is the clearest possible evidence of pricing power. Behind the equipment sits the real annuity: multi-decade service agreements on an installed base generating a quarter of the world's electricity, where the switching cost of moving to a third-party servicer is measured in warranty exposure and outage risk rather than in price. The same dynamic now applies in Electrification, where transformer and HVDC lead times of three to four years mean backlog is priced on availability. More than 55% of the $176B backlog is services, which is why the earnings profile is far less cyclical than the equipment order chart suggests.

Offshore wind contract liabilities. The Haliade-X backlog is a fixed-price obligation signed against a pre-inflation cost structure, and each quarter of execution has produced further charges rather than a clean exit. H1 2026 segment EBITDA losses of $657M already exceed the roughly $400M management guides for the full year, meaning the guidance depends on an H2 improvement investors have not yet seen. Exiting is not costless: cancellation would trigger penalties and litigation with counterparties who priced projects around delivery.

Customer concentration in a single demand narrative. An outsized share of incremental orders now traces to data-center load growth — over $5B of data-center orders year-to-date, more than double all of 2025. Hyperscaler capital spending is set by a handful of decision-makers with a history of stepping capex in discrete moves. A pause of even two quarters in that cohort would not dent the existing backlog, but it would immediately break the order-growth trajectory the multiple is priced on.

Execution and supply chain on a 50% capacity ramp. Going from ~20 GW of annual gas output to 30 GW by 2030 requires the same castings, forgings and skilled-labour base that constrains everyone. GEV has effectively pre-sold this capacity; failing to deliver on schedule converts a pricing advantage into liquidated damages and customer defection to Mitsubishi and Siemens Energy, both of whom are expanding.

Technology substitution over the long term. The gas franchise depends on gas remaining the marginal firm-capacity choice. Grid-scale storage economics, nuclear SMRs (including GEV's own BWRX-300), and geothermal or fuel-cell alternatives all compete for the same "firm power for data centers" dollar. The threat is not to the 2026–2030 backlog, which is contracted, but to the terminal value implied by a ~49x forward multiple.

Notable One-Off Items. GE Vernova's trailing GAAP figures are among the most distorted of any large-cap right now. Strip these before valuing anything:

· Prolec GE step-acquisition gain — $3.99B pre-tax. When GEV acquired the remaining interest in the Prolec GE transformer joint venture, accounting rules required the previously held stake to be remeasured to fair value, producing a roughly $4.0B non-cash pre-tax gain. This single item is the reason trailing net income ($9.53B) is nearly double FY2025's ($4.88B), why TTM EPS is $34.94 against a ~$15.01 forward consensus, and why ROE reads 82.6%. It also explains the split between Electrification's +68% reported and +29% organic growth.

· Offshore wind contract losses. Recurring rather than one-off, but non-representative of the go-forward business: Wind segment EBITDA was −$275M in Q2 2026 and −$657M in H1, against −$312M in H1 2025. Management's ~$400M full-year loss guidance requires a materially better H2. Treat any "ex-Wind" earnings bridge with care — the losses are real cash.

· Portfolio actions. A $330M gain on the sale of Proficy (H1 2026), approximately $600M of pre-tax proceeds from the China XD Electric divestiture, and $106M of Prolec inventory step-up amortization running through cost of sales.

· Separation, restructuring and pension. Roughly $102M of restructuring and separation costs in Q2 2026 (residual spin-related expense is finally running off) plus a $500M voluntary contribution to the GE Energy Pension Plan — a real cash outflow that does not appear in adjusted EBITDA.

· Cash flow quality. TTM free cash flow of $12.44B against $1.8B of GAAP operating income is not sustainable conversion. The gap is customer deposits, slot-reservation payments and contract-liability build on a $176B backlog. Cash arriving years ahead of revenue is a genuine competitive advantage — but it is a balance-sheet timing benefit, not earnings, and it unwinds as those units ship.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Readings above 70 suggest the stock has run hot and may be due for a pause; below 30 suggests it has been beaten down and may be due for a bounce. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation tool — and on a 29-month series it should be read as indicative rather than statistically robust.

Since-spin trajectory. GE Vernova closed its first month as an independent company at roughly $153.75 (April 2024) and closed at $1,039.90 on August 12, 2026 — a price return of approximately +576% in twenty-eight months, or on the order of 115% annualized. Over the same window the S&P 500 returned roughly +48% on price. It is among the largest destructions of a "conglomerate discount" on record: a business the market valued at roughly $40B at separation, embedded inside a company nobody wanted to own for the energy exposure, is now a $277B index heavyweight. Almost none of that came from the renewable narrative the ticker was originally associated with. It came from gas turbines and grid hardware becoming the scarce input to artificial intelligence.

Against the electrical-equipment peer group the comparison is less flattering than it looks: Siemens Energy, Hitachi Energy's parent and the US electrical names have all re-rated hard on the same grid-capex theme, so GEV's outperformance is a matter of degree rather than of kind. The technical picture at August 12 is mixed — price is above all three moving averages after the July drawdown, but 13% below the June high, and monthly RSI has cooled from clearly overbought readings in the spring. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

GE Vernova sells the physically scarce input to the AI build-out and has already contracted the next four years of it. Gas equipment backlog stands at 53 GW with a further 63 GW under paid slot reservations, capacity is effectively sold out through 2028–29, and output is being ramped from ~20 GW to 30 GW by 2030 — every incremental gigawatt already spoken for at scarcity pricing. Q2 orders grew 88% organically to $24.2B, backlog reached $176B (4.3x revenue, 55%+ services), and Electrification has become a second engine at 18.4% margins on 29% organic growth. The financial profile is unusual for an industrial: $9.4B net cash, 41% ROIC, and free cash flow guided to $11.5–12.5B because customers fund the working capital. With offshore wind losses running off and consensus EPS growth of ~66% in 2027, the PEG of 1.5 is the cheapest thing about the stock.

Bear Case

At ~49x forward earnings — the honest multiple, since trailing 29.8x rests on a $4.0B one-time Prolec gain — the market is extrapolating peak gas pricing and a hyperscaler capex cycle into perpetuity. Operating margin is only 6.6%; the 23% net margin and 82.6% ROE are accounting artefacts. Wind lost $657M of EBITDA in H1 against a ~$400M full-year guide, so the bridge to guidance is already strained. The Q2 reaction proved the setup: record orders, record cash, raised guidance and the stock still fell 16% off its high on a single EPS miss. Morgan Stanley's downgrade to Underweight and Mizuho's $949 target frame the risk precisely — this is a good business at a price that requires the AI power narrative to remain intact for a decade, and the 52-week range from $530 to $1,196 shows how quickly that narrative reprices.

Data-center orders — now above $5B year-to-date and more than double all of 2025 — depend on a handful of hyperscaler capex decisions. Both reverse — the gain never repeats, and the deposits unwind as units ship. An investor anchoring on 29.8x trailing earnings or a 30% FCF yield on operating income is underwriting numbers that do not describe the business.
  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Electricity Demand — The Defining Driver
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Everything in this analysis reduces to one dynamic: after two decades of essentially flat US electricity demand, load growth has returned, and the marginal new load is a data center that needs firm, dispatchable power immediately. The constraint is no longer capital or permitting — it is manufacturing capacity for turbines and transformers. GE Vernova's gas equipment backlog rose from 44 GW to 53 GW in a single quarter, with a further 63 GW of paid slot reservations and a year-end target of at least 125 GW contracted. Delivery slots into 2028–29 are gone. That is why slot reservation agreements exist at all — customers are paying deposits for the right to a place in a queue, which is the most direct evidence of pricing power available in any industrial market today. Data-center-linked orders alone passed $5B year-to-date, more than double the full-year 2025 total. The bull reading is that this is a decade-long structural shift with contracted visibility. The bear reading is that a bottleneck is, by definition, temporary: GEV is expanding to 30 GW by 2030, and so are Mitsubishi Power and Siemens Energy. The pricing that justifies a ~49x multiple exists precisely because the shortage does.
The Grid Capital Expenditure Supercycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
The less-discussed half of the story, and arguably the better business. Global grid capital spending is heading toward roughly $400B a year against an installed base built for a different century, and the electrification of transport, heat and industry compounds the same demand. Lead times for large power transformers now run three to four years, high-voltage cable and HVDC converter capacity is similarly booked, and every utility interconnection queue in the developed world is congested. GE Vernova's response was to buy out its partner in Prolec GE, taking full control of a large North American transformer manufacturing base — a transaction that added 39 points of reported growth to Electrification in Q2 2026 and pushed segment EBITDA margin to 18.4%, now level with Power. FY2026 Electrification guidance is $14.5–15.0B at an 18–20% margin. Competitive intensity here is higher than in gas — Hitachi Energy leads at roughly 20% share, with Schneider and Eaton strong in adjacent voltage classes — but with the entire industry supply-constrained, share competition has been replaced by capacity allocation. The risk is the classic capital-goods one: everyone is adding capacity into the shortage, and 2028 order books will tell you whether pricing held.
Offshore Wind — The Legacy Liability, and How to Frame the Whole Stock
ONGOING HEADWIND
Western offshore wind has been one of the worst capital-allocation episodes in modern industrial history, and GE Vernova owns a share of it. Fixed-price Haliade-X contracts signed before the 2021–2023 inflation in steel, vessels and installation labour cannot be repriced, and the industry-wide record — cancellations, impairments, walked-away leases — shows that hope is not a strategy for this backlog. GEV's Wind segment lost $275M of EBITDA in Q2 2026 and $657M in H1, worse than the $312M lost in H1 2025, on revenue down 10%. Management is running a disciplined wind-down: no new offshore orders, execute the remaining book, preserve the onshore services annuity on a 59,000-turbine, 120 GW+ installed base. The ~$400M full-year loss guidance is the single most vulnerable number in the model, and Q3 is where it gets tested.

Framing the two sides. The bull synthesis: this is a $176B backlog business with sold-out gas capacity, a second high-margin growth engine in Electrification, $9.4B of net cash, customer-funded working capital, and a known ~$1B annualized drag that is contractually finite and shrinking. Value it on 2028 earnings with Wind at zero and the multiple looks defensible. The bear synthesis: strip the Prolec gain and you have an industrial earning 6.6% operating margins at 49x forward earnings, with a guidance bridge already broken in the loss-making segment, in a cycle whose defining input — hyperscaler capex — is set by five companies and has historically moved in steps rather than lines. Both readings use the same disclosure. The July 22 print, where record orders and a $5B free-cash-flow quarter produced a 16% drawdown, is the market telling you which risk it currently weights more heavily.
