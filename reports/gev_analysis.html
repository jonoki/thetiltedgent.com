01 Company Overview

GE Vernova is the energy half of the old General Electric conglomerate, separated on April 2, 2024 and now the largest pure-play Western supplier of the equipment that generates and moves electricity: heavy-duty gas turbines, nuclear reactors, hydro, wind turbines, and the transformers, switchgear and HVDC systems that make up the grid. Customers using GE Vernova's installed base generate roughly 25% of the world's electricity. FY2025 revenue was $38.07B, up 9.0% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue has since accelerated to $44.04B (+16.9%). Total backlog — signed orders not yet delivered — reached $197B at the end of Q3 2026, up $20B in the quarter and about 4.5x trailing revenue; management expects it to pass $200B in the fourth quarter.

At a ~$235B market capitalization GEV is one of the larger companies in the S&P 500, up from roughly $40B at the spin. The rise in the stock is not a story about renewables: it is a story about gas turbines and grid equipment being the physical bottleneck of the AI build-out. Q3 2026 orders were $29.3B, up 96% organically (organic strips out acquisitions and currency), Power alone booked $21.8B, and gas equipment under contract rose to 122 GW — 64 GW in firm backlog plus 58 GW under slot reservation agreements, up from 116 GW in June. Management raised its year-end target from at least 125 GW to at least 130 GW.

What changed between September 1 and October 29. The share price ended almost where it started — $898.53 to $887.85, down 1.2% — but the path was not quiet. The stock closed September at $950.49, fell 10.6% on October 22 on a day with no company filing or release, and reached $815.00 on October 23 before the print. Then the Q3 report on October 28 beat on every headline line: revenue $12.64B (+27%), diluted EPS $4.81 against a Nasdaq consensus of $4.03, free cash flow $5.4B, and higher full-year guidance for revenue ($46.5–47.5B), adjusted EBITDA margin (13.5–14.5%) and free cash flow ($16–17B, from $11.5–12.5B). The same morning GE Vernova agreed to buy Trench Group, a high-voltage grid equipment maker, for $9.0B, and doubled its buyback authorization to $20B. The shares still closed down 1.90% that day; the Motley Fool tied the selling to Wind, where the full-year loss guide widened from about $400M to $400–500M. Electrification orders also fell 9% organically from a year earlier, and TD Cowen cited order concerns when it cut its target the next day. At $887.85 the stock trades at about 34x analysts' consensus FY2027 EPS of $25.79 (adjusted basis, StockAnalysis, read Oct 30).

This is the profit engine and the entire bull case. GE Vernova is one of three Western-plus-Japanese suppliers of heavy-duty gas turbines and has the world's largest installed base of them, about 7,000 gas turbines (company figures). The factories are effectively sold out. In Q3 2026 Power booked $21.8B of orders, up 178%, and gas equipment in firm backlog rose to 64 GW from 53 GW, while slot reservations — paid deposits that hold a future manufacturing slot and convert into orders later — fell to 58 GW from 63 GW. Power's segment EBITDA nearly doubled to $1.30B, a 21.4% margin against 13.4% a year earlier, and its full-year margin guide rose to 19–21%. CEO Scott Strazik on the call: "We are seeing strong equipment pricing and total lifecycle economics in gas." Annual output is being ramped toward 24 GW in 2028 and 30 GW by 2030 (targets given with the Q2 2026 results). Because delivery slots for 2028–29 are already spoken for, GEV is pricing new bookings against scarcity rather than against competitors — the single most important fact in the equity story. The problem child, and the reason the stock still has a bear case. GE Vernova has an installed base of roughly 59,000 turbines totalling 120 GW+, and its onshore business — concentrated in North America — is a reasonable, low-margin equipment-and-service franchise with global share in the mid-single digits (roughly low-teens excluding the Chinese domestic market). Offshore is the wound. The Haliade-X fixed-price contracts signed in the 2020–2022 period were priced before steel, vessel and installation cost inflation, and each quarter of execution has produced further charges: Wind posted −$275M of segment EBITDA in Q2 2026 and −$657M across the first half. The strategy is explicit: build out the remaining offshore backlog, book no new offshore orders, and let the segment shrink to an onshore services annuity.

CAPTIVE TRANSFORMER CAPACITY (PROLEC)

GE Vernova's moat is best understood as a physical capacity constraint that the company controls and cannot quickly be replicated. Heavy-duty gas turbines are built in a handful of plants worldwide by three credible suppliers; the forgings, castings and single-crystal hot-section components sit behind a supply base that takes half a decade and heavy capital to expand, and a new entrant would need a decade plus a reference fleet before a utility would commit to running its machine for thirty years. That scarcity has been converted into contractual form through slot reservation agreements — customers now pay deposits simply for the right to a future manufacturing slot, which is the clearest possible evidence of pricing power. Behind the equipment sits the real annuity: multi-decade service agreements on an installed base generating a quarter of the world's electricity, where the switching cost of moving to a third-party servicer is measured in warranty exposure and outage risk rather than in price. The same dynamic now applies in Electrification, where transformer and HVDC lead times of three to four years mean backlog is priced on availability. Services — maintaining and upgrading machines already installed — are a large share of the $197B backlog, which is why the earnings profile is far less cyclical than the equipment order chart suggests. Customers also pay ahead: contract liabilities and deferred income, mostly cash received before delivery, reached $45.7B at September 30, up from $25.8B at the start of the year (Q3 10-Q).

Offshore wind contract liabilities. The Haliade-X backlog is a fixed-price obligation signed against a pre-inflation cost structure, and the first half produced $657M of segment EBITDA losses. Q3 turned a $72M profit, but management widened the full-year loss guide to $400–500M, so the run-off is still costing more than first planned. Exiting is not costless: cancellation would trigger penalties and litigation with counterparties who priced projects around delivery.

Customer concentration in a single demand narrative. An outsized share of new orders traces to data-center load growth: data-center orders passed $6B in the first nine months of 2026, about $1.4B of them in the third quarter (CEO Scott Strazik on the Q3 call), and the $5B reached by mid-year was already more than double all of 2025. Hyperscaler capital spending is set by a handful of decision-makers with a history of stepping capex in discrete moves. A pause of even two quarters in that group would not dent the existing backlog, but it would break the order growth the share price is built on.

Execution and supply chain on a 50% capacity ramp. Going from ~20 GW of annual gas output to 30 GW by 2030 requires the same castings, forgings and skilled-labour base that constrains everyone. GEV has effectively pre-sold this capacity; failing to deliver on schedule converts a pricing advantage into liquidated damages and customer defection to Mitsubishi and Siemens Energy, both of whom are expanding.

Technology substitution over the long term. The gas business depends on gas remaining the usual choice for new power plants that can run whenever they are needed. Grid-scale storage economics, nuclear SMRs (including GEV's own BWRX-300), and geothermal or fuel-cell alternatives all compete for the same "firm power for data centers" dollar. The threat is not to the 2026–2030 backlog, which is contracted, but to the long-run value implied by a price of ~34x FY2027 consensus earnings.

Notable One-Off Items. GE Vernova's trailing GAAP figures are among the most distorted of any large-cap right now. Strip these before valuing anything:

· Prolec GE step-acquisition gain — $3.99B pre-tax. When GEV acquired the remaining interest in the Prolec GE transformer joint venture, accounting rules required the previously held stake to be remeasured to fair value, producing a roughly $4.0B non-cash pre-tax gain. It sits in the nine-month 2026 "other income" line of $4,947M (against $455M a year earlier, Q3 10-Q) and is the main reason trailing net income ($10.36B) is more than double FY2025's ($4.88B), why TTM EPS is $38.20 against a $15.62 FY2026 consensus, and why ROE reads 93%. The Prolec consolidation also explains the split between Electrification's +51% reported and +16% organic revenue growth in Q3.

· Offshore wind contract losses. Recurring rather than one-off, but not representative of the business going forward: Wind segment EBITDA was −$657M in H1 2026 and +$72M in Q3, −$585M for the nine months against −$373M a year earlier. Full-year loss guidance is now $400–500M. Treat any "ex-Wind" earnings bridge with care — the losses are real cash.

· Portfolio actions. A $366M pre-tax gain on the sale of the Proficy software business, gains on selling the rest of the stake in China XD Electric, and $106M of extra cost of sales because Prolec's inventory was marked up to fair value when it was bought, all in the first nine months of 2026 (Q3 release).

· Separation, restructuring and pension. $109M of restructuring and other charges and $92M of separation costs in the first nine months of 2026, both left out of adjusted EBITDA. In Q3 the company also paid about $350M under agreements moving part of its retiree benefit obligations to an insurer (Q3 release).

· Cash flow quality. TTM free cash flow of $17.12B against nine-month 2026 GAAP operating income of $2.23B is not sustainable conversion. The gap is customer deposits, slot-reservation payments and contract liabilities, which rose from $25.8B to $45.7B in nine months on a $197B backlog. Cash arriving years ahead of revenue is a genuine competitive advantage — but it is a balance-sheet timing benefit, not earnings, and it unwinds as those units ship.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

GE Vernova sells the physically scarce input to the AI build-out and has already contracted the next four years of it. Gas equipment under contract stands at 122 GW — 64 GW in firm backlog and 58 GW under paid slot reservations — with a target of at least 130 GW by year-end, capacity is effectively sold out through 2028–29, and output is being ramped toward 30 GW a year by 2030. Q3 orders grew 96% organically to $29.3B, backlog reached $197B (about 4.5x trailing revenue), Power's margin jumped to 21.4% and Electrification earned 19.1%. The financial profile is unusual for an industrial: roughly $13B of net cash, a 51% return on invested capital (StockAnalysis), and free cash flow now guided to $16–17B because customers fund the working capital. With consensus EPS growth of 65.1% in 2027 ($15.62 to $25.79), the PEG is 0.98. And the quarter delivered without the price following: guidance went up on every line except Wind's loss, and the shares ended the two sessions after the print below where they were on September 1. Every target in StockAnalysis's range sits above the market, the lowest at $940.

Bear Case

At ~34x FY2027 consensus earnings — the honest multiple, since the trailing 23.2x rests on a $4.0B one-time Prolec gain — and ~57x the FY2026 consensus of $15.62, the market is still paying for peak gas pricing and a hyperscaler spending cycle a long way out. Trailing operating margin is only 8.2%; the 23.5% net margin and 93% ROE are accounting artefacts. Wind's full-year loss guide widened again, to $400–500M, and Electrification orders fell 9% organically in Q3, a reminder that the grid business can cool too. Cash has run far ahead of profit: contract liabilities, mostly customer prepayments, rose by $19.9B in nine months, and that cash is used up as the equipment ships. The October action is the bear case in miniature. The stock fell 10.6% in a day with no company news, then sat flat through a beat-and-raise, and most analysts who moved cut their targets. The bear reading: the market is already looking past this year's numbers to the end of the order boom. The 52-week range from $530 to $1,196 shows how quickly this story is repriced in both directions.

  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Electricity Demand — The Defining Driver
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Everything in this analysis reduces to one dynamic: after two decades of essentially flat US electricity demand, load growth has returned, and much of the new load is data centers that need power that can run around the clock, immediately. The constraint is no longer capital or permitting — it is manufacturing capacity for turbines and transformers. GE Vernova's gas equipment backlog rose from 44 GW in March to 53 GW in June and 64 GW in September, with a further 58 GW of paid slot reservations and a year-end target of at least 130 GW under contract. Delivery slots into 2028–29 are gone. That is why slot reservation agreements exist at all — customers are paying deposits for the right to a place in a queue, which is direct evidence of pricing power. Data-center orders passed $6B in the first nine months of 2026; the $5B reached by mid-year was already more than double the full-year 2025 total. The bull reading is that this is a decade-long structural shift with contracted visibility. The bear reading is that a bottleneck is, by definition, temporary: GEV is expanding to 30 GW by 2030, and so are Mitsubishi Power and Siemens Energy. The pricing that supports a price of ~34x FY2027 earnings exists precisely because the shortage does.

The peak-cycle argument, and why it now matters more than the order book. The clearest version came not from a GE Vernova analyst but from Barclays, which downgraded Siemens Energy to Underweight on July 7, 2026, arguing that gas-sector orders will peak this year and that data-center orders could slow because the next three to four years of deliveries are already covered by order books (as reported by Il Sole 24 Ore). GEV closed down 6.5% that day on a competitor's downgrade, which tells you the market treats these as one trade. Q3 put that thesis to a test, and on orders the bears were wrong for another quarter: Power orders rose 178% to $21.8B, total orders 96% organically, and the backlog grew $20B in three months to $197B. Yet the shares did not rise. One reading: the peak-cycle argument is about the years after the backlog — how fast new orders slow once the shortage eases — and one more record quarter does not settle that. The softer points the bears could use were outside gas: Electrification orders down 9% organically, and Wind's loss guide widened.
The Grid Capital Expenditure Supercycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
The less-discussed half of the story, and arguably the better business. Global grid capital spending is heading toward roughly $400B a year against an installed base built for a different century, and the electrification of transport, heat and industry compounds the same demand. Lead times for large power transformers now run three to four years, high-voltage cable and HVDC converter capacity is similarly booked, and every utility interconnection queue in the developed world is congested. GE Vernova's response was to buy out its partner in Prolec GE, taking full control of a large North American transformer manufacturing base — a transaction that added 35 points of reported growth to Electrification in Q3 2026 (51% reported against 16% organic) and helped lift segment EBITDA margin to 19.1%. Then, on October 28, it agreed to buy Trench Group, a high-voltage equipment maker, for $9.0B, closing expected in late 2027. FY2026 Electrification guidance is $14.5–15.0B at an 18–20% margin, trending toward the high end. Competitive intensity here is higher than in gas — Hitachi Energy leads at roughly 20% share, with Schneider and Eaton strong in adjacent voltage classes — but with the entire industry supply-constrained, share competition has been replaced by capacity allocation. The risk is the classic capital-goods one: everyone is adding capacity into the shortage, and 2028 order books will tell you whether pricing held.
Offshore Wind — The Legacy Liability, and How to Frame the Whole Stock
ONGOING HEADWIND
Western offshore wind has been one of the worst capital-allocation episodes in modern industrial history, and GE Vernova owns a share of it. Fixed-price Haliade-X contracts signed before the 2021–2023 inflation in steel, vessels and installation labour cannot be repriced, and the industry-wide record — cancellations, impairments, walked-away leases — shows that hope is not a strategy for this backlog. GEV's Wind segment lost $657M of EBITDA in H1 2026, then earned $72M in Q3, for −$585M over nine months against −$373M a year earlier. Management is running a disciplined wind-down: no new offshore orders, execute the remaining book, preserve the onshore services annuity on a 59,000-turbine, 120 GW+ installed base. The full-year loss guide, now $400–500M from about $400M, implies a profitable fourth quarter in Wind, and that is the most fragile number in the guidance.

Framing the two sides. The bull synthesis: this is a $197B backlog business with sold-out gas capacity, a second high-margin growth engine in Electrification now being enlarged by Trench, roughly $13B of net cash, customer-funded working capital, and a Wind drag that is contractually finite and shrinking. Value it on 2028 earnings with Wind at zero and the price looks defensible. The bear synthesis: strip the Prolec gain and you have an industrial earning an 8.2% trailing operating margin at ~34x next year's expected earnings, with the Wind guide widened again, in a cycle whose defining input — hyperscaler spending — is set by a handful of companies and has historically moved in steps rather than lines. The two prints of 2026's second half point the same way: July's record orders and $5B free-cash-flow quarter were followed by a 16% fall to the July month-end; October's beat-and-raise left the price where it started. For a report reader the practical conclusion is narrow and worth stating plainly: the business keeps beating its own targets, and the share price is now set by how long investors believe the order boom will last, not by the latest quarter.
