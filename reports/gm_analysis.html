01 Company Overview

General Motors designs, builds and finances trucks, crossovers, cars and parts under Chevrolet, GMC, Buick and Cadillac, through three reported segments — GM North America, GM International and GM Financial. FY2025 revenue was $185.02B, down 1.3% from $187.44B in FY2024; trailing-twelve-month revenue through Q3 2026 is $186.95B, down 0.3% on the same twelve months a year earlier (our calculation from GM's releases). At a $64.62B market capitalisation GM ranks #190 in the S&P 500 — a company selling nearly $187B of product a year is valued at about a third of one year's revenue.

The central debate on this page is not whether GM is profitable. It is. Third-quarter 2026 EBIT-adjusted was $4.1B at an 8.2% margin, up from $3.4B a year earlier, and adjusted diluted EPS of $3.70 was up 32%, on top of GM's best first half ever for adjusted EPS. The debate is what that profit is worth. Essentially all of it comes from full-size pickups and SUVs built and sold in North America — a franchise with a 42%+ share and no credible near-term challenger, but also one that is cyclical, tariff-exposed, and being used to fund an electric transition that has so far produced $11.1B of charges rather than returns. The market's answer is a 5.4× forward earnings multiple (price divided by analysts' forecast earnings), lower than before the quarter: the shares fell 6.84% on the day of a results beat. Sections 04 and 08 take that apart rather than calling it cheap.

Pricing is fading as a driver: Jacobson put North American pricing $600M above the prior year in the first half on the Q2 call, and $400M above it for the year to date on the Q3 call. The franchise underneath it is the Silverado / Sierra / Tahoe / Yukon / Suburban / Escalade complex: GM held more than 42% of the U.S. full-size pickup market through the first half of 2026, more than 10 percentage points above its closest competitor (Barra, Q2 call); by the Q3 call the new Silverado and Sierra were in production at Silao in Mexico, with Fort Wayne and Orion to follow in 2027. On the Q3 call Barra said GM expects to build roughly as many trucks in 2027 as in 2026, with extra capacity arriving in 2028 for segment and share growth.

FY26 EV Loss Improvement

Quantify this half honestly, because it is where the equity story was supposed to live. GM booked $7.6B of EV-related charges in the second half of 2025 — impairments, capacity right-sizing, contract cancellations and supplier settlements, including discontinuing the BrightDrop electric van — then a further $1.1B in Q1 2026 and $2.3B in Q2 2026, of which $700M was specifically to "right size the battery supply chain," and a smaller $238M in Q3 2026 (the "EV strategic realignment" line in the Q3 release). Cumulative: $11.1B; through Q2, $7.2B of cash impact was expected and $4.5B already paid (about 60% of the total). Jacobson stated on the Q2 call that these actions "substantially complete the material cash charges we expect to incur as we align our EV capacity" with the changed regulatory environment. Physically, the retrenchment means: about 1,200 layoffs at Factory Zero in Detroit (Hummer EV, Silverado EV, Sierra EV, Escalade IQ); Ultium Cells battery plants in Ohio and Tennessee — the joint venture with LG Energy Solution — halting cell production from January, with ~1,550 workers on temporary layoff and a further 550 laid off indefinitely at the Ohio plant (Ohio restarted in August after seven months; Tennessee has been partly converted to make cells for energy storage); a GM stake in one Ultium plant sold outright; and Orion Assembly switched from EVs to gasoline full-size pickups, which Barra said on the Q3 call will launch there in the second quarter of 2027. Jobs affected across EV and battery operations, temporary layoffs included: more than 3,400 (WardsAuto, October 2025). On the demand side, Jacobson pointed on the Q2 call to "a smaller EV market following reductions in consumer incentives" — the U.S. federal purchase credit expiry — while framing the volume decision as a deliberate choice, and guiding EV wholesale volumes to rise slightly in H2 2026. The financial payoff is real but modest: EV losses are expected to improve $1.0–1.5B in 2026, a range repeated on the Q3 call, where Jacobson also pointed to a further step up in EV profitability in 2028–2029 as new battery chemistry and next-generation cells arrive. That is the size of the prize from unwinding a programme that cost $11.1B to unwind.

Honest framing: GM is not exiting EVs — it held 13% of the U.S. EV market in Q1 2026, second only to Tesla (Barra, Q1 call). It is de-risking the capital commitment. The charge is the price of having built capacity for a demand curve that did not arrive.

China is now a story about not losing money. GM's China joint ventures contributed $32M of equity income in Q3 2026, down from $80M a year earlier and $83M in Q2 — under 1% of consolidated EBIT-adjusted, and on a simple annualisation about $130M against a company guided to $14.5–15.5B of EBIT-adjusted. GM International as a whole earned $28M of EBIT-adjusted in the quarter, against $226M a year earlier. Jacobson's framing on the Q2 call was that the China team "deserves a lot of credit for the restructuring work" that enabled profitability "despite the very difficult environment." Read that plainly: the achievement in China is now positive equity income rather than growth. Equity income is GM's share of the after-tax profit of joint ventures it does not consolidate — so China contributes to earnings without appearing in the revenue line at all, which is why GM's China exposure is easy to miss in a revenue-mix chart and impossible to miss in a P/E. The cause is not GM-specific: domestic Chinese OEMs have taken the volume, and the price competition among them has been severe enough to push most foreign joint ventures toward or below breakeven. Today GM's China equity income is tens of millions of dollars a quarter.

Analytical point: a segment that contributes under 1% of operating profit cannot support a valuation argument in either direction. China has already been de-rated to approximately zero in GM's earnings — which also means it is no longer a source of downside surprise.

GM Financial originates the loans and leases that move the trucks, and it is a genuinely good business inside a mediocre one: $668M of EBT-adjusted in Q3 2026 (down from $804M a year earlier), after $605M in Q2, and guided to $2.5–3.0B for the full year, a range repeated on the Q3 call — roughly 18% of the midpoint of consolidated EBIT-adjusted guidance, on our arithmetic. Two things follow. First, a meaningful slice of GM's earnings is a spread-lending business whose profitability depends on funding costs, used-vehicle residual values and credit losses, not on how many Silverados leave the lot. Second — and this is the single most abused number in GM valuation work — the captive's debt sits in the consolidated enterprise value. GM's enterprise value of about $165B (stockanalysis) against a $64.62B market cap and a 2.00 debt-to-equity ratio is not an industrial balance sheet under stress; it is a finance book. Anyone computing EV/EBITDA on GM without separating the captive is measuring the wrong company.

Watch item: residual values. A captive lender's earnings quality is only as good as the used-truck market that backs its lease portfolio.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
FULL-SIZE TRUCK FRANCHISE 42%+ PICKUP SHARE DEALER NETWORK SCALE BODY-ON-FRAME PLATFORM DEPTH CAPTIVE FINANCE ARM SUPER CRUISE INSTALLED BASE US MANUFACTURING FOOTPRINT

GM's moat is one product category, and it is a deep one. The U.S. full-size pickup and body-on-frame SUV market is a near-duopoly with GM at more than 42% share, over 10 points clear of its nearest rival, defended by three things a new entrant cannot buy: assembly plants tooled for body-on-frame at volume, a dealer and service network that services a working vehicle, and a customer base whose brand loyalty in this segment is measured in decades and generations. That structure is why an 8.7% segment margin is achievable in a business whose global peers are currently losing tens of billions — Ford lost $7.40B and Stellantis $22.25B on a trailing-twelve-month basis against GM's positive $3.96B (itself depressed by EV charges; our sum of GM's last four reported quarters). A second, newer layer is Super Cruise — GM's hands-off, eyes-on highway driver-assistance system, now being made standard on high-end Silverado and Sierra trims and optional on most of the rest, which Barra sized at ~160,000 incremental Super Cruise units from that packaging change alone; on the Q3 call she said full-size-truck Super Cruise take-up should grow four to five times next year, and put the fleet of Super Cruise-equipped vehicles at about one million. It is sold as a subscription: a small, high-margin software annuity attached to the truck franchise rather than a separate bet. The moat's limit is equally clear — it does not extend to compact cars, it does not extend to China, and it did not extend to electric vehicles, where GM spent $11.1B discovering that a 100-year-old distribution advantage is worth less when the product is new.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Single-category profit concentration. GMNA generated roughly 88% of Q3 2026 EBIT-adjusted, and within GMNA the full-size truck and SUV line generates a disproportionate share of that. Any shock specific to that category — a fuel-price spike, a credit tightening that prices $70,000 pickups out of reach, a regulatory change to light-truck standards — hits the earnings base directly, with no offsetting segment. China at $32M in Q3 cannot cushion anything.
▸
Tariffs and input inflation. Gross tariff costs are now expected at the low end of the $2.5–3.5B range for 2026 (Jacobson, Q3 call; $1.8B so far this year), against $14.5–15.5B of EBIT-adjusted — about a 16–17% pre-mitigation bite out of operating profit. GM's mitigation is onshoring: $4B committed across three domestic plants, taking U.S. production capacity above 2 million units and structurally reducing exposure. But onshoring itself carries $1.0–1.5B of additional cost in 2026, and commodity inflation, including logistics, energy and chips, now adds about $2B, the high end of GM's earlier forecast (Reuters, Oct 20). Jacobson told analysts that if commodity prices stay at recent levels they would be a further $1.0–1.5B headwind to EBIT-adjusted in 2027. In Q1 GM booked a ~$500M benefit from refunds of tariffs covered by the U.S. Supreme Court's ruling; Jacobson said about 60% had been received by October. The mitigation works; it is not free, and it is policy-dependent in both directions.
▸
The EV transition is deferred, not resolved. $11.1B of charges buys GM time, not an answer. Capacity has been cut, Orion goes over to gasoline pickups in 2027, and the Ultium JV plants sat idle for much of 2026 — and yet regulatory trajectories in Europe, California and eventually the U.S. federal fleet still point toward electrification. GM will at some point have to rebuild what it has just written down, and the second build-out will start from a weaker cost position relative to Chinese and Korean cell makers than the first one did.
▸
Cyclicality and the credit channel. GM's earnings are levered twice to the consumer: once through vehicle volume and pricing, and again through GM Financial's loan book and lease residuals. In a downturn those correlate — volumes fall, incentives rise, used values fall, and credit losses rise, all at once. This is the structural reason a 5.4× forward multiple is not automatically an error.

Notable One-Off Items

This period is unusually distorted, and every distortion runs the same way — it depresses GAAP earnings while leaving the operating business intact.

EV capacity and battery charges — $11.1B cumulative since H2 2025. $7.6B in the second half of 2025 (impairments, BrightDrop discontinuation, contract cancellations, supplier settlements), $1.1B in Q1 2026 (largely contract cancellations and supplier claims), $2.3B in Q2 2026 ($1.6B of cash charges, including $700M to right-size the battery supply chain with its joint-venture partners, plus $700M of non-cash write-offs) and $238M in Q3 2026. Through Q2, $7.2B of the total was expected to be cash and $4.5B had been paid, about 60%. Management described the cash charges as "substantially complete" in July, and Q3's smaller figure is consistent with that. The Q4 2025 loss these charges caused is why the trailing P/E is 17.5× on a company now guiding to $12.75–13.75 of adjusted EPS.

Tariffs — now expected at the low end of $2.5–3.5B gross in 2026 ($1.8B year to date, per Jacobson on the Q3 call), partly offset by a ~$500M tariff refund booked in Q1 2026 after the U.S. Supreme Court's tariff ruling, and being structurally mitigated by $4B of onshoring investment across three domestic plants lifting U.S. capacity above 2 million units. The mitigation carries its own $1.0–1.5B of cost in 2026, on top of about $2B of commodity, logistics, energy and chip inflation, the high end of the earlier forecast (Reuters).

Cruise. GM announced on December 10, 2024 that it would stop funding the Cruise robotaxi programme after more than $10B invested since 2016 — a decision that followed the October 2023 San Francisco pedestrian incident and GM's July 2024 halt to the Origin, a robotaxi without a steering wheel. The restructuring was expected to cut annual spending from about $2B to about $1B. The technical teams were folded into GM proper.

Buyback. $5.3B of stock repurchased in the first nine months of 2026 (cash-flow statement in the Q3 release), $2.8B of it in the first half, completing the $6.0B programme announced in January; on October 20 the board approved a new $20.0B multi-year authorisation — about 31% of the company's market value at the October 21 close. Shares outstanding fell to 849M at September 30 from 904M at the end of 2025. $6.0B was repurchased during FY2025. This is not dilution-offset — it is a deliberate shrinking of the equity, and Section 08 quantifies exactly how much of the EPS growth it accounts for.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $54.43 to the October 21, 2026 close of $76.11, GM returned +39.8% in price — a compound annual rate of about 7.0% before dividends, which at a sub-1% yield add little. SPY returned +68.3% in price over the same dates. That respectable headline hides two completely different regimes. The first three years were a grind: the stock fell 51.9% from its December 2021 close of $58.63 to the October 2023 monthly-close low of $28.20, spending most of 2022 and 2023 below its 10-month average as rates rose, the UAW struck, and the market priced the EV transition as a value-destroying capital commitment. The second regime began in late 2023 and accelerated sharply: from $28.20 to the July 2026 close of $88.86 is +215% in under three years, with the steepest leg between September 2025 ($60.97) and January 2026 ($84.00) — +37.8% in four months, precisely the window in which GM wrote down its EV programme by $7.6B. The stock rose on the write-down. That is the market telling you, unambiguously, what it thought the EV capital was worth. Since July the direction has reversed: September closed at $77.00, 10.8% below August's $86.32, and the October 20 print day took the stock from $82.75 to $77.09; at $76.11 it is 14.3% below the July month-end close.

Relative performance. Over the past 52 weeks GM is +14.24% in price (October 21, 2025 close $66.62 to October 21, 2026 close $76.11), against +15.16% for SPY, the S&P 500 fund, and +18.62% for QQQ, the Nasdaq-100 fund, over the same dates. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The profit machine, stated plainly. In Q3 2026 GM produced $50.0B of revenue and $4.1B of EBIT-adjusted at an 8.2% consolidated margin. Of that $4.1B, $3.6B came from GM North America at an 8.7% margin, up 2.5 points year over year, on 870,000 wholesale units; North American pricing was $400M above the prior year through September, down from $600M at the half-year (Jacobson). What pricing power GM has comes from one place: more than 42% of the U.S. full-size pickup market in the first half, more than 10 points clear of the nearest competitor, running with full-size pickup inventory 9% below the prior year in Q1. China contributed $32M of equity income. GM Financial contributed $668M of EBT-adjusted, down from $804M a year earlier. Everything else, net of corporate, is roughly a rounding error. This is a full-size truck company with a bank attached and a global footprint that no longer earns its cost of capital.

The other half, quantified with the same honesty. Since the second half of 2025 GM has booked $11.1B of EV-related charges — $7.6B in H2 2025, $1.1B in Q1 2026, $2.3B in Q2 2026 and $238M in Q3 2026; through Q2, $7.2B was expected to be cash and $4.5B had been paid. Physically: about 1,200 layoffs at Factory Zero; Ultium Cells plants in Ohio and Tennessee idled from January (Ohio for seven months), with ~1,550 temporary and 550 indefinite layoffs; a GM stake in one Ultium plant sold; BrightDrop discontinued; Orion Assembly switching to gasoline pickups in Q2 2027; more than 3,400 jobs affected across EV and battery operations, temporary layoffs included. The demand cause is named by management: "a smaller EV market following reductions in consumer incentives" — the U.S. federal purchase credit's expiry — against which GM held 13% of the U.S. EV market in Q1. The return on all of this: EV losses improving $1.0–1.5B in 2026. That is the ratio to hold in your head — $11.1B of charges to buy $1.0–1.5B a year of loss reduction. It may well be the right decision; it is not a triumph.

Tariffs and China, in one paragraph each. Gross tariff cost is now expected at the low end of $2.5–3.5B for 2026 against $14.5–15.5B of EBIT-adjusted — call it 16–17% of operating profit before mitigation. The mitigation is physical and expensive: $4B across three domestic plants, U.S. capacity above 2 million units, plus a ~$500M refund of tariffs covered by the U.S. Supreme Court's ruling. It costs $1.0–1.5B in 2026 to execute, on top of about $2B of commodity, logistics, energy and chip inflation, and Jacobson flagged a further $1.0–1.5B commodity headwind for 2027 if prices hold. GM is absorbing all of that and has raised guidance twice this year, then held the EBIT-adjusted midpoint in October — which is the real evidence of pricing power, more than any share statistic. The 6.84% print-day fall, which Reuters tied to the forecast missing analysts' estimates, shows the market wanted more than a held midpoint. China, meanwhile, has completed its journey from profit engine to breakeven: $32M of equity income in Q3 after $83M in Q2, under 1% of consolidated EBIT-adjusted, achieved through restructuring rather than growth, with Jacobson crediting the team on the Q2 call for profitability "despite the very difficult environment." There is no China recovery in these numbers, and no China collapse left to fear.

The buyback — and how much of the EPS growth it is. Do the arithmetic explicitly, because it changes the story. FY2021: net income $9,837M, diluted EPS $6.70, implying about 1,468M diluted shares. Q3 2025 diluted count: 964M. Q3 2026 diluted count: 887M, 8.0% lower (Q3 release). Shares outstanding at September 30: 849M, down from 904M at the end of 2025. That is a ~40% reduction in the diluted share count in five years, funded by $6B of repurchase in FY2025 and $5.3B in the first nine months of 2026, with a new $20B authorisation approved on October 20. Now apply it: the new 2026 guidance of $12.75–13.75 adjusted EPS at a midpoint of $13.25 on ~887M diluted shares implies about $11.75B of adjusted net income. On the FY2021 share count of 1,468M, that same $11.75B would be $8.01 per share. So of the $13.25 midpoint, roughly $5.24 — about 40% — is share-count reduction, not incremental profit (our calculation). Buying back 40% of a company at 5–8× earnings is one of the more defensible uses of capital available to management, and it is genuinely accretive. It is also not the same thing as a growing business, and a bear who points this out is not being unfair.

Cruise, and what replaced it. GM stopped funding the Cruise robotaxi programme on December 10, 2024 after more than $10B invested since 2016, following the October 2023 San Francisco pedestrian incident and the July 2024 halt to the Origin robotaxi. The restructuring was expected to cut annual spending from about $2B to about $1B, and the Cruise and GM technical teams were merged. What replaced it is deliberately less ambitious and considerably more capital-efficient: Super Cruise, the hands-off/eyes-on highway system, with about one million equipped vehicles on the road, going standard on high-end Silverado and Sierra trims for an estimated 160,000 incremental units, sold as a subscription; and a 2028 "eyes-off, hands-off" personal-autonomy launch that Barra confirmed on the Q2 call is "on track" and "largely meeting their milestones." Selling autonomy in a vehicle a customer owns, rather than operating a fleet, is a lower-capital, lower-optionality path. At a 5.4× forward multiple, none of it is in the price.

Why does a company with these margins trade at a mid-single-digit P/E? Resist the reflex to call it cheap. There are four defensible reasons, and only the fourth is an argument for the bull.

(i) Cyclicality — the earnings are peak, not average. A 5.4× forward multiple applied to trough earnings would be extraordinary. Applied to peak earnings it is ordinary. GM has just posted its best first half ever for adjusted EPS and a Q3 with GMNA margin up 2.5 points again, into tight inventory and a strong used market. Those conditions mean-revert, and the Q3 call added a named threat to 2027: a $1.0–1.5B commodity headwind if prices hold. The market is not paying 5.7× for $13.25 of EPS; it is paying something closer to 8–9× for a mid-cycle number, and that is a normal industrial multiple. Wells Fargo's $61 Underweight, raised by one dollar in July while other firms raised theirs, is that argument stated in a price target.

(ii) Capital intensity — the cash flow is not free. GM's ROIC of 6.97% is the best of the four peers (Toyota 4.17%, Tesla 4.28%, Ford 0.91%, Stellantis −5.29%) and is still, on any plausible weighted cost of capital, roughly break-even at value creation. A business that earns approximately its cost of capital is worth approximately its invested capital — which is close to where GM trades at a 1.06 price-to-book. And the reinvestment requirement is not optional: $4B of onshoring, $11.1B of EV charges to unwind a prior capital cycle, and a product cadence that must be funded whether or not it earns a return. $14.40B of trailing free cash flow against a $64.62B market cap looks like a 22.3% yield, and adjusted automotive free cash flow of $11.5–12.5B looks like 18–19% — but a cash yield that never compounds into a higher earnings base is a coupon, not growth.

(iii) Terminal value — what is a truck franchise worth in 2040? This is the reason the multiple has a floor problem rather than a ceiling problem. Almost all of GM's value sits in one category, in one country, powered by one technology, and the direction of regulatory and competitive travel — however delayed — is away from all three. The company has just demonstrated, at a cost of $11.1B, that it cannot yet earn a return on the alternative. Chinese OEMs have taken the Chinese market and are exporting; Tesla trades at a $1.53 trillion market cap on $103.57B of revenue, roughly 24× GM's market cap on 55% of GM's revenue, because the market assigns terminal value differently to the two. A discounted cash flow with a low or negative terminal growth rate produces a mid-single-digit multiple mechanically. That is not the market being stupid; it is the market declining to underwrite 2040.

(iv) The bull's counter. Every one of the above has been true for a decade, and over that decade GM has retired 40% of its equity at these multiples. If the multiple never re-rates, the buyback alone compounds per-share value at a high single-digit to low double-digit rate; if it re-rates even to Toyota's 8.8× trailing, the stock is worth materially more. The rise from the April 2025 low to the July 2026 high (+96% on month-end closes) showed the re-rating can happen — driven, tellingly, by reducing ambition rather than increasing it — and the fall since shows how quickly it can stall. TD Cowen's $132 and Tigress's $130 (set July 28) are simply the multiple normalising to 10× guidance-midpoint EPS. The new $20B authorisation, about 31% of today's market value, is management's own answer to the price. The honest summary: GM is not obviously cheap and it is not obviously expensive; it is a high-quality cyclical earnings stream priced as if it will not be reinvested well, which is a judgement about capital allocation rather than about the business. The buyback is management agreeing with that judgement and acting on it.

Bull Case

GM's best first half ever for adjusted EPS — $8.2B EBIT-adjusted, $7.27 adjusted EPS, +37% — was followed by a Q3 beat: $4.1B EBIT-adjusted, $3.70 adjusted EPS, +32%, delivered while absorbing tariffs, about $2B of input inflation and $1.0–1.5B of onshoring cost. GMNA margin of 8.7% rests on a 42%+ pickup share with a 10-point lead. EV charges fell to $238M in Q3, EV losses are on track to improve $1.0–1.5B, and Orion returns to gasoline trucks in 2027. Adjusted automotive free cash flow guidance was raised to $11.5–12.5B against a $64.6B market cap, and a new $20B buyback authorisation follows one that has already helped retire ~40% of the diluted shares in five years. At 5.4× forward earnings after a 6.8% fall on a beat, with Ford and Stellantis losing $7.4B and $22.3B respectively, and Super Cruise plus a 2028 autonomy launch carried at zero — the entry price demands very little to go right.

Bear Case

These are peak-cycle earnings from peak pricing on tight inventory, and North American pricing gains shrank from $600M at the half-year to $400M for the year to date by September. Commodity, logistics, energy and chip costs are already about $2B this year, and management flagged another $1.0–1.5B for 2027 if prices hold. Trailing GAAP EPS is $4.36 and ROIC is 6.97% — the business roughly earns its cost of capital, which is why it trades at 1.06× book. Roughly 40% of the 2026 EPS midpoint is share-count reduction rather than profit growth. Revenue has been flat at $185–187B a year since 2024. China earned $32M in Q3. The company burned more than $10B on Cruise and $11.1B on EV capacity within two years, and will eventually have to rebuild the latter from a weaker cell-cost position. Wells Fargo's $61 Underweight implies about −20%, and the market's reaction to a beat-and-buyback quarter was a 6.84% fall.

  Industry Commentary
The EV Retreat Is an Industry Event, Not a GM Event
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
GM's $11.1B of charges is the visible edge of a sector-wide capital reversal. The removal of U.S. consumer purchase incentives produced a demand air-pocket that management described as "a smaller EV market following reductions in consumer incentives," and the industry response has been uniform: idle capacity, cancel contracts, settle with suppliers, convert plants back to internal combustion. GM alone has affected more than 3,400 jobs across EV and battery operations (temporary layoffs included), halted cell production at two Ultium Cells joint-venture plants with LG Energy Solution, laid off about 1,200 workers at Factory Zero, discontinued BrightDrop and sold a stake in an Ultium plant. The strategic problem is that none of this makes the eventual transition cheaper — it makes it later, and it means the second build-out starts against Chinese and Korean cell makers who did not stop.
Tariffs Have Rewritten the Manufacturing Map
ONGOING HEADWIND
GM now expects gross tariff cost at the low end of its $2.5–3.5B range for 2026 and is answering it with $4B of onshoring across three domestic plants, taking U.S. production capacity above 2 million units — with a ~$500M refund of tariffs covered by the U.S. Supreme Court's ruling booked in Q1. Layered on top are about $2B of commodity, logistics, energy and chip cost and $1.0–1.5B of onshoring execution expense. The sector-wide read: North American production is being reshored at real cost, structurally raising the industry's fixed-cost base, and the OEMs with existing U.S. truck plants — GM chief among them — carry the smallest relative burden. That relative advantage is visible in the numbers: GM absorbed all of it, raised guidance twice and held the midpoint in October, while Ford lost $7.40B and Stellantis $22.25B on a trailing-twelve-month basis.
Capital Discipline Is the New Auto Narrative
EMERGING TAILWIND
The most informative price action in this report is that GM closed 8.7% higher, roughly $6B of market value, on January 27, 2026, the day it reported a fourth quarter carrying more than $7.2B of special charges, mostly for the EV pullback. The market has stopped paying legacy OEMs for ambition and started paying them for cash returned. GM has now retired ~40% of its diluted shares in five years, killed Cruise after more than $10B, taken $11.1B of EV charges, and roughly doubled from its April 2025 low to its July 2026 high on month-end closes — then fell 6.84% on a quarter that beat and came with a $20B buyback. Bull framing: at 5.4× forward earnings with $11.5–12.5B of adjusted automotive free cash flow guided and a new $20B buyback authorisation, the arithmetic compounds per-share value even if the multiple never moves — and Super Cruise plus the 2028 personal-autonomy programme are carried at zero. Bear framing: a company that shrinks its share count faster than it grows its profit is liquidating gracefully, not compounding. Roughly 40% of the 2026 EPS midpoint is buyback, revenue has been flat since 2024, China earned $32M in Q3, and 6.97% ROIC says the reinvestment opportunity that would justify a higher multiple does not currently exist.
