01 Company Overview

General Motors designs, builds and finances trucks, crossovers, cars and parts under Chevrolet, GMC, Buick and Cadillac, through three reported segments — GM North America, GM International and GM Financial. FY2025 revenue was $185.02B, down 1.3% from $187.44B in FY2024; trailing-twelve-month revenue through Q2 2026 is $185.53B, down 1.1%. At a $77.01B market capitalisation GM ranks roughly #311 globally — a company selling nearly $186B of product a year is valued at less than half of one year's revenue.

The central debate on this page is not whether GM is profitable. It is. First-half 2026 EBIT-adjusted was $8.2B and adjusted diluted EPS of $7.27 was the best first half in company history. The debate is what that profit is worth. Essentially all of it comes from full-size pickups and SUVs built and sold in North America — a franchise with a 42%+ share and no credible near-term challenger, but also one that is cyclical, tariff-exposed, and being used to fund an electric transition that has so far produced $10.9B of charges rather than returns. The market's answer is a 6.4× forward earnings multiple. Sections 04 and 08 take that apart rather than calling it cheap.

This is the company. GMNA's Q2 2026 EBIT-adjusted margin of 8.6% was up 2.5 percentage points from 6.1% a year earlier, on 848,000 wholesale units, with roughly $700M of the improvement attributed to pricing discipline alone. The franchise underneath it is the Silverado / Sierra / Tahoe / Yukon / Suburban / Escalade complex: GM held more than 42% of the U.S. full-size pickup market through the first half of 2026, more than 10 percentage points above its closest competitor, while setting record production volumes and launching trucks at three assembly plants simultaneously. Below the trucks, Barra has flagged that the crossovers — Trax, Equinox, Traverse, Terrain, Acadia, Envista — have become "significant contributors to our profitability," which matters because it is the first evidence in years that GM can make money on something other than a body-on-frame truck. Structural note: a 4,000-lb margin base. Full-size trucks carry transaction prices and contribution per unit multiples of a compact crossover's — which is why an 8.6% consolidated-segment margin is compatible with much of the rest of the portfolio earning close to nothing.

FY26 EV Loss Improvement

Quantify this half honestly, because it is where the equity story was supposed to live. GM booked $7.6B of EV-related charges in the second half of 2025 — impairments, capacity right-sizing, contract cancellations and supplier settlements, including discontinuing the BrightDrop electric van — then a further $1.1B in Q1 2026 and $2.3B in Q2 2026, of which $700M was specifically to "right size the battery supply chain." Cumulative: $10.9B, with $7.2B of cash impact, $4.5B already paid through Q2 2026 (about 60% of the expected total). Jacobson stated on the Q2 call that these actions "substantially complete the material cash charges we expect to incur as we align our EV capacity" with the changed regulatory environment. Physically, the retrenchment means: Factory Zero in Detroit cut from three shifts to one (Hummer EV, Silverado EV, Sierra EV, Escalade IQ); Ultium Cells battery plants in Ohio and Tennessee — the joint venture with LG Energy Solution — halting cell production for roughly six months from January, temporarily affecting ~1,550 workers with a further 550 laid off indefinitely at the Ohio plant; a GM stake in one Ultium plant sold outright; and Orion Assembly converted back to gasoline full-size trucks and SUVs from early 2027. Total announced job losses across EV, battery and adjacent functions: approximately 3,300. On the demand side, management attributed lost EV share to "a smaller EV market following reductions in consumer incentives" — the U.S. federal purchase credit expiry — while framing the volume decision as a deliberate choice, and guiding EV wholesale volumes to rise slightly in H2 2026. The financial payoff is real but modest: EV losses are expected to improve $1.0–1.5B in 2026. That is the size of the prize from unwinding a programme that cost $10.9B to unwind.

Honest framing: GM is not exiting EVs — it holds roughly 13% of the U.S. EV market, second behind Tesla. It is de-risking the capital commitment. The charge is the price of having built capacity for a demand curve that did not arrive.

China is now a story about not losing money. GM's China joint ventures contributed $100M of equity income in Q2 2026 — roughly 2.6% of consolidated EBIT-adjusted, and on a simple annualisation about $400M against a company guided to $14.0–16.0B of EBIT-adjusted. Jacobson's framing on the call was that the China team "deserves a lot of credit for the restructuring work" that enabled profitability "despite the very difficult environment." Read that plainly: the achievement in China is now positive equity income rather than growth. Equity income is GM's share of the after-tax profit of joint ventures it does not consolidate — so China contributes to earnings without appearing in the revenue line at all, which is why GM's China exposure is easy to miss in a revenue-mix chart and impossible to miss in a P/E. The cause is not GM-specific: domestic Chinese OEMs have taken the volume, and the price competition among them has been severe enough to push most foreign joint ventures toward or below breakeven. The precise historical peak of GM's China equity income was not verified from the sources fetched and is deliberately not stated here; what is verified is that today it is $100M a quarter.

Analytical point: a segment that contributes ~2.6% of operating profit cannot support a valuation argument in either direction. China has already been de-rated to approximately zero in GM's earnings — which also means it is no longer a source of downside surprise.

GM Financial originates the loans and leases that move the trucks, and it is a genuinely good business inside a mediocre one: $600M of EBT-adjusted in Q2 2026 at a 14.7% return on tangible common equity, guided to $2.5–3.0B for the full year — roughly 18% of the midpoint of consolidated EBIT-adjusted guidance, on my arithmetic. Two things follow. First, a meaningful slice of GM's earnings is a spread-lending business whose profitability depends on funding costs, used-vehicle residual values and credit losses, not on how many Silverados leave the lot. Second — and this is the single most abused number in GM valuation work — the captive's debt sits in the consolidated enterprise value. GM's reported EV of $181.06B against a $77.01B market cap and a 2.02 debt-to-equity ratio is not an industrial balance sheet under stress; it is a finance book. Anyone computing EV/EBITDA on GM without separating the captive is measuring the wrong company.

Watch item: residual values. A captive lender's earnings quality is only as good as the used-truck market that backs its lease portfolio.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
FULL-SIZE TRUCK FRANCHISE 42%+ PICKUP SHARE DEALER NETWORK SCALE BODY-ON-FRAME PLATFORM DEPTH CAPTIVE FINANCE ARM SUPER CRUISE INSTALLED BASE US MANUFACTURING FOOTPRINT

GM's moat is one product category, and it is a deep one. The U.S. full-size pickup and body-on-frame SUV market is a near-duopoly with GM at more than 42% share, over 10 points clear of its nearest rival, defended by three things a new entrant cannot buy: assembly plants tooled for body-on-frame at volume, a dealer and service network that services a working vehicle, and a customer base whose brand loyalty in this segment is measured in decades and generations. That structure is why an 8.6% segment margin is achievable in a business whose global peers are currently losing tens of billions — Ford lost $7.40B and Stellantis $22.25B on a trailing-twelve-month basis against GM's positive $1.86B (itself depressed by EV charges). A second, newer layer is Super Cruise — GM's hands-off, eyes-on highway driver-assistance system, now being made standard on high-end Silverado and Sierra trims and optional on most of the rest, which Barra sized at ~160,000 incremental Super Cruise units from that packaging change alone. It carries a subscription (historically $25/month after a free period) across more than 20 models: a small, high-margin software annuity attached to the truck franchise rather than a separate bet. The moat's limit is equally clear — it does not extend to compact cars, it does not extend to China, and it did not extend to electric vehicles, where GM spent $10.9B discovering that a 100-year-old distribution advantage is worth less when the product is new.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Single-category profit concentration. GMNA generated roughly 87% of Q2 2026 EBIT-adjusted, and within GMNA the full-size truck and SUV line generates a disproportionate share of that. Any shock specific to that category — a fuel-price spike, a credit tightening that prices $70,000 pickups out of reach, a regulatory change to light-truck standards — hits the earnings base directly, with no offsetting segment. China at $100M a quarter cannot cushion anything.
▸
Tariffs and input inflation. Gross tariff costs are guided at $2.5–3.5B for 2026, against $14.0–16.0B of EBIT-adjusted — a 16–25% pre-mitigation bite out of operating profit. GM's mitigation is onshoring: $4B committed across three domestic plants, taking U.S. production capacity above 2 million units and structurally reducing exposure. But onshoring itself carries $1.0–1.5B of additional cost in 2026, and commodity, logistics and DRAM inflation adds a further $1.5–2.0B. A $500M Section 232 tariff refund was anticipated in Q1 2026. The mitigation works; it is not free, and it is policy-dependent in both directions.
▸
The EV transition is deferred, not resolved. $10.9B of charges buys GM time, not an answer. Capacity has been cut, Orion goes back to gasoline trucks in early 2027, the Ultium JV plants are idled — and yet regulatory trajectories in Europe, California and eventually the U.S. federal fleet still point toward electrification. GM will at some point have to rebuild what it has just written down, and the second build-out will start from a weaker cost position relative to Chinese and Korean cell makers than the first one did.
▸
Cyclicality and the credit channel. GM's earnings are levered twice to the consumer: once through vehicle volume and pricing, and again through GM Financial's loan book and lease residuals. In a downturn those correlate — volumes fall, incentives rise, used values fall, and credit losses rise, all at once. This is the structural reason a 6.4× forward multiple is not automatically an error.

Notable One-Off Items

This period is unusually distorted, and every distortion runs the same way — it depresses GAAP earnings while leaving the operating business intact.

EV capacity and battery charges — $10.9B cumulative since H2 2025, $7.2B of it cash. $7.6B in the second half of 2025 (impairments, BrightDrop discontinuation, contract cancellations, supplier settlements), $1.1B in Q1 2026 (largely contract cancellations and supplier claims), and $2.3B in Q2 2026 ($700M non-cash to right-size the battery supply chain, $1.6B cash). $4.5B had been paid through Q2 2026, about 60% of the expected cash total. Management describes the cash charges as "substantially complete." This is the entire explanation for a 44× trailing P/E on a company guiding to $12.00–14.00 of adjusted EPS.

Tariffs — $2.5–3.5B gross in 2026, partially offset by an anticipated $500M Section 232 refund flagged in Q1 2026, and being structurally mitigated by $4B of onshoring investment across three domestic plants lifting U.S. capacity above 2 million units. The mitigation carries its own $1.0–1.5B of cost in 2026, on top of $1.5–2.0B of commodity, logistics and DRAM inflation.

Cruise. GM announced on December 10, 2024 that it would stop funding the Cruise robotaxi programme after roughly $10B invested since 2017 — a decision that followed the October 2023 San Francisco pedestrian incident and a $600M charge taken when Origin production was paused in Q2 2024. The restructuring was expected to lower spending by more than $1B annually. The technical teams were folded into GM proper.

Buyback. $2.0B in Q2 2026 alone retiring ~24.9M shares (≈$80/share average, below today's price); $2.8B and 36M shares in the first half; $6B repurchased during FY2025, with $3.5B of authorisation remaining. GM also retired $1.8B of debt in 2025. This is not dilution-offset — it is a deliberate shrinking of the equity, and Section 08 quantifies exactly how much of the EPS growth it accounts for.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $54.43 to the September 4, 2026 close of $87.76, GM returned +61.2% in price — a compound annual rate of about 10.0% before dividends, which at a sub-1% yield add little. That respectable headline hides two completely different regimes. The first three years were a grind: the stock fell 51.9% from its December 2021 close of $58.63 to the October 2023 monthly-close low of $28.20, spending most of 2022 and 2023 below its 10-month average as rates rose, the UAW struck, and the market priced the EV transition as a value-destroying capital commitment. The second regime began in late 2023 and accelerated sharply: from $28.20 to the July 2026 close of $88.86 is +215% in under three years, with the steepest leg between September 2025 ($60.97) and January 2026 ($84.00) — +37.8% in four months, precisely the window in which GM wrote down its EV programme by $7.6B. The stock rose on the write-down. That is the market telling you, unambiguously, what it thought the EV capital was worth.

Relative performance. Over the past 52 weeks GM is +51.97%, a period in which the S&P 500 — GM's NYSE benchmark index here — delivered nothing remotely comparable; on the sources fetched, Tesla returned +5.98% over the same 52 weeks and Stellantis −39.17%, while Ford and Stellantis both swung to large trailing losses. GM has therefore massively outperformed both its sector and, on any reasonable reading, the index over the last year. An exact same-window S&P 500 and NASDAQ-100 total-return figure could not be verified from the sources fetched and is deliberately not stated. The five-year picture is more sobering: a 10.0% price CAGR from a cyclical trough-to-recovery window is a good outcome for the sector and an ordinary one against a broad index. Beta of 1.32 and the shape of the chart above are consistent with each other — this is an amplified cyclical, and the entry date has determined the outcome.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The profit machine, stated plainly. In Q2 2026 GM produced $48.0B of revenue and $3.9B of EBIT-adjusted at an 8.2% consolidated margin. Of that $3.9B, $3.4B came from GM North America at an 8.6% margin, up 2.5 points year over year, on 848,000 wholesale units — with roughly $700M of the improvement from pricing alone. The pricing came from one place: more than 42% of the U.S. full-size pickup market, more than 10 points clear of the nearest competitor, running with full-size pickup inventory 9% below the prior year. China contributed $100M of equity income. GM Financial contributed $600M of EBT-adjusted at a 14.7% return on tangible common equity. Everything else, net of corporate, is roughly a rounding error. This is a full-size truck company with a bank attached and a global footprint that no longer earns its cost of capital.

The other half, quantified with the same honesty. Since the second half of 2025 GM has booked $10.9B of EV-related charges, $7.2B of it cash, $4.5B already paid — $7.6B in H2 2025, $1.1B in Q1 2026, $2.3B in Q2 2026. Physically: Factory Zero cut from three shifts to one; Ultium Cells plants in Ohio and Tennessee idled for roughly six months from January, with ~1,550 temporary and 550 indefinite layoffs; a GM stake in one Ultium plant sold; BrightDrop discontinued; Orion Assembly converting back to gasoline trucks in early 2027; approximately 3,300 jobs cut across EV, battery and adjacent functions. The demand cause is named by management: "a smaller EV market following reductions in consumer incentives" — the U.S. federal purchase credit's expiry — against which GM still holds roughly 13% of the U.S. EV market. The return on all of this: EV losses improving $1.0–1.5B in 2026. That is the ratio to hold in your head — $10.9B of charges to buy $1.0–1.5B a year of loss reduction. It may well be the right decision; it is not a triumph.

Tariffs and China, in one paragraph each. Gross tariff cost is guided at $2.5–3.5B for 2026 against $14.0–16.0B of EBIT-adjusted — call it 16–25% of operating profit before mitigation. The mitigation is physical and expensive: $4B across three domestic plants, U.S. capacity above 2 million units, plus an anticipated $500M Section 232 refund. It costs $1.0–1.5B in 2026 to execute, on top of $1.5–2.0B of commodity, logistics and DRAM inflation. GM is absorbing all of that and still raised guidance twice — which is the real evidence of pricing power, more than any share statistic. China, meanwhile, has completed its journey from profit engine to breakeven: $100M of equity income in Q2, roughly 2.6% of consolidated EBIT-adjusted, achieved through restructuring rather than growth, with Jacobson crediting the team for profitability "despite the very difficult environment." There is no China recovery in these numbers, and no China collapse left to fear.

The buyback — and how much of the EPS growth it is. Do the arithmetic explicitly, because it changes the story. FY2021: net income $9,837M, diluted EPS $6.70, implying about 1,468M diluted shares. FY2025: net income $3,180M, diluted EPS $3.27, implying about 972M. Q2 2026 diluted count: 893M, roughly 8% below Q2 2025. Shares outstanding today: 877.45M. That is a ~40% reduction in the share count in five years, funded by $6B of repurchase in FY2025, $2.8B in H1 2026 (36M shares), and $2.0B in Q2 alone (~24.9M shares at roughly $80). Now apply it: 2026 guidance of $12.00–14.00 adjusted EPS at a midpoint of $13.00 on ~890M shares implies about $11.6B of adjusted net income. On the FY2021 share count of 1,468M, that same $11.6B would be $7.90 per share. So of the $13.00 midpoint, roughly $5.10 — about 39% — is share-count reduction, not incremental profit. Adjusted net income is up perhaps 18% since 2021; adjusted EPS is up around 94%. Both facts are real. Buying back 40% of a company at 5–8× earnings is one of the more defensible uses of capital available to management, and it is genuinely accretive. It is also not the same thing as a growing business, and a bear who points this out is not being unfair.

Cruise, and what replaced it. GM stopped funding the Cruise robotaxi programme on December 10, 2024 after roughly $10B invested since 2017, following the October 2023 San Francisco pedestrian incident and a $600M charge on paused Origin production. The restructuring was expected to cut spending by more than $1B annually, and the Cruise and GM technical teams were merged. What replaced it is deliberately less ambitious and considerably more capital-efficient: Super Cruise, the hands-off/eyes-on highway system now on more than 20 models, going standard on high-end Silverado and Sierra trims for an estimated 160,000 incremental units, with a subscription (historically $25/month after a free period); and a 2028 "eyes-off, hands-off" personal-autonomy launch that Barra confirmed on the Q2 call is "on track" and "largely meeting their milestones." Selling autonomy in a vehicle a customer owns, rather than operating a fleet, is a lower-capital, lower-optionality path. At a 6.4× forward multiple, none of it is in the price.

Why does a company with these margins trade at a mid-single-digit P/E? Resist the reflex to call it cheap. There are four defensible reasons, and only the fourth is an argument for the bull.

(i) Cyclicality — the earnings are peak, not average. A 6.4× forward multiple applied to trough earnings would be extraordinary. Applied to peak earnings it is ordinary. GM has just posted the best first half in its history, with GMNA margin up 2.5 points on $700M of pricing, into tight inventory and a strong used market. Those conditions mean-revert. The market is not paying 6.4× for $13 of EPS; it is paying something closer to 9–10× for a mid-cycle number, and 9–10× is a normal industrial multiple. Wells Fargo's $61 Underweight, raised by exactly one dollar on the day everyone else raised by ten, is that argument stated in a price target.

(ii) Capital intensity — the cash flow is not free. GM's ROIC of 6.11% is the best of the four peers fetched (Toyota 4.17%, Tesla 5.46%, Stellantis −4.67%) and is still, on any plausible weighted cost of capital, roughly break-even at value creation. A business that earns approximately its cost of capital is worth approximately its invested capital — which is close to where GM trades at a 1.24 price-to-book. And the reinvestment requirement is not optional: $4B of onshoring, $10.9B of EV charges to unwind a prior capital cycle, and a product cadence that must be funded whether or not it earns a return. $14.40B of trailing free cash flow against a $77.01B market cap looks like an 18.7% yield, and adjusted automotive free cash flow of $9.5–11.5B looks like 12–15% — but a cash yield that never compounds into a higher earnings base is a coupon, not growth.

(iii) Terminal value — what is a truck franchise worth in 2040? This is the reason the multiple has a floor problem rather than a ceiling problem. Almost all of GM's value sits in one category, in one country, powered by one technology, and the direction of regulatory and competitive travel — however delayed — is away from all three. The company has just demonstrated, at a cost of $10.9B, that it cannot yet earn a return on the alternative. Chinese OEMs have taken the Chinese market and are exporting; Tesla trades at a $1.40 trillion market cap on $103.62B of revenue, roughly 18× GM's market cap on 56% of GM's revenue, because the market assigns terminal value differently to the two. A discounted cash flow with a low or negative terminal growth rate produces a mid-single-digit multiple mechanically. That is not the market being stupid; it is the market declining to underwrite 2040.

(iv) The bull's counter. Every one of the above has been true for a decade, and over that decade GM has retired 40% of its equity at these multiples. If the multiple never re-rates, the buyback alone compounds per-share value at a high single-digit to low double-digit rate; if it re-rates even to Toyota's 8.5× trailing, the stock is worth materially more. The 52-week move of +51.97% is evidence the re-rating has partially begun — driven, tellingly, by reducing ambition rather than increasing it. TD Cowen's $132 and Tigress's $130 are simply the multiple normalising to 10× guidance-midpoint EPS. The honest summary: GM is not obviously cheap and it is not obviously expensive; it is a high-quality cyclical earnings stream priced as if it will not be reinvested well, which is a judgement about capital allocation rather than about the business. The buyback is management agreeing with that judgement and acting on it.

Bull Case

The best first half in company history — $8.2B EBIT-adjusted, $7.27 adjusted EPS, +35% — was delivered while absorbing $2.5–3.5B of gross tariffs, $1.5–2.0B of input inflation and $1.0–1.5B of onshoring cost, and guidance was raised twice. GMNA margin of 8.6% rests on a 42%+ pickup share with a 10-point lead and 9% below-normal inventory. The EV cash charges are "substantially complete," delivering $1.0–1.5B of loss improvement, and Orion returns to gasoline trucks in 2027. Adjusted automotive free cash flow of $9.5–11.5B against a $77B market cap funds a buyback that has already retired ~40% of the shares in five years, with $3.5B of authorisation left. At 6.4× forward earnings, Ford and Stellantis losing $7.4B and $22.3B respectively, and Super Cruise plus a 2028 autonomy launch carried at zero — the entry price demands very little to go right.

Bear Case

These are peak-cycle earnings from peak pricing on tight inventory, and $700M of a $3.4B segment profit came from pricing that will lap. Trailing GAAP EPS is $1.99 and ROIC is 6.11% — the business roughly earns its cost of capital, which is why it trades at 1.24× book. Roughly 39% of the 2026 EPS midpoint is share-count reduction rather than profit growth; adjusted net income is up ~18% since 2021 while EPS is up ~94%. Revenue has been flat at ~$185B for three years. China is $100M a quarter. The company burned $10B on Cruise and $10.9B on EV capacity within two years, and will eventually have to rebuild the latter from a weaker cell-cost position. Wells Fargo's $61 Underweight implies −30%, and the stock is already +51.97% over 52 weeks and 89% of the way up its range.

Top 3 Risks

01
The pricing tailwind laps and GMNA margin reverts. Roughly $700M of Q2's GMNA improvement was pricing. Guidance calls for an 8–10% full-year margin; the second half has to hold that against a comparison base that already contains the improvement. A consumer credit tightening, an inventory normalisation, or an incentive war in full-size pickups would show up here first and would compress the earnings base that the entire valuation rests on — while simultaneously hitting GM Financial through residual values and credit losses.
02
Tariff and trade policy in either direction. $2.5–3.5B of gross annual cost against $14.0–16.0B of EBIT-adjusted, mitigated by $4B of onshoring that itself costs $1.0–1.5B this year. Escalation raises the cost line directly; a full reversal strands part of the onshoring investment and hands GM's import-exposed competitors back their advantage. This is the largest single line item on the page that can move by billions on an announcement GM does not control.
03
Terminal value on a one-category franchise. Nearly all of GM's profit is U.S. full-size trucks and SUVs. The company has spent $20.9B across Cruise and EV capacity in two years demonstrating that it cannot yet earn a return on the alternatives, while Chinese OEMs have taken China and continue to export. If the market's low terminal-growth assumption is right, the multiple never expands and the return is limited to free cash flow plus buyback — a decent outcome, but nothing like the re-rating the $130+ price targets require.
  Industry Commentary
The EV Retreat Is an Industry Event, Not a GM Event
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
GM's $10.9B of charges is the visible edge of a sector-wide capital reversal. The removal of U.S. consumer purchase incentives produced a demand air-pocket that management described as "a smaller EV market following reductions in consumer incentives," and the industry response has been uniform: idle capacity, cancel contracts, settle with suppliers, convert plants back to internal combustion. GM alone has cut ~3,300 jobs across EV, battery and adjacent functions, halted cell production at two Ultium Cells joint-venture plants with LG Energy Solution, cut Factory Zero to a single shift, discontinued BrightDrop and sold a stake in an Ultium plant. The strategic problem is that none of this makes the eventual transition cheaper — it makes it later, and it means the second build-out starts against Chinese and Korean cell makers who did not stop.
Tariffs Have Rewritten the Manufacturing Map
ONGOING HEADWIND
GM is guiding to $2.5–3.5B of gross tariff cost in 2026 and answering it with $4B of onshoring across three domestic plants, taking U.S. production capacity above 2 million units — with a $500M Section 232 refund anticipated. Layered on top are $1.5–2.0B of commodity, logistics and DRAM cost and $1.0–1.5B of onshoring execution expense. The sector-wide read: North American production is being reshored at real cost, structurally raising the industry's fixed-cost base, and the OEMs with existing U.S. truck plants — GM chief among them — carry the smallest relative burden. That relative advantage is visible in the numbers: GM absorbed all of it and still raised guidance twice, while Ford lost $7.40B and Stellantis $22.25B on a trailing-twelve-month basis.
Capital Discipline Is the New Auto Narrative
EMERGING TAILWIND
The most informative price action in this report is that GM rose up to 9% — roughly $7B of market capitalisation — on the day it announced a $7.6B EV write-down. The market has stopped paying legacy OEMs for ambition and started paying them for cash returned. GM has now retired ~40% of its shares in five years, killed Cruise after $10B, cut EV capacity by $10.9B, and been re-rated +51.97% over 52 weeks for doing so. Bull framing: at 6.4× forward earnings with $9.5–11.5B of adjusted automotive free cash flow and $3.5B of buyback authorisation left, the arithmetic compounds per-share value even if the multiple never moves — and Super Cruise plus the 2028 personal-autonomy programme are carried at zero. Bear framing: a company that shrinks its share count faster than it grows its profit is liquidating gracefully, not compounding. Roughly 39% of the 2026 EPS midpoint is buyback, revenue has been flat for three years, China is $100M a quarter, and 6.11% ROIC says the reinvestment opportunity that would justify a higher multiple does not currently exist.
