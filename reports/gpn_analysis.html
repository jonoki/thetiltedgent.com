01 Company Overview

In January 2026 Global Payments did something few large companies attempt: it swapped one half of itself for another in a single set of transactions. It sold Issuer Solutions — the old TSYS card-processing business it bought for $21.5B in 2019, which runs the back end of credit cards for banks — to FIS at a $13.5B enterprise value, and in the same set of transactions bought Worldpay, the big merchant acquirer (a company that processes card payments for shops and other businesses), from private-equity firm GTCR and FIS at a $24.25B enterprise valuation, paid in cash and stock. What came out the other side is what management calls a "pure-play, commerce solutions provider": a company that only helps merchants take payments, serving more than 6 million merchant locations, processing about $3.7 trillion of payment volume and roughly 94 billion transactions a year across more than 175 countries.

The price of that trade shows up everywhere in the accounts. Trailing-twelve-month GAAP revenue is $11.49B, almost all of the rise Worldpay arriving rather than organic growth. GAAP net income over the same window is a loss of $1.29B (EPS −$4.67), because the first nine months of 2026 booked about $1.49B of losses from discontinued operations (10-Q), driven by tax on non-deductible goodwill when Issuer Solutions was sold. Management guides to adjusted EPS of $13.60–$13.80 for 2026, reaffirmed on November 5. The stock trades at $97.40 — roughly 7.1x that adjusted number and about 1.1 times its book value per share of $87.45. That gap between the adjusted story and the GAAP ledger, plus $23.70B of debt (StockAnalysis, including lease obligations), is the whole debate: either the market is mispricing a scaled acquirer at a single-digit multiple, or it is correctly discounting integration risk, leverage and the gap between "adjusted" and real.

Since the last edition the stock has had a second driver besides the business. On October 30 the Financial Times reported that the private-equity firms CD&R and GTCR had teamed up to explore a buyout valuing Global Payments at about $40B including debt, in talks it said may not result in a deal. Asked on the November 5 earnings call, CEO Cameron Bready said "we do not comment on market rumors or speculation." The shares rose 13.0% that day on 16.7M shares traded, about seven times the average of the previous 60 sessions. The quarter reported on November 5 was in line: adjusted EPS of $3.58 (up 10%), adjusted net revenue of $3.18B (up more than 4% on a like-for-like basis, faster than in Q2), an adjusted operating margin of 42.5% and an unchanged full-year outlook. Management raised its target for annual run-rate Worldpay cost savings to $650M.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$3.7T annual payment volume 6M+ merchant locations 175+ countries Software-partner distribution Genius POS bundle Enterprise margins (~78% adj.)

Merchant acquiring is a volume business: every transaction earns a sliver, so the processor with the most volume running over the same technology wins. The Worldpay deal is a bet on exactly that — combining an SMB-and-software franchise with a global enterprise acquirer to run more of the world's card swipes through one cost base. The moat is distribution, not technology: software partners that embed Global Payments, and sales forces that sign merchants one location at a time. The vulnerability is that payments technology itself is increasingly a commodity, and the fastest-growing competitors sell to the same merchants with newer software.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage while integrating. $23.70B of debt (including leases) and net leverage of 3.4x at September 30 (Q3 call). The Altman Z-score (a distress screen where lower is weaker) is 0.65. More than 90% of the debt is at fixed rates, at an average cost of about 4% (CFO, Q3 call), but there is little room for an integration stumble and the buyback at the same time. Nine-month operating cash flow was $212.9M against $2,141.4M a year earlier.
02
"Adjusted" is doing heavy lifting. Q3 2026 adjusted EPS was $3.58; GAAP diluted EPS was $1.06. For the first nine months GAAP EPS was −$5.59 against $10.00 adjusted, and the trailing GAAP EPS is −$4.67 while 2026 adjusted guidance is $13.60–$13.80. Some of that gap is one-off (the Issuer tax charge), but acquisition accounting will keep GAAP well below adjusted for years. The stock's 7x multiple is a multiple of the adjusted number.
03
SMB is the slowest and most contested channel. Nearly half of adjusted net revenue grew 5% normalized in Q3, excluding businesses sold, and SMB GAAP operating income fell 3.5% year on year ($659.3M to $636.1M). Genius is the answer management points to; it has to show up in the growth rate, not just in bookings.
04
A 15% holder that got its shares in the deal. GTCR owns 15.45% of the shares per its January 13G, received as part of the Worldpay price. If it sells, the shares land on the market; it is also reported to be one of the two firms exploring a buyout.

Notable One-Off Items

The big one is the Issuer Solutions sale: in the first nine months of 2026, discontinued operations lost $1.49B net of tax, about $1.5B of it tax expense from derecognising goodwill that was "not deductible for U.S. federal income tax purposes." That single item turns a profitable company into a GAAP loss on every trailing measure and will fall out of the numbers after Q1 2027. Second, comparability: Q3 2026 GAAP revenue of $3.31B versus $2.02B a year earlier is +63.7% reported, but it compares a company with Worldpay and without Issuer Solutions to one with the opposite — use the normalized rate of over 4%. Third, share count: 43.3M shares (43,268,041, per the January 2026 8-K) were issued for Worldpay; the 10-Q cover shows 264,635,810 shares outstanding at October 30, and Q3 buybacks retired 1.73M shares for $141.4M.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $119.04 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $97.40, GPN lost 18.2% of its price. Adding back roughly $5 per share of dividends ($1.00 a year) gives an estimated total return near −14%. Over the same window the S&P 500 (SPY, price only) rose 61.4%, from $455.56 to $735.29. The highest month-end close of the window is $149.88 in January 2022; the stock has not seen it since. The path had three acts: a 2022 slide to under $100, a 2023–early 2024 recovery to $133.66, and then a two-year slide to a five-year low month-end close of $67.30 in March 2026 — the quarter the Worldpay swap closed and a $1.8B GAAP net loss was booked. Since then the stock is up 44.7%, including 13.0% on October 30.

The 6-period monthly RSI bottomed at 22.02 in May 2025, the month after the Worldpay deal was agreed — the most oversold reading in the series — and sat below or near 30 repeatedly through early 2026. It peaked at 67.79 in August 2026 and stands at 67.39 on November 6, near that peak. Unlike most of the last five years, the price now sits well above its 10-month moving average (about $81.48): $97.40 is 19.5% over it. The stock is up 25.8% from its $77.40 December 2025 close.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 7.1x management's 2026 adjusted EPS guide, and about 1.1 times book value, for the owner of one of the largest merchant-acquiring franchises in the world: $3.7 trillion of volume, 6 million-plus locations, an Enterprise channel earning ~79% adjusted margins, and a Platforms business growing its value-added services 22% (Q3). The Issuer Solutions loss that makes trailing GAAP ugly is a one-time tax charge. Consensus sees adjusted EPS rising 18.5% to $16.17 in 2027, the quarter just reported was in line with guidance reaffirmed, the cost-savings target was raised to $650M, and the company is paying down debt while buying back stock. The reported $40B buyout interest, if it led to a bid, would put a price on the business. If normalized growth merely holds 4–5% and leverage reaches 3.0x, a 7x multiple is hard to defend.

Bear Case

The cheap multiple is a multiple of an adjusted number; on GAAP the company lost money over the past twelve months and earned $1.06 per share in Q3 against $3.58 adjusted. It carries $23.70B of debt and a 0.65 Altman Z-score through the hardest phase of the largest integration in its history, and nine-month operating cash flow was $212.9M against $2,141.4M a year earlier. The largest channel, SMB, grew 5% normalized in Q3 excluding businesses sold, the slowest of the three, in the most competitive corner of payments. Guidance was cut once in the first year, a 15% holder (GTCR) could sell, and the stock has lost 18% in five years through three separate strategies. The Financial Times said the buyout talks may not result in a deal, and the company will not comment on them; if no deal comes, part of the October 30 jump has nothing underneath it. "Cheap" has been the description of this stock for most of that time.

Top 3 Risks

01
Integration execution under leverage. Worldpay added ~$10B of goodwill and pushed total debt to $23.70B. Missing the 3.0x leverage target, or weak free cash flow (nine-month operating cash flow $212.9M; adjusted free cash flow conversion of about 92% in Q3 against a 90% full-year expectation), would force a choice between buybacks and paying down debt — and the equity story depends on both.
02
Growth at the low end of the guide. 2026 guidance was cut on August 5 to 4–5% normalized growth, citing the Middle East conflict's effect on travel, and reaffirmed on November 5 after a Q3 of just over 4%. Enterprise is the most travel-exposed channel, and the CFO said travel volumes remain very much below pre-conflict levels. A second cut would reset the adjusted-EPS base on which the whole valuation rests.
03
The adjusted-to-GAAP gap, GTCR's stake and deal talk. Investors are pricing adjusted EPS; accounting earnings are a fraction of it and will stay so while acquisition amortisation runs. GTCR's 15.45% stake is a large block of stock that could come to market if it decides to sell. And the price now includes reported buyout interest on which the company will not comment.
Industry Commentary
Merchant acquiring is consolidating — and repricing
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The three-way trade that closed in January 2026 unwound two earlier bets at once: FIS's ownership of Worldpay (it agreed in July 2023 to sell a majority to GTCR, completed that sale in early 2024, and has now sold its last 45% to Global Payments) and Global Payments' 2019 merger with TSYS. Each company ended up concentrated in one side of the card network — FIS on the issuing banks, Global Payments on the merchants. The listed peers are not being rewarded for scale in a straight line: at the November 6 close our calculation puts Fiserv at $33.46B (up 35.3% since September 21), PayPal at $49.21B (+12.6%) and Block at $51.67B (+9.7%), against Global Payments' $25.78B (+14.2%). Scale is the defence; for Global Payments, the rise in the stock since September has come mostly with the reported buyout interest.

Cross-border and travel volumes
ONGOING HEADWIND

Global Payments cut its 2026 forecast in August because the Middle East conflict is reducing travel-related spending and cross-border transactions, and reaffirmed it on November 5. The CFO put the Q3 impact at less than 1 percentage point and said travel volumes were still very much below pre-conflict levels. The risk is how long that lasts.

Software-led distribution — the growth channel
EMERGING TAILWIND

The fastest-growing parts of the business are the ones sold as software rather than as a processing rate: on the Q3 call, Genius POS bookings more than doubled year over year, the number of locations sold rose about 65% and Platforms value-added revenue grew 22%, normalized. The bull framing: payments bundled into software is how an acquirer escapes price competition, and Global Payments now has both the software and, with Worldpay, the global processing scale behind it. The bear framing: bookings and location counts are leading indicators management chose to highlight; the lagging indicator, SMB revenue growth, was 5% in Q3 excluding businesses sold. Watch the Q4 print for whether the two converge.
