01 Company Overview

In January 2026 Global Payments did something few large companies attempt: it swapped one half of itself for another in a single set of transactions. It sold Issuer Solutions — the old TSYS card-processing business it bought for $21.5B in 2019, which runs the back end of credit cards for banks — to FIS at a $13.5B enterprise value, and in the same set of transactions bought Worldpay, the big merchant acquirer, from private-equity firm GTCR and FIS at a $24.25B enterprise valuation, paid in cash and stock. What came out the other side is what management calls a "pure-play commerce solutions provider": a company that only helps merchants take payments, serving more than 6 million merchant locations, processing about $3.7 trillion of payment volume and roughly 94 billion transactions a year across more than 175 countries.

The price of that trade shows up everywhere in the accounts. Trailing-twelve-month GAAP revenue is $10.21B, up 32.2% — almost all of it Worldpay arriving, not organic growth. GAAP net income over the same window is a loss of $934M (EPS −$3.67), because the first half of 2026 booked about $1.69B of losses from discontinued operations, driven by tax on non-deductible goodwill when Issuer Solutions was sold. Meanwhile management guides to adjusted EPS of $13.60–$13.80 for 2026. The stock trades at $85.29 — roughly 6.2x that adjusted number, and just under its book value per share of $86.94. That gap between the adjusted story and the GAAP ledger, plus $23.56B of debt, is the whole debate: either the market is mispricing a scaled acquirer at a single-digit multiple, or it is correctly discounting integration risk, leverage and the gap between "adjusted" and real.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$3.7T annual payment volume 6M+ merchant locations 175+ countries Software-partner distribution Genius POS bundle Enterprise margins (~78% adj.)

Merchant acquiring is a scale game with thin unit economics: every transaction earns a sliver, so the processor with the most volume over the same fixed technology stack wins. The Worldpay deal is a bet on exactly that — combining an SMB-and-software franchise with a global enterprise acquirer to run more of the world's card swipes through one cost base. The moat is distribution, not technology: software partners that embed Global Payments, and sales forces that sign merchants one location at a time. The vulnerability is that payments technology itself is increasingly a commodity, and the fastest-growing competitors sell to the same merchants with newer software.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage while integrating. $23.56B of debt, a StockAnalysis debt-to-EBITDA of 5.18 on trailing GAAP EBITDA and management's own adjusted net leverage just under 3.5x. The Altman Z-score is 0.61. There is no near-term funding stress in the disclosures — the debt is >90% fixed at ~4% — but there is little room for an integration stumble and the buyback at the same time.
02
"Adjusted" is doing heavy lifting. Q2 2026 adjusted EPS was $3.46; GAAP diluted EPS from continuing operations was $0.43 and total GAAP EPS $0.05. On a trailing basis GAAP EPS is −$3.67 while 2026 adjusted guidance is $13.60–$13.80. Some of that gap is one-off (the Issuer tax charge), but acquisition accounting will keep GAAP well below adjusted for years. The stock's 6x multiple is a multiple of the adjusted number.
03
SMB is the slowest and most contested channel. Nearly half of adjusted net revenue grew 4% normalized, and SMB GAAP operating income fell 6.1% year on year. Genius is the answer management points to; it has to show up in the growth rate, not just in bookings.
04
A 15% holder with a cost basis in someone else's deal. GTCR owns 15.45% of the shares, received as part of the Worldpay price. Whenever it chooses to sell, the supply lands on the market. Lock-up terms were not found in the sources fetched.

Notable One-Off Items

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $157.58 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $85.29, GPN lost 45.9% of its price. Adding back roughly $5 per share of dividends ($1.00 a year) gives an estimated total return near −43%. September 2021 was the top of the window; the stock never saw it again. The path had three acts: a 2021–2022 de-rating from $157 to under $100, a 2023–early 2024 recovery to $133.66, and then a two-year slide to a five-year low month-end close of $67.30 in March 2026 — the quarter the Worldpay swap closed and the first GAAP loss arrived.

The 6-period monthly RSI bottomed at 21.98 in May 2025, the month after the Worldpay deal was agreed — the most oversold reading in the series — and sat below or near 30 repeatedly through early 2026. It peaked at 67.78 in August 2026 and has eased to 57.04. Unlike most of the last five years, the price now sits above its 10-month moving average (about $77.33): July–August 2026 put it back above that line for the first time since January 2025. The stock is up 10.2% from its $77.40 December 2025 close.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 6.2x management's 2026 adjusted EPS guide, and just under book value, for the owner of one of the largest merchant-acquiring franchises in the world: $3.7 trillion of volume, 6 million-plus locations, an Enterprise channel earning ~78% adjusted margins, and a Platforms business growing its value-added services 25%. The Issuer Solutions loss that makes trailing GAAP ugly is a one-time tax charge. Consensus sees adjusted EPS rising 17% to $15.99 in 2027, the company is retiring stock ($1.1B in six months) while deleveraging, and brokers including Morgan Stanley and Wolfe raised targets in August. If normalized growth merely holds 4–5% and leverage reaches 3.0x, a 6x multiple is hard to defend.

Bear Case

The cheap multiple is a multiple of an adjusted number; on GAAP the company lost money over the past twelve months and earned $0.43 per share from continuing operations in its first clean quarter against $3.46 adjusted. It carries $23.56B of debt and a 0.61 Altman Z-score through the hardest phase of the largest integration in its history. The largest channel, SMB, grew only 4% and saw GAAP operating income fall, in the most competitive corner of payments. Guidance has already been cut once in the first year, a 15% holder (GTCR) can sell at any time, and the stock has lost 46% in five years through three separate strategies. "Cheap" has been the description of this stock for most of that time.

Top 3 Risks

01
Integration execution under leverage. Worldpay added ~$10B of goodwill and pushed total debt to $23.56B. Missing the 3.0x leverage target, or a dip in free cash flow conversion (Q2: ~75%, guide: >90%), would force a choice between buybacks and deleveraging — and the equity story depends on both.
02
Growth at the low end of the guide. 2026 guidance was cut on August 5 to 4–5% normalized growth, with Middle East travel costing ~100bps. Enterprise is the most travel-exposed channel; SMB is already growing at the bottom of the range. A second cut would reset the adjusted-EPS base on which the whole valuation rests.
03
The adjusted-to-GAAP gap and share overhang. Investors are pricing adjusted EPS; accounting earnings are a fraction of it and will stay so while acquisition amortisation runs. Separately, GTCR's 15.45% stake is a known supply of stock whenever it decides to monetise.
Industry Commentary
Merchant acquiring is consolidating — and repricing
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The three-way trade that closed in January 2026 unwound two earlier bets at once: FIS's ownership of Worldpay (it sold a majority to GTCR in July 2023 and its last 45% to Global Payments now) and Global Payments' 2019 merger with TSYS. Each company ended up concentrated in one side of the card network — FIS on the issuing banks, Global Payments on the merchants. The listed peers are not being rewarded for scale: on September 21, 2026 Fiserv was valued at $24.71B after lowering its 2026 guidance and acknowledging the possibility of negative organic growth, and PayPal at $45.01B after a reported buyout approach fell away. Scale is the defence; it has not yet been a re-rating.

Cross-border and travel volumes
ONGOING HEADWIND

Global Payments cut its 2026 forecast because the Middle East conflict is reducing travel-related spending and cross-border transactions. The drag was about 400bps on the Enterprise channel and about 100bps on the company. Management calls it "modest and transitory." The risk is duration: the guide assumes it fades; nothing in the sources fetched says when.

Software-led distribution — the growth channel
EMERGING TAILWIND

The fastest-growing parts of the business are the ones sold as software rather than as a processing rate: Genius POS bookings up more than 25% sequentially, new Genius locations up more than 50% year over year, and Platforms value-added revenue up 25%. The bull framing: payments bundled into software is how an acquirer escapes price competition, and Global Payments now has both the software and, with Worldpay, the global processing scale behind it. The bear framing: bookings and location counts are leading indicators management chose to highlight; the lagging indicator — SMB revenue growth of 4% — has not moved yet. Watch whether the two converge in the Q3 and Q4 prints.
