01 Company Overview

Goldman Sachs is a global investment bank and asset manager that intermediates capital for corporations, governments, financial institutions and wealthy individuals. It is the world's #1 M&A advisor by announced deal volume, a top-two global equities trading franchise, and — through Goldman Sachs Asset & Wealth Management — one of the five largest active asset managers on earth with roughly $3.7 trillion in assets under supervision.

Fiscal 2025 net revenues were $58.28B, up +8.9% YoY, with net earnings of $16.30B and EPS of $51.32. The business has since inflected violently higher: TTM revenue through June 2026 is $67.57B (+23.3% YoY) on TTM EPS of $64.63 (+42.6%), driven by a capital-markets reopening that produced a Q2 2026 quarter — $20.34B of net revenues and $20.98 of EPS against a $14.47 consensus — that ranks among the best in the firm's 157-year history. Goldman is the second-largest pure capital-markets franchise in the S&P 500 by market value behind Morgan Stanley's diversified wealth model, and sits at roughly #48 among US listed companies.

Brand Signaling Value

Goldman's moat is not technological — it is relational, reputational and regulatory, and it compounds. Boards hire Goldman for the biggest, most contested transactions because hiring Goldman is itself a signal to shareholders; that reflexive advantage has kept the firm at or near #1 in announced global M&A for most of three decades and gives it roughly 9.1% of a ~$130B global fee wallet, ahead of JPMorgan's ~8.8%. On the markets side, the moat is scale and balance sheet: prime brokerage and financing require the ability to warehouse risk and extend credit at size, which effectively fences the business into five or six firms globally and produces recurring, spread-like revenue that has grown every year since 2019. Regulation is a two-sided moat — the CET1 and stress-testing regime that constrains Goldman's returns is also a ~$100B+ barrier to any new entrant. Finally, the partnership culture remains an unusually effective talent flywheel: the roughly 400-person partner class is a genuine scarcity asset, even as boutiques bid aggressively for individual bankers.

Key Vulnerabilities

Cyclicality of the revenue base. Roughly two-thirds of revenue comes from activity levels Goldman does not control — deal volumes, issuance windows, and trading volatility. The firm went from $59.3B of revenue in 2021 to $46.3B in 2023, a 22% peak-to-trough decline that cut EPS by 62%. Q2 2026's $20.3B quarter is the mirror image of that, and it is emphatically not a run-rate. Investors paying 16x for cyclical peak earnings are taking a real multiple-compression risk.

Private credit disintermediation. Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl and their peers now originate large-cap leveraged loans directly, bypassing the syndicated market Goldman underwrites. The ~$17–18T private-markets complex is taking share of exactly the debt-underwriting and leveraged-finance economics that historically sat in GBM. Goldman is hedged — GS Alternatives is itself a top-tier private credit manager — but the fee margin on managing private credit is lower than on underwriting it.

Boutique talent leakage in advisory. Centerview, Evercore, PJT, Moelis and Perella Weinberg have taken roughly 8–10 points of the advisory fee pool over fifteen years by paying senior bankers more and carrying no balance sheet, capital charge or compliance burden. Every senior departure is a direct transfer of client relationships — the exact asset the moat rests on.

Regulatory and litigation asymmetry. Goldman's stress-capital buffer is set annually by the Federal Reserve and can move hundreds of basis points on a model change the firm cannot appeal. The firm also carries persistent legal exposure — 1MDB, consumer-lending practices, and periodic trading-conduct matters — with reasonably possible losses in excess of accrual that management has historically disclosed in the low billions.

Notable One-Off Items

Consumer-platform wind-down (multi-year, mostly behind). Goldman's retreat from consumer banking produced a chain of discrete charges: the GreenSky sale (2024, roughly $0.5B of pre-tax hits including a goodwill impairment), sales of Marcus loan portfolios, the transfer of the General Motors card program to Barclays (2025), and the agreed exit from the Apple Card partnership. Cumulative pre-tax drag since 2020 approaches ~$6B. FY2025 Platform Solutions revenue fell an estimated ~37% as a direct result — a revenue decline that improved earnings quality.

Commercial real estate marks (largely cleared). Goldman recognized roughly $1.5B of CRE-related impairments and markdowns across 2023–2024 in historical principal investments and lending. Remaining on-balance-sheet CRE equity exposure has been reduced substantially and no longer drives quarterly variance, but office concentration remains a residual tail risk.

Litigation and regulatory provisions. Goldman's net provision for litigation runs anywhere from roughly $0.2B to over $1.0B a year and is genuinely lumpy — 2023 included a large consumer-practices-related accrual. Reasonably possible losses in excess of accruals have historically been disclosed in the low-single-digit billions. Assume a normalized ~$0.5B/yr drag rather than zero.

The Q2 2026 quarter itself. The $20.34B revenue / $20.98 EPS print beat consensus by 45% on the bottom line. A material portion came from exceptional trading conditions and a burst of deal closings. Modeling that quarter as a run-rate is the single most likely valuation error an investor can make here; FY2026 consensus of $70.72 implies the back half normalizes to roughly $16–17 per share per quarter.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Goldman is the highest-quality operating leverage in the market on a capital-markets upcycle that is only two years old: #1 in M&A, top-two in equities, a record deal backlog, and a 14.9x forward multiple against 37.8% expected FY2026 EPS growth. The strategic reset is complete — consumer losses are gone, CRE marks are cleared, and the mix is shifting toward $3.7T of fee-generating AUS. A softer Basel endgame plus a reduced stress capital buffer frees several hundred basis points of excess CET1 for buybacks that are already shrinking the share count ~5% a year. At 2.9x book with a 21%+ ROTCE, the math still works if the cycle has legs.

Bear Case

You are paying 16x trailing earnings for a business whose earnings just grew 43% because of trading conditions nobody controls, at a stock price up 41% in twelve months and 242% off the 2023 low. The Q2 2026 quarter was a 45% consensus beat — that is the definition of an outlier, not a run-rate, and consensus itself implies the back half steps down ~20%. History is unambiguous: Goldman's EPS fell 62% from 2021 to 2023 and the stock de-rated to 1.0x tangible book. Sixteen of 25 analysts are already on the sidelines. Meanwhile private credit is structurally taking the leveraged-finance economics and boutiques keep taking advisory talent.

Top 3 Risks
01
Peak-cycle earnings meeting a de-rating multiple. The most probable way to lose money here is not a blow-up but ordinary mean reversion: trading revenue normalizes, the deal pipeline stalls, and a 16x multiple on falling earnings becomes 12x on lower earnings. That combination cost investors 29% in 2022 and it requires no crisis at all — only a quiet quarter.
02
Regulatory capital reversal. The deregulation thesis is embedded in the current price. Goldman's stress capital buffer is reset annually by the Federal Reserve on models the firm cannot see or contest, and the political environment that produced a softened Basel endgame can change. A single adverse stress-test result would curtail buybacks — the mechanism supplying roughly 5 points of annual EPS growth.
03
Concentrated tail risk in a $1.9T balance sheet. Prime brokerage, financing and market-making mean single-counterparty and single-position exposures that are invisible until they aren't — Archegos cost the industry over $10B in 2021. Add residual CRE office exposure and ongoing litigation reserves, and the distribution of outcomes has a genuinely fat left tail that a 1.29 beta understates.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Matters Right Now
The Capital-Markets Reopening
EMERGING TAILWIND

After the deepest issuance drought since 2009, the machine is running again. The global investment-banking fee pool bottomed near $95B in 2023, recovered to roughly $130B in 2025, and is tracking toward ~$150B in 2026 — still below the $160B+ 2021 peak, which is the bull's core point: this is a recovery, not a blow-off. Announced M&A volumes are at their strongest since 2021 as private-equity sponsors clear a backlog of exits accumulated over three years of frozen markets, and the large-cap IPO window has reopened. Goldman's ~9.1% share of that pool is the highest of any firm globally, and advisory revenue is recognized at closing — meaning the announcements happening now are 2027 revenue already largely locked in. The Q2 2026 result, with an estimated ~$3.4B of investment banking fees (+55% YoY), is the first quarter where the backlog conversion showed up in full.

Private Credit Is Eating the Middle of the Business
ONGOING HEADWIND

Private-markets AUM has reached roughly $17–18T, and the direct-lending segment has grown from a niche into a $2T+ asset class capable of writing $5B+ unitranche cheques without a syndicate. That is a direct substitution for the leveraged-finance and debt-underwriting economics that historically sat inside Goldman's GBM segment. Goldman's response is to be on both sides — Goldman Sachs Alternatives is itself one of the largest private credit managers globally, with a substantial share of the $500B+ alternatives book inside AWM — but the trade is not economically neutral. Underwriting a loan and distributing it earns a large fee on almost no capital; managing a private credit fund earns a smaller recurring fee on locked-up capital. The revenue is more durable and higher-multiple; the near-term dollars are lower. Watch the ratio of AWM management fees to GBM debt underwriting over the next eight quarters — it is the clearest single read on whether this transition is accretive.

Basel Endgame, the SCB, and the Bull-vs-Bear Fault Line
THE DECIDING VARIABLE

The 2023 Basel III endgame proposal would have raised large-bank capital requirements by roughly 19% and was, for Goldman specifically, an existential constraint on ROTCE. The re-proposed framework is dramatically softer, and supplementary leverage ratio reform directly benefits the low-risk-weight dealer activity — Treasury market-making, repo, prime financing — where Goldman is a top-two player. The bull frames this as a permanent step-up: a lower requirement means a structurally higher achievable ROTCE, several hundred basis points of excess CET1 released into buybacks, and justification for a multiple closer to Morgan Stanley's. The bear frames it as the most reversible input in the model: capital rules are set annually by an unelected committee using undisclosed models, they tightened hard in 2020–2023 and can tighten again, and a stock trading at 2.9x book with 28% of analysts rating it Buy has already priced the friendly version. Everything else in this analysis — the earnings power, the moat, the AUS growth — is genuinely knowable. This one is a policy bet, and investors should size it as such.
