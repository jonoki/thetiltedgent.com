01 Company Overview

In 1919 Erle P. Halliburton started a well-cementing business in Duncan, Oklahoma, with a borrowed wagon, a team of mules and a pump. A century later the company he founded is one of the three big names in oilfield services, alongside SLB and Baker Hughes. Halliburton does not own oil; it rents out know-how, crews and equipment to the companies that do. FY2025 revenue was $22.18B, down 3.3% from $22.94B in FY2024, and GAAP EPS fell to $1.50 from $2.83, dragged by $831M of special charges; adjusted EPS was $2.42. Its history is not quiet: the 1998 Dresser Industries merger, the 2007 separation of KBR, Dick Cheney's tenure as CEO (1995–2000), the cement job on Deepwater Horizon, and a $3.5B breakup fee paid when the 2016 attempt to buy Baker Hughes collapsed. The ticker has not changed hands through any of it — HAL is Halliburton, and has been an S&P 500 member since the index's launch in March 1957.

The story of 2026 is a service company caught between two geopolitical currents. The first is Venezuela: the stock jumped in January as the market priced a return of Western service work to one of the world's largest oil reserves, and on September 21, 2026 Halliburton signed memoranda of understanding with Eneva and WESCA for field evaluation and development there. The second is the Middle East conflict, which cost roughly 2–3 cents of EPS in Q1 2026 and was guided at 7–9 cents in Q2, as activity in Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the UAE, Iraq and Kuwait was disrupted. The shares ran from a $28.26 close at the end of 2025 to $42.30 at the end of April and have given back a fifth of that since, to $33.64. The central debate: is the international growth engine — highest second-quarter international revenue in more than a decade — strong enough to carry a North American fracking business that is barely growing, while margins sit roughly five points below their 2024 peak?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale in North American fracking Century of cementing know-how Global base network (Venezuela ~9 decades) Integrated well-construction contracts Three-player oligopoly at the top Proprietary tools & reservoir software

The moat is scale and presence. Oilfield service is a business where the customer pays for a crew to show up, in a remote place, with the right equipment, and not to make a mistake that costs a well. Only a handful of companies can do that across dozens of countries at once — and Halliburton has had bases in Venezuela for nearly nine decades, which is exactly why it is first in line if work reopens there. Recent wins show the model: an integrated contract from bp for the Bumerangue field appraisal in Brazil, and a bundled deepwater well-construction and completions award. The weakness is that none of this protects price. When producers cut budgets, three well-capitalised competitors fight over fewer jobs, and margins show it: operating margin fell from roughly 18% at its Q2 2024 peak to 12.6% in Q1 2026, per TIKR's calculation.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A price-taker in a cyclical market. Halliburton's customers set their budgets off the oil price and their own return targets. Revenue shrank 3.3% in FY2025 and gross margin fell from 18.75% to 15.71%; trailing gross margin is now 15.08%. The company cannot will demand into existence.
02
North America is ~40% of revenue and not growing. Q2 2026 North America revenue of $2.3B was flat YoY. The fracking fleet is the part of the business with the highest operating leverage — which cuts both ways.
03
Geopolitics is now in the income statement. The Middle East conflict cost an estimated 2–3 cents of EPS in Q1 2026 and was guided at 7–9 cents in Q2. The Venezuela opportunity, on the other side of the ledger, is so far memoranda of understanding — not contracts, not revenue.
04
The smallest of the big three. At about $28B of market value, Halliburton is roughly a third the size of SLB ($76.82B) and half the size of Baker Hughes ($57.41B). Both rivals have larger non-oilfield or international businesses; Halliburton is the purest bet on well activity.

Notable One-Off Items

Two things distort the reported figures. First, special charges: FY2025 carried $831M of them, which is why GAAP EPS ($1.50) sat so far below adjusted EPS ($2.42). As those quarters roll out of the trailing window, TTM EPS rises mechanically — the move from $1.50 to $1.91 largely reflects this rather than a better business. Second, Q2 2026 GAAP profit was flattered: GAAP operating income of $778M exceeded adjusted operating income of $683M by roughly $95M, in a quarter in which the company completed a partial sale of its chemical business. Net income of $534M ($0.64 per diluted share, up from $0.55 a year earlier) should be read with that in mind. Buybacks continue at roughly $200M a quarter (Q2 2026), after $1.007B in FY2025.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $21.62 to the September 18, 2026 settled close of $33.64, HAL gained 55.6% on price. Adding back roughly $3 per share of dividends paid over the period (an estimate from the annual dividend-per-share figures, not reinvested) brings the total return to roughly +69%. But the path was a full oil-services cycle compressed into five years: a 2022 surge as the post-pandemic energy shortage hit, a 2023–2025 grind lower as North American activity faded and margins came down, a May 2025 month-end low of $19.59, and then a 116% run to $42.30 at the end of April 2026 on Venezuela hopes and a recovering international business. Since then the stock has given back 20.5%.

The 6-period monthly RSI captures both extremes. It printed 18.5 at the May 2025 low — the most oversold reading in the series — and 89.1 at the April 2026 high, the most overbought. It now sits at 49.5, dead neutral. The 10-month moving average, at about $35.46, is above the current price: the 2026 uptrend has stalled: the stock slipped below its ten-month average in July, recovered above it in August, and is below it again now.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 14.3x consensus 2026 earnings and 11.6x 2027 earnings for the purest listed bet on well activity, at a moment when international revenue is at a decade-plus second-quarter high, Latin America grew 22% YoY in Q1, the Venezuela door is opening with a company that has had bases there for nine decades, and management is guiding margins up in both divisions for Q3. Free cash flow of $1.73B TTM is a ~6.2% yield, the dividend is covered almost three times, and buybacks run at ~$200M a quarter. Consensus expects EPS to rise 23.5% in 2027; if the Gulf normalises, the 7–9 cents a quarter of disruption comes back as earnings, and the average target of $43.24 is 28.5% above the price.

Bear Case

Service companies are paid last and cut first. FY2025 revenue fell 3.3%, gross margin dropped three points in a year, and consensus sees 2026 revenue up only 0.8% with EPS down 2.9%. North America — ~40% of revenue — is flat. The 2026 rally was built on geopolitics: a Venezuela opening that is still memoranda, and a Middle East conflict that raises the oil price while disrupting the very region where Halliburton does its drilling work. The stock has already given back a fifth of its gains and sits below its 10-month average. A P/E of 17.6 on trailing GAAP earnings is not cheap for a business whose revenue has not grown since FY2023.

Top 3 Risks

01
Customer budget cuts. Halliburton's revenue is its customers' capital spending. A lower oil price, or producers choosing buybacks over drilling, flows straight through to fleet utilisation and pricing — as FY2025's 3.3% revenue decline and margin compression showed.
02
Middle East escalation. Conflict has already cut Middle East/Asia revenue 13% YoY in Q1 2026 and was guided to cost 7–9 cents of EPS in Q2. Crews and equipment in a war zone are both a cost and a safety exposure.
03
Venezuela disappointment. Part of the 2026 re-rating priced a return to Venezuela. MOUs with Eneva and WESCA are a start, not revenue; sanctions, payment risk and politics can all stall it — and the market has shown it will reprice the hope quickly.
Industry Commentary
Oil up, activity down: the Gulf paradox
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For an oil producer, a Middle East war can be a windfall. For a service company, it is a paradox: the oil price may rise, but work in the region stops. Halliburton's Q1 2026 Middle East/Asia revenue fell 13% YoY to $1.3B on disruption across Qatar, the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Iraq and Kuwait, and CFO Eric Carre pointed to "lost revenue from Middle East disruptions and elevated logistics, fuel, and supply chain costs." Q2 revenue in the region slipped another 2% sequentially. The rest of the international business — Europe/Africa +19% sequentially, Latin America +3% — carried the quarter.

North American shale: flat is the new normal
ONGOING HEADWIND

The US fracking market that made Halliburton's last decade is no longer a growth engine. Q1 2026 North America revenue fell 4% YoY on reduced stimulation activity and lower artificial-lift sales; Q2 was flat YoY at $2.3B, though up sequentially. Jeff Miller expects "incremental improvements through the year." Incremental is the right word — this is a business being run for cash, not growth.

The international cycle — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: international revenue of $3.4B in Q2 2026 was the highest second quarter in more than a decade, the COO calls the opportunity set "the strongest I've seen in many years," and a pipeline of deepwater, Brazil and Venezuela work is arriving just as the Gulf could normalise. The bear framing: SLB and Baker Hughes, at roughly 2.7x and 2x Halliburton's market value, are chasing the same international contracts, and the region with the most upside is also the one with the most war risk. The way to adjudicate is not the P/E; it is whether the Q3 margin guidance lands and whether Venezuela MOUs turn into signed work.
