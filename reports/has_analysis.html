01 Company Overview

Hasbro is the century-old toy company behind Monopoly, Transformers, Nerf, Play-Doh, My Little Pony and G.I. Joe — and, since it bought Wizards of the Coast in 1999 for $325 million, the owner of Magic: The Gathering and Dungeons & Dragons. It was founded in 1923 by the Hassenfeld brothers, took the name Hasbro, Inc. in 1985, and moved its listing from the NYSE to Nasdaq in 2010. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $5.18B, up 18.9% (StockAnalysis), with GAAP EPS of $6.71 — at the $114.00 close, a market value of about $16.08B (our calculation: 141.04M shares × $114.00).

Here is the hand the market is actually playing: Hasbro is a card-game company wearing a toy company's jacket. Even in Q3 2026 — the quarter when retailers stock up for the holidays — the Wizards of the Coast and Digital Gaming segment earned $367.4M of segment operating profit against $173.8M for Consumer Products, on less revenue ($747.2M vs $827.9M). Over the first nine months of 2026 Wizards made $935.1M of operating profit; the whole company made $1,040.1M. Magic alone did $616.1M of revenue in the quarter, up 34%, and the CEO said the company had delivered its fourth consecutive quarter of double-digit revenue growth. The round trip in the stock tells the same story: a $101.78 month-end close in December 2021, a five-year low of $45.15 in October 2023 after the Entertainment One misadventure (bought for ~$4B in 2019, sold to Lionsgate for $500M, agreed in 2023), and a climb to $114.00 on October 21, 2026, the highest point on the five-year chart, as Magic did the lifting. The central debate has not changed, only the stakes: is Magic a durable, compounding franchise that deserves a higher price per dollar of earnings than a toy company gets, or a hit-driven product cycle — powered by licensed crossover sets such as The Hobbit and Marvel Super Heroes — that is being pulled forward? Management itself says the fourth quarter will see Magic down, partly because sales shipped early into Q3.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Magic's 30-year player base Collectible scarcity economics Dungeons & Dragons IP Century-old brand library Licensing income (Monopoly Go!) Retail shelf relationships

The real moat is a network of players, not a factory. A Magic collection is sunk capital that only has value if the game stays alive, so the player base defends the franchise on the company's behalf; every new set sells into an installed base of existing decks. That is why a segment with a margin near 50% can exist inside a company whose toy division lost money as recently as Q2. The vulnerability is that the same scarcity economics can be pushed too hard: more sets, more premium products and more licensed crossovers lift revenue now, but the history of collectibles says over-supply eventually shows up as falling secondary-market prices and fatigued players.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One franchise carries the company. Wizards earned $935.1M of Hasbro's $1,040.1M operating profit in the first nine months of 2026. A single weak Magic release window moves group earnings, and management has already said Magic will likely be down in Q4, in part because sales were pulled into Q3 (Q3 call, Investing.com transcript).
02
Tariffs sit on the toy line. The Q2 2025 goodwill impairment of $1.02B in Consumer Products was tied to tariffs; the segment lost $14.5M in Q2 2026, and its Q3 profit included $48M of tariff refunds. Guidance now assumes a lower 12.5% tariff rate on goods from China, the CFO said on the call. Trade policy is an input cost Hasbro cannot hedge away, and this year it has run in Hasbro's favour.
03
Leverage and a thin equity cushion. $3.51B of total debt against $1.43B of cash and short-term investments at September 27, 2026 (release balance sheet); $893.9M of that debt is due within a year. After two large write-downs in three years, book equity of $938.2M is small enough that debt-to-equity reads 3.74 and ROE reads 140%. Neither ratio is meaningful — but the shrunken equity base is the scar tissue of eOne and the tariff impairment.
04
Digital has been a cash sink. The $56M Q2 2026 impairment cancelled titles slated for 2028 and beyond. The profitable digital line is licensing (Monopoly Go! contributed $41M in Q3); Hasbro's own video games, starting with Exodus on April 7, 2027, have yet to show they can earn back what they cost.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported history. First, the $1.02B non-cash goodwill impairment in Consumer Products in Q2 2025, tied to tariffs, turned FY2025 into a $322.4M net loss despite revenue growing 13.7%; it has now rolled out of the trailing twelve months, which is why TTM EPS is now $6.71. Second, the $56M digital-games impairment in Q2 2026, which put GAAP EPS at $1.12 against adjusted EPS of $1.28. A new item runs the other way: about $55M of tariff refunds in Q3 2026, counted in both reported and adjusted profit. Spread over 142.5M diluted shares that is about $0.39 a share before tax; take the whole pre-tax amount off and Q3 adjusted EPS would still have been roughly $2.51 against the $1.86 Nasdaq consensus (our calculation). The release also excludes $11.8M of costs this year from unauthorised access to Hasbro's network, identified in late March 2026. Third, the FY2023 net loss of $1.49B, the year Entertainment One was sold for $500M after being bought for about $4B. Hasbro has booked write-down-driven GAAP losses in two of the last three fiscal years (FY2023 and FY2025); the free-cash-flow line ($590M FY2023, $760M FY2024, $830M FY2025, $1.23B TTM) is the steadier guide.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over the five years from the October 2021 month-end close of $95.76 to the October 21, 2026 settled close of $114.00, HAS gained 19.0% on price, before dividends ($2.80 a share a year at the current rate). Until this month the five-year price return was roughly flat; one quarter's results changed that. And the endpoints hide a full round trip: the stock fell 56% from the $101.78 December 2021 month-end to $45.15 in October 2023, then rose about 150% to $114.00 (our calculations from the chart's closes).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Even after a 30% month you are paying 17.0x trailing GAAP earnings for a company whose main segment grew 31% in Q3 at a 49% operating margin, generating $1.23B of TTM free cash flow — a ~7.6% FCF yield — and paying a 2.46% dividend while doubling its buyback target to $400M. Magic crossed $600M in a quarter for the first time, and Chris Cocks told the call: "In 2027, we expect Magic to grow on top of a record 2026. Each of our six tentpole releases next year has a full product suite." (Investing.com transcript). The toy business earned real money in the holiday-loading quarter even before the tariff refunds, the Zelda licence lands in 2027, and guidance now calls for a 27–28% adjusted operating margin for the whole company.

Bear Case

This is a single-franchise stock priced after its best quarter. Part of Q3 was borrowed from Q4 — the CEO said some Magic sales shipped a quarter early and Magic will likely be down in Q4 — and about $55M of the quarter's profit was refunds of tariffs paid in earlier years. The raised full-year guide still implies Q4 revenue roughly 7% below last year's (our calculation). Growth leaning on licensed crossover sets such as The Hobbit and on premium products is the classic late-cycle shape of a collectible boom. Meanwhile the toy business lost money as recently as Q2, $893.9M of debt falls due within a year, and the stock now sits above Wells Fargo's raised $100 target and within about 5% of UBS's $120. A 56% drawdown in 2021–23 is the reminder of what happens when this company's hit cycle turns.

Top 3 Risks

01
Magic cycle risk. Wizards earned about 90% of the company's operating profit in the first nine months of 2026 ($935.1M of $1,040.1M). A Q4 decline already flagged by management, or a 2027 set that disappoints, hits group earnings directly.
02
Tariffs and the toy P&L. Consumer Products has produced a $1.02B tariff-linked impairment, a Q2 2026 operating loss and, in Q3, a $48M profit boost from tariff refunds. Trade-policy changes flow straight into its cost of goods, in both directions.
03
Capital-allocation history. eOne (~$4B in, $500M out) and a $56M digital write-off in 2026 show a pattern of expensive diversification away from the core. With $3.51B of debt, $893.9M of it due within a year, the room for another mistake is limited.
Industry Commentary
The adult collector is the new customer
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

The most important shift in this industry is who is buying. Hasbro's growth is not coming from children's toy aisles but from adult hobbyists — trading-card players and collectors whose spending is less seasonal and whose products carry software-like margins. Magic's 34% Q3 growth, to $616.1M, is that customer in one number; on the Q2 call the CFO said Hasbro had made "a deliberate decision to increase initial print and distribution runs for Magic releases" (Investing.com transcript). The discipline question is supply: collectible economics depend on scarcity, and the incentive to print more is always strongest at the top of a cycle.

Tariffs and the retail toy aisle
ONGOING HEADWIND

Traditional toys are a low-margin, retailer-dependent, import-heavy business. Hasbro's $1.02B Consumer Products impairment in Q2 2025 was the accounting recognition that tariffs had permanently lowered what that business is worth. The peer picture is consistent: Mattel's market value at the October 21 close is about $4.84B (our calculation), roughly 30% of Hasbro's, for a company with larger trailing revenue ($5.49B vs Hasbro's $5.18B, StockAnalysis). The market pays for game IP; it does not pay for plastic.

Licensing over building
STRATEGIC SHIFT

Hasbro's most profitable digital revenue comes from licensing — $41M from Monopoly Go! in Q3 2026 — while its own game development produced a $56M impairment in Q2. The same logic runs through the Entertainment segment, which shrank to a licensing desk after eOne was sold. The bull framing: an IP owner that rents its brands to specialists earns high-margin revenue without the development risk. The bear framing: it hands the upside of a hit to the partner and keeps a royalty.
