01 Company Overview

Hasbro is the century-old toy company behind Monopoly, Transformers, Nerf, Play-Doh, My Little Pony and G.I. Joe — and, since it bought Wizards of the Coast in 1999 for $325 million, the owner of Magic: The Gathering and Dungeons & Dragons. It was founded in 1923 by the Hassenfeld brothers, took the name Hasbro, Inc. in 1985, and moved its listing from the NYSE to Nasdaq in 2010. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $4.97B, up 17.0%, with GAAP EPS of $5.60 — at the $87.72 close, a market value of about $12.37B (our calculation: 141.04M shares × $87.72).

Here is the hand the market is actually playing: Hasbro is a card-game company wearing a toy company's jacket. In Q2 2026 the Wizards of the Coast and Digital Gaming segment booked $663.8M of the company's $1,139.6M revenue (58%) and $270.0M of segment operating profit — more than Hasbro's entire reported operating profit of $252.5M, because Consumer Products lost $14.5M in the quarter. Magic alone passed $500M of quarterly revenue for the first time. The toy business the brand is famous for is, at the operating line, currently a break-even-to-loss operation that the card game carries. The round trip in the stock tells the same story: a $101.78 month-end close in December 2021, a five-year low of $45.15 in October 2023 after the Entertainment One misadventure (bought for ~$4B in 2019, sold to Lionsgate for $500M, agreed in 2023), and a climb back to $99.59 by February 2026 as Magic did the lifting. The central debate: is Magic a durable, compounding franchise that deserves a premium multiple on its own, or a hit-driven product cycle — powered by licensed crossover sets such as Marvel Super Heroes — that has been pulled forward?

The economics are those of a printing press for scarce cardboard: design once, print, and let the secondary market's scarcity pricing do the marketing. The risk is the flip side — sets such as Marvel Super Heroes are licensed crossovers, and a franchise that increasingly sells other people's intellectual property is renting part of its growth. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Magic's 30-year player base Collectible scarcity economics Dungeons & Dragons IP Century-old brand library Licensing income (Monopoly Go!) Retail shelf relationships

The real moat is a network of players, not a factory. A Magic collection is sunk capital that only has value if the game stays alive, so the player base defends the franchise on the company's behalf; every new set sells into an installed base of existing decks. That is why a segment with a 40% operating margin can exist inside a company whose toy division struggles to break even. The vulnerability is that the same scarcity economics can be pushed too hard: more sets, more premium products and more licensed crossovers lift revenue now, but the history of collectibles says over-supply eventually shows up as falling secondary-market prices and fatigued players.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One franchise carries the company. Wizards' $270.0M Q2 operating profit exceeded Hasbro's total reported operating profit of $252.5M. A single weak Magic release window now moves group earnings, and management has already guided the back half of 2026 to low-single-digit Magic growth (Q2 call, Investing.com transcript).
02
Tariffs sit on the toy line. The Q2 2025 goodwill impairment of $1.02B in Consumer Products was tied to tariffs; the segment lost $14.5M in Q2 2026 on growing revenue. Trade policy is an input cost Hasbro cannot hedge away.
03
Leverage and a thin equity cushion. $3.88B of total debt against $1.38B of cash and short-term investments; after two large write-downs in three years, book equity is small enough that debt-to-equity reads 5.31 and ROE reads 160%. Neither ratio is meaningful — but the shrunken equity base is the scar tissue of eOne and the tariff impairment.
04
Digital has been a cash sink. The $56M Q2 2026 impairment cancelled titles slated for 2028 and beyond. The profitable digital line is licensing (Monopoly Go! contributed about $44M in Q2); Hasbro's own video-game development has so far cost more than it has returned in this window.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported history. First, the $1.02B non-cash goodwill impairment in Consumer Products in Q2 2025, tied to tariffs, turned FY2025 into a $322.4M net loss despite revenue growing 13.7%; it has now rolled out of the trailing twelve months, which is why TTM EPS jumped to $5.60. Second, the $56M digital-games impairment in Q2 2026, which put GAAP EPS at $1.12 against adjusted EPS of $1.28. Third, the FY2023 net loss of $1.49B, the year Entertainment One was sold for $500M after being bought for about $4B. Hasbro has booked write-down-driven GAAP losses in two of the last three fiscal years (FY2023 and FY2025); the free-cash-flow line ($590M FY2023, $760M FY2024, $830M FY2025, $1.21B TTM) is the steadier guide.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 15.7x trailing and about 14x forward earnings for a company whose main segment grew 27% in Q2 at a 40%-plus operating margin, generating $1.21B of TTM free cash flow — a ~9.8% FCF yield — and paying a 3.19% dividend. Magic crossed $500M in a quarter for the first time; the Zelda licence lands in 2027; the digital slate has been pruned to stop the cash burn; and the $1.02B tariff write-down is behind it. If Consumer Products merely breaks even across a full year, Wizards' profit flows through to a company guiding to a 25–26% adjusted operating margin, and every analyst target on the board sits above the price.

Bear Case

This is a single-franchise stock priced as if the franchise's best year repeats. Management itself guided Magic to low-single-digit growth in the back half after +32% in Q2; revenue growth is guided down from 17% TTM to 5–7% for 2026; FY2027 consensus EPS growth is 6%. Growth leaning on licensed crossover sets and premium products is the classic late-cycle shape of a collectible boom. Meanwhile the toy business loses money in quarters that are not Christmas, sits under tariff risk, and net debt runs about 1.9x EBITDA. A 56% drawdown in 2021–23 is the reminder of what happens when this company's hit cycle turns.

Top 3 Risks

01
Magic cycle risk. Wizards' Q2 operating profit exceeded the whole company's. A deceleration already in the guidance, or a set that disappoints, hits group earnings directly.
02
Tariffs and the toy P&L. Consumer Products has produced a $1.02B tariff-linked impairment and a Q2 2026 operating loss. Trade-policy changes flow straight into its cost of goods.
03
Capital-allocation history. eOne (~$4B in, $500M out) and a $56M digital write-off in 2026 show a pattern of expensive diversification away from the core. With $3.88B of debt, the room for another mistake is limited.
Industry Commentary
The adult collector is the new customer
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

The most important shift in this industry is who is buying. Hasbro's growth is not coming from children's toy aisles but from adult hobbyists — trading-card players and collectors whose spending is less seasonal and whose products carry software-like margins. Magic's 32% Q2 growth, is that customer in one number; on the Q2 call the CFO said Hasbro had made "a deliberate decision to increase initial print and distribution runs for Magic releases" (Investing.com transcript). The discipline question is supply: collectible economics depend on scarcity, and the incentive to print more is always strongest at the top of a cycle.

Tariffs and the retail toy aisle
ONGOING HEADWIND

Traditional toys are a low-margin, retailer-dependent, import-heavy business. Hasbro's $1.02B Consumer Products impairment in Q2 2025 was the accounting recognition that tariffs had permanently lowered what that business is worth. The peer picture is consistent: Mattel's market value at the September 21 close is about $3.74B (our calculation), roughly 30% of Hasbro's, for a company with larger revenue ($5.49B vs Hasbro's $4.97B on StockAnalysis's peer table). The market pays for game IP; it does not pay for plastic.

Licensing over building
STRATEGIC SHIFT

Hasbro's most profitable digital revenue comes from licensing — about $44M from Monopoly Go! in Q2 2026 — while its own game development produced a $56M impairment. The same logic runs through the Entertainment segment, which shrank to a licensing desk after eOne was sold. The bull framing: an IP owner that rents its brands to specialists earns high-margin revenue without the development risk. The bear framing: it hands the upside of a hit to the partner and keeps a royalty.
