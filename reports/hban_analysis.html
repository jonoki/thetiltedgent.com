01 Company Overview

Huntington Bancshares is a 160-year-old Columbus, Ohio bank holding company that operates The Huntington National Bank — consumer and commercial deposits, lending, payments, mortgage banking, wealth management and capital markets, distributed through branches, ATMs, digital channels and call centres. FY2025 revenue was $7.69B, up 10.7% on FY2024's $6.95B; the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $9.18B, up 26.8%. That acceleration is not organic. It is the arithmetic of two acquisitions: Veritex Holdings, closed October 20, 2025 (adding $9.3B of loans and $10.5B of deposits) and Cadence Bank, closed February 1, 2026 (adding $36.9B of loans and $43.5B of deposits). Total assets went from $204.2B at end-2024 to $284.0B at June 2026 — a 39% expansion in eighteen months. At $33.80B of market capitalisation Huntington is the sixth-largest US regional bank by market value and roughly the #746 most valuable listed company globally.

The central debate on this page is whether Huntington has bought scale or bought risk. The bull version: a Midwest deposit franchise has grafted on Texas and the Southeast, the two fastest-growing US banking markets, at a moment when the deposit base was already funding itself cheaply — and the combined bank earns a 15.1% return on tangible common equity while trading at 1.7× tangible book. The bear version: goodwill rose from $6.0B to $9.5B, tangible book value per share went backwards (from $9.87 at end-2025 to $9.65 at June 2026), CET1 sits at 10.0% — thinner than most peers carry — and the entire earnings uplift is being delivered by a balance sheet 42% larger than a year ago rather than by a better bank. Every number in Section 04 is chosen to let you decide which reading the evidence supports.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
$222.5B deposit base 160-year Midwest franchise Texas + Southeast entry 27.7% fee income mix 1.78% ACL coverage 0.25% net charge-offs Switching costs in treasury

A bank's moat is its deposits, and Huntington's is now $222.5B — a base built over 160 years in Ohio, Michigan and the industrial Midwest, where branch density and generational relationships still meaningfully suppress the rate a bank has to pay. That is the asset the Veritex and Cadence deals were designed to redeploy: the acquired loan books sit in Texas and the Southeast, where loan demand and population growth are structurally higher than the Midwest, funded by a Midwest liability base that is structurally cheaper. Layer on treasury-management and commercial-card relationships that are genuinely sticky — moving a mid-market company's payables and receivables is a multi-quarter project, not a rate decision — and you have the two defensible pieces. What is not a moat is anything on the asset side: a commercial loan is a commodity, priced in a market with 323 listed competitors plus private credit, and Huntington has no measurable pricing power there.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Integration risk is concentrated, not diversified. Two acquisitions closed fifteen weeks apart added $46.2B of loans and $54.0B of deposits — roughly a third of the pre-deal bank. The Cadence systems conversion completed in mid-June 2026, which is when customer attrition typically shows up, and the remaining cost synergies are only guided to be "visible by the fourth quarter." If deposits leak from the acquired footprints, the funding advantage that justified the deals erodes before the cost saves arrive.
02
Capital is the thinnest part of the story. CET1 stands at 10.0% and tangible common equity to tangible assets at 7.1%. Tangible book value per share fell from $9.87 at end-2025 to $9.65 at June 2026 because the Cadence purchase created goodwill faster than retained earnings rebuilt equity. A bank running 10.0% CET1 into a credit downturn has less room to absorb provisions without cutting the buyback or, eventually, the dividend.
03
Commercial real estate and commercial concentration. Commercial loans grew 59% year over year and now dominate the incremental book. The acquired portfolios were underwritten by other institutions under other standards; the ACL at 1.78% of loans and net charge-offs at 0.25% both look comfortable today, but purchase accounting resets the starting point and the true seasoning of $46.2B of acquired credit will not be visible for several quarters.
04
The margin is a rate bet nobody controls. At a 3.21% NIM, a 25-basis-point compression across $250B of earning assets is roughly $625M of annualised revenue — more than a fifth of a quarter's total revenue. Huntington does not set the yield curve, and the same deposit-cost pressure that made 2023 painful for regional banks can return without warning.

Notable one-off items. Huntington recorded $152M of pre-tax acquisition-related notable items in Q2 2026, principally integration expense from the Cadence and Veritex transactions. That gap is the whole reason GAAP EPS of $0.33 and adjusted EPS of $0.39 differ by 18%, and why reported ROTCE of 15.1% becomes 17.5% adjusted and reported noninterest expense of $1,809M becomes adjusted expense of $1,657M. Treat the adjusted figures with appropriate caution: acquisition integration costs are genuinely non-recurring for a given deal, but a bank that has closed two deals in fifteen weeks has made "non-recurring" a recurring line item. A second distortion runs the other way: TTM EPS of $1.28 is down 4.9% while TTM net income is up 13.6%, because the share count rose to fund the deals. Per-share earnings are the number a shareholder owns, and they went backwards.

That is the wrong way to read this stock. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The arithmetic of the last eighteen months, stated plainly. Huntington's total assets went from $204.2B (end-2024) to $225.1B (end-2025) to $284.0B (June 2026). Deposits went from $162.4B to $176.6B to $222.5B. Net loans went from $127.8B to $147.1B to $186.2B. Shareholders' equity went from $19.8B to $24.4B to $32.7B. Every one of those lines grew roughly 40%. Two lines did not: TTM EPS fell 4.9%, and tangible book value per share fell from $9.87 to $9.65. Goodwill, meanwhile, rose from $6.0B to $9.5B. That is the shape of a balance sheet bought rather than built, and the per-share investor experience of the last eighteen months has been dilution now in exchange for earnings later.

What the operating numbers actually say. Set the M&A aside and look at Q2 2026 on its own terms and it is a good quarter for a good bank. ROA of 1.02% clears the traditional one-percent threshold. ROTCE of 15.1% — 17.5% adjusted — comfortably exceeds any reasonable estimate of the cost of equity, which is the test of whether a bank creates value at all. NIM of 3.21% is respectable. Credit is quiet: 0.25% net charge-offs against 1.78% ACL coverage and an 0.85% nonperforming asset ratio. Fee income at 27.7% of revenue is a genuinely diversified mix for a regional, with payments and cash management ($204M), capital markets ($140M) and wealth ($134M) each material. On those numbers, 13.1× trailing earnings and 1.7× tangible book is not an expensive stock.

Where the argument sits. Two things are simultaneously true and they pull in opposite directions. First, the efficiency ratio of 61.5% is mediocre — a well-run regional bank targets the mid-50s — and the adjusted 58% only gets there by excluding $152M of integration cost. Second, CET1 of 10.0% and TCE/TA of 7.1% mean the capital cushion was spent on the deals. A bank with a 61.5% efficiency ratio and 10.0% CET1 has limited ability to absorb a credit cycle without a visible hit to earnings or to the buyback. Against that, the entire bull case is a scheduled, dated event: management has committed to the remaining cost synergies being visible by Q4 2026, and the forward P/E of 9.8× versus trailing 13.1× is the market's estimate of what happens if they arrive. The gap between those two multiples is not a valuation opinion; it is a bet on execution with a deadline.

The credit question nobody can answer yet. Huntington took on $46.2B of loans underwritten by two other institutions — Veritex in Texas, Cadence across the Southeast — inside fifteen weeks. Purchase accounting marks those books to fair value at closing, which resets the loss history and makes the acquired portfolios look pristine by construction. The 1.78% ACL and 0.25% charge-off rate on the combined book are therefore genuinely reassuring about the legacy portfolio and genuinely uninformative about the acquired one. Commercial loans grew 59% year over year. The seasoning of that growth — whether the acquired commercial and commercial-real-estate exposures behave like Huntington's own book — will not be visible until 2027, and it is the largest unquantified risk on this page.

Bull Case

A cheap, sticky Midwest deposit franchise has been redeployed into Texas and the Southeast, the two fastest-growing US banking markets, in two deals that added $46.2B of loans and $54.0B of deposits. The combined bank already earns a 15.1% return on tangible common equity (17.5% adjusted) and a 1.02% return on assets with credit quiet at 0.25% net charge-offs, while trading at 13.1× trailing and 9.8× forward earnings and paying a 3.71% dividend. The Cadence systems conversion is done, the remaining cost synergies are guided visible by Q4, and the consensus target of $20.30 implies 21.3% upside with no sell ratings on the primary panel. If the synergies land, an investor is buying a franchise earning above its cost of equity at a single-digit forward multiple.

Bear Case

Everything good in the numbers was purchased. TTM EPS is down 4.9% while net income is up 13.6% — shareholders paid for the growth in dilution. Tangible book value per share went backwards, goodwill rose to $9.5B, CET1 sits at a thin 10.0% and the efficiency ratio of 61.5% is well off best-in-class. Two of the largest banks' analysts downgraded within four days of a quarter that beat on every operating line, which suggests the sell side sees the same thing. The stock has not made a new high since November 2024 despite two transformational acquisitions, and $46.2B of loans underwritten by someone else will not season until 2027 — by which time the reserve build, if one is needed, comes straight out of a capital base that has no spare room.

Top 3 Risks

01
Acquired credit seasons badly. $46.2B of loans from two other underwriters, marked to fair value at closing and therefore starting with a clean slate that tells you nothing. If commercial or commercial-real-estate losses in the Texas and Southeast books run above the legacy portfolio's 0.25% charge-off rate, the reserve build lands on a 10.0% CET1 ratio with no cushion to absorb it.
02
Synergies slip past Q4 2026. Management has put a date on the remaining cost saves. The 3.3-turn gap between the 13.1× trailing and 9.8× forward multiple exists only because the market believes that date. A miss on October 22 or in the Q4 print removes the single justification for owning the stock at a discount to its own trailing earnings power.
03
Rates move against the margin. At a 3.21% NIM on roughly $250B of earning assets, a 25-basis-point compression is approximately $625M of annualised revenue — more than a fifth of a quarter's total. Deposit competition returning, or the curve flattening, would erase the acquisition benefit faster than the cost synergies can replace it, and Huntington controls neither variable.
Industry Commentary
Regional Bank Consolidation Has Actually Arrived
EMERGING TAILWIND
For a decade the regional banking sector talked about consolidation and did very little of it, because deal approval was slow and uncertain. Huntington's two closings — Veritex on October 20, 2025 and Cadence on February 1, 2026, fifteen weeks apart — are evidence that the approval environment changed materially. The sector remains extraordinarily fragmented: 323 listed US regional banks share $2.05T of market capitalisation and $561.0B of revenue, and the top nine account for roughly $451B of that market cap. Scale matters more than it used to, because technology spend, compliance cost and deposit-gathering all have fixed-cost characteristics. Huntington has moved from the middle of that pack toward the top, and the opportunity to keep doing so has not closed.
Deposits Are No Longer Free, and Everyone Knows It
ONGOING HEADWIND
The March 2023 crisis permanently changed depositor behaviour: money-market funds, high-yield digital accounts and instant transfers turned a sticky liability into a shoppable one. HBAN's own chart records the repricing — a 26.9% fall in a single month in March 2023 and a trough at $9.65 seven months later. The structural consequence is that NIM of 3.21% is now earned against a more competitive funding market than at any time in the pre-2022 era, and the cost of deposits is the line most likely to move against a bank without warning. Huntington does not disclose it in the material fetched here, which is itself worth noting — it is the number a careful investor should look for first on October 22. Loans growing faster than deposits (+42% vs +37% year over year) pushes the loan-to-deposit ratio toward 85% and adds marginal pressure in the same direction.
Midwest Funding, Sunbelt Lending — The Whole Trade
STRUCTURAL REPOSITIONING
The strategic logic of both acquisitions is a geographic arbitrage: gather deposits where competition for them is low and population is flat, lend where loan demand is high and population is growing. Texas and the Southeast supply the asset growth; Ohio, Michigan and the industrial Midwest supply the cheap liabilities. Bull framing: this is the single most durable structural advantage available to a US regional bank, it is not replicable by a competitor without equivalent M&A, and it explains why a bank with a mediocre 61.5% efficiency ratio still earns 15.1% on tangible common equity. Bear framing: the arbitrage only works if the Midwest deposits stay put while being lent 1,200 miles away, and if the acquired Sunbelt credit behaves. Neither has been tested through a downturn. The mid-June Cadence conversion was the moment customers first experienced the combined bank, and the first full quarter of evidence arrives October 22 — which is precisely why the stock has gone nowhere since June.
