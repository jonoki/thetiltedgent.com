01 Company Overview

HCA Healthcare is the largest for-profit hospital operator in the United States — a network of acute-care hospitals, ambulatory surgery centres, freestanding emergency departments, urgent care clinics, imaging centres, physician practices, home health and hospice agencies across the US and the United Kingdom. FY2025 revenue was $75.60B, up 7.1% from $70.60B in FY2024, and the TTM figure through June 2026 is $78.01B, up 7.3%. At $87.68B of market capitalisation it is the world's #282 most valuable company, and it employs 320,000 people.

The debate on this page is not about hospital operations. It is about policy arithmetic. The enhanced premium tax credits that inflated ACA (Affordable Care Act) marketplace enrolment expired at the end of 2025, and HCA's exchange-covered adjusted admissions fell 15% in each of Q1 and Q2 2026. Management's original assumption was that 80–85% of those patients would land somewhere else insured; the actual migration was, in CEO Sam Hazen's words, "almost one for one to uninsured." That is a roughly $1.0–1.2 billion unfavourable swing to 2026 pre-tax income. Against it sits a second policy line — Medicaid supplemental / state-directed payment programmes — which delivered roughly $400 million of incremental net benefit in Q2 alone. Two government line items, of similar magnitude, moving in opposite directions, are currently the dominant swing factor in HCA's earnings. Everything else on this page is secondary to that.

The three numbers that define this business are all in that stats row, and they need translating. Admissions counts inpatients. Equivalent admissions grosses that up for outpatient activity by scaling admissions by the ratio of total to inpatient revenue — it is HCA's single best volume measure. Revenue per equivalent admission is the pricing-and-mix measure. In Q2 2026 volume grew +2.7% and price/mix grew +6.4% to $19,391, which together produced +8.7% revenue growth — a perfectly healthy top line. The problem is beneath it: that 6.4% rate growth came from contracted commercial rate increases and government payment updates fighting a negative payer mix, not sailing with it. Same-facility payer volumes in Q2: Medicare +3.6%, Medicaid +2.7%, commercial excluding exchanges +2.4%, exchanges −15%, uninsured +15%. Uninsured patients still get treated — emergency care is a legal obligation, not a commercial choice — so the volume shows up in the numerator and almost nothing shows up in cash. That is the whole $400 million quarterly hit in one sentence.

This is not a business line — it is a reimbursement mechanism, and it is the single largest reason HCA's 2026 numbers do not look worse than they do. States direct their Medicaid managed-care plans to make supplemental payments to hospitals, funded in part by provider taxes and requiring federal CMS approval. HCA recognised approximately $400 million of incremental net benefit in Q2 2026, predominantly from Florida's state-directed payment programme. The mechanics matter enormously to how you read the quarter: Florida's approval covered the period October 1, 2024 through June 30, 2026, so HCA booked $1.372 billion of incremental revenue for a 21-month retroactive window inside a single three-month quarter (the Q2 call cited roughly $540 million of Florida incremental benefit recognised in the quarter; the press release's ~$400 million is the net figure across all such programmes). That is real cash and a legitimate accounting treatment — and it is also, by construction, unrepeatable. Management has already guided to a $100–300 million headwind in the second half precisely because prior-year retroactive payments exceed the new incremental Florida benefit. Anyone marking HCA's Q2 adjusted EBITDA of $4.027B as a run-rate is annualising a catch-up payment.

Net policy arithmetic for FY2026: exchange coverage losses −$1.0B to −$1.2B versus Medicaid supplemental programmes +$300M to +$500M. Net policy drag of roughly $500M to $900M against guided adjusted EBITDA of $15.4–16.1B — a 3–6% hit to EBITDA from two legislative line items.

HCA's advantage is geographic density, not national size. In its core Sun Belt markets — Texas, Florida, Tennessee, the South Atlantic — it does not own one hospital, it owns the network: hospitals plus the freestanding ERs, urgent care clinics and surgery centres that feed them. That density is what gives HCA the strongest commercial rate negotiating position in the for-profit sector: an insurer cannot build a saleable network in Nashville or Houston without HCA in it. It is reinforced by the sheer capital intensity of the business — $5.0–5.5B of capex a year, $7B+ approved over three years — which no new entrant can replicate, and by certificate-of-need and licensing regimes in many states that make new hospital capacity a regulatory question before it is a financial one. The visible proof of the moat is in the margin: HCA runs a 15.48% operating margin and 21.80% ROIC in an industry where the non-profit majority routinely operates near break-even.

There is a second, less obvious source of advantage worth naming: administrative scale against the payer. Extracting a state-directed Medicaid payment covering a 21-month retroactive window, in a single state, and recognising $1.372B of revenue from it, is not an operating skill — it is a regulatory-and-reimbursement capability that a 320,000-employee, $78B-revenue enterprise can staff and a small regional system cannot. The same is true of commercial contracting, of denial management, and of the compliance apparatus around a revenue base collected from thousands of payers. In an industry where Medicare and Medicaid volumes are a large share of admissions and their rates are set by formula rather than negotiated, the ability to work the formula is itself a competitive advantage — and it is the one least visible in any operating statistic.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The revenue base is set by legislation, not by management. Two federal policy decisions — the expiry of enhanced ACA premium tax credits and the future of Medicaid supplemental payment programmes — are worth more to 2026 earnings than any operating initiative HCA can run. The exchange headwind alone is $1.0–1.2B of pre-tax income; HCA cannot negotiate, price or cost-cut its way around a patient who no longer has coverage.
02
Uncompensated care is a one-way ratchet. Federal law requires emergency treatment regardless of ability to pay. So as the uninsured share of equivalent admissions passes 10% — now above the 6.8% exchange share — HCA absorbs the cost of volume it will largely never collect on. Every point of coverage loss converts a paying admission into a bad-debt admission at close to full incremental cost.
03
Balance-sheet fragility funded the buyback. Stockholders' equity is negative $3.21B and total debt is $51.55B against $1.13B of cash. The company has been buying back far more stock than it generates in free cash flow, closing the gap with new borrowing. That works beautifully while EBITDA grows and credit markets are open; it removes the option of retrenching quietly if they are not.
04
Site-of-care migration and elective softness. Same-facility inpatient surgeries fell 2.3% and outpatient surgeries 3.4% in Q2 2026. Procedures are moving to ambulatory settings where reimbursement is lower and where ASC specialists like Tenet's USPI and physician-owned centres compete on cost. HCA is buying its way into that channel with 250–300 new outpatient sites, but it is defending a higher-margin business with a lower-margin one.

The five-year record, in four lines. The table above is a snapshot; the trend is the argument. Revenue has compounded from $58.75B (FY2021) to $75.60B (FY2025), about 6.5% a year, with no year below 2.5% growth. Free cash flow ran $5.38B → $4.13B → $4.69B → $5.64B → $7.69B. And the funding line: total debt went $36.73B → $51.55B while shareholders' equity went +$1.5B → −$3.2B and book value per share went −$3.05 → −$30.47. Over five years HCA converted roughly $27B of cumulative free cash flow and $15B of incremental debt into share repurchases. That is a deliberate, disclosed and — at 21.8% ROIC — defensible capital structure. It is also why the equity has no book value to fall back on, and why every metric on this page that divides by equity has been marked "not meaningful" rather than dressed up.

Notable one-off items. Three, and they all point the same way. (1) The Florida retroactive Medicaid payment. HCA recognised $1.372B of incremental revenue covering October 2024–June 2026 inside Q2 2026, contributing roughly $400M of incremental net benefit across all Medicaid supplemental programmes. Q2 revenue growth of 8.7% and adjusted EBITDA of $4.027B both flatter the underlying run-rate as a result, and management has guided to a $100–300M reversal headwind in the second half. (2) The exchange payer-mix shift cost roughly $400M of pre-tax income in Q2 and is guided at $1.0–1.2B for the full year — raised from an original $600–900M estimate when the one-for-one migration to uninsured became apparent. (3) The share count. This is not a one-off but it distorts every per-share number: net income rose 2.8% in Q2 while diluted EPS rose 11.6%, meaning roughly 8 points of "EPS growth" was share retirement. Over the TTM the effect is larger still — net income +14.6%, EPS +25.9%, implying a ~9% reduction in the diluted share count. HCA spent $10.07B on buybacks in FY2025 against $7.69B of free cash flow, and issued a net $3.29B of new debt in the same year. Growth in earnings per share and growth in earnings are not the same thing here, and the difference is financed.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and on monthly data it turns slowly, so treat it as a description of the last half-year, not a trading trigger.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 monthly close of $250.46 to the September 4, 2026 close of $405.00, HCA returned +61.7% on price alone over roughly 4.85 years — a compound annual rate of about 10.2%, plus a dividend yield that has never exceeded about 1%. That is a good but unspectacular headline, and it hides two completely different regimes. The first four years were a grinding, high-quality compounding story: from the June 2022 low of $168.06 the stock rose 215% to its February 2026 monthly-close peak of $529.70, driven by post-pandemic volume normalisation, easing labour costs and a share count shrinking by high single digits a year. The last seven months have been a policy de-rating: −23.5% from that February peak, with a trough at the May 2026 close of $378.54 (−28.5%). Nothing in HCA's operating performance changed by 28% in three months. What changed was the market's estimate of how many of HCA's patients would still have insurance in 2027.

Against the directly comparable listed operators the picture is worse, not better: Tenet's market cap is up 29.6% over the last year with EPS up 64.5%, while Universal Health Services is down 13.5% and Community Health Systems — the most leveraged, least diversified name in the group — trades at a $394M equity value on $11.98B of revenue with revenue falling 5.3%. The dispersion across the four names tells you the market is not repricing "hospitals"; it is repricing exposure to the exchange population, and HCA's Sun Belt concentration puts it closer to the wrong end of that spectrum than its scale would suggest.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the best-run asset in the sector at 13.6× trailing and 13.2× forward earnings with a 21.8% ROIC, in the trough year of an identifiable, dated policy shock. If Hazen is right that "most of the attrition this year is directly attributable to patients who were benefiting from the enhanced premium tax credits," exchange attrition moderates in 2027 and the $1.0–1.2B headwind does not repeat — it becomes the base. Meanwhile the company retires roughly 9% of its shares a year with $7.21B of authorisation remaining, grows revenue 7%, and is adding 250–300 outpatient sites into markets where it already owns the referral network. On flat operating earnings and continued buyback alone, EPS compounds high single digits. The consensus $451.48 target implies +11.5%; UBS's $579 implies +42.9%.

Bear Case

The 2027 earnings base is lower than it looks, and the balance sheet has no give. Q2's headline was flattered by $1.372B of retroactive Florida Medicaid revenue booked in one quarter, with a guided $100–300M reversal in the second half. Uninsured is now over 10% of equivalent admissions and rising; Medicaid work requirements are a second coverage-contraction wave aimed at the same divisions. Elective surgery — the most profitable volume — is already falling (inpatient −2.3%, outpatient −3.4%). And the EPS growth that supports the multiple is bought with borrowed money: $10.07B of buybacks against $7.69B of free cash flow in FY2025, funded by a net $3.29B of new debt, leaving $51.55B of debt, negative $3.21B of equity and a 0.99 current ratio. Morgan Stanley at $380 and Wells Fargo at $381 are both below today's price for a reason.

Top 3 Risks

01
Coverage contraction compounds rather than stabilises. The base case assumes exchange attrition is a one-time reset. If 2027 open enrolment shows continued declines, and Medicaid work requirements remove additional covered lives in the 40% of Medicaid revenue that comes from expansion states, the uninsured share keeps climbing past 10% and uncompensated care becomes a permanent margin tax rather than a 2026 event.
02
Medicaid supplemental payment programmes are withdrawn or curtailed. These programmes require federal CMS approval and are funded through state provider-tax mechanisms that have been a recurring target of federal reform. HCA has just demonstrated how large they are — $1.372B of Florida revenue in one recognition event. A programme that can add $400M in a quarter can subtract it, and the offset that currently masks a third of the exchange headwind would disappear.
03
Leverage meets a lower earnings base. Net debt/EBITDA of 3.20× is comfortable against $15.4–16.1B of guided adjusted EBITDA. It is considerably less comfortable if 2027 EBITDA resets 5–8% lower on policy and the company is still committed to $5.0–5.5B of annual capex plus a buyback larger than free cash flow. With negative book equity there is no cushion in the capital structure — the only shock absorbers are the buyback and the capital plan, and cutting either one removes the EPS growth that supports the multiple.
  Industry Commentary
Coverage Is the Sector's Revenue Line
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A hospital's revenue is not set by what it charges but by who is insured. The expiry of enhanced ACA premium tax credits at the end of 2025 removed coverage from a population that, at HCA, migrated almost one for one into uninsured status rather than into other plans — roughly 22,000 exchange patients becoming 26,500 uninsured admissions year to date. Because federal law requires emergency treatment regardless of ability to pay, that volume does not disappear; it simply stops being paid for. Uninsured now exceeds 10% of HCA's equivalent admissions against 6.8% for exchanges. The effect is concentrated: the Gulf Coast, North Florida and South Atlantic divisions saw exchange admissions fall 25–28% and account for half the company-wide impact. Every listed operator is exposed to the same mechanism — Community Health Systems' revenue is already down 5.3% year over year — but exposure scales with Sun Belt and exchange-heavy market mix, not with size.
State-Directed Payments: The Offset Nobody Underwrites
ONGOING HEADWIND
The mechanism currently absorbing a third of the coverage hit is the least predictable line in the whole industry. States direct Medicaid managed-care plans to make supplemental payments to hospitals; the programmes are funded partly by provider taxes, require federal approval, and are approved for defined retroactive periods. HCA's Q2 illustrates both the size and the shape of the problem: $1.372B of Florida incremental revenue covering a 21-month window recognised inside one quarter, producing roughly $400M of incremental net benefit — and a guided $100–300M headwind in the second half as those retroactive payments lap. Guided full-year benefit is $300–500M against a $1.0–1.2B exchange hit. The industry-wide implication is that reported hospital EBITDA in 2026 is materially less comparable quarter to quarter than it has been in years, and any peer comparison that ignores state-directed payment timing is measuring noise.
The Migration Out of the Hospital
EMERGING TAILWIND
Procedures continue moving from inpatient wards to ambulatory surgery centres, freestanding EDs and urgent care, where payers pay less and specialists like Tenet's USPI and physician-owned groups compete directly with the incumbent systems. HCA's answer is ownership rather than resistance — about 250 outpatient sites added in twelve months (+5%) and a pipeline of 250–300 more, roughly 10% network capacity growth, inside $7B+ of approved three-year capital. Bull framing: HCA is the only operator with both the balance-sheet capacity and the market density to own the whole care continuum in its Sun Belt markets, so the migration transfers volume between HCA's own sites rather than away from it — and it does so while non-profit systems, running near break-even, cannot fund the same build. Bear framing: the mix shift is dilutive by construction — ambulatory reimbursement is lower per case — and it is happening while elective volumes are already falling (inpatient surgeries −2.3%, outpatient −3.4% in Q2). HCA is spending $5B+ a year to defend a high-margin business with a lower-margin one, in the same year its most profitable payer class is shrinking.
