01 Company Overview

HCA Healthcare is the largest for-profit hospital operator in the United States — a network of acute-care hospitals, ambulatory surgery centres, freestanding emergency departments, urgent care clinics, imaging centres, physician practices, home health and hospice agencies across the US and the United Kingdom. FY2025 revenue was $75.60B, up 7.1% from $70.60B in FY2024, and the TTM figure through September 2026 is $78.70B (FY2025 less the first nine months of 2025 plus the first nine months of 2026, from HCA's releases). At $98.85B of market capitalisation it ranks #120 in the S&P 500, and it employs 320,000 people.

The debate on this page is still not about hospital operations. It is about policy arithmetic, and the third quarter, reported on October 27, 2026, showed it again. The enhanced premium tax credits that inflated ACA (Affordable Care Act) marketplace enrolment expired at the end of 2025. Exchange-covered equivalent admissions fell about 19% in Q3 (management had expected a 15–16% decline, CFO Mike Marks said on the call), and patients who lost exchange coverage moved almost one for one to uninsured, which reached about 10.5% of equivalent admissions, up from 8.5% a year earlier. HCA's release says the payer-mix shift cut Q3 income before taxes by approximately $400 million. Against it sit Medicaid supplemental / state-directed payment programmes, whose net benefit "modestly increased" in Q3 (release) after roughly $400 million of incremental net benefit in Q2. The result for investors: HCA now expects full-year 2026 operating results in the upper half of the ranges it issued on July 14, and, on the Q3 call, a preliminary view that 2027 earnings will be roughly flat with 2026, with the supplemental-payment benefit $550–650 million lower. Two government line items of similar size, moving in opposite directions, remain the dominant swing factor in HCA's earnings.

The three numbers that define this business are all in that stats row, and they need translating. Admissions counts inpatients. Equivalent admissions grosses that up for outpatient activity by scaling admissions by the ratio of total to inpatient revenue — it is HCA's single best volume measure. Revenue per equivalent admission is the pricing-and-mix measure. In Q3 2026 same-facility equivalent admissions grew +3.0% but revenue per equivalent admission rose only +0.6% to $18,614, so revenue grew +3.6% to $19.849B. That is a sharp slowdown from Q2, when price/mix grew 6.4% with the retroactive Florida Medicaid payment inside it: in Q3 the rate line had to carry a worse payer mix on its own. Emergency room visits rose 5.1%; inpatient surgeries fell 1.4% and outpatient surgeries 3.7% (release). Uninsured patients still get treated — emergency care is a legal obligation, not a commercial choice — so the volume shows up in the patient counts and almost nothing shows up in cash. That is the roughly $400 million quarterly hit in one sentence.

Concentration (Q2 2026 call): three divisions — Gulf Coast, North Florida and South Atlantic — saw exchange admissions fall 25–28% and accounted for roughly 50% of the company-wide impact. The subsidy expiry is a Sun Belt event, not a national average.

Ambulatory surgery centres, freestanding emergency departments, urgent care and imaging. Strategically this is HCA's answer to the single most important structural shift in the industry — procedures migrating out of the hospital to lower-cost settings, where payers want them and where independent ASC (ambulatory surgery centre) operators and physician-owned groups compete directly. HCA's response is to own the ambulatory network rather than lose volume to it: outpatient sites grew about 5% year over year, roughly 250 additions, with a pipeline of another 250–300 sites, about 10% network capacity growth (Q2 2026 call). But note the tension in the stats row: even as HCA builds ambulatory capacity, same-facility outpatient surgeries fell 3.7% in Q3 and inpatient surgeries fell 1.4% (release). On the Q2 call management attributed the decline to elective procedure weakness tied to exchange coverage losses and general affordability pressure. Elective surgery is the most price-elastic thing a hospital sells, and it is the first line to break when patients lose insurance.

This is not a business line — it is a reimbursement mechanism, and it is the single largest reason HCA's 2026 numbers do not look worse than they do. States direct their Medicaid managed-care plans to make supplemental payments to hospitals, funded in part by provider taxes and requiring federal CMS approval. HCA recognised approximately $400 million of incremental net benefit in Q2 2026, predominantly from Florida's state-directed payment programme. The mechanics matter enormously to how you read the quarter: Florida's approval covered the period October 1, 2024 through June 30, 2026, so HCA booked $1.372 billion of incremental revenue for a 21-month retroactive window inside a single three-month quarter (the Q2 call cited roughly $540 million of Florida incremental benefit recognised in the quarter; the press release's ~$400 million is the net figure across all such programmes). That is real cash and a legitimate accounting treatment — and it is also, by construction, unrepeatable. Q3 confirmed it: the release says the net benefit "modestly increased" (about $50 million, primarily from a recently approved Virginia programme, per the Q3 call), against roughly $400 million in Q2. Management's 2026 benefit estimate moved up to $600–800 million from $300–500 million in July, and for 2027 it expects $550–650 million less (Q3 call; the July range is from HCA's July 14 release). Anyone marking HCA's Q2 adjusted EBITDA of $4.027B as a run-rate is annualising a catch-up payment; Q3 adjusted EBITDA was $3.918B, up 1.2%.

Net policy arithmetic for FY2026: exchange coverage losses at or slightly above the top of −$1.0B to −$1.2B versus Medicaid supplemental programmes +$600M to +$800M (Q3 call). Net policy drag of roughly $200M to $600M (our arithmetic: $1.0–1.2B less $0.6–0.8B at the extremes) against guided adjusted EBITDA of $15.4–16.1B, with results now expected in the upper half — roughly a 1–4% hit to EBITDA from two legislative line items.

HCA's advantage is geographic density, not national size. In its core Sun Belt markets — Texas, Florida, Tennessee, the South Atlantic — it does not own one hospital, it owns the network: hospitals plus the freestanding ERs, urgent care clinics and surgery centres that feed them. That density is what gives HCA the strongest commercial rate negotiating position in the for-profit sector: an insurer cannot build a saleable network in Nashville or Houston without HCA in it. It is reinforced by the sheer capital intensity of the business — $5.0–5.5B of capex guided for 2026 and more than $7B of approved projects due over three years — which no new entrant can replicate, and by certificate-of-need and licensing regimes in many states that make new hospital capacity a regulatory question before it is a financial one. The visible proof of the moat is in the margin: HCA runs a 15.31% operating margin and 21.36% ROIC in an industry where the non-profit majority routinely operates near break-even.

There is a second, less obvious source of advantage worth naming: administrative scale against the payer. Extracting a state-directed Medicaid payment covering a 21-month retroactive window, in a single state, and recognising $1.372B of revenue from it, is not an operating skill — it is a regulatory-and-reimbursement capability that a 320,000-employee, $78B-revenue enterprise can staff and a small regional system cannot. The same is true of commercial contracting, of denial management, and of the compliance apparatus around a revenue base collected from thousands of payers. In an industry where Medicare and Medicaid volumes are a large share of admissions and their rates are set by formula rather than negotiated, the ability to work the formula is itself a competitive advantage — and it is the one least visible in any operating statistic.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The revenue base is set by legislation, not by management. Two federal policy decisions — the expiry of enhanced ACA premium tax credits and the future of Medicaid supplemental payment programmes — are worth more to 2026 earnings than any operating initiative HCA can run. The exchange headwind alone is at or slightly above the top of a $1.0–1.2B range for 2026 (Q3 call); HCA cannot negotiate, price or cost-cut its way around a patient who no longer has coverage.
02
Uncompensated care is a one-way ratchet. Federal law requires emergency treatment regardless of ability to pay. So as the uninsured share of equivalent admissions reaches about 10.5% (8.5% a year earlier), HCA absorbs the cost of volume it will largely never collect on. Every point of coverage loss converts a paying admission into a bad-debt admission at close to full incremental cost.
03
Balance-sheet fragility funded the buyback. At September 30, 2026 the stockholders' deficit attributable to HCA was $8.08B ($4.60B including noncontrolling interests) and total debt was $50.49B against $0.91B of cash. The company has been buying back far more stock than it generates in free cash flow, closing the gap with new borrowing: $6.61B of buybacks in the first nine months of 2026 against $5.10B of operating cash flow less capital spending. That works while EBITDA grows and credit markets are open; it removes the option of retrenching quietly if they are not.
04
Site-of-care migration and elective softness. Same-facility inpatient surgeries fell 1.4% and outpatient surgeries 3.7% in Q3 2026. Procedures are moving to ambulatory settings where reimbursement is lower and where ASC specialists like Tenet's USPI and physician-owned centres compete on cost. HCA is buying its way into that channel with 250–300 new outpatient sites, but it is defending a higher-margin business with a lower-margin one.

The five-year record, in four lines. The table above is a snapshot; the trend is the argument. It is also why the equity has no book value to fall back on, and why every metric on this page that divides by equity has been marked "not meaningful" rather than dressed up.

Notable one-off items. Three, and they all point the same way. (1) The Florida retroactive Medicaid payment. HCA recognised $1.372B of incremental revenue covering October 2024–June 2026 inside Q2 2026, contributing roughly $400M of incremental net benefit across all Medicaid supplemental programmes. It flattered Q2 (revenue +8.7%, adjusted EBITDA $4.027B) and is why Q3's +3.6% revenue growth looks slow beside it; management expects the supplemental benefit to be $550–650M lower in 2027 (Q3 call). (3) The share count. Growth in earnings per share and growth in earnings are not the same thing here, and the difference is financed.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 monthly close of $250.46 to the October 30, 2026 close of $473.19, HCA returned +88.9% on price alone over five years — a compound annual rate of about 13.6%, plus a small dividend ($3.12 a year, 0.66% of today's price). The path had two regimes. From the June 2022 low of $168.06 the stock rose 215% to its February 2026 monthly-close peak of $529.70, driven by post-pandemic volume normalisation, easing labour costs and a share count shrinking by high single digits a year. Then came a policy sell-off: −28.5% from that peak to the May 2026 close of $378.54. Since May the stock has recovered 25.0%, and at $473.19 it is 10.7% below the February peak. Nothing in HCA's operating performance changed by 28% in three months. What changed was the market's estimate of how many of HCA's patients would still have insurance in 2027 — and, with the Q3 print, how much of the hit the Medicaid supplemental payments can offset.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the best-run asset in the sector at 15.7× trailing and 15.7× forward (2026 consensus) earnings with a 21.4% ROIC, in the trough year of an identifiable, dated policy shock. If management is right that there will be fewer exchange coverage losses in 2027 than in 2026 (Q3 call), the $1.0–1.2B headwind stops growing and becomes the base. Meanwhile the company retired roughly 8% of its shares in a year with $4.24B of authorisation remaining, grew Q3 revenue 3.6% and is adding 250–300 outpatient sites into markets where it already owns the referral network. Management now expects the year in the upper half of its ranges. On flat operating earnings and continued buyback alone, EPS compounds in the high single digits. The consensus $474.52 target implies +0.3%; the highest target on stockanalysis.com, $550, implies +16.2%.

Bear Case

The 2027 earnings base is no higher than 2026's, and the balance sheet has no give. Management's preliminary view is flat earnings in 2027, with the supplemental-payment benefit $550–650M smaller. Q2's headline was flattered by $1.372B of retroactive Florida Medicaid revenue booked in one quarter. Uninsured is now about 10.5% of equivalent admissions and rising; Medicaid work requirements are a second coverage-contraction wave aimed at the same divisions. Elective surgery — the most profitable volume — is already falling (inpatient −1.4%, outpatient −3.7% in Q3). And the EPS growth that supports the share price is bought with borrowed money: $10.07B of buybacks against $7.69B of free cash flow in FY2025, funded by a net $3.29B of new debt, leaving $50.49B of debt, a $4.60B stockholders' deficit and a 0.82 current ratio. The consensus target is only 0.3% above the price, and the $380 low target is 19.7% below it.

Top 3 Risks

01
Coverage contraction compounds rather than stabilises. The base case assumes exchange attrition is a one-time reset. If 2027 open enrolment shows continued declines, and Medicaid work requirements remove additional covered lives in the 40% of Medicaid revenue that comes from expansion states, the uninsured share keeps climbing past 10.5% and uncompensated care becomes a permanent margin tax rather than a 2026 event.
02
Medicaid supplemental payment programmes are withdrawn or curtailed. These programmes require federal CMS approval and are funded through state provider-tax mechanisms that have been a recurring target of federal reform. HCA has just demonstrated how large they are — $1.372B of Florida revenue in one recognition event. A programme that can add $400M in a quarter can subtract it, and the offset that currently covers at least half of the exchange headwind ($600–800M guided against about $1.0–1.2B) would disappear.
03
Leverage meets a lower earnings base. Net debt/EBITDA of 3.14× is comfortable against $15.4–16.1B of guided adjusted EBITDA (results now expected in the upper half). It is considerably less comfortable if 2027 EBITDA resets 5–8% lower on policy and the company is still committed to $5.0–5.5B of annual capex plus a buyback larger than free cash flow. With negative book equity there is no cushion in the capital structure — the only shock absorbers are the buyback and the capital plan, and cutting either one removes the EPS growth that supports the share price.
  Industry Commentary
Coverage Is the Sector's Revenue Line
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A hospital's revenue is not set by what it charges but by who is insured. The expiry of enhanced ACA premium tax credits at the end of 2025 removed coverage from a population that, at HCA, migrated almost one for one into uninsured status rather than into other plans: in Q3 2026 exchange equivalent admissions fell about 19% and the uninsured share rose to about 10.5% of equivalent admissions from 8.5% a year earlier (Q3 call). Because federal law requires emergency treatment regardless of ability to pay, that volume does not disappear; it simply stops being paid for. HCA put the Q3 cost at approximately $400M of pre-tax income (release). The effect is concentrated: in Q2 the Gulf Coast, North Florida and South Atlantic divisions saw exchange admissions fall 25–28% and accounted for half the company-wide impact. Every listed operator is exposed to the same mechanism, but exposure scales with Sun Belt and exchange-heavy market mix, not with size.
State-Directed Payments: The Offset Nobody Underwrites
ONGOING HEADWIND
The mechanism currently absorbing at least half of the coverage hit is the least predictable line in the whole industry. States direct Medicaid managed-care plans to make supplemental payments to hospitals; the programmes are funded partly by provider taxes, require federal approval, and are approved for defined retroactive periods. HCA's Q2 illustrates both the size and the shape of the problem: $1.372B of Florida incremental revenue covering a 21-month window recognised inside one quarter, producing roughly $400M of incremental net benefit. For 2026 management now estimates a $600–800M benefit (raised from $300–500M in July) against an exchange hit at or slightly above $1.0–1.2B, and for 2027 it expects $550–650M less (Q3 call). The industry-wide implication is that reported hospital EBITDA in 2026 is materially less comparable quarter to quarter than it has been in years, and any peer comparison that ignores state-directed payment timing is measuring noise.
The Migration Out of the Hospital
EMERGING TAILWIND
Procedures continue moving from inpatient wards to ambulatory surgery centres, freestanding EDs and urgent care, where payers pay less and specialists like Tenet's USPI and physician-owned groups compete directly with the incumbent systems. HCA's answer is ownership rather than resistance — about 250 outpatient sites added in twelve months (+5%) and a pipeline of 250–300 more, roughly 10% network capacity growth, alongside more than $7B of approved projects due over three years. Bull framing: HCA is the only operator with both the balance-sheet capacity and the market density to own the whole care continuum in its Sun Belt markets, so the migration transfers volume between HCA's own sites rather than away from it — and it does so while non-profit systems, running near break-even, cannot fund the same build. Bear framing: the mix shift is dilutive by construction — ambulatory reimbursement is lower per case — and it is happening while elective volumes are already falling (inpatient surgeries −1.4%, outpatient −3.7% in Q3). HCA is spending $5B+ a year to defend a high-margin business with a lower-margin one, in the same year its most profitable payer class is shrinking.
