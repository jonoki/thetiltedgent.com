01 Company Overview

The Hartford Insurance Group, Inc. is a 216-year-old (founded 1810) US insurer that underwrites commercial property-casualty ("P&C" — insurance covering physical damage and legal liability), personal auto and home, and group employee benefits. Its mutual-fund manager, Hartford Funds, is being sold: on June 3, 2026 The Hartford agreed to sell it to Wellington Management for $300 million in cash at closing plus seven years of payments tied to the business's results, which the announcement valued at about $1.9 billion in today's money (expected net present value). Hartford Funds is now reported as a discontinued operation (kept in net income, left out of core earnings). The June release expected the sale to close in the first quarter of 2027; on the Q3 call CFO Beth Costello said it could close as early as December 1. Over the four quarters to September 2026 The Hartford had revenue of $28.77 billion and net income of $4.59 billion (sum of the last four quarters as StockAnalysis lists them). Within StockAnalysis's "Insurance — Diversified" industry group of 11 companies, HIG is the fourth-largest by market capitalization behind Berkshire Hathaway ($1.16T), Sun Life ($44.91B) and AIG ($41.77B), at the October 28, 2026 close.

The structural point that matters for this name: HIG is not one business but two very different ones stapled together. Business Insurance and Personal Insurance are weather-and-litigation businesses driven by the P&C pricing cycle. Employee Benefits is a payroll-and-mortality business driven by employment levels and disability incidence. They rarely go wrong at the same time — and for the second quarter running, both showed strain. The company is leaning further into the second engine: on October 13 it closed the purchase of Equitable's employee-benefits business (about $500 million of premium from small and midsize employers, and about 300 staff; terms not disclosed). Management told the Q3 call the deal should trim core earnings slightly in 2027.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SMALL COMMERCIAL FRANCHISE INDEPENDENT AGENT NETWORK 216 YEARS OF LOSS DATA AARP AFFINITY CHANNEL WORKERS' COMP UNDERWRITING TWO UNCORRELATED EARNINGS ENGINES REGULATORY CAPITAL SCALE

The Hartford's moat is distribution and data in small commercial, not price. Small-account commercial insurance is a high-touch, low-premium-per-policy business where the economics belong to whoever can quote, bind and service a policy cheapest — which means whoever has the deepest agent relationships and the best-automated underwriting. The evidence that this still works shows up in the margin split: Small Business ran an 84.5 combined ratio in Q3 2026 while Middle & Large Business ran 101.7. The same company, the same quarter, a 17-point spread. Larger accounts come through brokers, are individually negotiated and are exposed to large single claims; small accounts are formulaic and sticky. Layered on top, the Employee Benefits business gives HIG an earnings stream whose driver — employment and disability incidence — has nothing to do with hurricanes or jury verdicts, which is genuinely rare among P&C peers. The stock's five-year beta is 0.49 (StockAnalysis; beta measures how much a stock moves with the market, where 1.0 moves in step). The result over the cycle has been a 23.25% return on equity and a 19.04% return on invested capital (StockAnalysis, trailing twelve months), earned at a debt-to-equity of 0.23.

Social inflation and casualty severity. Social inflation — the rise in liability claim costs driven by larger jury awards, more aggressive plaintiff bar tactics and third-party litigation financing, running faster than general economic inflation — is the single live threat to every commercial casualty writer, and it landed here in Q2 2026, which carried $46 million of adverse (unfavorable) prior-year development in general liability and $26 million in commercial auto, after $70 million in general liability in Q1. Q3 was quieter on that front: core net favorable prior-year reserve development (the correction booked when reserves set for earlier accident years prove wrong) was $97 million against $95 million a year earlier, and Business Insurance's $61 million came mainly from lower workers' compensation and commercial property reserves. The company's financial supplement shows no general liability reserve change in Q3. The headline $594 million of favorable development is mostly a one-off: a $497 million gain from ending the asbestos-and-environmental reinsurance cover, which is excluded from core earnings.

Middle & large commercial execution. The previous edition set the test: one quarter above 100 could be lumpy large losses, two would look like a trend. Middle & Large ran 101.7 in Q3 after 101.9 in Q2, against 90.8 a year ago, and its underlying ratio — before catastrophes and reserve changes — rose to 99.5 from 91.4, so the weakness is in this year's claims, not old ones. Management called the quarter's non-catastrophe property losses normal volatility, with a few large fire and water losses; a third quarter near 100 would make that harder to argue.

Group disability is a leveraged bet on payrolls. The disability loss ratio rose again, by 5.7 points to 76.3, on more claims and fewer recoveries, after a 6.3-point rise in Q2. Disability claims are counter-cyclical: incidence rises and recovery slows when the labour market weakens, precisely when premium growth — which is a function of insured payrolls — also slows. That is a double-hit structure, and it is the risk least appreciated by investors who file HIG as a "P&C name". The Equitable purchase adds to this exposure.

Pricing-cycle turn. Business Insurance renewal written pricing excluding workers' compensation was 5.3% in Q3 2026, down from 7.3% a year earlier (the company's Q3 2025 release), while written premiums grew 6%. Tooker told the call that new business in Small Business had gone negative for the first time in 20-some quarters. Price increases are slowing; the growth in casualty claim costs is not.

Notable One-Off Items. Q3 2026 again shows net income and core earnings moving in opposite directions: net income of $1.317 billion ($4.82 per diluted share) rose 23% while core earnings of $956 million ($3.50 per share) fell 6%. Most of the gap is one item. On September 23 The Hartford agreed with National Indemnity, a Berkshire Hathaway subsidiary, to end the asbestos-and-environmental ("A&E") adverse development cover — reinsurance in place since the end of 2016 that protected it if old asbestos and pollution claims grew — and received $1.12 billion in cash on September 25. The deal also settled an arbitration between the two. It produced a $497 million pre-tax gain ($393 million added to net income) with no effect on core earnings, per the company's 8-K. Hartford Funds' $61 million of quarterly pre-tax income also sits in net income, as a discontinued operation. Q3 also booked $116 million of pre-tax net realised losses, mainly on interest-rate derivatives. Use the core number. On capital, the company bought back $475 million of stock (3.4 million shares) and paid $163 million of dividends in the quarter, and Costello told the call: "As of September 30th, we had $4.37 billion remaining on our share repurchase authorization." That is about 12.6% of the current market cap.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A 9.5× forward P/E and 1.90× book for a business earning a 17.9% core ROE is a low bar. Small Business — the historic franchise and the part with real distribution advantage — improved to an 84.5 combined ratio, Personal Insurance's underlying ratio keeps improving (87.1 from 90.0), and net investment income rose 13%. The A&E commutation brought in $1.12B of cash and ended an arbitration, the dividend just rose 12%, $4.37B of buyback authority (about 12.6% of the market cap) remained at September 30, and the Hartford Funds sale adds $300M of cash at closing. Beta of 0.49 means you are paid to wait in a business that does not need the market to cooperate.

Bear Case

The two Q2 weak spots did not heal. Middle & Large ran an underwriting loss for a second straight quarter (101.7 after 101.9), with its underlying ratio up 8.1 points, so the damage is in this year's claims. Group disability rose again to 76.3 and group life worsened too, cutting Employee Benefits core earnings 19% — and the Equitable deal adds more of that business. Renewal pricing ex-comp slipped to 5.3% from 7.3% a year earlier, small-business new business turned negative and management describes a more competitive small-account market. Core earnings fell 6%; the headline net income rise is a one-off gain. Consensus has 2026 adjusted EPS down 2.6%, and 67% of analysts rate it Hold. Cheap is what a cyclical looks like at the top.

01
Social inflation and casualty reserve adequacy. Q2 2026 brought $46M of adverse general liability and $26M of adverse commercial auto development; Q3's core favorable development held at $97M, but Middle & Large's current-year claims ran hot again. Ending the A&E cover also leaves The Hartford carrying any further growth in its old asbestos and pollution claims. If litigation-driven severity keeps outrunning the claim-cost estimates built into current-year pricing, the reserve releases that have been flattering the combined ratio reverse into charges — and casualty reserves take years to fully develop, so one quarter is never the end of the story.
02
The pricing cycle rolling over. Renewal written pricing ex-workers' comp fell to 5.3% in Q3 from 7.3% a year earlier, and President Mo Tooker described a step change in competition for small accounts. Business Insurance written premiums grew 6%, barely ahead of rate, meaning little exposure growth. When price increases fall behind the growth in claim costs, the underlying combined ratio worsens, with a lag of a few quarters.
03
Employment-driven deterioration in Employee Benefits. The group disability loss ratio rose 5.7 points to 76.3 in Q3, after 6.3 points in Q2, on more claims and fewer recoveries. This segment is 26% of premium, growing with the Equitable book, and is a direct bet on payrolls: a softening labour market simultaneously raises disability claims, slows return-to-work, and shrinks the insured payroll base the premium is calculated on. It is the least weather-correlated and least discussed risk in the story.
  Industry Commentary
Social inflation is now showing up in the numbers, not the commentary
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Every commercial casualty writer is exposed to the same force: jury awards, plaintiff-bar sophistication and third-party litigation financing pushing liability severity above general inflation. At The Hartford it showed up in Q2 2026 as a $46M adverse charge in general liability (after $70M in Q1) plus $26M in commercial auto on old reserves. Q3 did not repeat that on the reserve line — core net favorable development held at $97M, and the financial supplement shows no general liability change — but Middle & Large Business ran a 101.7 combined ratio after 101.9, with its underlying ratio up to 99.5 from 91.4, so the pressure moved into this year's claims. Management called the quarter's non-catastrophe property losses normal volatility, with a few large fire and water losses. Two quarters above 100 make lumpiness a harder argument; the Q4 print is the next test.

The commercial pricing cycle is turning while loss trend is not
ONGOING HEADWIND

Insurance is a cycle business and the cycle governs everything two years out. The Hartford's Business Insurance renewal written pricing excluding workers' compensation ran 5.3% in Q3 2026, against 7.3% a year earlier, and written premiums grew 6% — so nearly all growth comes from higher prices, with little growth in the business insured. Competition is now reaching the best part of the book: Tooker told the call "there was an incremental step change in the competitiveness in small in the quarter," and small-business new business turned negative. The critical question is whether price increases still outpace the growth in claim costs. The segment's underlying combined ratio deteriorating from 89.4 to 90.1 says the margin is narrowing. Small Business at 84.5 and Middle & Large at 101.7 say the answer differs sharply by line — which is precisely why the small-commercial franchise matters so much, and why competition there is the thing to watch.

Two engines, two clocks — and what that means for bulls and bears
EMERGING TAILWIND

The Hartford's structural argument is that Business Insurance and Employee Benefits should not fail together: one runs on weather, rate and litigation, the other on employment, disability incidence and mortality. Historically that has been true, and the shares carry a five-year beta of 0.49. The bull reading is that Q2 and Q3 2026 were two unrelated sets of shocks — large property losses in middle-market accounts and a run of disability claims, which Swift called "mean-reverting now" — against a backdrop of +13% net investment income, an 84.5 small-commercial combined ratio, an improving Personal Insurance book, $1.12B of fresh cash from the A&E commutation and a 9.5× forward P/E with $4.37B of buyback authority left. The bear reading is that the two clocks are more synchronised than advertised: a slowing economy softens commercial pricing, raises disability incidence, shrinks insured payrolls and makes lawsuits more likely all at once, while a falling-rate environment would also cap the investment income that is currently doing the offsetting. Both readings fit the same two prints — which is exactly why 16 of 24 analysts sit on Hold.
