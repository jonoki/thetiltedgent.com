01 Company Overview

Huntington Ingalls is the shipyard the U.S. Navy cannot replace. Its Newport News yard in Virginia is, in Wikipedia's words, "the nation's sole designer, builder and refueler of nuclear-powered aircraft carriers and one of only two shipyards capable of designing and building nuclear-powered submarines"; its Ingalls yard in Mississippi builds amphibious ships, destroyers and Coast Guard cutters; and a third segment, Mission Technologies, sells defence technology services and unmanned systems. The company was carved out of Northrop Grumman on March 31, 2011 and is named for two founders — Collis Potter Huntington (Newport News) and Robert Ingersoll Ingalls Sr. (Ingalls). FY2025 revenue was $12.48B, up 8.2%, with diluted EPS of $15.39, and the order book stood at $57.3B at June 30, 2026 — more than four years of revenue.

The hand the market is playing is a strange one. Demand is not the question: a $57.3B backlog and a President who in January 2026 called for a $1.5 trillion FY2027 defence budget (DefenseScoop, Jan 7, 2026) settle that. The question is whether a yard with a fixed labour pool can convert demand into margin and cash. In January and February 2026 the market bet yes, taking the stock from a $340.07 December close to a $444.52 February close. Then Q1 printed a 5.0% operating margin and a $461M free-cash-flow deficit, and the bet was re-priced. Q2 was better — revenue +10.9%, EPS $5.27 versus $3.86, guidance raised — and the stock jumped 14.09% on the day. It has since given all of that back and more, closing at $276.10 on September 21, 2026, 37.9% below the February month-end. The central debate: is HII a throughput story where each point of labour productivity drops through to margin, or a fixed-price shipbuilder whose cost overruns show up faster than its volume gains?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Sole U.S. nuclear carrier builder One of two nuclear-sub yards $57.3B backlog Carrier refuelling franchise Irreplaceable dry docks & nuclear workforce Unmanned systems (ROMULUS, REMUS)

No moat in the S&P 500 is harder to cross. There is no second yard that can build a nuclear carrier, and no customer who can walk away. But a moat protects volume, not margin. HII's customer is, in substance, one buyer with its own budget process, and the contracts it signs carry cost risk that the yard absorbs when labour productivity misses plan. That is why a company with a monopoly on its most important product earns a single-digit operating margin — FY2025 operating margin was 5.3% on the company's own figures — and why the stock trades on execution, not on demand.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Carrier cost performance. The Q2 2026 release says Newport News results were "partially offset by lower performance in aircraft carriers", and the Q1 release cited "lower performance in aircraft carrier construction" at the same yard. When the largest segment's largest programme is the recurring source of negative adjustments, every quarter carries the risk of another one.
02
Cash conversion. TTM free cash flow is −$85M (StockAnalysis). The full-year guide requires the second half to deliver more than $1.1B. HII has missed a cash commitment before: in October 2024 it withdrew its five-year free-cash-flow outlook, and the stock fell 30% that month (per The Motley Fool).
03
Labour is the binding constraint. Throughput growth — the entire 2026 plan — depends on hiring, retention and productivity at two yards. Demand above capacity doesn't lift margins on its own; it turns into delays and overruns if the workforce can't keep up.
04
One customer, political budget. A proposed $1.5T FY2027 defence budget is a proposal, not an appropriation. A January 7, 2026 executive order also directs the Pentagon to write contract terms prohibiting buybacks and distributions by contractors it judges to be underperforming or under-investing (as reported by DefenseScoop) — a new kind of policy risk for a dividend-paying shipbuilder.

Notable One-Off Items

Two things distort the reported figures. First, contract adjustments: shipbuilding profit is booked on estimated cost-to-complete, so a change in estimate on a multi-year hull hits the current quarter in full. Q2 2026 Newport News margin benefited from favourable adjustments and incentives; Ingalls' margin fell year-on-year because Q2 2025 carried favourable adjustments of its own. Second, working-capital timing: H1 2026 free cash flow of −$611M compares with +$268M in H1 2025 on higher revenue, which reflects when the Navy pays against milestones rather than profitability. No share repurchases were reported in 2025 (versus $162M in 2024), so per-share figures are not buyback-driven.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $193.06 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $276.10, HII gained 43.0% on price, before annual dividends that rose from $4.60 (FY2021) to $5.43 (FY2025) per share. The endpoints hide a round trip. The stock spent 2022–2024 grinding between month-end closes of roughly $187 and $292, collapsed 30% in a single month on the October 2024 Q3 miss, bottomed at a $175.58 February 2025 close — the lowest month-end in the window — and then more than doubled to a $444.52 February 2026 close, the highest. Since then it has given back 37.9%, and is down 18.8% year-to-date from the $340.07 December 2025 close.

The 6-period monthly RSI captures the swing. It printed 89.6 in February 2026, the most overbought reading in the series, and 25.1 in October 2024, the most oversold. It now reads 38.8 — weak, but not yet oversold. The 10-month moving average, at about $343, sits well above the price: the long-term trend is down, and every month-end since May 2026 has closed below it, including July's post-earnings high.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 16.4x trailing and 14.9x forward earnings for the only yard that can build a U.S. nuclear carrier, with $57.3B of backlog — over four years of revenue — and a customer asking for more ships than the industry can deliver. Q2 showed the model working: revenue +10.9%, EPS +36.5% ($5.27 vs $3.86), shipbuilding guide raised, Mission Technologies margin up from 4.6% to 7.2%. If the second half delivers the $1.1B+ of free cash flow the reaffirmed guide implies, the cash scare that took the stock from $444 to $276 is revealed as timing. Even the lowest of the 12 analysts' targets ($304) is above the current price.

Bear Case

A monopoly that earns a 5–6% operating margin and burned $611M of free cash in six months is not a monopoly with pricing power; it is a fixed-price contractor carrying its customer's schedule risk. Carrier performance has been named as a drag in both 2026 quarters, and the company has withdrawn a cash outlook before (October 2024). A $1.5T budget proposal doesn't fix the yard's real constraint, which is labour — and more demand than capacity can mean more overruns, not more profit. The stock gave back its entire post-Q2 pop within weeks, which says the market doesn't believe the second-half cash story yet.

Top 3 Risks

01
Second-half cash shortfall. The reaffirmed $500–600M FY2026 free-cash-flow guide needs roughly $1.1–1.2B in H2 after −$611M in H1. A cut to that guide would repeat the October 2024 pattern.
02
Negative contract adjustments at Newport News. Carrier performance was cited as a drag in both Q1 and Q2 2026. On multi-year fixed-price hulls, a revised cost-to-complete lands in one quarter's earnings.
03
Budget and policy risk from a single customer. The $1.5T figure is a proposal; appropriations, continuing resolutions and the reported executive order on contractor buybacks and dividends all sit outside management's control.
Industry Commentary
Labour, not demand, sets the pace
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

HII's whole 2026 plan is expressed as a labour number: ~15% shipbuilding throughput growth after ~14% in 2025, credited in February to "better hiring, employee retention, and increased efficiency in operations" (as summarised by StockStory on TradingView). Recent HII announcements point the same way — a virtual-reality welding lab at Ingalls that passed 1,000 trainees (Sep 18, 2026 headline), an unmanned-facility expansion (Sep 17), and mechanised welding with HD Hyundai Heavy Industries at Ingalls. The market will judge each quarter on whether throughput growth shows up as margin, or only as revenue.

The budget tailwind and its strings
POLICY WILDCARD

On January 7, 2026 the President called for a $1.5 trillion FY2027 defence budget and issued an executive order directing the Secretary of Defense to put contract terms "prohibiting both any stock buy-back and corporate distributions by the contractor" in cases of "underperformance, non-compliance with the contractor's contract, insufficient prioritization of the contract, insufficient investment, or insufficient production speed" (DefenseScoop, Jan 7, 2026). For HII both halves matter: more shipbuilding money supports the backlog, while a delivery-linked capital-return test hits a company whose dividend is 33% of earnings and whose results are judged on delivery pace. HII reported no buybacks in 2025.

The valuation after the round trip
SENTIMENT RESET

At $276.10 HII is back near its July pre-earnings level with a better guide than it had then, a 2.0% dividend yield, and a price 9% below the lowest target of the 12 analysts. The bull framing: the market has swung from pricing a boom (RSI 89.6 in February) to pricing a problem, and the backlog hasn't changed. The bear framing: a thin-margin contractor with negative trailing free cash flow deserves no premium, and the right comparison is the 2022–2024 range, not the February peak. The thing to watch is Q3 cash, not the P/E.
