01 Company Overview

Hitachi has spent the last five years narrowing from a sprawling Japanese conglomerate to a group that now runs four businesses: Energy (power grids through Hitachi Energy, plus nuclear), Digital Systems & Services (IT for Japanese banks, government and industry, plus GlobalLogic), Mobility (trains and rail signalling) and Connective Industries (elevators and building systems, industrial equipment, and Hitachi High-Tech's chip-inspection tools). In fiscal 2025 — Hitachi's year ended March 31, 2026 — revenue was ¥10,586.7 billion, up 8.2%, adjusted EBITA rose 21% to ¥1,311.4 billion, and net income attributable to shareholders was ¥802.3 billion, or ¥176.76 basic EPS. 63% of revenue came from outside Japan. The latest quarter, Q2 FY2026 (July–September 2026, reported October 28), set second-quarter records for revenue and adjusted EBITA: revenue rose 15% to ¥2,904.4 billion, just under a ¥2.93 trillion consensus, and adjusted EBITA rose 16% to ¥376.1 billion. Basic EPS of ¥50.98 missed the ¥54.72 consensus by 6.8% (consensus from Investing.com's earnings table). Net income fell 19% to ¥227.1 billion, which the company puts down to a ¥91.7 billion gain on the sale of its air-conditioning joint venture in the year-earlier quarter. Hitachi raised its full-year forecast for the second time this year and lifted the interim dividend to ¥32 from a ¥28 forecast; the shares fell 9.8% the next day.

The stock tells the story of that shift. From an October 2021 month-end close of ¥1,310.8 (adjusted for the 5-for-1 split of July 1, 2024) it reached ¥5,094 on October 30, 2026, up 288.6%, while the Nikkei 225 rose 119.1% over the same window. The main case for the shares is electricity: AI data centers need power, and power needs transformers, cables and high-voltage links that take years to build. Hitachi Energy's order backlog stood at ¥10.5 trillion on September 30, 2026. The October 2025 agreement with OpenAI to work on data-center power and equipment added a second line to that story. The question now is price. At 30.7 times trailing earnings, and 24.7 times the company's own forecast for this year against 16.56 times forecast earnings for the Nikkei 225 (Nikkei Inc.'s figure, October 30, 2026), the market already counts a lot of that backlog as delivered, and the October 29 fall showed how quickly a single soft line in the results can move the price.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

¥10.5T grid-equipment backlog HVDC & transformer capacity Installed base of grids, trains, elevators Japan's mission-critical IT Lumada / HMAX software layer Net cash balance sheet

The moat is capacity plus installed base. Transformer and HVDC factories take years to build and qualify, so a full order book is itself a barrier: a customer who wants equipment several years out has to book it now. Once a grid, a rail-signalling system or a bank's core system is in, the vendor also maintains it for decades. Hitachi's plan, branded HMAX, is to sell AI-based monitoring and maintenance on top of that installed base; HMAX revenue was ¥238.0 billion in the first half of FY2026, with ¥510.0 billion forecast for the year (+70%). The weak points are the yen, the price paid for the growth, and the execution risk of very large fixed projects.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A weak yen is doing part of the work. 63% of fiscal 2025 revenue was earned outside Japan (67% in the first half of FY2026), and the company's Q2 FY2026 bridge attributes ¥123.0 billion of the ¥375.4 billion revenue increase, and ¥15.0 billion of the ¥51.8 billion adjusted EBITA increase, to currency. Excluding FX, revenue grew about 10%, not 15%. A stronger yen would reverse that in reported figures.
02
The valuation leaves little room. At ¥5,094 the stock trades at 30.7 times trailing earnings and 24.7 times the company's own FY2026 EPS forecast, against 16.56 times forecast earnings for the Nikkei 225 (Nikkei Inc.'s figure, October 30, 2026). This year shows how fast it can fall: March 2026 (−14.6%, with the Nikkei 225 down 13.2%) and June 2026 (−13.5%, while the Nikkei 225 rose 5.6%) on month-end closes, and −9.8% in the single session of October 29, when the Nikkei 225 rose 1.1%.
03
Large projects, large surprises. HVDC links and rail contracts run for years, so a cost overrun on one of them can be large. Q2 showed the risk in a small way: cost increases on certain domestic IT projects were part of about ¥9.0 billion of one-off charges in DSS in the first half, per the company. Management's answer on the big contracts, from Kato on the July 29 call: for HVDC projects of five to six years "we have a contract structure that reflects cost increases in the sales prices charged to customers using various indices," and Hitachi is concentrating on engineering and procurement while reducing its exposure to construction. The Middle East situation cost about ¥33.0 billion of revenue and ¥13.0 billion of adjusted EBITA in the first half, per the company's bridge, and the full-year plan carries ¥47.0 billion of extra corporate strategic investment (AI, R&D, sales pipeline).
04
Cash flow will dip this year. Core free cash flow is guided to ¥920.0 billion for FY2026 (raised from ¥900.0 billion), down ¥250.2 billion, which the company attributes to about ¥340 billion of large advance payments received in the prior year; without that, it would rise about ¥90 billion. Advances that flattered fiscal 2025 cash flow reverse as the projects are built.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the comparisons. First, the prior-year gains: the first half of FY2025 carried gains from business-portfolio restructuring, chiefly the sale of the air-conditioning joint venture to Bosch (a ¥91.7 billion effect on Q2 FY2025 net income, per the company), plus a special dividend tied to the restructuring. That is why H1 FY2026 net income fell ¥56.2 billion to ¥416.6 billion while adjusted EBITA rose ¥137.8 billion; excluding the joint-venture gain, Q2 net income rose ¥38.3 billion. Second, one-off project costs: DSS absorbed about ¥9.0 billion of one-off factors in the first half, including cost increases on certain domestic projects in Q2. Third, advance payments: fiscal 2025 operating cash flow of ¥1,668.0 billion was lifted by advances on large orders, and the company's FY2026 cash-flow guide reverses about ¥340 billion of that. The Astemo stake sale (¥152.3 billion, completed October 1) is, per the October 28 release, not material to FY2026 net income.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of ¥1,310.8 to the October 30, 2026 close of ¥5,094, Hitachi's share price rose 288.6%, almost four times the starting price, before dividends. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Hitachi has turned itself into a company whose biggest segment sells something the world is short of: grid equipment. Hitachi Energy's backlog is ¥10.5 trillion, about 2.6 times the Energy segment's guided FY2026 revenue of ¥4,060.0 billion, and Energy orders rose 66% in the first half. Profit is rising faster than revenue — group adjusted EBITA margin from 11.1% to 12.4% in fiscal 2025, guided to 13.0% — and Lumada, at a 17% margin, is growing past 40% of sales. The balance sheet has net cash of ¥482.1 billion, and ¥814 billion is going back to shareholders this year. Management has raised the full-year forecast twice. If the guide is met (basic EPS up 16.8% to ¥206.41), the stock is on 24.7 times this year's earnings; one more year at that growth rate would put today's price at about 21 times. And on the July 29 call the CFO said large grid projects now carry delivery dates past 2030, with growth expected to continue until around 2035.

Bear Case

At 30.7 times trailing earnings, Hitachi is priced like a growth company while growing reported revenue 8% in its last full year — and about 5 points of Q2's 15% growth came from a weaker yen (10% excluding currency, per the company). Siemens Energy, GE Vernova and Schneider Electric compete for the same grid orders, and when industry capacity catches up with demand, pricing on new orders can soften before the backlog shows it. Core free cash flow is guided down ¥250.2 billion this year as prepayments unwind. The stock has already shown it can drop 13–15% in a month twice in 2026, and 9.8% in a day after a record quarter in which EPS missed consensus and DSS took one-off project costs. Not every unit is growing: the company expects GlobalLogic and Hitachi Digital Services' own dollar revenue to slip slightly this year.

Top 3 Risks

01
Yen appreciation. With 63% of revenue abroad and the FY2026 guide built on ¥160 per dollar and ¥185 per euro for the second half, a stronger yen cuts reported revenue and profit directly: by the company's own sensitivity, each yen of movement against the dollar shifts second-half revenue by about ¥8.0 billion and adjusted EBITA by ¥1.1 billion. Currency supplied ¥123.0 billion of Q2's ¥375.4 billion revenue gain.
02
Investors could pay less for each yen of profit. A price of 24.7 times this year's forecast earnings, against 16.56 for the Nikkei 225 on forecast earnings (Nikkei Inc., October 30, 2026), depends on the grid cycle lasting. A slower order quarter in Energy — orders come in lumps — could lower that price-to-earnings ratio, and the share price with it, even with earnings on track; the stock has already had two months of −13% to −15% this year (March 2026, −14.6%; June 2026, −13.5%) and a −9.8% day after the Q2 results.
03
Project execution and geopolitics. Multi-year HVDC and rail contracts carry cost-overrun risk; Q2 brought cost increases on certain domestic IT projects (part of about ¥9.0 billion of one-off factors in DSS in the first half), and the company is booking a Middle East impact (about ¥33.0 billion of revenue and ¥13.0 billion of adjusted EBITA in the first half). A large write-down on one project would hit a margin story the market is paying for.
Industry Commentary
AI data centers and the grid build-out
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Data centers for AI draw power at a scale that grids were not built for, and connecting them means new transformers, substations and long-distance lines. Equipment makers have long lead times, so orders placed now are for delivery years ahead — which is why Hitachi Energy's backlog keeps rising faster than its revenue. Hitachi's October 2025 agreement with OpenAI covers exactly this: reducing the load data centers put on transmission networks, securing long-lead equipment and standardising modular designs. The same demand is lifting every grid supplier — GE Vernova rose 49.4% and Siemens Energy 19.6% over the year to October 30, 2026 — so Hitachi's advantage is position, not exclusivity. On the July 29 call Kato pushed the horizon out: "Until last year, we explained that we expected growth through 2030, but at present there are large-system projects with delivery dates beyond 2030, and we recognize that growth will continue until around 2035."

Japan's conglomerate unwind
ONGOING

Hitachi is a prominent example of a Japanese group narrowing to fewer businesses. The current plan continues with home appliances (to a Nojima-managed company, KADENOVA, during FY2026), ATMs (into a 60/40 company with OKI, operating since October 1, 2026) and Astemo (Hitachi sold part of its stake to Honda for ¥152.3 billion on October 1, cutting it from 40.0% to 19.0%). The proceeds fund buybacks — ¥300 billion between April and December 2025, ¥100 billion between January and April 2026, and up to ¥500 billion in the current program, per Hitachi's shareholder-return page. Cross-shareholdings are going too: Hitachi sold Hitachi Construction Machinery shares on May 15, 2026 and again on August 21 (closed, per the October 28 presentation), which together give a ¥179.9 billion gain in its parent-only accounts; under IFRS the gain does not pass through consolidated profit or change the forecast (August 19 release).

Japanese IT modernisation
STEADY DEMAND

Hitachi's domestic IT growth comes from modernising and migrating core systems for financial, public-sector and transport clients, per its Q1 presentation; Fujitsu and NEC compete for the same work. That demand is behind DSS's ¥2.0 trillion backlog (+12% since March 31, 2026) and its 15.5% Q2 margin, the highest of the four segments that quarter. Q2 also showed the downside: cost increases on certain domestic projects, which the company treats as one-off. Hitachi says AI tools are raising productivity on these projects; the open question, as for every IT-services company, is whether clients eventually ask for the savings back in lower prices.
