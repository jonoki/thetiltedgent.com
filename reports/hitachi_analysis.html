01 Company Overview

Hitachi has spent the last five years narrowing from a sprawling Japanese conglomerate to a group that now runs four businesses: Energy (power grids through Hitachi Energy, plus nuclear), Digital Systems & Services (IT for Japanese banks, government and industry, plus GlobalLogic), Mobility (trains and rail signalling) and Connective Industries (elevators and building systems, industrial equipment, and Hitachi High-Tech's chip-inspection tools). In fiscal 2025 — Hitachi's year ended March 31, 2026 — revenue was ¥10,586.7 billion, up 8.2%, adjusted EBITA rose 21% to ¥1,311.4 billion, and net income attributable to shareholders was ¥802.3 billion, or ¥176.76 basic EPS. 63% of revenue came from outside Japan. The latest quarter, Q1 FY2026 (April–June 2026, reported July 29), came in ahead of expectations: revenue rose 20% to ¥2,709.5 billion against a ¥2.48 trillion consensus, and basic EPS of ¥42.24 beat the ¥36.69 consensus by 15% (consensus from Investing.com's earnings table). Hitachi raised its full-year forecast in all four segments.

The stock tells the story of that shift. From a September 2021 month-end close of ¥1,328.0 (adjusted for the 5-for-1 split of July 1, 2024) it reached ¥5,522 on October 2, 2026, up 315.8%, while the Nikkei 225 rose 131.9% over the same window. The main case for the shares is electricity: AI data centers need power, and power needs transformers, cables and high-voltage links that take years to build. Hitachi Energy's order backlog stood at ¥10.3 trillion on June 30, 2026. The October 2025 agreement with OpenAI to work on data-center power and equipment added a second line to that story. The question now is price. At 31.2 times trailing earnings, and 27.5 times the company's own forecast for this year against 17.37 times estimated earnings for the Nikkei 225 (Nikkei Inc.'s figure, market-cap basis), the market already counts a lot of that backlog as delivered.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

¥10.3T grid-equipment backlog HVDC & transformer capacity Installed base of grids, trains, elevators Japan's mission-critical IT Lumada / HMAX software layer Net cash balance sheet

The moat is capacity plus installed base. Transformer and HVDC factories take years to build and qualify, so a full order book is itself a barrier: a customer who wants equipment several years out has to book it now. Once a grid, a rail-signalling system or a bank's core system is in, the vendor also maintains it for decades. Hitachi's plan, branded HMAX, is to sell AI-based monitoring and maintenance on top of that installed base; HMAX revenue was ¥111.0 billion in Q1 FY2026, with ¥505.0 billion forecast for the year. The weak points are the yen, the price paid for the growth, and the execution risk of very large fixed projects.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A weak yen is doing part of the work. 63% of fiscal 2025 revenue was earned outside Japan, and the company's Q1 FY2026 bridge attributes ¥182.0 billion of the ¥451.3 billion revenue increase, and ¥23.5 billion of the ¥86.0 billion adjusted EBITA increase, to currency. Excluding FX, revenue grew about 12%, not 20%. A stronger yen would reverse that in reported figures.
02
The valuation leaves little room. At ¥5,522 the stock trades at 31.2 times trailing earnings and 27.5 times the company's own FY2026 EPS forecast, against 17.37 times Nikkei Inc.'s estimated earnings for the Nikkei 225 (market-cap basis). Two months this year show how fast it can fall: March 2026 (−14.6%, with the Nikkei 225 down 13.2%) and June 2026 (−13.5%, while the Nikkei 225 rose 5.6%) on month-end closes.
03
Large projects, large surprises. HVDC links and rail contracts run for years, so a cost overrun on one of them can be large. Management's answer, from Kato on the July 29 call: for HVDC projects of five to six years "we have a contract structure that reflects cost increases in the sales prices charged to customers using various indices," and Hitachi is concentrating on engineering and procurement while reducing its exposure to construction. The company's own FY2026 bridge books ¥17.0 billion of adjusted EBITA drag from the Middle East situation, which in Q1 cost about ¥16.0 billion of revenue and ¥7.0 billion of EBITA, and ¥47.0 billion of extra corporate strategic investment (AI, R&D, sales pipeline).
04
Cash flow will dip this year. Core free cash flow is guided to ¥900.0 billion for FY2026, down ¥270.2 billion, which the company attributes partly to about ¥340 billion of large advance payments received in the prior year. Advances that flattered fiscal 2025 cash flow reverse as the projects are built.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the comparisons. First, the prior-year dividend: Q1 FY2025 net income included about ¥50 billion from an extraordinary dividend related to business-portfolio restructuring, which the company cites for why Q1 FY2026 net income fell ¥2.7 billion to ¥189.4 billion while adjusted EBITA rose ¥86.0 billion. Second, other income and expenses in fiscal 2025 were large and lumpy — ¥133.5 billion of other income against ¥200.8 billion of other expenses. Third, advance payments: fiscal 2025 operating cash flow of ¥1,668.0 billion was lifted by advances on large orders, and the company's FY2026 cash-flow guide reverses about ¥340 billion of that. The home-appliance sale (about ¥110 billion) is, per the company, not material to FY2026 net income.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF OCT 2, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of ¥1,328.0 to the October 2, 2026 close of ¥5,522, Hitachi's share price rose 315.8%, a little over four times the starting price, before dividends. The Nikkei 225 rose 131.9% over the same window (29,452.66 → 68,309.46, Yahoo month-end and daily closes). The path had two phases. From the February 2022 low of the window (¥1,130.0) to June 2024 the stock rose in eight of nine quarters on quarter-end closes. Since then it has moved in bigger swings: a 35.3% month in October 2025, then months of −14.6% (March 2026) and −13.5% (June 2026), each recovered within a few months.

The 6-period monthly RSI stands at 63.3, firm but below the 70 overbought line. It reached 97.2 in June 2024, the most stretched reading in the series, just before the split took effect and the stock fell 8.7% in July 2024. The 10-month moving average is about ¥5,129, and the price is 7.7% above it: the long-term trend is up. The October 2 close is the highest point on this month-end chart, though the highest daily close in the period was ¥5,818 on February 9, 2026.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Hitachi has turned itself into a company whose biggest segment sells something the world is short of: grid equipment. Hitachi Energy's backlog is ¥10.3 trillion, about 2.5 times the Energy segment's guided FY2026 revenue of ¥4,060.0 billion, and Q1 orders rose 87%. Profit is rising faster than revenue — group adjusted EBITA margin from 11.1% to 12.4% in fiscal 2025, guided to 13.0% — and Lumada, at a 17% margin, is growing past 40% of sales. The balance sheet has net cash of ¥447.6 billion, and about ¥800 billion is going back to shareholders this year. If the guide is met (basic EPS up 13.8% to ¥201.14), the stock is on 27.5 times this year's earnings; one more year at that growth rate would put today's price at about 24 times. And on the July 29 call the CFO said large grid projects now carry delivery dates past 2030, with growth expected to continue until around 2035.

Bear Case

At 31.2 times trailing earnings, Hitachi is priced like a growth company while growing reported revenue 8% in its last full year — and about 8 points of Q1's 20% growth came from a weaker yen (12% excluding currency, per the company). Siemens Energy, GE Vernova and Schneider Electric compete for the same grid orders, and when industry capacity catches up with demand, pricing on new orders can soften before the backlog shows it. Core free cash flow is guided down ¥270.2 billion this year as prepayments unwind. The stock has already shown it can drop 13–15% in a month twice in 2026, and the lowest analyst target (¥5,500) is about where it trades now. Not every unit is growing: GlobalLogic's revenue and profit slipped in Q1, per the CFO.

Top 3 Risks

01
Yen appreciation. With 63% of revenue abroad and the FY2026 guide built on ¥160 per dollar and ¥185 per euro for Q2–Q4, a stronger yen cuts reported revenue and profit directly. Currency supplied ¥182.0 billion of Q1's ¥451.3 billion revenue gain.
02
Valuation compression. A 27.5x multiple of this year's forecast earnings, against 17.37 for the Nikkei 225 on the same basis, depends on the grid cycle lasting. A slower order quarter in Energy — orders come in lumps — could cut the multiple even with earnings on track; the stock has already had two months of −13% to −15% this year (March 2026, −14.6%; June 2026, −13.5%).
03
Project execution and geopolitics. Multi-year HVDC and rail contracts carry cost-overrun risk, and the company is already booking a Middle East impact (about ¥16.0 billion of revenue and ¥7.0 billion of EBITA in Q1; ¥26.0 billion of revenue and ¥17.0 billion of EBITA in the full-year bridge). A large write-down on one project would hit a margin story the market is paying for.
Industry Commentary
AI data centers and the grid build-out
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Data centers for AI draw power at a scale that grids were not built for, and connecting them means new transformers, substations and long-distance lines. Equipment makers have long lead times, so orders placed now are for delivery years ahead — which is why Hitachi Energy's backlog keeps rising faster than its revenue. Hitachi's October 2025 agreement with OpenAI covers exactly this: reducing the load data centers put on transmission networks, securing long-lead equipment and standardising modular designs. The same demand is lifting every grid supplier — GE Vernova rose 63.1% and Siemens Energy 34.1% over the year to October 2, 2026 — so Hitachi's advantage is position, not exclusivity. On the July 29 call Kato pushed the horizon out: "Until last year, we explained that we expected growth through 2030, but at present there are large-system projects with delivery dates beyond 2030, and we recognize that growth will continue until around 2035."

Japan's conglomerate unwind
ONGOING

Hitachi is a prominent example of a Japanese group narrowing to fewer businesses. The current plan continues with home appliances (to a Nojima-managed company) and ATMs (into a 60/40 company with OKI). The proceeds fund buybacks — ¥300 billion between April and December 2025, ¥100 billion between January and April 2026, and up to ¥500 billion in the current program, per Hitachi's shareholder-return page. Cross-shareholdings are going too: Hitachi sold Hitachi Construction Machinery shares on May 15, 2026 and planned a further sale for August 21, which together give a ¥179.9 billion gain in its parent-only accounts; under IFRS the gain does not pass through consolidated profit or change the forecast (August 19 release).

Japanese IT modernisation
STEADY DEMAND

Hitachi's domestic IT growth comes from modernising and migrating core systems for financial, public-sector and transport clients, per its Q1 presentation; Fujitsu and NEC compete for the same work. That demand is behind DSS's ¥2.1 trillion backlog (+19% in a quarter) and its 15.3% margin, the highest in the group. Hitachi says AI tools are raising productivity on these projects; the open question, as for every IT-services company, is whether clients eventually ask for the savings back in lower prices.
