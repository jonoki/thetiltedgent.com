01 Company Overview

Hilton Worldwide Holdings is a global hotel franchisor and manager — not, in any meaningful sense, a hotel owner. As of June 30, 2026 its system comprised 9,332 hotels and 1,363,441 rooms, of which 1,081,678 rooms (79.3%) were franchised (Hilton licenses the brand and reservation system to a third-party owner in exchange for a royalty on that hotel's room revenue), 266,477 rooms (19.5%) were managed (Hilton runs the hotel day-to-day for a fee but the building belongs to someone else), and just 15,286 rooms — 1.1% of the system, across 46 properties — were owned or leased by Hilton itself. That single ratio is the whole company. Nearly every dollar of profit is a royalty on somebody else's real estate.

This is what "asset-light" means in practice: the capital, the construction risk, the property taxes and the mortgage all sit on the balance sheets of thousands of independent hotel owners, while Hilton collects a percentage off the top. For the twelve months ended June 30, 2026, Hilton's fee-basis revenue (revenue excluding cost reimbursements — see the note in Section 02) was $5.10B, up 5.7% year over year, on which it earned a operating margin of 56.7% and a net profit margin of 31.0%. Full-year 2025 revenue on the same basis was $4,954M (+4.4%). Hilton ranks #353 among the world's most valuable public companies by market capitalization.

The consequence of asset-light economics plus fifteen years of aggressive buybacks is a balance sheet that looks alarming and isn't: shareholders' equity is negative $6.27B. Section 04 explains why ROE and debt-to-equity are arithmetically meaningless here and are marked n/m rather than printed as nonsense.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
260M HONORS MEMBERS 1.36M ROOM NETWORK 541K ROOM PIPELINE 20-YR CONTRACT TERMS CO-BRAND CARD ECONOMICS MULTI-BRAND LADDER NEAR-ZERO CAPITAL INTENSITY

Hilton's moat is a two-sided network with contractual teeth. On the demand side, Hilton Honors has more than 260 million members; members book direct, which lowers the hotel owner's distribution cost versus paying an online travel agency, which is precisely why an owner signs with Hilton rather than flying an independent flag. On the supply side, franchise agreements typically run for roughly two decades, so the revenue Hilton will collect in 2035 is already substantially contracted today. The co-brand credit card relationships close the loop: banks purchase Hilton Honors points in bulk to award to cardholders, which means Hilton is paid cash up front for a liability it settles later in unsold room nights at marginal cost. That revenue is high-margin, it is contractually committed, and — crucially — it does not fall when travel falls, because people keep spending on their credit cards in a downturn. Hilton does not break the figure out in its quarterly release, so it cannot be sized precisely here, but it sits inside the $808M franchise and licensing line and is one of the main reasons a franchisor's earnings trough far above a hotel owner's.

Owner economics, not guest preference, is the real vulnerability. Hilton's customer is the developer, not the traveller. If construction financing stays expensive, new-build hotels do not pencil, approvals slow, and net unit growth — the compounding engine — decelerates with a two-to-three-year lag that is invisible in current earnings.

Rate is doing the heavy lifting and rate is macro-sensitive. Q2 2026 RevPAR grew 3.9%, of which roughly 2.5 points came from ADR. If pricing power stalls, Hilton has very little occupancy headroom left to compensate at 74.9%.

Valuation leaves no room for disappointment. At 47.3× trailing earnings against a 27.5× S&P 500 and 36.4× for Marriott, HLT is priced as a compounder. A single guidance cut on net unit growth would be repriced hard.

Negative equity removes the balance-sheet shock absorber. $14.02B of total debt against $1.01B of cash and a current ratio of 0.65 is entirely serviceable while fee revenue compounds; it is a much less comfortable position if a travel shock coincides with a refinancing window.

Why ROE and Debt-to-Equity are marked "n/m"

Hilton's shareholders' equity is negative $6.27B. This is not distress — it is arithmetic. Book equity falls when a company buys back stock for more than its book value, and Hilton has been retiring shares aggressively for a decade (2.9 million shares at an average of $326.99 in Q2 2026 alone, against a book value per share that is deeply negative). Because the business needs almost no assets to operate — it owns 1.1% of its rooms — there is no large asset base to hold equity up. So the accumulated buyback charge has driven the equity line through zero and kept going.

The trap is that ROE = net income ÷ equity. With net income of $1.58B and equity of −$6.27B, the formula returns −25% — a number that would imply Hilton destroys a quarter of its capital every year, when in fact it earns a 17.37% return on invested capital. Debt-to-equity fails identically: $14.02B ÷ −$6.27B = −2.24×, which is not a leverage ratio, it is a division artefact. Any screen or model that ranks Hilton on ROE or D/E is producing garbage. The correct substitutes are ROIC (17.37%), return on assets (11.03%), and leverage measured as net debt to EBITDA — approximately $13.0B net debt ÷ $4.06B guided 2026 adjusted EBITDA ≈ 3.2× (computed from $14.02B debt less $1.01B cash and the midpoint of guidance). That is a real, interpretable leverage figure, and it is the one management and the rating agencies actually use.

Notable One-Off Items

One item deserves a flag rather than an explanation, because the driver could not be verified from the sources fetched. Hilton's FY2025 net income was $1,457M, yet full-year 2026 guidance calls for $1,883M–$1,911M — roughly a 30% jump — against RevPAR guidance of only +3.0% to +3.5% and net unit growth of 6.0%–7.0%. Fee growth of that magnitude does not by itself produce a 30% increase in net income. The gap most likely reflects the absence of a prior-year charge, a tax item, or a below-the-line movement, but the specific driver was not disclosed in the material reviewed for this report and should be confirmed against the 10-K and 10-Q before relying on the 2026 net income figure as a clean run-rate. Note also that FY2025 net income ($1,457M) fell versus FY2024 ($1,535M) even as revenue rose 4.4% — another sign of non-operating noise in the reported line. Adjusted EPS guidance of $8.89–$9.01 is the cleaner comparison.

Five-year trajectory. From the September 2021 monthly close of $132.11 to the August 28, 2026 close of $321.85, HLT returned +143.6% on price alone — roughly 19.9% compounded annually before dividends, which at a 0.19% yield add almost nothing. Measured from the August 2021 close of $124.86, the clean five-year number is +157.8%, or 20.8% a year. That is a franchisor's return profile: Hilton owns almost no hotels, so what compounded here was a fee stream on a room base growing 6–7% a year, levered by a share count that buybacks have shrunk every single year of the window.

The path was not smooth. On unadjusted month-end closes the calendar years run −19.0% in 2022 ($155.99 → $126.36), +44.1% in 2023 (→ $182.09), +35.7% in 2024 (→ $247.16), +16.2% in 2025 (→ $287.25), and +12.0% year to date in 2026. Two drawdowns define the shape of the line. The first was the 2022 rate shock: April's $155.29 became June's $111.44 in two months, a 28.2% fall and the cheapest monthly close of the entire window — from there the stock has tripled, up 188.8%. The second was March 2025, when a tariff-and-consumer-demand scare took the close from $264.96 to $227.55 in a single month, −14.1%, with April adding a further slip to $225.48. Neither was an earnings failure. Both were multiple compressions, which is the characteristic risk of an asset-light franchisor: the cash flow is contractual, so what moves is the price the market will pay for it.

Where it sits now. The highest monthly close of the five years is June 2026's $330.46; the 52-week intramonth high of $358.00 was also set that month. Since then July closed at $320.49 and August at $321.85 — a flat, slightly heavy two months that leaves the stock 10.1% below its 52-week high and 26.9% above the 52-week low of $253.54. It is currently trading below its 3-month average close of $324.27 and comfortably above its 10-month average of $311.12: short-term momentum has stalled, the longer trend has not broken. The 6-period monthly RSI reads 67.9, just under the 70 overbought line, having spent most of the first half of 2026 in the high 70s and low 80s. It has been more stretched than this — 89.4 in March 2024, at the peak of the post-COVID re-rating — and it has been washed out only once, at 32.3 in June 2022.

The last three months are where the near-term story is. Daily closes show HLT at $350.22 on June 16, 2026, followed by a steady derating to $310.86 on August 10, an 11.2% slide, then a partial recovery to $334.58 on August 19 and a fade into the August 28 close. RevPAR (revenue per available room — total room revenue divided by every room in the hotel, occupied or not) is the single number the lodging industry lives by, and it decomposes cleanly into two factors: occupancy (what share of rooms were sold) multiplied by ADR (average daily rate — the average price actually paid for a sold room). For Q2 2026 the arithmetic works out exactly: occupancy of 74.9% multiplied by an ADR of $166.97 gives RevPAR of about $125.06, and the growth decomposes as roughly +1.4% from occupancy (74.9% against a prior-year 73.9%) compounded with +2.5% from rate, which multiplies out to the reported +3.9%.

That split matters enormously, and most readers get it backwards. A point of occupancy and a point of rate are not worth the same thing. Selling an additional room night costs real money — housekeeping, laundry, utilities, breakfast, the front-desk labour to check the guest in, and the commission if the booking came through a third party. Raising the rate on a room you were going to sell anyway costs nothing: the same guest, the same room, the same housekeeper, a bigger number on the folio. Industry rules of thumb put the incremental margin on rate-led RevPAR growth near 90%–100% and on occupancy-led growth closer to 50%–60%. So Hilton's Q2 mix — roughly two-thirds of the RevPAR gain coming from rate — is materially higher-quality than the same 3.9% delivered by filling more rooms would have been.

And there is a ceiling problem on the other side. At 74.9%, Hilton's occupancy is already close to practical full for a global system that includes seasonal resorts and midweek-only business hotels; there simply are not many more room nights to sell. Future RevPAR growth has to come predominantly from rate, which makes Hilton's earnings a leveraged bet on nominal pricing power — good in an inflationary economy, unpleasant in a deflationary or promotional one.

Net unit growth (the year-over-year increase in rooms in the system, after removing hotels that leave) is the franchisor's compounding engine, and it is the reason a hotel brand can grow earnings in a year when RevPAR does nothing. Hilton added rooms at 6.1% year over year to June 30, 2026, opening 207 hotels and 24,100 rooms in the quarter for a net addition of 21,600 rooms. Stack 6.1% more rooms on top of 3.9% RevPAR and fee revenue grows at roughly 10% before any margin expansion — which is precisely how a business in a mature, cyclical industry produces a growth-stock multiple.

What makes this unusually forecastable is that the growth is contractually visible years ahead. The development pipeline stood at 541,300 rooms across 3,853 hotels at June 30, 2026 — 39.7% of the entire existing system, already signed. Per the company's disclosure, more than half of those rooms are already under construction, which is the part that matters: a signed agreement can be abandoned, but a half-built hotel almost always opens. More than half of the pipeline sits outside the United States, which shifts Hilton's future fee mix toward international markets where management contracts (rather than franchises) are more common and where the growth runway is longer. A further 42,900 rooms were approved for development during Q2 2026 alone — approvals are the leading indicator; watch them, not the openings, if you want to know what 2029 looks like.

Hilton Honors and co-brand credit card fees are the most underappreciated line in the business. Honors has more than 260 million members. Co-brand fees — the money banks pay Hilton to buy Hilton Honors points in bulk, which they then award to cardholders as a sign-up bonus or spend reward — turn that membership base into a financial asset. The mechanics are excellent for Hilton: it receives cash today for points it will redeem later, and it redeems them in hotel rooms that would frequently have gone empty, so the true cost of settling the liability is a fraction of the cash collected. Better still, this revenue is largely counter-cyclical relative to the rest of the company: in a recession people cancel trips, which crushes RevPAR, but they do not stop putting groceries and utilities on their credit card, so co-brand fees hold up while franchise royalties fall. It is the closest thing Hilton has to a hedge against its own cycle. The honest caveat: Hilton does not break the figure out in its quarterly earnings release, and no separately disclosed co-brand revenue number was found in the sources fetched for this report, so it is described here mechanically rather than quantified. It is reported within the $808M franchise and licensing fee line.

Demand mix — leisure, business transient and group. The three demand segments behave very differently and are worth tracking separately: leisure travel is discretionary and price-elastic but recovered first and fastest after 2020; business transient (the individual corporate traveller) is the highest-rate, highest-margin midweek demand and the one most damaged by remote work; group (conferences, conventions, weddings) books far in advance, which makes it the most visible and the most useful for forecasting. Hilton's Q2 2026 disclosure of a 1.0 point occupancy gain and 2.5% rate growth is consistent with a healthy overall demand environment, but the segment-level breakdown of leisure versus business transient versus group performance was not disclosed in the earnings release reviewed for this report, and no segment figures have been invented here. That detail typically appears in the earnings call commentary and the 10-Q; a reader who cares about mix should go there.

  Hilton versus Marriott — Where They Actually Differ

Hilton and Marriott are usually presented as interchangeable rivals. On the verified numbers available here they are not, and the differences are specific.

Marriott is the larger business; Hilton carries the higher multiple. Marriott's market capitalization is $89.10B against Hilton's $72.4B, and its trailing twelve-month revenue of $7.39B is 45% larger than Hilton's $5.10B on the same fee basis. Yet Hilton trades at 47.33× trailing earnings versus Marriott's 36.41× — a 30% premium — and at 35.60× forward versus 28.44×, a 25% premium. An investor buying HLT over MAR is explicitly paying up.

On the two metrics where a premium would have to be justified, Hilton is currently behind. Marriott's trailing revenue grew +8.9% against Hilton's +5.7%. And on profitability, Marriott's net margin computes to 35.05% ($2.59B net income on $7.39B revenue) versus Hilton's 31.03% — Marriott converts about four percentage points more of each fee dollar into profit. Marriott also earns more per share in absolute terms: $9.64 of trailing EPS versus Hilton's $6.80.

Where Hilton's premium may be earned is unit growth — the 6.1% net unit growth and the 541,300-room pipeline equal to 39.7% of the existing system are genuinely strong. But this report will not assert that Hilton out-grows Marriott on units, because Marriott's comparable net unit growth and pipeline figures were not obtained from a live source in this pass. That is the decisive comparison for two franchisors and it is precisely the one that cannot be made honestly from the data gathered here. Anyone weighing HLT against MAR should pull Marriott's most recent net unit growth and pipeline disclosure and put it directly alongside Hilton's 6.1% and 541,300 rooms before concluding anything about which deserves the higher multiple.

One more real difference: relative position in their own ranges. Hilton closed at $321.85 against a 52-week range of $253.54–$358.00, leaving it 10.1% below its high. Marriott closed at $351.08 against a range of $256.76–$410.98, leaving it 14.6% below its high. Both have derated from their peaks; Marriott somewhat more.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Hilton is a royalty stream on 1.36 million hotel rooms it does not own, earning a 17.37% return on invested capital, a 56.7% operating margin and $2.03B of free cash flow ($9.02 per share against $6.80 of EPS) with essentially no capital intensity. Net unit growth of 6.1% plus RevPAR of 3.9% compounds fee revenue near double digits, and the 541,300-room pipeline — 39.7% of the current system, more than half already under construction — makes that growth contractually visible for years. Layer on ~$3.5B of guided 2026 capital return, roughly 4.8% of market cap, and per-share earnings compound faster than the business itself.

Bear Case

At 47.33× trailing and 35.60× forward earnings, HLT is priced for uninterrupted compounding while growing revenue at 5.7% — slower than Marriott's 8.9% and at a four-point lower net margin, yet carrying a 30% higher P/E. RevPAR growth of 3.0%–3.5% guided for the full year is barely ahead of inflation, occupancy at 74.9% leaves little headroom, and future growth must come from rate, which is macro-dependent. Meanwhile $14.02B of debt against $1.01B of cash, a 0.65 current ratio and negative $6.27B of equity leave no balance-sheet cushion if a travel shock arrives — and the FY2026 net income guide of $1,883M–$1,911M against FY2025's $1,457M has an unexplained gap that has not been reconciled here.

01
Multiple compression, not earnings failure, is the primary risk. The stock fell from $350.22 on June 16 to $310.86 on August 10 — 11.2% — without any deterioration in reported results. At 47× earnings, a shift in how the market prices lodging growth costs more than a bad quarter would.
02
Net unit growth decelerating because developers cannot finance hotels. Hilton's customer is the hotel owner. Expensive construction debt kills new-build economics, and the damage appears in approvals (42,900 rooms in Q2 2026) two to three years before it shows in fee revenue — meaning current earnings tell you nothing about it.
03
Rate-dependence in a soft pricing environment. With occupancy at 74.9% and roughly two-thirds of RevPAR growth coming from ADR, any loss of nominal pricing power flows almost directly to the fee line. Franchise royalties are a percentage of the owner's room revenue — if rates flatten, Hilton's revenue flattens with them, with no volume offset available.
  Industry Commentary
Development Financing Is the Binding Constraint
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Hilton does not build hotels — thousands of independent developers do, using construction debt. The company's entire growth algorithm depends on those projects penciling. The buffer today is unusually good: a 541,300-room pipeline equal to 39.7% of the existing system, with more than half already under construction, which means several years of openings are effectively locked in regardless of what happens to financing next. The vulnerability is on the other side of that buffer — the 42,900 rooms approved in Q2 2026 are what determines openings in 2029. A sustained slowdown in approvals would be invisible in earnings for years and then very hard to reverse.
RevPAR Growth Has Normalised to Low Single Digits
ONGOING HEADWIND
The post-2020 recovery is finished. Q2 2026 RevPAR of +3.9% and full-year guidance of +3.0% to +3.5% imply a decelerating second half, and at 74.9% occupancy there is very little room left to grow by filling hotels. Lodging is now a low-single-digit organic growth industry, and Hilton's above-industry earnings growth has to come from the two levers that are not RevPAR: 6.1% net unit growth and ~$3.5B of annual buyback. That is a perfectly sound algorithm — but it is a different investment case than the one that carried the stock from $125 to $350, and the 47× multiple has not fully adjusted to it.
Bull vs Bear — What It Comes Down To
EMERGING TAILWIND
The bull and bear cases here are not really disagreeing about Hilton's business quality — a 17.37% ROIC, 79% gross margins, $2.03B of free cash flow and a pipeline worth 39.7% of the existing system are not in dispute. They are disagreeing about the price. The bull says a contractually visible 6% unit growth engine, an international mix shift, and counter-cyclical co-brand card economics justify 47× and that owning the toll booth beats owning the road. The bear says you are paying a 30% premium to Marriott for 3.2 points less revenue growth and 4 points less net margin, into RevPAR guidance of 3.0%–3.5%, with a balance sheet carrying $13.0B of net debt and no equity cushion. The resolution is empirical and arrives on October 21: if net unit growth holds at 6%+ and approvals stay near 40,000 rooms a quarter, the premium is defensible. If either slips, there is a long way down to Marriott's multiple.
