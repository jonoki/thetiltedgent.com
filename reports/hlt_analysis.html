01 Company Overview

Hilton Worldwide Holdings is a global hotel franchisor and manager — not, in any meaningful sense, a hotel owner. As of September 30, 2026 its system comprised 9,494 hotels and 1,386,489 rooms (excluding 126 Hilton Grand Vacations timeshare properties with 21,928 rooms), of which 1,101,990 rooms (79.5%) were franchised (Hilton licenses the brand and reservation system to a third-party owner in exchange for a royalty on that hotel's room revenue), 269,618 rooms (19.4%) were managed (Hilton runs the hotel day-to-day for a fee but the building belongs to someone else), and just 14,881 rooms — 1.1% of the system, across 45 properties — were owned or leased by Hilton itself. That single ratio is the whole company. Nearly every dollar of profit is a royalty on somebody else's real estate.

This is what "asset-light" means in practice: the capital, the construction risk, the property taxes and the mortgage all sit on the balance sheets of thousands of independent hotel owners, while Hilton collects a percentage off the top. For the twelve months ended September 30, 2026, Hilton's total revenue was $12.69B, up 8.1% year over year (our calculation from the quarterly figures), of which most is pass-through cost reimbursement (see Section 02); it earned a operating margin of 23.9% and a net profit margin of 13.4% on that total. In Q3 2026 alone, revenue before cost reimbursement was $1,358M and net income $536M, a 39.5% margin on that narrower base (our calculation). Hilton ranks #159 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization.

The consequence of asset-light economics plus years of heavy buybacks is a balance sheet that looks alarming and isn't: shareholders' equity is negative $6.56B at September 30, 2026. Section 04 explains why ROE and debt-to-equity are arithmetically meaningless here and are marked n/m (not meaningful) rather than printed as nonsense.

Strip those out and the real business is $1,358M of fee, ownership and other revenue. Data providers differ: stockanalysis.com's income statement now shows the total basis ($12.69B over the last twelve months), and the analysts' consensus revenue estimate for FY2026 is $12.99B, also on the total basis. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
270M HONORS MEMBERS 1.39M ROOM NETWORK 543K ROOM PIPELINE 20-YR CONTRACT TERMS CO-BRAND CARD ECONOMICS MULTI-BRAND LADDER NEAR-ZERO CAPITAL INTENSITY

Hilton's moat is a two-sided network with contractual teeth. On the demand side, Hilton Honors has 270 million members; members book direct, which lowers the hotel owner's distribution cost versus paying an online travel agency, which is precisely why an owner signs with Hilton rather than flying an independent flag. On the supply side, franchise agreements typically run for roughly two decades, so the revenue Hilton will collect in 2035 is already substantially contracted today. The co-brand credit card relationships close the loop: banks purchase Hilton Honors points in bulk to award to cardholders, which means Hilton is paid cash up front for points that members redeem later. Because the fees follow card spending rather than hotel stays, a fall in travel hits them less directly than it hits room royalties. Hilton does not break the figure out in its quarterly release, so it cannot be sized here; its 10-Q places these licensing fees inside the $815M franchise and licensing line.

Owner economics, not guest preference, is the real vulnerability. Hilton's customer is the developer, not the traveller. If construction financing stays expensive, the numbers on new hotels stop working, approvals slow, and net unit growth — the main growth driver — slows years later, a lag that current earnings do not show.

Rate is doing the heavy lifting and rate is macro-sensitive. Q3 2026 RevPAR grew 5.8%, of which roughly 3.9 points came from ADR (average daily rate). Occupancy rose 1.4 points to 75.8%; if pricing power stalls, Hilton has little occupancy headroom left to compensate.

Valuation leaves no room for disappointment. At 48.3× trailing earnings against a 25.11× S&P 500 and 41.3× for Marriott, HLT is priced for years of steady growth. A single guidance cut on net unit growth would likely hit the shares hard.

Negative equity removes the balance-sheet shock absorber. $13.5B of total debt (the Q3 release, before deducting financing costs) against $830M of cash and a current ratio of 0.59 is entirely serviceable while fee revenue keeps growing; it is a much less comfortable position if a travel shock coincides with a refinancing window. The release shows the only material maturity before April 2029 is $600M of senior notes due April 2027, and an undrawn revolver with $1,894M available.

Why ROE and Debt-to-Equity are marked "n/m"

Hilton's shareholders' equity is negative $6.56B. This is not distress — it is arithmetic. Book equity falls when a company buys back stock for more than its book value, and Hilton has been retiring shares heavily for years (2.6 million shares at an average of $320.05 in Q3 2026 alone, against a book value per share that is deeply negative). Because the business needs almost no assets to operate — it owns 1.1% of its rooms — there is no large asset base to hold equity up. So the accumulated buyback charge has driven the equity line through zero and kept going.

The trap is that ROE = net income ÷ equity. With TTM net income of $1.70B and equity of −$6.56B, the formula returns −26% — a number that would imply Hilton destroys a quarter of its capital every year, when in fact it earns an 18.74% return on invested capital. Debt-to-equity fails identically: $13.97B of debt and lease liabilities ÷ −$6.56B = −2.13×, which is not a leverage ratio, it is a division artefact. Any screen or model that ranks Hilton on ROE or D/E is producing garbage. The correct substitutes are ROIC (18.74%), return on assets (11.32%), and leverage measured as net debt to adjusted EBITDA — 3.2× at September 30, 2026 on Hilton's own calculation, with net debt of $12.6B (Q3 release). That is a real, interpretable leverage figure, and it is the one management and the rating agencies actually use.

Notable One-Off Items

Hilton's Q3 2026 net income was $536M against $421M, and nine-month net income $1,401M against $1,163M (+20%). Full-year 2026 guidance is net income of $1,909M–$1,923M, which with the fourth-quarter guide of $508M–$522M is up about 71%–76% on Q4 2025's $297M (stockanalysis.com). Net income is therefore moving much faster than RevPAR guidance of +4.0% to +4.5% and net unit growth of 6.0%–7.0% would suggest on their own. Adjusted EPS — diluted EPS excluding what Hilton calls special items — is the cleaner comparison: the full-year guide of $9.07–$9.13 sits $0.69 above the diluted EPS guide of $8.38–$8.44, and adjusted nine-month EPS was $6.75 against $6.03 a year earlier (+12%).

Computed on the same 61 monthly closes plotted above. Plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be oversold. Between 30 and 70 is ordinary. On monthly data the swings are slower and the extremes are rarer than on a daily chart.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 monthly close of $143.95 to the October 30, 2026 close of $356.92, HLT returned +147.9% on price alone — roughly 19.9% compounded annually before dividends, which at a 0.17% yield add almost nothing. That is a franchisor's return profile: Hilton owns almost no hotels, so what grew here was a fee stream on a room base that grew 6.0% in the year to September 30, 2026, boosted by a share count that buybacks have shrunk.

The path was not smooth. On unadjusted month-end closes the calendar years run −19.0% in 2022 ($155.99 → $126.36), +44.1% in 2023 (→ $182.09), +35.7% in 2024 (→ $247.16), +16.2% in 2025 (→ $287.25), and +24.3% year to date in 2026. Two drawdowns define the shape of the line. The first was the 2022 rate shock: April's $155.29 became June's $111.44 in two months, a 28.2% fall and the cheapest monthly close of the entire window — from there the stock has more than tripled, up 220.3%. The second was March 2025, when a tariff-and-consumer-demand scare took the close from $264.96 to $227.55 in a single month, −14.1%, with April adding a further slip to $225.48. Neither was an earnings failure. Both were falls in the price investors would pay per dollar of earnings, which is the characteristic risk of an asset-light franchisor: the cash flow is contractual, so what moves is the price the market will pay for it.

Where it sits now. The highest monthly close of the five years is now October 2026's $356.92, ahead of June 2026's $330.46; the 52-week intraday high of $364.50 was set on October 21, 2026. July closed at $320.49, August at $316.28 and September at $318.12 before October's 12.2% jump (our calculation from month-end closes). The stock sits 2.1% below its 52-week high and 40.8% above the 52-week low of $253.54 (Nov 3, 2025). It is trading above both its 3-month average close of $330.44 and its 10-month average of $320.84. The 6-period monthly RSI reads 81.7, above the 70 overbought line, after 80.7 in June, 67.1 in July, 61.8 in August and 63.3 in September. It has been more stretched — 89.3 in March 2024 — and it has been washed out only once, at 28.1 in June 2022.

The last four months are where the near-term story is. Daily closes show HLT at $350.22 on June 16, 2026, followed by a slide to a summer low close of $302.54 on September 17, a 13.6% fall. From there the stock rose 19.7% to a record close of $362.25 on October 21, including gains of 4.57% on October 15 and 3.83% on October 20 (the day Hilton announced its move to Nasdaq). On the Q3 print on October 27 it closed at $344.65, down 2.06% from $351.89, then recovered to $356.92 by October 30. Revenue missed press-reported estimates and the EBITDA guide midpoint sat below consensus per press coverage, but adjusted EPS beat and the RevPAR guide rose; the market looked through it within three sessions.

Relative performance. On month-end closes, HLT gained +38.9% over the trailing twelve months ($256.96 on Oct 31, 2025 to $356.92 on Oct 30, 2026), against +10.8% for the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO) on the same dates ($627.04 to $694.81, price return, Yahoo Finance): Hilton beat the index by about 28 percentage points while its business kept growing. The question this report keeps returning to is whether 48× earnings already pays for that — it is either the setup or the warning, depending on what you think that multiple is worth.

RevPAR (revenue per available room — total room revenue divided by every room in the hotel, occupied or not) is the single number the lodging industry lives by, and it decomposes cleanly into two factors: occupancy (what share of rooms were sold) multiplied by ADR (average daily rate — the average price actually paid for a sold room). For Q3 2026 the arithmetic works out: occupancy of 75.8% multiplied by an ADR of $164.57 gives RevPAR of about $124.74, and the growth decomposes as roughly +1.9% from occupancy (75.8% against a prior-year 74.4%) compounded with +3.9% from rate, which multiplies out to about 5.9% against the reported +5.8% (rounding).

That split matters, and most readers get it backwards. A point of occupancy and a point of rate are not worth the same thing. Selling an additional room night costs real money — housekeeping, laundry, utilities, breakfast, the front-desk labour to check the guest in, and the commission if the booking came through a third party. Raising the rate on a room you were going to sell anyway costs nothing: the same guest, the same room, the same housekeeper, a bigger number on the folio. So Hilton's Q3 mix — roughly two-thirds of the RevPAR gain coming from rate — is higher-quality than the same 5.8% delivered by filling more rooms would have been.

And there is a ceiling problem on the other side. At 75.8%, Hilton's occupancy is already high for a global system that includes seasonal resorts and midweek-only business hotels; there are not many more room nights to sell. Future RevPAR growth has to come predominantly from rate, which makes Hilton's earnings a bet on nominal pricing power — good in an inflationary economy, unpleasant in a deflationary or promotional one.

Net unit growth (the year-over-year increase in rooms in the system, after removing hotels that leave) is the franchisor's main growth driver, and it is the reason a hotel brand can grow earnings in a year when RevPAR does nothing. Hilton added rooms at 6.0% year over year to September 30, 2026, opening 182 hotels and 27,000 rooms in the quarter for a net addition of 23,000 rooms. Management and franchise fee revenues rose 9.5% in Q3 (Q3 release) — which is how a business in a mature, cyclical industry produces a growth-stock multiple.

What makes this unusually forecastable is that the growth is contractually visible years ahead. The development pipeline stood at 543,100 rooms across 3,869 hotels at September 30, 2026 — 39.2% of the entire existing system, already signed. Per the company's release, nearly half of those rooms are already under construction, which is the part that matters: a signed agreement can be abandoned, but a half-built hotel almost always opens. More than half of the pipeline sits outside the United States, which shifts Hilton's future fee mix toward international markets where management contracts (rather than franchises) are more common and where the growth runway is longer. A further 30,500 rooms were approved for development during Q3 2026 (42,900 in Q2) — approvals are the leading indicator; watch them, not the openings, if you want to know what 2029 looks like.

Hilton Honors and co-brand credit card fees are the most underappreciated line in the business. Honors has 270 million members. Co-brand fees — the money banks pay Hilton to buy Hilton Honors points in bulk, which they then award to cardholders as a sign-up bonus or spend reward — turn that membership base into a financial asset. Hilton receives cash today for points members redeem later. And the fees follow card spending, not hotel stays: in a recession people cancel trips, which hits RevPAR directly, while everyday card spending on groceries and utilities is a different driver, so a travel slump reaches these fees less directly than it reaches franchise royalties. Per Hilton's Q3 2026 10-Q, licensing fees from co-branded credit card providers are reported within franchise and licensing fees (the $815M line in Q3 2026), not as a revenue line of their own.

Demand mix — leisure, business transient and group. The three demand segments behave very differently and are worth tracking separately: leisure travel is discretionary and price-elastic but recovered first and fastest after 2020; business transient (the individual corporate traveller) is the highest-rate, highest-margin midweek demand and the one most damaged by remote work; group (conferences, conventions, weddings) books far in advance, which makes it the most visible and the most useful for forecasting. Hilton's Q3 2026 disclosure of a 1.4 point occupancy gain and 3.9% rate growth is consistent with a healthy overall demand environment.

  Hilton versus Marriott — Where They Actually Differ

Hilton and Marriott are usually presented as interchangeable rivals. On the numbers below they are not, and the differences are specific. Both sets of revenue figures are total revenue, cost reimbursements included.

Marriott is the larger business; Hilton carries the higher multiple. Marriott's market capitalization is $104.12B against Hilton's $79.4B (our calculation: October 30 closes of $399.30 and $356.92 times 260.77M and 222.5M shares from stockanalysis.com), and its revenue over the four quarters to June 30, 2026 (the last it had reported by October 30) of $26.90B is more than twice Hilton's $12.69B. Yet Hilton trades at 48.30× trailing earnings versus Marriott's 41.34× — a 17% premium. An investor buying HLT over MAR is explicitly paying up.

On the two metrics where a premium would have to be justified, Hilton is currently ahead. Hilton's trailing revenue grew +8.1% against Marriott's +4.7% (both our calculation from quarterly figures). And on profitability, Hilton's net margin computes to 13.40% ($1.70B on $12.69B) versus Marriott's 9.62% ($2.59B on $26.90B). Marriott earns more per share in absolute terms: $9.66 of trailing EPS versus Hilton's $7.39 — which is why a lower P/E still means a higher share price.

Unit growth is the other half of the comparison — Hilton's 6.0% net unit growth and 543,100-room pipeline equal to 39.2% of the existing system are the figures to set beside Marriott's own unit-growth disclosure.

One more real difference: relative position in their own ranges. Hilton closed at $356.92 against a 52-week range of $253.54–$364.50, leaving it 2.1% below its high. Marriott closed at $399.30 against a range of $256.76–$410.98, leaving it 2.8% below its high. Both are near their peaks.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Hilton is a royalty stream on 1.39 million hotel rooms it does not own, earning an 18.74% return on invested capital and $1.93B of free cash flow ($8.68 per share against $7.39 of EPS) with essentially no capital intensity. Net unit growth of 6.0% plus RevPAR of 5.8% in Q3 lifted fee revenue 9.5%, and the 543,100-room pipeline — 39.2% of the current system, nearly half already under construction — makes that growth contractually visible for years. Management raised the 2026 RevPAR guide to +4.0%–4.5%. Layer on about $3.5B of guided 2026 capital return, roughly 4.4% of market cap, and per-share earnings grow faster than the business itself.

Bear Case

At 48.30× trailing and 39.09× forward earnings, HLT is priced for uninterrupted growth, a 17% P/E premium to Marriott. Fourth-quarter RevPAR growth is guided to just +3.0%–3.5% after a World Cup-boosted Q3, occupancy at 75.8% leaves little headroom, and future growth must come from rate, which is macro-dependent. Q3 revenue missed press-reported estimates, and Q3 approvals fell to 30,500 rooms from 42,900. Meanwhile $13.5B of debt against $830M of cash, a 0.59 current ratio and negative $6.56B of equity leave no balance-sheet cushion if a travel shock arrives.

01
A fall in the price investors pay per dollar of earnings, not earnings failure, is the primary risk. The stock fell from $350.22 on June 16 to $302.54 on September 17 — 13.6% — without any deterioration in reported results. At 48× earnings, a shift in how the market prices lodging growth costs more than a bad quarter would.
02
Net unit growth decelerating because developers cannot finance hotels. Hilton's customer is the hotel owner. Expensive construction debt kills new-build economics, and the damage appears in approvals (30,500 rooms in Q3 2026, against 42,900 in Q2) years before it shows in fee revenue — meaning current earnings tell you nothing about it.
03
Rate-dependence in a soft pricing environment. With occupancy at 75.8% and roughly two-thirds of RevPAR growth coming from ADR, any loss of nominal pricing power flows almost directly to the fee line. Franchise royalties are a percentage of the owner's room revenue — if rates flatten, Hilton's revenue flattens with them, with no volume offset available.
  Industry Commentary
Development Financing Is the Binding Constraint
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Hilton does not build hotels — thousands of independent developers do, using construction debt. The company's growth depends on the numbers on those projects working for their developers. The buffer today is unusually good: a 543,100-room pipeline equal to 39.2% of the existing system, with nearly half already under construction, which means several years of openings are effectively locked in regardless of what happens to financing next. The vulnerability is on the other side of that buffer — the 30,500 rooms approved in Q3 2026 are what determines openings in 2029. A sustained slowdown in approvals would be invisible in earnings for years and then very hard to reverse.
RevPAR Is Running Hot in 2026, With a Slower Q4 Guided
WATCH ITEM
Nine-month 2026 RevPAR is +4.7% and Q3 was +5.8%, helped, per the CFO on the call (as reported by Hotel Dive), by business and group demand, momentum after the World Cup and favourable calendar holiday shifts. Full-year guidance of +4.0% to +4.5% against Q4 guidance of +3.0% to +3.5% means growth slows, and at 75.8% occupancy there is little room left to grow by filling hotels. Hilton's above-industry earnings growth has to come from the two levers that are not RevPAR: 6.0% net unit growth and about $3.5B of annual buyback. That is a sound algorithm, but the 48× multiple is paying for it already.
Bull vs Bear — What It Comes Down To
EMERGING TAILWIND
The bull and bear cases here are not really disagreeing about Hilton's business quality — an 18.74% ROIC, $1.93B of free cash flow and a pipeline worth 39.2% of the existing system are not in dispute. They are disagreeing about the price. The bull says a contractually visible 6% unit growth engine, an international mix shift, and co-brand card fees that depend less on travel justify 48× and that owning the toll booth beats owning the road. The bear says you are paying a 17% premium to Marriott's P/E into Q4 RevPAR guidance of 3.0%–3.5%, with a balance sheet carrying $12.6B of net debt and no equity cushion. The next test arrives with Q4 results in early 2027: if net unit growth holds at 6%+ and approvals recover toward 40,000 rooms a quarter, the premium is defensible. If either slips, there is a long way down to Marriott's multiple.
