01 Company Overview

Honeywell International Inc. — now operating under the Honeywell Technologies brand — is a pure-play industrial automation company following the completion of a three-way corporate breakup. Solstice Advanced Materials was spun off in late 2025, and Honeywell Aerospace was spun off on June 29, 2026 (now trading separately as HONA, ~$51.76B market cap at the October 23 close), accompanied by a 1-for-2 reverse stock split of HON shares. What remains is a roughly $20 billion revenue automation business built around three segments — Building Automation, Industrial Automation, and Process Automation & Technology. Its first quarter as a standalone company, reported October 22, 2026, brought 7% organic sales growth (growth excluding acquisitions, divestitures and currency) and adjusted EPS up 34%, and management raised its 2026 guidance to $20.0–$20.1B of sales on 4–5% organic growth, with a backlog of nearly $19 billion.

The reported numbers are finally getting cleaner. The Q3 2026 release is the first to show Aerospace as a discontinued operation: sales from continuing operations were $4.99B in the quarter (up 1% reported, held back by the sale of the PSS and WWS businesses) and $15.00B for the first nine months, up 2.0%. Trailing-twelve-month GAAP earnings still carry Aerospace through June and the large non-cash gain booked on its spin-off, so backward-looking ratios on HON remain a hybrid of a company that no longer exists. The forward view is the one to use: management now guides 2026 adjusted EPS of $8.40–$8.45, up 30–31%, while the analyst consensus on StockAnalysis (read October 24, 2026) shows $20.15B of 2026 revenue and $8.30 of 2026 EPS, rising to $9.82 in 2027. At $70.09B, Honeywell's market value is far smaller than before the breakup, largely because two businesses were handed to shareholders: the Aerospace shares alone were worth $51.76B at the October 23 close.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Installed Base Lock-In Safety Certification Barriers Process Technology IP & Licensing ~$19B Backlog Forge Software Layer Global Service Network Switching Cost Economics

Honeywell's moat is the least glamorous and most durable kind: things that are expensive and dangerous to replace. A distributed control system running an operating refinery cannot be swapped out without shutting the plant down; a fire and life-safety system in a hospital carries certifications, code approvals and liability that make re-specification a multi-year project. That produces an installed base that generates aftermarket parts, service contracts and software attach at margins well above the original equipment sale — visible in BA's 27.0% Q3 segment margin and PA&T's 22.2%. Process Technology adds a second moat type: licensed process designs and proprietary catalysts, where Honeywell earns royalties on plants it never operates and, through the July 2026 Johnson Matthey Catalyst Technologies acquisition, has consolidated a scarce technology asset. The backlog of nearly $19B, up 7% year over year excluding divestitures, converts these advantages into forward revenue visibility that pure short-cycle industrials do not have. What is genuinely new is that the automation company now competes for capital, talent and investor attention on its own terms rather than as the smaller sibling of an aerospace franchise — the entire premise of the breakup.

Key Vulnerabilities
Scale disadvantage versus European majors. At roughly $20B of revenue, Honeywell Technologies is now materially smaller than ABB ($35.75B TTM revenue, growing 18.6%) and Siemens' industrial businesses. Those competitors fund R&D and electrification capacity from a broader base, and ABB in particular is compounding revenue at more than double Honeywell's guided 4–5% organic rate. Scale matters most in the sensing and drives businesses where IA competes.
Process Technology cyclicality and catalyst volume risk. PA&T sales grew 8% organically in Q3 2026 on LNG and petrochemical projects, but segment margin fell 210 bps on project mix and the dilutive catalyst acquisition, after a 180 bps fall in Q2 blamed on catalyst volumes. Refining and petrochemical capital cycles are lumpy and politically exposed, and conversion of orders to revenue in long-cycle process work runs quarters, not weeks, and can slip.
Software competition from asset-light rivals. The value in automation is migrating up the stack toward the data layer, where Honeywell Forge competes against Schneider's AVEVA, Siemens' Xcelerator, Rockwell's PlantPAx/FactoryTalk and a long tail of cloud-native industrial software. Software multiples reward recurring revenue disclosure that Honeywell does not break out; if the market cannot see software attach rates, it will not pay for them.
Separation and portfolio-churn execution risk. Three entities were carved out of one balance sheet in under a year, alongside a 1-for-2 reverse split, the PSS and WWS divestitures closing in the third quarter of 2026, and the Johnson Matthey integration starting in July 2026. Stranded costs (overheads left behind when a business is spun off) and transition service agreements are the live risks. The first standalone quarter helped: the release credits part of the 79% rise in operating income to eliminating stranded costs, and Kapur said on the call he now expects all remaining stranded costs gone by the end of 2026 — one clean quarter is still a short record.

Notable One-Off Items

This is one of the most distorted reported-earnings pictures in the S&P 500 right now, and essentially every trailing metric requires an asterisk.

1. The Aerospace spin gain. Q2 2026 consolidated GAAP EPS was $17.83 against adjusted EPS of $4.52; for Honeywell Technologies standalone, GAAP EPS of $16.65 versus adjusted $1.95. The $13–15 per share gap is non-cash separation accounting. It is what produces the 9.46 trailing P/E and the 44.4% ROE. None of it recurs. In Q3 2026 the gap ran the other way and was small: GAAP EPS from continuing operations of $2.40 against adjusted $2.27, with a $0.67 gain on the sale of a business and a $0.35 equity loss from Quantinuum among the adjustments.

2. 1-for-2 reverse stock split. Executed alongside the June 29, 2026 spin. Share count fell to 316.94M and all historical per-share figures have been restated ×2 by data providers. Any per-share comparison to pre-June-2026 published figures is invalid without adjustment.

3. Fiscal 2025 impairments. The Q2 2026 reconciliation discloses $724M of goodwill impairment in the 2025 base period, alongside impairments of assets held for sale, Quantinuum-related losses and divestiture costs. FY2025 net income was depressed accordingly, which mechanically inflates 2026 growth optics.

4. Portfolio churn in 2026. Solstice Advanced Materials was spun off on October 30, 2025. The PSS and WWS divestitures closed in the third quarter; without them, Industrial Automation’s Q3 sales fell from $1,450M to $1,115M even as its organic sales grew 4%. The Johnson Matthey Catalyst Technologies acquisition closed July 17, 2026. The Q3 release restates prior periods for continuing operations only, so Q3 2025 sales now read $4,929M rather than the consolidated figure published a year ago.

5. Legacy liabilities. Honeywell has historically carried significant asbestos-related (Bendix, NARCO) and environmental remediation liabilities. The Q3 2025 comparison period carried a $0.35-a-share loss on an asbestos settlement. Readers should check the latest Form 10-Q and the separation agreements for how these obligations are allocated among Honeywell Technologies, Honeywell Aerospace and Solstice before sizing this exposure.

Five-year trajectory. On the actual-close series, HON closed at $218.62 at the end of October 2021 and $221.15 on October 23, 2026 — a +1.2% price return over five years, roughly 0.2% a year, before dividends and before the value of the Solstice and Aerospace shares holders received. The S&P 500 (SPY) rose 65.5% in price over the same window (October 29, 2021 close to October 23, 2026 close). Even allowing for the spun-off shares, that is a poor five years in absolute terms for a Dow-component industrial, and it is precisely the underperformance that gave Elliott Management its opening in late 2024. The path was not a slow drift: the stock bottomed at $166.96 in September 2022 during the rate shock, spent all of 2023 dead money between $183 and $210, and only broke out once the breakup narrative took hold in late 2024.

The breakup trade. The clearest structural feature of the chart is the November 2024 gap — $205.68 to $232.93, +13.3% in a month — coinciding with Elliott's disclosure of a >$5B stake and public demand for separation. Notably, the stock then gave back most of it: by April 2025 it was at $210.50 — down 9.6% from the November peak and only 2.3% above the pre-Elliott level of $205.68, effectively retracing nearly the entire pop. The market bought the announcement and sold the execution. The genuine re-rating came much later — from $195.09 in December 2025 to $243.59 by February 2026 (+24.9% in two months) — as the Aerospace spin moved from plan to imminent reality.

Relative performance. From October 23, 2025 to October 23, 2026 HON's price rose +1.4% against +13.1% for the S&P 500 (SPY) and +15.4% for the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) — about 12 percentage points of underperformance against the broad market, with the Aerospace shares handed out in June not counted. HON trades at 23.3x forward earnings (StockAnalysis), below the automation peer median of 28.0x (peer figures as of August 2026). The bull reading is that a post-breakup automation pure-play guiding 30–31% adjusted EPS growth should not trade at a discount to its peers. The bear reading is that the market has already collected the breakup premium in the separate companies and is now valuing what remains on its organic growth rate — 4–5% guided for 2026, after 7% in Q3.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The breakup is done and the first clean quarter delivered: a $20B automation pure-play with a backlog of nearly $19B, 7% organic growth in Q3, adjusted EPS up 34%, and 2026 segment margin now guided to ~20.5%, up about 290 bps. Building Automation grew 8% organically, data center orders more than doubled, and stranded costs from the spin-offs are guided to be gone by the end of 2026. At 23.3x forward earnings and a 1.50 PEG, HON trades below the automation peer median (28.0x forward, 2.08 PEG) despite guiding 30–31% adjusted EPS growth, and the Street's average target of $254.91 implies 15.3% upside with the top of the range at $303.

Bear Case

Strip out the accounting noise and the underlying business is guided to grow 4–5% organically in 2026 — slower than ABB (+18.6%) or Rockwell (+11.3%) on their latest reported revenue growth — and Q3 book-to-bill was only 1.0, so orders merely kept pace with sales. Process margin fell 210 bps on project mix and the dilutive catalyst deal. Gross margin of 40.4% in Q3 is still about 9 points below Rockwell's. Free cash flow of ~$2.1B on a $70.09B market cap is a 3.0% yield, and it must fund a $2.80 dividend, the push to cut gross leverage below 3x (debt-to-equity 0.73) and the Johnson Matthey integration at once. The stock trails the S&P 500 by 12 points over twelve months, and nine Holds plus a Sell on StockAnalysis's panel suggest much of the sum-of-the-parts value has been collected.

Top 3 Risks
01
Trailing numbers stay distorted until mid-2027. Every trailing metric on HON today — a 9.46 P/E, a 44.4% ROE — still includes the Aerospace spin gain booked in Q2 2026 and Aerospace's profits through June. Q3 2026 was the first quarter reported on a clean basis, and the spin quarter leaves the trailing twelve months only after Q2 2027 is reported. Until then, screens that rank on trailing earnings misread the stock, and the standalone cost base has one quarter of history.
02
Leverage still constrains the equity story. Debt fell sharply with the spin — $16.59B at September 30 against $34.57B at the end of 2025, for a debt-to-equity of 0.73, close to the 0.70 peer median — but net debt of about $10.4B remains. With ~$2.1B of guided free cash flow supporting a $2.80 dividend (about $0.89B a year on 316.94M shares), deleveraging, and the Johnson Matthey integration, there is limited room for the buyback-driven EPS support that peers use. A rate back-up or a credit-rating action would hit both the multiple and the capital-return capacity.
03
Order-to-revenue conversion may disappoint. The bull case rests on backlog: nearly $19B, up 7%, with data center orders more than doubling in Q3. But Q3 book-to-bill was 1.0, Process margin has fallen two quarters running, and management guides Q4 organic growth of 5–6%. Long-cycle projects slip. If Q4 or 2027 conversion misses, the raised $8.40–$8.45 EPS guide and the $9.82 consensus for 2027 are at risk and the discounted forward multiple stops looking like a bargain.
  Industry Commentary
Conglomerate Breakup Value Unlock — The Verdict Is Now Measurable
EMERGING TAILWIND
Honeywell is the largest completed test of the industrial-conglomerate breakup thesis since GE, and unlike GE the scorecard is already readable. As of October 23, 2026 the two listed pieces are worth $70.09B (HON) + $51.76B (HONA) = $121.9B, plus separately listed Solstice Advanced Materials. Set against the combined company's 2024 peak, the headline arithmetic is not obviously a win, but that comparison ignores nearly two years of dividends and the fact that the combined company's share price fell 5.9% between the end of October 2021 and the end of October 2024, before Elliott arrived. The more useful evidence is behavioural: the stock re-rated +24.9% from December 2025 to February 2026 as the Aerospace spin became imminent, then, with the spin done, rallied on the Q2 print and slid 19.1% from its August 4 close to September 14. The pattern fits a market that pays for the anticipation of a separation and then prices each piece on its own results. HON is now in that second phase, which is where genuine multiple expansion — or a permanent de-rating to a mid-single-digit grower's multiple — actually gets decided; the first clean quarter, with 7% organic growth, was a point for the bulls.
Aerospace Aftermarket Is No Longer Honeywell's Story
ONGOING HEADWIND
This is the structural change most likely to be missed by anyone reading a pre-June-2026 note on HON. The commercial aerospace aftermarket cycle — high-margin spares and repairs on an aging global fleet, the single best industrial end market of the post-pandemic period — now belongs to HONA, which carries $17.92B of TTM revenue and a $51.76B market cap at the October 23 close. Honeywell Technologies retains none of it. The consequence is a loss of cyclical ballast: aerospace aftermarket revenue is famously resilient because aircraft must be maintained regardless of macro conditions, and it was the segment carrying the company through the 2022–2023 dead-money period. Without it, HON's earnings are more directly exposed to construction, industrial capex and refining project cycles. Anyone modelling HON on historical earnings volatility is using a beta (0.89) and a cyclicality assumption calibrated to a company that included Aerospace for the entire lookback window.
Industrial Automation Demand: Data Centers Are Doing the Heavy Lifting
BULL vs BEAR — THE DECIDING VARIABLE
The bull framing: data center orders more than doubled in Q3 2026, Process project sales grew 14% on LNG and petrochemical work, and organic sales growth reached 7% — above the three-year target set at the June Investor Day, in Kapur's words in the Q3 release. Honeywell has direct exposure to the AI infrastructure buildout through fire suppression, building management, power and thermal controls in hyperscale facilities — a market where safety certification and installed-base relationships matter more than price. ABB's 18.6% revenue growth and Rockwell's 11.3% confirm the automation cycle is genuinely accelerating, not company-specific noise.

The bear framing: Q2's 16% order growth was followed by a Q3 book-to-bill of only 1.0, and the full-year organic guide is 4–5%. That pattern is either lumpy timing that resolves into a strong 2027 — or evidence that a meaningful share of these orders are long-dated, cancellable, or concentrated in a data center capex cycle that has historically inflected hard and without warning. Honeywell also enters this cycle at a scale disadvantage: at ~$20B of revenue it is roughly 56% the size of ABB, with a 40.4% Q3 gross margin against Rockwell's 49.1%. If the automation cycle is peaking rather than accelerating, HON is the least-defended participant in it.

What settles it: Q3 answered the first question with 7% organic growth. The next test is the Q4 print and the first full-year guide for 2027: whether orders that only match shipments can keep revenue growing 5% or more.
