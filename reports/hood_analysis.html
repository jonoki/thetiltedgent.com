01 Company Overview

Robinhood Markets operates a mobile-first brokerage and financial-services platform that lets US and (increasingly) international retail customers trade equities, options, cryptocurrencies and — since 2025 — exchange-listed event contracts, alongside a growing set of banking-adjacent products. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $4,473M, up 51.6% year over year, with net income of $1,883M and EPS of $2.05; on a TTM basis revenue is $4.93B and EPS $2.26. At a $94.23B market capitalisation it ranks roughly 264th most valuable listed company in the world.

This report belongs in a series about gambling for a simple, structural reason: Robinhood's revenue is, with unusual directness, a function of how much the public is speculating. That is not a rhetorical flourish — it is visible in the revenue mix. In the most recent reported quarter (Q2 2026), options, crypto and event contracts together produced $598M of the $776M of transaction-based revenue (77.1%), or 45.6% of total net revenues. Equities — the thing most people think a brokerage does — produced $129M, 9.8% of the total, on $956B of notional volume. The business is levered to the most speculative activity on the platform, not to investing.

Revenue per dollar traded is where it becomes undeniable. Using the company's own Q2 2026 disclosures: equities produced $129M on $956B of notional volume (the dollar value of shares changing hands) — about $0.135 of revenue per $1,000 traded, roughly 1.35 basis points. The conclusion: a dollar of speculative activity is worth many times a dollar of investing activity to this company.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
28.4M FUNDED CUSTOMERS $369B PLATFORM ASSETS 4.8M GOLD SUBSCRIBERS LOW-COST OPERATING MODEL MULTI-LICENCE STACK PRODUCT VELOCITY ORDER-FLOW SCALE

The moat is distribution and cost, not technology. Robinhood serves 28.4M funded customers (accounts that have actually been funded with money, as opposed to registered sign-ups) with 2,900 employees — roughly 9,800 funded customers per head — against a TTM gross margin of 91.9% and an operating margin of 46.0%. That cost base is what allows the company to give away the commission and still earn a 42% net margin. On top of it sits a licence stack (broker-dealer, clearing, crypto, banking-adjacent, and now a CFTC-regulated event-contract venue) that is genuinely slow and expensive to replicate, and an order-flow franchise whose value to market makers rises with its own scale: more retail flow is worth more per share to the firm buying it, which is a real, if uncomfortable, network effect.

Payment for order flow — the mechanism, honestly

This is the most misunderstood arrangement in retail broking, so it is worth being precise. When you press "buy" in the app, Robinhood does not send your order to a public exchange. It routes it to a wholesale market maker — Citadel Securities, Virtu, Susquehanna and a handful of others — and the market maker pays Robinhood for the right to fill it. That payment is payment for order flow (PFOF), and it is the mechanical reason your commission is zero. You are not the customer of the trade; you are the product being sold into it.

Why would a market maker pay for your order? Because retail orders are, in aggregate, uninformed — they are not the orders of a hedge fund that knows something. A market maker can fill uninformed flow inside the public bid-offer spread and still capture a reliable slice of that spread, with far less risk of being run over than when trading against institutions on a lit exchange. The customer usually does receive price improvement versus the displayed quote — brokers report this and it is real — but "better than the displayed quote" is not the same as "the best price obtainable." The cost to the customer sits in the execution price, not on the statement. It is a fraction of a cent per share, invisible, and paid on every trade. Zero commission is not free; it is a commission that has been moved somewhere the customer cannot see it and cannot itemise.

Three consequences follow. First, the arrangement is most lucrative in options, where per-contract rebates dwarf per-share equity rebates — which is precisely why the revenue mix leans where it leans. Second, it creates a structural incentive to increase trading frequency, because the broker is paid per order rather than per dollar of assets. Third — and this is the regulatory exposure — PFOF has been prohibited in some jurisdictions, including the United Kingdom, and European rules have moved to restrict or ban it. ⚠ The specific jurisdictional status was not verified from a fetched source in this build; treat the jurisdictional claim as directional and verify before relying on it. What is verified is the exposure: transaction-based revenue is 59.2% of the total, and PFOF is its primary mechanism.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Regulatory risk to the routing model. Any US rule that mandates order-by-order auctions, caps rebates, or bans PFOF outright would attack 59.2% of revenue at its source. The company would not go to zero — it would have to charge commissions or widen other monetisation — but the transition would be violent and the margin structure that supports a 46× multiple would be reset.

2. Event contracts sit on contested regulatory ground. $156M of quarterly revenue, growing more than tenfold, now depends on a product category that state gaming regulators and federal commodities regulators have not fully settled between them. This is the highest-growth line and simultaneously the one with the least legal certainty. A single adverse ruling could remove roughly 12% of revenue and the entire growth narrative attached to it.

3. The cycle itself. Crypto revenue fell 38% YoY in Q2 2026 while everything else grew — a live demonstration that speculative revenue lines do not decay gently. A genuinely quiet market (low volatility, falling retail engagement, no meme cycle, no crypto bid) would compress options, crypto and event contracts simultaneously, because they are driven by the same underlying variable: retail appetite for risk.

4. Rate sensitivity. Net interest revenue of $389M per quarter (~$1.56B annualised) is earned on a $21.6B margin book and $18.7B of cash and deposits. Falling policy rates compress that spread mechanically. As an illustration only: a 100bp reduction in the rate earned on ~$40B of interest-earning balances, with no offsetting change in what is paid out, is on the order of $400M of annualised revenue — roughly 8% of the TTM top line. Actual sensitivity depends on the pass-through to customer cash, which was not verified in this build.

Notable One-Off Items

No specific non-recurring item — write-down, litigation charge, acquisition accounting entry or discrete tax item — was verified from a fetched source in this build, so none is asserted. Two distortions are nonetheless visible in the verified numbers and should be flagged. First, the profitability step-change is recent and large: the company lost $3,687M in FY2021, $1,028M in FY2022 and $541M in FY2023 before earning $1,411M in FY2024 and $1,883M in FY2025. A swing of that shape across a three-year window frequently involves deferred-tax valuation-allowance releases and the roll-off of IPO-era share-based compensation, both of which flatter reported net margin relative to underlying economics. Verify against the 10-K tax footnote before treating a 42% net margin as a steady-state figure. Second, TTM free cash flow of $219M against roughly $2.07B of TTM net income is a definitional artefact of a broker's balance sheet (customer payables, receivables and segregated cash move through operating cash flow), not evidence of poor earnings quality — but it does mean FCF-based valuation on this name is close to meaningless without adjustment.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY — ⚠ STATIC AS OF AUGUST 31, 2026
In plain English: RSI compares the size of recent up-months to recent down-months. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a forecast. Computed on the full 62-month series, it prints 56 values from January 2022 onward — including a pegged 100 in September 2025 (six consecutive up-months) and a pegged 0 in February 2022 (six consecutive down-months). Those extremes are what a six-month window does to a stock this volatile; they are descriptive, not signals.

Trajectory since listing. The full monthly series makes the shape of this stock unmistakable, and it is not a line — it is a V with a very long left arm. HOOD's first month-end close, July 30, 2021, was $35.15, already below the $38.00 IPO price. It peaked one month later at $44.32 (August 2021) and then spent the next sixteen months losing 81.6% of its value, bottoming at a monthly close of $8.14 in December 2022. It closed below its IPO price in 40 of the 62 months in this series and did not reclaim $38.00 on a month-end basis until January 2025 — three and a half years after listing. From that December 2022 trough to the October 2025 peak close of $146.78, the stock rose 18-fold (+1,703%).

Total return and CAGR. From the first monthly close of $35.15 to the August 31, 2026 close of $104.81 is +198.2% over 5.08 years — a compound annual growth rate of 24.0%. Measured instead from the $38.00 IPO price, an investor who bought the offering is up +175.8%, a 22.1% CAGR. Both are excellent numbers, and both are close to meaningless as a description of the experience: the same position was down more than 80% at the end of 2022. Shorter windows tell entirely different stories — +1,188% from the end of 2022, +181% from the end of 2024, +203% across calendar 2025, and −7.3% year-to-date in 2026. Over the trailing twelve months the stock has gone essentially nowhere: +0.7%, from $104.03 at end-August 2025 to $104.81 now, having travelled to $146.78 and back down to $69.30 in between. Monthly returns have a standard deviation of 21.1% — roughly 73% annualised — with a worst month of −31.5% (December 2021) and a best of +59.8% (November 2024). Thirty of the 61 monthly returns were negative. The 52-week range of $63.52 – $153.86 spans a factor of 2.4×, consistent with a beta of 2.32.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Robinhood has converted a single-product trading app into a genuine platform: 28.4M funded customers, $369B of platform assets (+32%), $21.7B of net deposits in one quarter, 4.8M Gold subscribers (+39%), a credit card past one million customers and international funded accounts past one million. Revenue grew 32% YoY in Q2 2026 with a 46.0% operating margin and 42.0% net margin — operating leverage that few financial businesses possess. Event contracts went from nothing to $156M a quarter, showing the company can create a new revenue line from a standing start. Consensus sees $6.50B of revenue and $3.28 of EPS in FY2027.

Bear Case

Strip the framing away and 45.6% of total revenue comes from options, crypto and event contracts — activity that exists because retail customers want to speculate. Crypto revenue already fell 38% YoY, demonstrating how fast these lines reverse. Another 29.7% is interest income that shrinks as rates fall. At 46.4× trailing and 44.3× forward earnings with a PEG of 2.37, the market is paying a durable-compounder multiple for a business whose largest inputs are volatility and retail risk appetite. The July 2026 reaction — a −3.6% session on a +32% revenue quarter — is the tell: the bar is set where it becomes hard to clear.

Regulatory attack on payment for order flow. PFOF is the mechanism behind 59.2% of revenue and is already prohibited or restricted in some jurisdictions. A US rule change would not end the business but would force a monetisation model the current multiple does not contemplate.
02
A quiet market. The revenue mix is levered to speculation. A period of low volatility, no crypto bid and flat retail engagement would compress options, crypto and event contracts at once — they share one underlying driver. At 46× earnings there is no valuation cushion for a soft year.
03
Falling rates plus a shrinking margin book. Net interest revenue is ~30% of the top line, earned on ~$40B of interest-earning balances. Rate cuts compress it directly; a market drawdown shrinks the $21.6B margin book at exactly the same time transaction revenue is falling. The two exposures are correlated, not diversifying.
  Industry Commentary
The retail speculation cycle is the business
ONGOING HEADWIND
Options ($342M, +29%), event contracts ($156M, >10×) and crypto ($100M, −38%) together made 77.1% of transaction revenue in Q2 2026. The divergence inside that group is the warning: within a single quarter one speculative line grew more than tenfold while another shrank by more than a third. The aggregate held up because the mix rotated — retail money moved from crypto into prediction markets. Rotation is fine while there is somewhere for it to rotate to. The risk is a cycle where all of it goes quiet together, which is what a genuine bear market in risk appetite looks like.
The hidden rate trade inside the trading app
CRITICAL VARIABLE
$389M of quarterly net interest revenue — 29.7% of the total, growing only 9% YoY versus 44% for transactions — is earned on a $21.6B margin book (+127%) and $18.7B of cash and deposits (+34%). Balances grew far faster than the revenue they produced, which is what spread compression looks like. Most retail investors buying HOOD think they are buying a trading platform; roughly three-tenths of what they are buying is a rate-sensitive lender. As an illustration only, 100bp off the yield on ~$40B of balances is on the order of $400M of annualised revenue, about 8% of the TTM top line.
Recurring revenue is the whole bull/bear argument
EMERGING TAILWIND
Gold subscriptions, retirement ($34.5B of retirement assets, +82%), the Gold Card (past 1M customers) and international expansion (past 1M funded customers) are all attempts to convert a cyclical trading business into recurring revenue that survives a quiet market. Gold is at 4.8M subscribers — 16.9% of the 28.4M funded base — and "Other revenues" reached $143M, +54% YoY. Bull framing: at that growth rate the subscription and interest base eventually covers the fixed cost of the platform, so trading revenue becomes upside rather than survival, and a 46× multiple starts to look like a subscription multiple. Bear framing: "other revenues" are still only 10.9% of the total, and 45.6% of revenue still depends on someone deciding to place a speculative bet this quarter. The multiple is already priced as though the conversion has happened. It has not.
