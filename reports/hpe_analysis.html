01 Company Overview

Hewlett Packard Enterprise sells the plumbing of corporate computing: servers, storage, enterprise networking gear and the financing to pay for it. Fiscal 2025 (year to 31 October 2025) revenue was $34.3B, up 13.8% — but the company posted a net loss of $59M that year. The trailing twelve months through July 2026 tell a different story entirely: $41.87B of revenue, up 26.6%, and $2.68B of net income. HPE ranks roughly #373 globally by market capitalisation at $69.03B, and the stock is up 127.9% over the past 52 weeks.

Two things happened at once, and the central task of this page is to keep them apart. The $14B Juniper Networks acquisition closed in July 2025 after HPE settled a Department of Justice antitrust suit, roughly doubling the networking business and importing a genuinely high-margin product line. Simultaneously, HPE became a large seller of AI systems — a business that adds revenue at a lower gross margin than the company average. So the headline gross-margin expansion of 1,090 basis points in Q3 FY2026 is not the AI business working; it is the Juniper mix arriving faster than the AI mix dilutes it. Growth in AI orders, on this page, is reported separately from the margin story on purpose — because more of it makes the margin line look worse, not better.

AI systems are not a reportable segment — they live inside the Server line of Cloud & AI — but they drive the entire debate, so they are broken out here. Q3 FY2026: revenue of nearly $1.6B, orders of $2.4B (up more than 30% sequentially), $6.7B of year-to-date AI orders — a record — and a total AI backlog of $7.6B, of which $6.8B is AI systems, up 14% sequentially. The one number to watch: AI systems revenue ÷ total revenue. At $1.6B of $12.2B it is roughly 13%. If the $6.8B backlog converts while Networking growth normalises toward its +10% organic rate, the reported gross margin falls even if nothing goes wrong operationally.

HPE Financial Services leases and finances IT equipment for customers. It is a lending book, and lending books are funded with debt by design. This matters more than its 7% revenue share suggests, because it contaminates every leverage ratio computed off the consolidated balance sheet. HPE reports $20.32B of total debt against $6.22B of cash and a consolidated debt-to-equity of 0.76. Printing that as "operating leverage" is wrong — a meaningful share of those borrowings is matched against a lease receivable portfolio and is not a claim on the server business's cash flows. Management's own preferred figure is net leverage of 1.8× at Q3, below its stated 2× ceiling. Corporate Investments & Other — largely HPE Labs and the software incubations — runs a −24.1% operating margin on $278M of revenue; it is a rounding error on profit and a real one on culture. Separately, HPE completed the divestiture of its H3C stake on 28 May 2026 for $1.357B, removing a long-standing China exposure and funding deleveraging.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
#2 US Enterprise WLAN Juniper Mist AI Ops Installed Base + Captive Financing GreenLake 52,000 Customers Sovereign & Government Access Full-Stack Compute + Network $7.6B Order Backlog

The core advantage is not technological, it is positional. HPE is one of very few vendors that can quote a customer a GPU cluster, the switching and routing fabric around it, the storage behind it, the operational software above it and the lease that pays for it, on one contract, with one support relationship. Dell can match the compute and the financing but not the networking depth; Cisco and Arista can match the networking but not the server scale; Supermicro can match the AI rack and nothing else. The Juniper acquisition is what converted this from a slide into a fact — routing revenue of $788M and data-centre networking of $382M in a single quarter are lines HPE simply did not have two years ago. Layered on top, GreenLake (HPE's consumption-based "as-a-service" platform, where customers pay for capacity used rather than buying kit outright) reached 52,000 customers, up 18% year over year, with Private Cloud AI orders growing at triple-digit rates. Note: HPE has historically published a GreenLake ARR figure; no ARR number appeared on any source fetched for this report, and none has been estimated here. The customer count is the verified proxy.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The DOJ settlement armed a competitor. To close Juniper, HPE agreed on 27–28 June 2025 to divest its global Instant On campus and branch WLAN business within 180 days to a DOJ-approved buyer, and to run an auction licensing Juniper's AI Ops for Mist source code on a perpetual, non-exclusive basis, with optional transitional support and personnel transfers. HPE paid $14B for Juniper and then handed a rival a permanent licence to the software layer that made Mist valuable. Whoever bought that licence competes with HPE using HPE's own code, forever.
02
AI revenue dilutes the margin it is credited with improving. Management stated that AI Systems carry lower gross margins than traditional hardware and that this will pressure profitability as AI grows. Non-GAAP operating margin of 16.2% in Q3 was up 770bps and is flagged by management as potentially unsustainable. The market is currently paying a re-rating multiple for a revenue mix shift that mechanically compresses margin.
03
Component supply is the governor. HBM and DDR5 constraints are limiting order-to-revenue conversion and are expected to persist into 2027. Backlog growing faster than revenue looks bullish on a slide and is a working-capital and cancellation risk in practice — an order is not a sale, and a 12-month-old AI order in a market repricing GPUs every two quarters is a negotiation waiting to happen.
04
Customer concentration in AI. A single post-quarter deal — $3.5B of inferencing with one hyperscaler — is roughly half the entire AI systems backlog and about 7.6% of consensus FY2026 revenue. Concentration of that magnitude in the fastest-growing, lowest-margin line means one customer's capex decision moves the company's revenue line.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $14.65 to the September 4, 2026 close of $52.00, HPE returned +254.9% on price alone — a compound annual rate of about 28.8% before dividends, which added roughly another point a year at the current yield. But the shape is everything. For the first three and a half years of that window the stock did essentially nothing: it closed at $14.65 in October 2021 and $15.43 in March 2025, a total move of +5.3% over forty-one months, with a trough at $11.98 in September 2022. Every dollar of the return was made after March 2025, and most of it after March 2026 — the stock went from $23.81 at end-March 2026 to $52.00 today, +118% in five months. Anyone who owned HPE as a cheap, dull, dividend-paying hardware company for four years was right about the dullness and then handed a 128% twelve-month return by a business that changed underneath them.

Relative performance. Against its own peer set the picture is more sober. Over the trailing year HPE's +132.6% total return sits behind Dell's +328.0% and ahead of Cisco (+64.7%), Arista (+41.1%) and Supermicro (−1.3%). So HPE has been a strong second in the AI-infrastructure trade, not the winner: the market has paid more for Dell's cleaner AI-server exposure than for HPE's networking-plus-AI blend. A precise S&P 500 and NASDAQ-100 total-return comparison over the identical five-year window could not be verified from the sources fetched and is deliberately not stated; what can be said is that a 1.44 beta and a 60-month path that goes nowhere for forty-one months and then triples is a materially different risk profile from either index, and that the −4.48% single-day move on 4 September, two days after a beat-and-raise, is the behaviour of a stock priced for perfection rather than for value.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Two businesses, opposite margin signs, one stock. Start at the top of the Q3 FY2026 income statement. Revenue $12,213M, up 33.7%. Gross profit $4,910M — a 40.2% gross margin, up roughly 1,090 basis points year over year. That is an extraordinary move for a hardware company, and the instinct is to credit it to AI. That instinct is wrong. AI systems produced $1.6B of revenue in the quarter at gross margins management describes as below the traditional hardware average. What lifted the margin was Networking: $2.9B of revenue at a 22.0% operating margin, including $788M of routing and $382M of data-centre networking that did not exist inside HPE two years ago. Juniper carried the margin; AI diluted it. Both grew, so the net was up.

Which means the growth and the margin now point in opposite directions. AI systems orders were $2.4B in Q3 against $1.6B of AI revenue — a book-to-bill of roughly 1.5× — with $6.7B of year-to-date orders and a $6.8B AI systems backlog inside a $7.6B total AI backlog, up 14% sequentially, plus a $3.5B hyperscaler inferencing win booked after quarter-end. Every one of those numbers is a reason to own the stock and a reason the gross margin falls. Meanwhile Networking, the margin engine, grew +10% normalised — respectable, unspectacular, and nowhere near the pace of AI. Run those two trends forward for four quarters and the mix shifts toward the low-margin line by construction. Management said as much: the 16.2% non-GAAP operating margin may not be sustainable as AI becomes a larger share of revenue. Anyone extrapolating Q3's margin into FY2027 is extrapolating the one thing management explicitly told them not to.

What Juniper actually bought, and what it cost. $14B, closed July 2025, and the price included more than cash. To get the DOJ to settle rather than try the case on 9 July 2025, HPE agreed to divest its entire global Instant On campus and branch WLAN business within 180 days to a DOJ-approved buyer, and to auction a perpetual, non-exclusive licence to Juniper's AI Ops for Mist source code, with optional transitional support and personnel transfers to make the licensee a viable competitor. The DOJ's own framing was that Cisco was #1, HPE #2 and Juniper #3 in US enterprise WLAN, and that the combination would leave Cisco and HPE holding over 70% of the market. So HPE bought the #3 player, sold a business, and permanently licensed the software that made the target differentiated. The strategic logic still holds — routing at +270% and data-centre networking at +112% are real, and Networks-for-AI orders of $2.2B cumulative are real — but the acquisition delivered a smaller competitive moat than the headline price suggests, and ROIC of 8.29% is the visible cost of that. The quantified return is $600M of annualised run-rate synergies by end-FY2028, stated as ahead of plan.

On leverage — read the right number. Consolidated debt-to-equity is 0.76 on $20.32B of total debt. Printing that as a statement about the operating business is a category error, because HPE Financial Services is a captive leasing and financing book — it originates lease receivables and funds them with matched borrowings, exactly as a bank or a captive auto-finance arm does. Consolidating it inflates both debt and assets without telling you anything about the servers-and-switches business. The figure to use is management's own: net leverage of 1.8×, against a stated 2× ceiling, on $14.1B of net debt and $4.16B of trailing free cash flow. On that basis HPE is moderately, not aggressively, levered — and the $1.357B of H3C proceeds in May 2026 helped get it there.

GreenLake and the missing ARR. The as-a-service platform is the piece of the story that would justify a software multiple, and it is the piece with the weakest disclosure on this page. Verified: 52,000 GreenLake customers, up 18% year over year, with Private Cloud AI orders growing at triple-digit rates. Not verified: the annualised revenue run-rate. HPE has historically published an ARR figure and a growth rate; none appeared on any source fetched for this report, and none has been estimated here. That is a genuine gap in the analysis, and it matters — customer count grows when you sign small accounts, whereas ARR tells you what they actually spend. Anyone underwriting the "HPE is becoming a subscription business" argument needs that number from the 10-K before, not after, taking the position.

Valuation — and the honest version of the cheapness. HPE trades at 26.5× trailing GAAP earnings and 11.4× forward earnings, with a PEG of 0.40. Every one of those looks cheap and every one of them is distorted. The trailing multiple is computed off a base that includes a recovery from FY2025's $59M net loss; the forward multiple assumes FY2026 non-GAAP EPS lands at $3.75–3.85 and FY2027 at $4.40–4.60, both of which embed margin assumptions management has flagged as fragile; and the PEG is arithmetically flattered by lapping a loss. Against the fetched peer median trailing P/E of roughly 31.7×, HPE is cheaper than Dell (30.6×), Cisco (32.8×) and Arista (61.3×), and dearer than Supermicro (12.2×) — a rank order that tells you the market is paying for margin quality, and placing HPE in the middle. The $4.16B of trailing free cash flow against an $83.13B enterprise value is about 20× EV/FCF; against the FY2027 framework of at least $5.0B it is about 17×. That is the least-manipulable version of the valuation, and it is neither cheap nor demanding. At $52.00 against a $67.78 average target and a $64.25 52-week high, the stock is priced for the AI backlog to convert and the margin not to fall much. Those two things are in tension by management's own description.

Bull Case

HPE is the only vendor that can sell the compute, the network fabric, the storage and the financing for an AI deployment on one contract, and the numbers say customers are buying it that way: $2.4B of AI systems orders against $1.6B of revenue, a $7.6B AI backlog up 14% sequentially, $2.2B of cumulative Networks-for-AI orders, and a $3.5B hyperscaler inferencing win booked after quarter-end. Juniper is delivering — routing +270%, data-centre networking +112%, 22% segment operating margin — with $600M of synergies running ahead of plan. FY2026 guidance was raised to 34–37% revenue growth and $3.75–3.85 of non-GAAP EPS; the FY2027 framework calls for 13–17% growth and at least $5.0B of free cash flow. At 11.4× forward earnings, net leverage of 1.8×, and a dividend covered seven times by free cash flow, none of that is priced as though it will happen.

Bear Case

The margin expansion the market re-rated on is a Juniper mix effect that has now largely lapped, and the fastest-growing part of the business dilutes it. Management said AI Systems carry lower gross margins and that 16.2% non-GAAP operating margin may not hold. Networking — the profit engine — grew only 10% normalised. The $14B Juniper price included a forced divestiture and a perpetual source-code licence handed to a competitor, and ROIC is still 8.29%. HBM and DDR5 shortages mean backlog is not revenue and may not be, at these prices, into 2027. Half the AI backlog is one customer. The stock has tripled off its low, sits 19% below a 52-week high it cannot reclaim on a beat-and-raise, has a beta of 1.44, and four brokers cut targets on the same print four raised on.

Top 3 Risks

01
Mix-driven margin compression. Growth in AI systems mechanically lowers gross margin. If AI revenue triples toward the backlog while Networking compounds at its normalised 10%, the blended gross margin falls even with flawless execution — and the forward multiple that makes the stock look cheap is built on margins holding.
02
Backlog that does not convert on today's terms. HBM and DDR5 constraints delay revenue into 2027; memory pricing raises the cost of the systems in that backlog; and AI hardware pricing resets roughly every two quarters. A large, aging, single-customer-concentrated backlog is a negotiating position for the buyer, not a guaranteed revenue schedule for the seller.
03
The Juniper give-back compounding. The Instant On divestiture removes a product line and the perpetual AI Ops for Mist licence puts Juniper's differentiating software into a competitor's hands permanently. If that licensee builds a credible WLAN alternative while Cisco defends from above and Arista attacks the data centre, the 22% Networking operating margin — the single most valuable number in this company — is the thing that erodes.
  Industry Commentary
Memory Is the New Bottleneck
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
For two years the binding constraint on AI infrastructure was GPU supply. It has moved. HPE's management named high-bandwidth memory and DDR5 as the limiter on converting orders into revenue, and expects the constraint to persist into 2027. That is why HPE's AI backlog rose 14% sequentially while AI revenue was $1.6B — the orders exist, the parts do not. It is a double hit: it delays revenue recognition and it raises the input cost of the systems in backlog, squeezing a line that already carries below-average gross margins. Every server OEM — HPE, Dell, Supermicro, Lenovo — is buying the same scarce components on the same terms, which means none of them can differentiate on supply and all of them compete on price at the customer.
Antitrust Now Prices Networking Deals
ONGOING HEADWIND
The HPE-Juniper settlement is now the template for enterprise networking consolidation, and its terms were unusually invasive. The DOJ did not merely require a divestiture — it required HPE to auction a perpetual, non-exclusive licence to the acquired company's AI Ops for Mist source code, with personnel transfers to make the licensee competitive. That is a remedy designed to manufacture a rival out of the acquirer's own intellectual property. Any future buyer of a networking asset now has to underwrite the possibility of paying full price and then arming a competitor with the crown jewels. It raises the effective cost of consolidation across the sector, and it means the remaining independent networking vendors are worth less to a strategic buyer than a pure market-share calculation would suggest.
Networks-for-AI: Where the Margin Actually Is
EMERGING TAILWIND
The industry has spent three years discovering that the GPU is the cheapest-to-differentiate part of an AI cluster and the network is the hardest. Arista trades at 61× earnings on $10.5B of revenue precisely because the market has worked this out. HPE's version is $2.2B of cumulative Networks-for-AI orders against a $2.5–3.0B FY2026 target, with data-centre networking up 112% and routing up 270%. Bull framing: if HPE can attach high-margin Juniper fabric to every low-margin AI rack it ships, the blended margin problem solves itself and the company earns a networking multiple rather than a server one. Bear framing: the attach is not yet visible in the blend — Networking grew 10% normalised while AI systems orders grew more than 30% sequentially. Until the fabric grows as fast as the compute, HPE is a server company with a good networking division, and the market prices those at Dell's multiple, not Arista's.
