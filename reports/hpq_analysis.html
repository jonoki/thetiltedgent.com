01 Company Overview

HP Inc. is the half of the old Hewlett-Packard garage that kept the name. In 2015 Hewlett-Packard Company split in two: the servers, storage and services business left as Hewlett Packard Enterprise, and the PCs-and-printers stub renamed itself HP Inc. and stayed in the S&P 500 under the historic HPQ ticker. Today it sells laptops, desktops and workstations (Personal Systems) and printers plus the ink and toner that feed them (Printing). FY2025 (year to October 31, 2025) revenue was $55.30B, up 3.24%, with diluted EPS of $2.65.

2026 has been a strange year for this stock, and the strangeness is the story. Revenue is accelerating — the fiscal third quarter (to July 31, 2026) grew 12.5% to $15.7B, the fastest in years — yet Personal Systems units fell 16% in the same quarter. HP is selling fewer PCs for a lot more money each, because the memory and storage chips inside them have become far more expensive and the cost is being pushed through to customers. Meanwhile CEO Enrique Lores stepped down on February 3, 2026 "to pursue another professional opportunity," and a board member runs the company on an interim basis. The stock closed January at $19.44 and February at $18.99 — its lowest month-end of the five-year window — and has since run to $32.95, up 73% from that low, on AI-PC enthusiasm and two guidance raises. The question at $32.95: is HP a business whose earnings power is genuinely improving, or one enjoying a price-driven revenue bump while the unit base shrinks underneath it? On September 21 the company answered part of that itself — it assumes industry PC units fall roughly mid-single digits in calendar 2027 — and the stock fell 4.22%.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Printer installed base → supplies annuity Global brand & channel reach Commercial PC fleet relationships Procurement scale in memory & chips Z workstations franchise Cash generation (TTM FCF $3.88B)

HP's moat is narrower than its brand. In PCs it competes on scale and procurement — being big enough to secure memory supply when it is scarce, which management credits for Q3's "higher fulfillment rates." That is a real advantage in a shortage but not a pricing moat: the product is largely interchangeable with Lenovo's and Dell's. The genuine moat is in printing, where millions of installed printers generate a recurring stream of proprietary supplies. The vulnerability is that this installed base is shrinking slowly, and the moat protects the stream only as long as people keep printing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Component cost is setting the margin, not HP. Q3 gross margin fell to 18.8% from 20.4% and Personal Systems margin fell 0.8 points despite price increases. Management says Q4 is the trough; if memory prices keep rising, the trough moves.
02
Units are falling while prices rise. PC units −16% in Q3, and HP's own preliminary planning assumption (8-K, September 21, 2026) is that industry PC units fall roughly mid-single digits in calendar 2027. Price-driven revenue growth reverses if component costs normalise and pricing follows them down.
03
Leadership vacuum. An interim CEO since February 3, 2026 and a search still open. FY2027 planning, the cost programme and capital allocation are being set without a permanent chief executive.
04
Thin balance-sheet cushion. Book equity is negative (−$92M) after years of buybacks, the current ratio is 0.79, and net debt is $6.17B. Fine while free cash flow runs at $3–4B a year; less comfortable if a margin squeeze meets a unit decline.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, tariff refunds: Q3 FY2026 EPS includes an $0.11 favourable impact, Q4 guidance an estimated $0.08, and the full-year guide about $0.19 — earnings that will not recur. Third, buybacks: $300M in Q3 (about 12.2M shares) and a diluted share count down to 927M from 954M a year earlier, so per-share figures are growing faster than absolute profit.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $27.36 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $32.95, HPQ gained 20.4% on price. Dividends add meaningfully — $4.31 per share across FY2022–FY2025 alone, per StockAnalysis — but the path was anything but smooth. The stock peaked at a $38.84 month-end in May 2022, spent 2022–2025 drifting through a $25–$37 range, then slid through the second half of 2025 to a $18.99 low in February 2026. From there it has risen 73% in seven months.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how fast the mood swung. It printed 16.0 in June 2025 and 18.3 in February 2026 — deeply oversold both times — and now reads 71.4, just into overbought territory for the first time since May 2024. The price sits well above its 10-month moving average of roughly $24.00 (calculated). The trend has turned decisively up; the open question is whether the fundamentals — units falling, margins squeezed — have turned with it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 12.5x trailing and roughly 10x FY2026 consensus earnings for a business producing $3.88B of trailing free cash flow — a 13% FCF yield — while paying a 3.64% dividend and retiring about 3% of its shares a year. Revenue is growing double digits, AI PCs are approaching half of the PC mix at higher price points, full-year guidance has been raised twice, and management says Q4 is the margin trough. If memory costs peak and HP keeps the price increases, margins snap back on a larger revenue base. RBC's framing — a route to 2–4% long-term growth plus $1B of run-rate savings by FY2028 — makes the cost story tangible.

Bear Case

The revenue growth is price, not demand: units fell 16% in the quarter revenue rose 12.5%. Gross margin fell 1.6 points anyway. HP itself now assumes industry PC units fall again in 2027, consensus expects FY2027 EPS to decline to $3.14, the average target is 10% below the price, six analysts rate it Sell, and 8.6% of the float is short. The supplies annuity keeps shrinking, book equity is negative, and there is no permanent CEO. A 73% rally off the low on a business with falling units and falling margins asks a lot of the recovery that has not happened yet.

Top 3 Risks

01
Memory cost squeeze outlasts the "trough." Q3 gross margin 18.8% vs 20.4%, Personal Systems margin down 0.8 points. If component costs keep climbing into FY2027, the promised recovery slips and the earnings base under a 12.5x multiple shrinks.
02
Unit decline meets price normalisation. HP's own assumption is a mid-single-digit industry unit decline in calendar 2027. When memory prices eventually fall, PC prices tend to follow — so revenue can fall on both units and price at once.
03
Printing erosion and leadership uncertainty. Supplies −3% and printer units −7% in Q3 erode the highest-margin line, while strategy and capital allocation are run by an interim CEO pending a search with no announced end date.
Industry Commentary
The memory squeeze on PC makers
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

HP's Q3 FY2026 slides, as reported by Investing.com, cited "commodity cost headwinds from memory and storage components" that more than offset pricing and mix gains in Personal Systems, and said memory and storage costs "remain elevated but the pace of increases may be moderating." For a PC maker, memory is a cost it cannot design around: every machine needs it. HP's response has been to secure supply — management credits better "fulfillment rates" — and raise prices, accepting fewer units. That is the right move in a shortage, and it is also why revenue and units are moving in opposite directions.

2027: a smaller PC market
ONGOING HEADWIND

HP's September 21, 2026 8-K put a number on the cycle: "consistent with current third-party industry forecasts," industry PC units are assumed to fall roughly mid-single digits in calendar 2027 versus 2026, an assumption the company calls "fluid" and "subject to change." The first half of the AI-PC upgrade cycle has been absorbed into higher prices; the second half has to be absorbed into a smaller market.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $32.95 HPQ trades at 12.5x trailing EPS and a 13% trailing FCF yield (calculated). The bull framing: that is a cash machine priced for decline, where a normal memory cycle and a cost programme could lift earnings above a consensus that already expects a dip. The bear framing: the Street's own numbers put fair value below the price — a $29.66 average target, $28 median — and the multiple is low because the earnings are cyclical and currently flattered by pricing and tariff refunds. The way to adjudicate is not the P/E; it is Q4 gross margin and the FY2027 guide. Both arrive with the next print.
