01 Company Overview

HSBC is a London-headquartered universal bank — deposits, lending, trade finance, payments, markets and wealth management — whose two home markets are Hong Kong and the UK. It is listed in London (a FTSE 100 member), in Hong Kong (a Hang Seng Index member) and, through these American Depositary Shares, in New York; at the October 29, 2026 London close it was the second-largest FTSE 100 company by market value, after Shell (our calculation). FY2025 revenue was $68.3B, up 4%, profit before tax $29.9B, and basic EPS $1.21 per ordinary share ($6.05 per ADS). The latest reported period is the third quarter of 2026 (3Q26, July–September), published October 27, 2026: revenue $18.6B (+5%), profit before tax $9.6B (+32%), annualised RoTE 17.7%. For the first nine months (9M26), revenue was $56.4B (+9%) and profit before tax $29.2B (+26%).

Currency is simple here, which is rare for a foreign filer: HSBC reports in US dollars, so no FX conversion sits between its EPS and the NYSE price — only the ADR ratio (×5). The currency exposure lives underneath: most earnings are generated in Hong Kong dollars (pegged to the USD) and sterling, and the 3Q26 release notes currency translation added $0.7B to nine-month revenue. The story of the last two years is a concentration bet. CEO Georges Elhedery has sold or announced exits from a long list of peripheral markets (Canada, Argentina, France retail, Malta, Uruguay, Indonesia retail, Australian retail, Egyptian retail, Singapore life insurance, with a review of retail banking in Türkiye under way), spent $13.7B (HK$106B) buying out the minority of Hang Seng Bank — completed January 2026 — and set a target of RoTE of 17% or better, excluding notable items, in each of 2026, 2027 and 2028. The stock responded: the NYSE ADS went from a $30.07 month-end in October 2021 to $86.60 — it almost tripled in five years, for a bank. The October 2026 results sharpened the debate: returns are still around 18%, but revenue growth is slowing, deposit competition in Hong Kong and the UK is rising, and capital has slipped below the bank's own target range.

02 Core Businesses

Since January 2025 HSBC reports four businesses plus a Corporate Centre: Hong Kong, UK, Corporate & Institutional Banking (CIB), and International Wealth & Premier Banking (IWPB). Figures below are 9M26 (nine months to September 30, 2026), in US$ millions, on HSBC's constant-currency basis (9M25 comparatives restated at current exchange rates and for the January 2026 client transfers into CIB). Premise check — "most profits come from Hong Kong." It depends which cut you use, and the difference matters. The Hong Kong segment (retail, wealth and commercial banking in HK) earned 26.4% of 9M26 profit before tax (32.0% in FY2025) — the largest retail business, but not a majority. The legal entity view says something different: The Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation Limited — which books Hong Kong plus the rest of Asia-Pacific, including most of CIB's Asian business — produced $18.8B, or 64.4%, of reported 9M26 profit before tax. So: Asia generates nearly two-thirds of profits; Hong Kong alone, as HSBC defines the segment, about a quarter to a third.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$1.84T low-cost deposit base Hong Kong retail scale (two brands) Global trade & payments network Asia wealth franchise Systemic importance / funding cost Structural hedge income

HSBC's advantage is cheap, sticky money. Customer accounts of $1.84 trillion fund only $1.03 trillion of loans (a 56.1% loan-to-deposit ratio at September 30, 2026), and the surplus is invested — partly in a multi-year structural hedge — at a spread over what depositors are paid. No competitor can rebuild a century of Hong Kong retail deposits or a trade-finance network spanning both ends of most Asia–West corridors. The moat is real; its yield is not fixed. It is a stream of income that rises and falls with interest rates, which is why the NII guidance is the number the market watches.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Rate sensitivity — HIBOR and US rates. Because the Hong Kong dollar is pegged to the US dollar, HIBOR broadly follows US rates, and HSBC's deposit-heavy Asian balance sheet earns less when they fall. The 3Q26 release says nine-month NIM rose despite "lower market interest rates", thanks to the structural hedge and currency moves, but the third-quarter margin slipped to 1.57% from 1.62%, and analysts (Jefferies among them, per Reuters) expect deposit competition in Hong Kong and the UK to squeeze it further.
02
Capital below its own range. CET1 of 13.9% at September 30 sits under the 14.0–14.5% range HSBC targets. The bank still launched another buyback of up to $1B, counting on Basel 3.1 to add about 0.4–0.5 points in January 2027; any rise in risk-weighted assets (the regulator's risk-adjusted measure of the loan book) or a credit shock competes directly with shareholder returns.
03
Credit: Hong Kong CRE and wholesale surprises. 9M26 expected credit losses (ECL) of $3.2B ran at 0.41% of loans annualised, against about 0.45% guided for 2026 and a 0.30–0.40% medium-term range. They included a $0.5B fraud-related UK securitisation loss and continued Hong Kong commercial real estate (CRE) charges; the third quarter alone ran lower, at 0.31%.
04
Geopolitics. A bank regulated in London, earning nearly two-thirds of its profit through its Hong Kong-incorporated Asian subsidiary, with China's Ping An among its largest reported shareholders, is permanently exposed to US–China and UK–China tension, sanctions and trade-tariff shocks. The 3Q26 release also cites allowances for uncertainty from "the ongoing conflict in the Middle East".

Notable One-Off Items

HSBC flags "notable items" itself, and they swing the reported numbers by billions. 3Q26: $0.4B in all — disposal and wind-down losses and costs, and restructuring from the organisational simplification. 9M26 also carried a $0.3B disposal loss on classifying Malta as held for sale and a $0.2B loss on recycling currency reserves when the UK life insurance sale completed. 9M25 carried $2.1B of dilution and impairment losses on the Bank of Communications (BoCom) stake and $1.4B of legal provisions (booked in 3Q25) — which is why reported profit growth (+32% in 3Q26, +26% over nine months) flatters the underlying: constant-currency profit before tax excluding notable items rose 10% to $10.0B in the quarter and 7% to $30.4B over nine months. FY2025 notables: BoCom $2.1B, French loan-portfolio reserve recycling $1.5B, legal provisions $1.4B, restructuring $1.0B. Hang Seng Bank: privatisation approved January 8, 2026, effective January 26, delisted January 27 — HK$106B ($13.7B), booked through equity, not the income statement. Ahead (per the 3Q26 release, all subject to regulatory approval): an expected $1.8B pre-tax gain on selling HSBC Life Singapore, up to $0.4B on Indonesian retail (1H27) and about $0.3B on Egyptian retail (2H27), against about $0.3B of restructuring costs on the Australian retail exit.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end of $30.07 to the October 29, 2026 settled close of $86.60, the HSBC ADS rose 188.0% on price alone (SPY +67.5% and QQQ +85.3% over the same dates) — before a dividend stream that, at FY2025's $3.75 per ADS, now runs at about 12% of the 2021 entry price every year. The shape is a staircase, not a spike: range-bound between $26 and $36 through 2022, a steady climb through 2023–24 as higher rates flowed into NII, then a faster climb from early 2025 as returns targets moved from mid-teens to 17%+ and the Hang Seng buy-out made the Hong Kong bet explicit. October 2026 broke the pattern: the ADS is down 12.8% for the month to October 29, its biggest monthly fall since September 2022, though still 10.1% above the December 2025 month-end.

The 6-period monthly RSI spent most of 2025–26 above 70 — it peaked at 95.8 in February 2026, an extreme reading — and has now dropped to 44.2, its lowest since October 2022. On daily closes the 14-day RSI is 26.9, below the 30 line usually read as oversold. The price has fallen below its 10-month moving average (~$93.98) for the first time since October 2023, and below the 3-month (~$96.35); on daily data it is 12.3% under its 50-day average ($98.79) and 7.3% under its 200-day ($93.40). The uptrend is broken for now; whether this is a pause or a turn depends on the next results.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

HSBC is earning an 18–19% RoTE excluding one-offs while guiding to at least 17% for three years, kept its NII guide at "at least $46B" despite falling rates, runs costs at 46.6% of revenue, and owns 100% of Hang Seng Bank, with $0.5B a year of synergies targeted by 2028. It sits on $1.84 trillion of deposits against $1.03 trillion of loans, targets a 50% payout ratio with a ~4.3% trailing yield, keeps buying back stock, and expects disposal gains (about $1.8B on Singapore insurance, up to $0.4B on Indonesia, about $0.3B on Egypt) in 2027. At 11.2x trailing and ~10.1x FY2026 earnings, and 1.84x tangible book, October's fall made a high-return bank cheaper without any change to its targets.

Bear Case

The easy money has been made: the ADS almost tripled in five years, and the October results showed how hard that is to extend — revenue missed forecasts, wealth fees stopped growing in the third quarter, and the margin slipped to 1.57% from 1.62% as deposit competition rises in Hong Kong and the UK. Reported growth is flattered by lapping $3.5B of 2025 one-offs (BoCom losses and legal provisions); underlying profit grew 7% over nine months. CET1 is now below its own range, credit losses include a $0.5B fraud and Hong Kong CRE is still leaking, the CFO is leaving, and the biggest earnings engine is a deposit base whose yield falls as US rates and HIBOR fall. The broad sell side sits at Hold: 10 of MarketBeat's 16 ratings.

Top 3 Risks

01
Falling US rates and HIBOR compress NII. The structural hedge has so far out-run rate cuts, but the hedge reinvestment tailwind is finite. The ≥$46B banking NII guide rests on market-implied rates as of mid-October 2026, and the third-quarter margin already dipped to 1.57% from 1.62%.
02
Capital below the floor. A 13.9% CET1 against a 14.0–14.5% range leaves no room for a surprise rise in risk-weighted assets until Basel 3.1's expected lift of about 0.4–0.5 points in January 2027 — and buybacks are a large part of the per-share return case.
03
Hong Kong credit and geopolitics. Commercial real estate charges continue ($0.7B in FY2025, lower in 2026), and nearly two-thirds of profit is booked through the Asian subsidiary — exposing the group to US–China tension, tariffs and sanctions risk.
Industry Commentary
The rate cycle turns — and the structural hedge buys time
ONGOING HEADWIND

Deposit-rich banks were the biggest winners of the 2022–24 rate cycle, and HSBC's Hong Kong and UK books were among the most leveraged to it. As policy rates fall, the question for every such bank is how fast deposit income resets. HSBC's answer so far is its structural hedge: low-yield swaps and bonds locked in during 2020–21 are maturing and being reinvested at higher yields, which is why nine-month NIM rose 0.03 percentage points to 1.60% while "lower market interest rates" worked against it. The third quarter showed the limit: NIM slipped to 1.57% from 1.62% in the second quarter, and analysts cited by Reuters expect deposit competition in Hong Kong and the UK to push funding costs up. That offset has a shelf life. Once the hedge book has rolled over, NII follows market rates more directly — and in Hong Kong, the USD peg means HIBOR follows the Federal Reserve.

Asia wealth as the growth engine
EMERGING TAILWIND

HSBC's strategic pivot is a bet that Hong Kong becomes the world's leading hub for cross-border wealth, with mainland Chinese and regional money flowing through it. Wealth fee income rose 13% to $8.2B in 9M26 and net new money was $105B, $95B of it booked in Asia (3Q26 release) — but third-quarter wealth fees were flat on a year earlier, one reason Reuters gave for the results-day fall. Fee growth is less rate-sensitive than NII, the natural hedge against the rate cycle above. The disposals programme (a long list of exits since 2024, plus Australia, Egypt and Indonesia retail and Singapore insurance in 2026, and a review of retail banking in Türkiye) is the other side of the same coin: capital leaves low-return markets and is redeployed into Hong Kong, UK wealth and wholesale transaction banking.

Valuation after the run-up and the October fall
KEY DEBATE

At 1.84x tangible NAV after October's fall (2.2x at the last edition), HSBC is still priced like a high-return franchise, not a sprawling global conglomerate. The bull framing: a sustained 17–19% RoTE justifies roughly 2x tangible book on a simple rule of thumb, RoTE divided by the cost of equity, the return shareholders demand (illustration: 18–19% ÷ an assumed 9–10%), and the business is now simpler. The bear framing: the returns are partly a rate-cycle windfall, and the price assumes they persist into a falling-rate world. The summer downgrades (BNP Paribas Exane and Shore Capital to Sell, KBW and Erste to Hold) came after a run-up, not a profit miss; October's fall came with a revenue shortfall.
