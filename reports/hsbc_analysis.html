01 Company Overview

HSBC is a London-headquartered universal bank — deposits, lending, trade finance, payments, markets and wealth management — whose two home markets are being Hong Kong and the UK. It is the #1 company by market capitalisation in the UK on companiesmarketcap's country ranking (a FTSE 100 constituent, also listed on HKEX and in the Hang Seng Index) and #44 globally. FY2025 revenue was $68.3B, up 4%, profit before tax $29.9B, and basic EPS $1.21 per ordinary share ($6.05 per ADS). The latest reported period is the first half of 2026 (1H26, January–June 2026), published August 4, 2026: revenue $37.7B (+11%), profit before tax $19.5B (+23%), annualised RoTE 18.2%.

Currency is simple here, which is rare for a foreign filer: HSBC reports in US dollars, so no FX conversion sits between its EPS and the NYSE price — only the ADR ratio (×5). The currency exposure lives underneath: most earnings are generated in Hong Kong dollars (pegged to the USD) and sterling, and the release itself notes foreign-currency translation added $0.7B to 1H26 revenue. The story of the last two years is a concentration bet. CEO Georges Elhedery has sold or announced exits from a long list of peripheral markets (Canada, Argentina, France retail, Malta, Indonesia retail, Australian retail, Egyptian retail, Singapore life insurance), spent $13.7B (HK$106B) buying out the minority of Hang Seng Bank — completed January 2026 — and set a target of RoTE of 17% or better, excluding notable items, in each of 2026, 2027 and 2028. The stock responded: the NYSE ADS went from a $26.15 month-end in September 2021 to $103.21 — nearly a 4x in five years, for a bank. The debate now is whether 18–19% returns survive falling rates and a thinner capital buffer after the Hang Seng purchase, or whether the market has already paid for them.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$1.83T low-cost deposit base Hong Kong retail scale (two brands) Global trade & payments network Asia wealth franchise Systemic importance / funding cost Structural hedge income

HSBC's advantage is cheap, sticky money. Customer accounts of $1.83 trillion fund only $1.02 trillion of loans (a 55.9% loan-to-deposit ratio), and the surplus is invested — partly in a multi-year structural hedge — at a spread over what depositors are paid. No competitor can rebuild a century of Hong Kong retail deposits or a trade-finance network spanning both ends of most Asia–West corridors. The moat is real; its yield is not fixed. It is a rate-sensitive annuity, which is why the NII guidance is the number the market watches.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Rate sensitivity — HIBOR and US rates. Because the Hong Kong dollar is pegged to the US dollar, HIBOR broadly follows US rates, and HSBC's deposit-heavy Asian balance sheet earns less when they fall. The 1H26 release says NIM rose despite "lower market interest rates", thanks to the structural hedge and FX. A quantified NII sensitivity (e.g. the +/−100bp table) is published in the Interim Report and was not retrieved this session, so no number is given here.
02
Thin capital headroom after Hang Seng. CET1 of 14.1% sits 0.1pp above the bottom of the 14.0–14.5% range. Buybacks were paused for three quarters after the privatisation was announced and have only resumed at $1B. Any RWA inflation or credit shock competes directly with shareholder returns.
03
Credit: Hong Kong CRE and wholesale surprises. 1H26 ECL of $2.4B ran at 47bps annualised against ~45bps full-year guidance and a 30–40bps medium-term range, including a $0.4B fraud-related UK securitisation loss and continued Hong Kong CRE charges.
04
Geopolitics. A bank regulated in London, earning two-thirds of its profit through its Hong Kong-incorporated Asian subsidiary, with China's Ping An as its largest reported shareholder, is permanently exposed to US–China and UK–China tension, sanctions and trade-tariff shocks. The release also cites allowances for "the ongoing conflict in the Middle East".

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end of $26.15 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $103.21, the HSBC ADS rose 294.7% on price alone — before a dividend stream that, at FY2025's $3.75 per ADS, now runs at over 14% of the 2021 entry price every year. The shape is a staircase, not a spike: range-bound between $26 and $36 through 2022, a steady climb through 2023–24 as higher rates flowed into NII, then acceleration from early 2025 as returns targets moved from mid-teens to 17%+ and the Hang Seng buy-out made the Hong Kong bet explicit. The ADS is up 31.2% since December 2025 alone.

The 6-period monthly RSI has spent most of 2025–26 above 70 — it peaked at 95.8 in February 2026, an extreme reading — and currently sits at 74.5, still overbought on this measure despite the post-results pullback. The 10-month moving average (~$93.65) is well below the price and rising; the 3-month (~$104.48) sits just above it. Trend intact, momentum stretched, and the first down-month after results in a while.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

HSBC is earning an 18–19% RoTE while guiding to at least 17% for three years, raised its NII guide to at least $46B despite falling rates, cut its cost efficiency ratio to 46.2%, and owns 100% of Hang Seng Bank's deposit franchise with $0.3B of cost synergies still to come. It sits on $1.83 trillion of deposits against $1.02 trillion of loans, pays a 50% payout ratio with a ~3.6% yield, and has disposal gains of ~$2B lined up for 2027 to rebuild buybacks. At 14.6x trailing and ~11.9x forward earnings, a bank compounding tangible book at high-teens returns is not obviously expensive.

Bear Case

The easy money has been made: the ADS has nearly quadrupled, trades at 2.2x tangible NAV, and monthly RSI has been overbought for most of eighteen months. Reported growth is flattered by lapping $2.1B of BoCom losses; underlying PBT grew ~6%. CET1 is at the floor of its range after Hang Seng, ECL is running above guidance with Hong Kong CRE still leaking, the CFO is leaving, and the stock's biggest earnings engine is a deposit base whose yield falls as US rates and HIBOR fall. The sell side has turned — four downgrades in two months, a 75% Hold panel and a consensus target barely 3% above the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Falling US rates and HIBOR compress NII. The structural hedge has so far out-run rate cuts, but the hedge reinvestment tailwind is finite. The ≥$46B banking NII guide is conditioned on mid-July 2026 market-implied rates.
02
Capital at the floor. A 14.1% CET1 against a 14.0–14.5% range leaves no room for an RWA surprise without cutting buybacks — and buybacks are a large part of the per-share return case.
03
Hong Kong credit and geopolitics. Commercial real estate charges continue ($0.7B in FY2025, $0.2B in 1H26), and two-thirds of profit is booked through the Asian subsidiary — exposing the group to US–China tension, tariffs and sanctions risk.
Industry Commentary
The rate cycle turns — and the structural hedge buys time
ONGOING HEADWIND

Deposit-rich banks were the biggest winners of the 2022–24 rate cycle, and HSBC's Hong Kong and UK books were among the most leveraged to it. As policy rates fall, the question for every such bank is how fast deposit income resets. HSBC's answer so far is its structural hedge: low-yield swaps and bonds locked in during 2020–21 are maturing and being reinvested at higher yields, which is why the 1H26 NIM rose 4bps to 1.61% while "lower market interest rates" worked against it. That offset has a shelf life. Once the hedge book has rolled over, NII follows market rates more directly — and in Hong Kong, the USD peg means HIBOR follows the Federal Reserve.

Asia wealth as the growth engine
EMERGING TAILWIND

HSBC's strategic pivot is a bet that Hong Kong becomes, in management's words, "the world's leading cross-border wealth hub by 2029", with mainland Chinese and regional money flowing through it. 1H26 Wealth revenue grew 18% at constant currency, and fee growth is less rate-sensitive than NII — the natural hedge against the rate cycle above. The disposals programme (thirteen exits announced across 2024–25, plus Australia and Egypt retail in 2026) is the other side of the same coin: capital leaves low-return markets and is redeployed into Hong Kong, UK wealth and wholesale transaction banking.

Valuation after the re-rating
KEY DEBATE

At 2.2x tangible NAV, HSBC is priced like a high-return franchise, not a sprawling global conglomerate. The bull framing: a sustained 17–19% RoTE justifies roughly 2x tangible book on a simple RoTE ÷ cost-of-equity rule of thumb (illustrative estimate: 18–19% ÷ an assumed ~9–10% cost of equity; not a sourced figure), and the business is now simpler. The bear framing: the returns are partly a rate-cycle windfall, and the multiple assumes they persist into a falling-rate world. The four downgrades since July — BNP Paribas Exane to Sell, Citi and KBW to Hold, Erste to Hold — read as valuation calls rather than disputes about the reported numbers.
