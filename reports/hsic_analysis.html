01 Company Overview

Walk into almost any dental office and look at what is on the tray: gloves, anaesthetic, composite, impression material, the handpiece, the chair, and the practice-management software on the front-desk screen. Henry Schein describes itself as the world's largest provider of healthcare solutions to office-based dental and medical practitioners, and it sells a large share of that list — through a distribution network carrying more than 300,000 branded products plus its own brands, to more than 1 million customers, with operations or affiliates in 34 countries and territories. FY2025 sales were $13.18B, up 4.03%, with GAAP EPS of $3.27 and non-GAAP EPS of $4.97.

The business is a middleman with a thin rake. Gross margin is about 32%, but after warehouses, trucks, sales reps and restructuring costs the GAAP operating margin is 5.2% and the net margin 3.1% over the last twelve months. Every tenth of a percentage point matters, and that is the whole story of the last two years. In January 2025 private-equity firm KKR agreed to buy in further, for a 12% position as the largest non-index fund shareholder, with two KKR-linked directors joining the board (the investment completed in May 2025); in November 2025 the company announced value-creation initiatives it expects to add over $200 million of operating income over the next few years, and KKR got the right to go to 19.9% (its latest filing, in March 2026, showed about 16%); on March 2, 2026 Stanley Bergman handed the CEO seat — his since 1989 — to an outsider, Fred Lowery from Thermo Fisher; and on November 4, 2026 a new CFO, Emmanuel Caprais from ITT, took over. The stock, which spent five years trapped between roughly $63 and $90, put together five straight monthly gains into August, gave some back in September and closed at $88.24 after a Q3 beat and a second guidance raise. The central question: will the market keep paying more for a better-run distributor, or is this the same range-bound stock being walked to the top of its range by a new dealer at the table?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Distribution scale & density 1M+ customer relationships Practice software lock-in Own-brand consumables Equipment service network Supplier rebate scale

The moat is habit plus logistics. A dental practice orders the same few hundred items again and again; the distributor that delivers next day, services the chair when it breaks and runs the software the front desk books patients in becomes the default. Scale lowers cost per delivery and earns bigger supplier rebates, and the field keeps consolidating — Patterson Companies, a dental and animal-health distributor, was taken private by Patient Square Capital in April 2025. The weakness is that a default is not a contract. Consumables are commodities, online sellers can undercut price on any single SKU, and the company's own risk factors list "competition from third-party online commerce sites" and "expansion of customer purchasing power" among the threats. The margin a middleman keeps is exactly the margin its customers and suppliers are always trying to take back.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A 3% net margin leaves little room. TTM net income was $421M on $13.73B of sales. A one-point swing in gross margin is worth about $137M before tax — a third of a year's net profit. Supplier rebates, freight costs and pricing all run through that one line.
02
Leverage has been rising to fund buybacks. Total debt climbed from $1.50B at the end of 2022 to $3.89B at September 2026, with net debt of $3.71B and debt/EBITDA of 3.28x (StockAnalysis, counting lease liabilities as debt). Buying back more than 6% of the share count in a year with borrowed money flatters EPS; it also narrows the margin for error if earnings stall.
03
The cyber scar. The company's risk factors still cite "the October 2023 incident." The stock fell 12.5% that month — the worst month-end move in this report's five-year window — and in November 2025 then-CEO Bergman described the company as "once again focused on driving growth now that the cyber incident is fully behind us." A distributor's systems are its product; a repeat would hit orders directly.
04
Execution risk from a new CEO, a new CFO and a reorganisation. Lowery replaced a CEO who had held the job since 1989 in March, Caprais took over as CFO on November 4, and in July the company announced it would replace its executive management committee with a new leadership team and fold global supply chain into distribution; its COO, chief strategy officer and head of global supply chain moved into senior-adviser roles on October 30 (company release, July 30, 2026). Removing layers speeds decisions; it also removes people who knew where the bodies were buried.

Notable One-Off Items

Two things distort the headline figures. First, the GAAP–non-GAAP gap is wide: FY2025 GAAP EPS was $3.27 against non-GAAP $4.97, and the company's non-GAAP measures exclude restructuring costs, amortisation of acquired intangibles, intangible impairments, contingent-consideration changes, value-creation implementation costs and litigation settlements. With a multi-year restructuring under way, that gap will not close soon, and investors should decide which EPS they are paying for. Second, buybacks, currency and tariff refunds: currency added 1.4 points to Q2's 6.7% sales growth but only 0.2 points to Q3's 4.0%, and the falling share count lifts per-share growth above profit growth — FY2025 non-GAAP net income was flat at $605M while non-GAAP EPS rose from $4.74 to $4.97. Q3 non-GAAP EPS of $1.56 included a $0.19 net tariff refund; the raised FY2026 guidance includes that refund but assumes no significant further refunds.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $71.06 to the November 4, 2026 settled close of $88.24, HSIC gained 24.2% in just under five years, about 4.5% a year compounded; it pays no dividend, so that is the whole return. Over the same dates SPY rose 68.3% and QQQ 75.7% on price (our calculations, Yahoo closes). For most of the window the stock was a range trade: every month-end close from November 2021 to July 2026 sat between $63.20 (October 2025) and $87.19 (March 2022). The break came at the end: August 2026 closed at $89.80, the highest month-end in the series, capping five consecutive monthly gains from April to August 2026. September gave back 6.5% ($83.97), October added 1.1% ($84.88), and the Q3 report left the stock at $88.24 on November 4.

The 6-period monthly RSI reached 75.4 in August 2026 — the highest reading anywhere in the five-year series — fell to 58.7 after September's pullback and stands at 66.3 on the November 4 close. The two lowest readings, both about 29.3, came in October 2023, the month of the cyber incident, and June 2024. The 10-month moving average, about $82.34, sits below the price and rising: unlike most of the last five years, the long-term trend is pointing up. On daily closes the 14-day RSI is 57.0, and the stock is 2.2% above its 50-day average ($86.32) and 8.7% above its 200-day average ($81.17). The test is whether the stock can hold above its old ceiling in the high $80s, or whether August was another visit to the top of the range.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 16.1x this year's consensus non-GAAP earnings (14.8x next year's) for a distributor that calls itself the world's largest provider of healthcare solutions to office-based dental and medical practitioners, with US dental merchandise up 8.6% in Q3, guidance raised twice in a row, a new operator-CEO from Thermo Fisher and a new CFO from ITT, and a self-help plan worth more than $200M of operating income against a GAAP TTM base of $709M. Consensus sees EPS of $5.48 this year and $5.98 next (StockAnalysis). The share count is shrinking more than 6% a year, KKR sits on the board with about 16% and the right to go to 19.9%, and Patterson, a listed rival, went private. If the full $200M lands, GAAP operating margin would rise from 5.2% to about 6.6% on today's sales (our arithmetic), and earnings would grow faster than sales for years — and the five Buy-rated firms that published after the print all kept their ratings, two of them with $110 targets.

Bear Case

The bear case is that this movie has played before. The stock has been range-bound between the low $60s and high $80s for five years, and it still sits below August's $89.80 month-end high. GAAP EPS is $3.65, putting the stock at about 24x reported earnings for a 3% net-margin business with a 7.0% ROIC — about its cost of capital. Internal growth slowed to 3.1% in Q3 from 4.6% in Q2, medical grew just 1.0% internally, and $0.19 of the quarter's $1.56 adjusted EPS was a tariff refund. Debt has risen from $1.50B to $3.89B, largely to fund buybacks that exceed free cash flow. Short interest has climbed to 9.8% of float. Restructuring charges will keep GAAP well below non-GAAP for years, and a new CEO, a new CFO and a flattened leadership team is precisely when execution slips.

Top 3 Risks

01
Value-creation plan under-delivers. The equity story leans on $200M+ of operating income improvement — more than a quarter of GAAP TTM operating income ($709M). If the $125M end-2026 run-rate slips, the forward multiple has little to support it beyond buybacks.
02
Balance-sheet stretch. Net debt of $3.71B at 3.28x EBITDA, with the company spending more on repurchases ($650M over the last twelve months) than it generated in free cash flow ($559M). A downturn in dental equipment demand would hit earnings and leverage at the same time.
03
Margin pressure from customers, suppliers and online rivals. The company's own risk factors list online commerce competition, customer purchasing power and supplier rebate changes. On a 3% net margin, small shifts in any of them are large shifts in profit.
Industry Commentary
Consolidation and private capital in dental distribution
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Patterson Companies, a listed US distributor of dental and animal-health products, was acquired by Patient Square Capital and delisted in April 2025. Henry Schein, meanwhile, took in its own private-equity partner: KKR agreed in January 2025 to become its largest non-index fund shareholder (completed May 2025), with two KKR-linked directors on the board. Private owners bring a different clock: cost programmes, portfolio pruning and capital allocation get more attention than they did under a CEO in the job since 1989. The value-creation plan, the outside CEO and CFO appointments and the leadership overhaul all came after KKR arrived; whether that is correlation or causation, the direction of travel is clear.

Distributors reinventing themselves as software partners
EMERGING TAILWIND

Distribution Strategy Group places Henry Schein alongside W.W. Grainger, Sysco and DNOW as distributors building AI into customer applications and internal operations. For HSIC the angle is the practice-management software: Lowery said, per that account, "We're advancing our Henry Schein One technology platforms, including AI-enabled solutions to improve workflows and collections, enhance practice performance and strengthen patient engagement." If the software that runs the dental office also becomes the ordering channel, the distributor is harder to replace. The bull framing: technology makes the relationship sticky and supports margins. The bear framing: at $181M a quarter, it is 5% of sales, and a distributor's valuation will be set by its distribution margins for years yet.

Steady demand, cyclical equipment
BACKDROP

Dental consumables are close to non-discretionary — cleanings, fillings and infection control happen in good years and bad — which is why the stock's 5-year beta is 0.86. Equipment is different: chairs, imaging and digital restoration systems are capital purchases that practices defer when financing is expensive or patient volumes soften. Q3 2026 showed the split again, with dental merchandise growing 5.1% internally and equipment 2.4%. Medical distribution adds a seasonal layer through respiratory and test-kit demand. None of it grows fast; all of it compounds if the company can keep more of each dollar shipped.
