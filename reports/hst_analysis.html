01 Company Overview

Host Hotels & Resorts is the largest lodging REIT in the United States. It owns the buildings, not the brands: 72 hotels in the US and five abroad, about 41,800 rooms after two purchases in October, under brands such as Marriott, Ritz-Carlton, Westin, W, Hyatt, Fairmont, 1 Hotels and Hilton, plus stakes in seven domestic joint ventures. The brands run the hotels for a fee, and Host keeps what's left after the manager is paid. It began in 1993, when Marriott Corporation split in two: the management business became Marriott International and the real estate became Host Marriott. It reorganised as a REIT at the end of the 1990s and took its current name in 2006. Only 162 people work for Host itself. The hotel staff work for the operators.

That structure is the whole bet. Owning luxury and upper-upscale hotels, partly with borrowed money, is a bet on one number, RevPAR, and on how much of each extra room-dollar is left after wage inflation. In 2026 that bet paid. Comparable RevPAR rose 8.8% to $219.50 in Q3, October ran about +6.5%, and management has raised its full-year RevPAR guide three times, to about +6.75%. The stock went from a $17.73 close on December 31, 2025 to a $26.11 close on November 4, 2026, the day of the Q3 release, then fell 5.67% the next session. At $24.62 it trades at about 11.2x trailing AFFO. The open question is whether 2026 is a new plateau for affluent and business travel or a high point. The World Cup turned out to be a small part of the answer: management puts it at about one percentage point of Q3 RevPAR growth and about 0.7 of a point for the full year.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hard-to-replicate resort real estate Scale: ~41,800 rooms Investment-grade balance sheet (Baa2) Renovation programs backed by the hotel brands Track record of selling hotels above cost Lean corporate cost (162 staff)

The moat is location and balance sheet, not operations. Oceanfront resort land in places like Maui and Miami is scarce, and that limits new supply at the luxury end. Host's second edge is its cost of capital. An investment-grade borrower paying a 4.8% average rate can buy, renovate and sell when smaller, more indebted owners can't. The February 2026 sale of two Four Seasons resorts shows that edge in action: $1.1 billion, against $925 million paid in 2021–22, with about $88 million of future capital spending passed to the buyer. In October Host spent part of the proceeds on two hotels at a combined 10.7 times estimated 2026 EBITDA, which management says is more than one times below what its own shares have recently traded at. The weakness is that an owner has no pricing power of its own. The brand sets the rate, the manager sets the payroll, and Host absorbs both.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Growth still leans on price. Management said first-half growth came mainly from higher room rates. Q3 added volume, with transient room-nights up 4.8%, but rates still rose 5.7% and they did more of the work. Hotels reprice every night, so outside group bookings there is little contracted revenue to cushion a downturn.
02
A high bar for 2027. The World Cup itself is a small part of it. Management says the event added about one percentage point to Q3 RevPAR growth and about 0.7 of a point for the full year, worth $15–$17 million of EBITDA. The harder comparison is the year as a whole: about 6.75% RevPAR growth in 2026, with 2027 guidance not due until February.
03
Wages sit between RevPAR and profit. The Q3 2026 release again names wage increases as the offset to rate gains, and management expects wage rates to rise about 5% this year, on roughly half of comparable hotel operating costs. FY2025 comparable hotel EBITDA margin fell 0.4 of a percentage point to 28.9%. The operators, not Host, employ the hotel staff, and wages don't fall when RevPAR does.
04
Concentration in weather-exposed resorts. Maui, Miami, Oahu and the Florida Gulf Coast are among the highest-RevPAR markets year to date, and all four are exposed to storms and fire. Hurricanes Helene and Milton in 2024 kept The Don CeSar closed until March 2025. Management now expects Hawaii weather to cut full-year EBITDA by about $10 million, including the March 2026 Kona Low rainstorm, whose property cost is estimated at $20–$25 million and is expected to be mostly insured above the deductible. A wildfire evacuation closed Alila Ventana Big Sur for a month in Q3.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort 2026's reported figures. The first is the Four Seasons sale. Four Seasons Resort Orlando (444 rooms) and Four Seasons Resort and Residences Jackson Hole (125 rooms) sold in February 2026 for $1.1B, producing a taxable gain of about $500M that was paid out as a $0.72 special dividend on July 15. The gain inflates GAAP EPS and ROE and is excluded from FFO. The second is condominium sales at a development next to the Orlando resort: $17M of net income in 2025 and about $18M net expected for 2026, with the remaining sales moving into 2027. Management says 29 of the 40 units have closed. That's real cash, but not recurring hotel income. The third is insurance. Final determination of the Hurricane Helene and Milton claims is expected by the end of 2026 but isn't in guidance beyond what was received in Q1, and the Kona Low storm claim is still being evaluated. Host also sold The St. Regis Houston ($51M, January 2026) and by February had the Sheraton Parsippany under contract for $15M; both are small.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $15.70 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $24.62, HST gained 56.8% on price, before dividends, against 61.4% for SPY and 66.6% for QQQ over the same dates. For most of that time the stock went nowhere. From November 2021 to December 2025, every month-end close sat between $14.12 and $20.74, while the business recovered from the pandemic underneath it. Then 2026 did in seven months what the previous four years hadn't: $17.73 at December 31, 2025 to $25.13 at July 31, 2026, a 41.7% gain. The stock lost 12.7% in August after the Q2 print, recovered to $25.30 at the end of October, the highest month-end close in the window, and lost 5.67% the day after the Q3 print. The drivers of the year's gain were asset sales above cost, a special dividend, and RevPAR growth that kept beating guidance.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 89.7 at the July 2026 close, behind only the series' first reading of 92.0 in May 2022. The August drop brought it to 56.8, October's rally to 72.0, and it now reads 66.2, below the 70 line for overbought. The other extreme was 20.1 at the April 2025 low of $14.12, the lowest month-end close in the window. The 10-month moving average is about $22.59, below the price. So the long-term trend is still up, and both post-earnings drops so far look like pullbacks within it rather than a break.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

About 11x AFFO buys the largest US owner of luxury and upper-upscale hotels. RevPAR guidance has been raised three times this year, to about +6.75%, and AFFO per share is guided to $2.21. The balance sheet is investment-grade, with no significant maturities until December 2028 and about $2.6B of liquidity after October's purchases, and the AFFO guide covers the regular dividend about 2.8 times. Host sold two resorts for $1.1B against $925M paid, then bought two hotels at about 10.7 times EBITDA, below what management says its own shares trade at. In Q3, business-travel room-nights rose about 5%, and group revenue booked for 2027 is running 6% ahead. The Marriott and Hyatt renovation programs come with operator-funded guarantees, and Maui is still recovering. If demand holds, guided AFFO is nearly 9% of the share price, before any growth.

Bear Case

2026 may be the peak. GAAP net income fell 56.4% in Q3, and revenue of $1.347B missed the Zacks consensus of $1.38B. Wage rates are rising about 5% this year on roughly half of hotel costs, and hotel sales keep the GAAP revenue guide at just +1.6%. Phoenix RevPAR fell 32.0%. The stock has now fallen the day after two quarters in a row that beat on AFFO and raised guidance, by 6.96% in August and 5.67% in November, which bears read as the good news already being in the price. A stock that swings a little more than the market (beta 1.11), tied to discretionary luxury spending and partly funded with debt, trades at 11x AFFO for a reason.

Top 3 Risks

01
A turn in affluent and business travel spending. Rates rose 5.7% in Q3 on top of first-half growth that was mainly price, so the portfolio is priced for high-income households and corporate travel budgets that keep paying more. Hotels reprice daily, which means a pullback hits revenue within weeks, and because many hotel costs are fixed in the short run, the hit to EBITDA is larger than the hit to revenue.
02
Tough 2027 comparisons. 2026 is guided to about +6.75% RevPAR, and the World Cup added about 0.7 of a point of that. Management points to group bookings running 6% ahead, Maui and San Francisco recoveries and renovated hotels coming back, but 2027 guidance doesn't arrive until February.
03
Margin squeeze and weather. Each recent release names wage increases as the offset to rate gains. Some of the highest-RevPAR markets year to date (Maui, Miami, the Florida Gulf Coast) carry storm and fire exposure, Hawaii weather is expected to cost about $10 million of 2026 EBITDA, and insurance pays only above the deductible and only after long claims processes.
Industry Commentary
The affluent traveller is still carrying Host's numbers, and business travel is joining in
CURRENT TAILWIND

Host's 2026 shows who is still travelling. Maui (+13.5%) accounted for more than two-thirds of its resort RevPAR growth in Q3, per management, and its CEO said demand "remains durable, supported by resilient leisure travel, growth in business travel, and healthy group demand in many markets." Growth came mostly through room rate in the first half. In Q3 volume joined in: transient room-nights rose 4.8% and transient revenue 11.5%, and management said business-travel room-nights rose about 5%. For an owner of luxury and upper-upscale hotels, higher rates carry more margin than more guests do, so that mix still matters. The thesis still depends on one group of consumers, joined now by corporate travel budgets, being willing to keep paying more.

The World Cup was a small part of the year; the 2027 bar is not
WATCH 2027

Management estimates the 2026 FIFA World Cup added about one percentage point to Q3 RevPAR growth and about 0.7 of a point for the year, and between $15 million and $17 million of EBITDA. That is small next to a $1.865B EBITDA guide. A one-off event still helps the year it lands in and hurts the year after, but the larger question for 2027 is whether RevPAR can grow at all on top of a year of about 6.75%.

Private-market value vs public-market price
SELLING ABOVE COST

The most important data point of the year may be the Four Seasons sale. Two resorts bought for $925M in 2021–22 sold for $1.1B in February 2026, with about $88M of future capital spending passed to the buyer. When private buyers pay more for Host's hotels than the stock market implies they're worth, management can sell hotels, pay special dividends, buy back stock and reinvest. That's what it has been doing: $1.4B of real estate sold across five properties during 2025 and early 2026, per the FY2025 release, and two hotels bought in October 2026 for a combined $374M. The bull reading: each sale proves the discount is too wide. The bear reading: an owner selling its best resorts at the top of the cycle is telling you where it thinks the top is.
