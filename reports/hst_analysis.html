01 Company Overview

Host Hotels & Resorts is the largest lodging REIT in the United States. It owns the buildings, not the brands: 70 hotels in the US and five abroad, about 41,300 rooms, flagged Marriott, Ritz-Carlton, Westin, W, Hyatt, Fairmont, 1 Hotels, Hilton and others, plus stakes in seven domestic joint ventures. The brands run the hotels for a fee, and Host keeps what's left after the manager is paid. It began in 1993, when Marriott Corporation split in two: the management business became Marriott International and the real estate became Host Marriott. It reorganised as a REIT at the end of the 1990s and took its current name in 2006. Only 162 people work for Host itself. The hotel staff work for the operators.

That structure is the whole bet. Owning luxury and upper-upscale hotels is a leveraged, operating claim on one number, RevPAR, and on how much of each extra room-dollar is left after wage inflation. In 2026 that claim paid. Comparable RevPAR rose 7.0% to $251.53 in Q2, July ran about +10% with a boost from the FIFA World Cup, and management raised guidance twice. The stock went from a $17.73 close on December 31, 2025 to a $25.13 close on July 31, 2026, then gave back 12.7% in August after the Q2 print. At $22.31 it trades at about 10.3x trailing AFFO. The open question is whether 2026 is a new plateau for affluent travel or a peak inflated by the World Cup that 2027's comparisons will expose.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hard-to-replicate resort real estate Scale: ~41,300 rooms Investment-grade balance sheet (Baa2) Brand-operator capital programs Capital-recycling track record Lean corporate cost (162 staff)

The moat is location and balance sheet, not operations. Oceanfront resort land in places like Maui and Miami is scarce, and that limits new supply at the luxury end. Host's second edge is its cost of capital. An investment-grade borrower paying a 4.8% average rate can buy, renovate and sell when smaller, more indebted owners can't. The February 2026 sale of two Four Seasons resorts shows that edge in action: $1.1 billion, against $925 million paid in 2021–22, with about $88 million of future capital spending passed to the buyer. The weakness is that an owner has no pricing power of its own. The brand sets the rate, the manager sets the payroll, and Host absorbs both.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Rate, not volume, is doing the work. In Q2 2026, transient room-nights fell 0.7% while transient revenue rose 6.9%. Growth that comes entirely from price depends on the affluent consumer continuing to accept higher prices. Hotels reprice every night, so there is no contracted backlog to cushion a turn.
02
The World Cup comparison. The 2026 guidance explicitly includes World Cup benefits, and July RevPAR rose about 10% with "a continued boost" from the matches. A one-off event in the base year makes the 2027 comparison harder. Management itself expects year-over-year comparisons to moderate in the second half as rate growth slows.
03
Wages sit between RevPAR and profit. Both the FY2025 and Q2 2026 releases name wage and benefit increases as the main offset to rate gains. FY2025 comparable hotel EBITDA margin fell 40bps to 28.9%. Hotel labour is the one cost an owner can't renegotiate, and it doesn't fall when RevPAR does.
04
Concentration in weather-exposed resorts. The highest-RevPAR markets (Maui, Miami, the Florida Gulf Coast, Oahu) are also the most exposed to storms and fire. Hurricanes Helene and Milton in 2024 kept The Don CeSar closed until March 2025. A March 2026 Kona Low rainstorm in Hawaii is expected to cost $27–$32 million in property repairs, which the company expects insurance to mostly cover above the deductible.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort 2026's reported figures. The first is the Four Seasons sale. Four Seasons Resort Orlando (444 rooms) and Four Seasons Resort and Residences Jackson Hole (125 rooms) sold in February 2026 for $1.1B, producing a taxable gain of about $500M that was paid out as a $0.72 special dividend on July 15. The gain inflates GAAP EPS and ROE and is excluded from FFO. The second is condominium sales at a development next to the Orlando resort: $17M of net income in 2025 and $16–$20M guided for 2026, with the remaining sales moving into 2027. That's real cash, but not recurring hotel income. The third is insurance. Final determination of the Hurricane Helene and Milton claims is expected in 2026 but isn't in guidance, and the Kona Low storm claim is still being evaluated. Host also sold The St. Regis Houston ($51M, January 2026) and agreed to sell the Sheraton Parsippany ($15M); both are small.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $16.33 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $22.31, HST gained 36.6% on price, before dividends. For most of that time the stock went nowhere. From September 2021 to December 2025, every month-end close sat between $14.12 and $20.74, while the business recovered from the pandemic underneath it. Then 2026 did in seven months what the previous four years hadn't: $17.73 at December 31, 2025 to $25.13 at July 31, 2026, a 41.7% gain. The drivers were asset sales above cost, a special dividend, and RevPAR growth that kept beating guidance.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 89.7 at the July 2026 close, the most overbought reading in the five-year series. The August drop brought it back to 56.8, and it now reads 59.0, which is neutral. The other extreme was 20.1 at the April 2025 low of $14.12, the five-year bottom. The 10-month moving average is about $21.22, below the price. So the long-term trend is still up, and the August drop so far looks like a pullback within it rather than a break.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

About 10.3x AFFO buys the largest US owner of luxury and upper-upscale hotels. RevPAR guidance has been raised twice this year, to +4.75–5.25%, and AFFO per share is guided to $2.15–$2.18. The balance sheet is investment-grade, with no 2026 maturities and about $3.6B of liquidity, and AFFO covers the regular dividend almost three times. Management has just shown that its hotels sell privately for more than it paid ($1.1B for two resorts bought for $925M), which suggests the public price undervalues the real estate. The Marriott and Hyatt renovation programs come with operator-funded guarantees, and Maui is still recovering. If affluent travel demand holds, the stock offers an AFFO yield near 10% plus growth.

Bear Case

2026 is a peak year dressed up as a trend. Transient room-nights are falling and all the growth is rate. The World Cup lifted the base year, and management's own guidance implies a much slower second half. GAAP revenue is guided flat because the best assets are being sold. Wages keep rising whether RevPAR does or not, and hotel margins were squeezed in 2025. The market sold a beat-and-raise quarter down 7% in a day, a sign the good news was already in the July price. A leveraged, 1.10-beta claim on discretionary luxury spending trades at 10x AFFO for a reason.

Top 3 Risks

01
A turn in affluent travel spending. Transient room-nights fell 0.7% in Q2 while rates rose, so the portfolio is priced for high-income households that keep paying more. Hotels reprice daily, which means a pullback hits revenue within weeks, and operating leverage magnifies it at the EBITDA line.
02
Tough 2027 comparisons. 2026 guidance includes World Cup benefits, and July ran about +10%. The event won't repeat, and management already expects year-over-year comparisons to moderate in the second half.
03
Margin squeeze and weather. Every recent release names wage and benefit increases as the offset to rate gains. The highest-RevPAR markets (Maui, Miami, the Florida Gulf Coast) carry storm and fire exposure, and insurance pays only above the deductible and only after long claims processes.
Industry Commentary
The affluent traveller is carrying Host's numbers
CURRENT TAILWIND

Host's 2026 shows who is still travelling. Its fastest-growing markets in Q2 were resorts (Maui +13.6%, Miami +13.1%), and its CEO's explanation was that "affluent consumers continue to prioritize travel." Growth came through room rate, not volume: transient room-nights fell 0.7% while transient revenue rose 6.9%. For an owner of luxury and upper-upscale hotels that's the best possible mix, because higher rates carry more margin than more guests do. It is also the most concentrated bet. The whole thesis depends on one group of consumers being willing to keep paying more.

Event-driven demand and the comparison problem
WATCH 2027

The 2026 FIFA World Cup lifted demand through June and July. Host said markets that hosted matches and markets that didn't both performed solidly, and July RevPAR rose about 10%. Full-year guidance (+4.75–5.25%) is well below the first half (+5.7%) and July, so management expects an ordinary second half. A one-off event helps the year it lands in and hurts the year after, when it becomes the comparison.

Private-market value vs public-market price
CAPITAL RECYCLING

The most important data point of the year may be the Four Seasons sale. Two resorts bought for $925M in 2021–22 sold for $1.1B in February 2026, with about $88M of future capex passed to the buyer. When private buyers pay more for Host's hotels than the stock market implies they're worth, management can sell hotels, pay special dividends, buy back stock and reinvest. That's what it has been doing: $1.4B of real estate sold across five properties during 2025 and early 2026, per the FY2025 release. The bull reading: each sale proves the discount is too wide. The bear reading: an owner selling its best resorts at the top of the cycle is telling you where it thinks the top is.
