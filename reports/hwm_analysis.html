01 Company Overview

Howmet Aerospace makes the engineered metal parts that sit in the hottest, most highly stressed places on a commercial aircraft: investment-cast turbine blades and vanes for the hot section of a jet engine (the part downstream of the combustor, where gas temperatures are highest), aerospace fasteners and fluid fittings, forged titanium and aluminium airframe structures, and — in a completely unrelated end market — forged aluminium wheels for heavy trucks. Its customers are the engine makers (GE Aerospace, Safran, RTX/Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce), the airframers (Boeing, Airbus), and the industrial gas turbine builders. FY2025 revenue was $8.252B, up 11.1% year over year; the trailing-twelve-month figure has since reached $9.731B, up about 22%, and Q3 2026 alone grew 29% to $2.703B (25% organic, meaning without the effect of businesses bought or sold). The company ranks #162 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $79.22B.

The third-quarter results, released before the market opened on October 29, 2026, came after two months in which investors questioned the gas-turbine half of the story. On August 31, after SpaceX said it plans to make its own blades and vanes for industrial gas turbines (CNBC), the stock fell 7.51%. On September 8, GE Aerospace — a customer worth about 14% of Howmet's sales in the first nine months of 2026 (10-Q) — agreed to buy the rival caster Consolidated Precision Products (CPP) for about $12 billion (Reuters), and the stock fell another 10.70%. By the intraday low of $183.52 on October 23 the shares were 41% below the August 6 intraday high of $310.00. The quarter answered with a beat and a raise: adjusted EPS of $1.46 against a Nasdaq consensus of $1.37, gas-turbine revenue up 45%, full-year guidance raised on every line, a first 2027 revenue figure of about $11.5 billion, and $5 billion added to the buyback. The stock rose 5.51% that day and gave back 3.26% the next, closing October 30 at $199.22, 18.7% below the previous edition.

Two things separate Howmet from a generic aerospace supplier, and both are addressed in detail below. First, an unusually large and fast-growing share of revenue is aftermarket (spare parts sold to replace worn components on engines already flying) rather than OE (original equipment — parts sold into brand-new engines and airframes). Second, the single-crystal turbine blade is one of the genuinely hard things to manufacture in the industrial economy, and the qualification process on an engine programme makes supplier switching close to impossible once you are designed in.

OE (original equipment) revenue is the part Howmet sells into a new engine or a new airframe as it is being built. Aftermarket (or "spares") revenue is the same physical part sold years later as a replacement, when the original one has been consumed by heat and cyclic stress and comes out at an engine shop visit. The two are driven by completely different variables. OE tracks how many aircraft Boeing and Airbus assemble in a given month — a number set by two companies and their supply chains. Aftermarket tracks how many hours the global installed fleet actually flies, which is a much larger, much more diversified and much more persistent base: an engine delivered in 2016 will generate replacement-blade demand for decades regardless of what happens to next year's build rate.
Aftermarket also carries materially higher margin. The tooling, the ceramic cores, the casting moulds and the engine-programme qualification are all sunk costs already paid for during the OE phase; a spare blade sells at a replacement-part price into a customer with no qualified alternative source, so the profit on each extra spare blade is far above the OE margin on the same part. This is the mechanism behind Howmet's margin expansion: Engine Products adjusted EBITDA margin rose 580 basis points (5.8 percentage points) year over year to 39.1% in Q3 2026 on 33% revenue growth. In Q3, though, spares grew about 30% against total company growth of 29% (25% organic), after 37% against 24% in Q2: this quarter the spares share held steady rather than climbing, and the margin gain came from Engine Products as a whole.
Why the market pays up: spares carry the highest margins and follow flight hours rather than build rates. In Q2 they outgrew the company by about 13 points; in Q3 by about 1.

The core of the company. Engine Products makes airfoils — turbine blades and vanes — plus rolled rings and rotating and structural parts for jet engines and industrial gas turbines. The blades are produced by investment casting (also called lost-wax casting: a wax pattern of the part is coated in ceramic slurry to build a shell, the wax is melted out, and molten nickel superalloy is poured into the resulting cavity). The hardest variant is the single-crystal blade, where the metal is solidified so that the finished part is one continuous crystal with no grain boundaries at all — grain boundaries are where creep failure starts under sustained high temperature and centrifugal load. Across the company, Q3 revenue rose 32% in commercial aerospace and 45% in gas turbines; Plant said in the release, "Of particular note was the 45% year-over-year gas turbines revenue growth …", and on the call he said he now expects gas-turbine sales to double from their 2025 level within three years. Bernstein's Douglas Harned puts Howmet's share of industrial gas turbine blade castings above 50%. The quarter also brought two would-be rivals in that market: SpaceX, which said on August 31 it will make its own blades and vanes for industrial gas turbines, and GE Aerospace, which agreed on September 8 to buy the caster CPP. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Single-Crystal Casting Yield Engine-Programme Qualification >50% IGT Blade Share Aftermarket Sole-Source Decades of Process Know-How Capital & Capacity Scarcity Multi-Decade Programme Life

The moat here is worth explaining mechanically rather than asserting, because the mechanism is unusually literal. A high-pressure turbine blade sits immediately behind the combustor in a gas path whose temperature is above the melting point of the nickel superalloy the blade is made from. It survives because of three things layered together: the blade is hollow, with internal serpentine cooling passages formed by a fragile ceramic core placed inside the wax pattern before shelling; hundreds of laser-drilled film-cooling holes bleed compressor air out across the surface to form an insulating boundary layer; and a ceramic thermal barrier coating is applied on top. Then the casting itself is grown as a single crystal — the mould is withdrawn slowly from the furnace through a helical "pigtail" grain selector so that exactly one crystal orientation propagates up the entire part. There are no grain boundaries, because grain boundaries are precisely where creep and fatigue cracks nucleate when a part is held at 1,000°C-plus while spinning under tens of thousands of g of centrifugal load. Getting all of that right simultaneously, at scale, with acceptable scrap rates, is the constraint — the process is not secret, but the accumulated yield knowledge is, and yield is what separates a profitable caster from an unprofitable one.

Layered on top is qualification. A part on a certified engine programme is qualified to a specific supplier, a specific plant, a specific process route, and often a specific furnace. Changing any of those requires re-qualification with the engine OEM and, ultimately, the airworthiness authority — a multi-year exercise involving test castings, metallurgical validation, and in some cases engine testing. No engine maker undertakes that mid-programme to save a few points on a blade that represents a small fraction of engine cost but 100% of the risk of an uncontained failure. The practical effect is that once Howmet is designed into a programme, it holds that content for the life of the programme, which for a narrowbody engine is measured in decades — and it holds the spares revenue from that content too, which is where the profit is. The IGT (industrial gas turbine) position is the same mechanism in a different end market: over 50% of blade castings by Bernstein's estimate, with gas-turbine revenue up 45% in Q3. The weeks before the print tested that argument. SpaceX's plan to cast its own blades and GE Aerospace's agreement to buy CPP each add a would-be supplier, and the stock fell 7.51% and 10.70% on the two days. Qualification cuts both ways here: a new caster needs years to qualify on an engine, which protects the programmes Howmet already holds, but owning CPP gives GE its own source of castings for the programmes still to come.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Extreme customer concentration. The buyer set is effectively six names — GE Aerospace, Safran (jointly, via CFM), RTX/Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce on engines, Boeing and Airbus on airframes. GE Aerospace alone was about 14% of third-party sales in the first nine months of 2026 (10-Q), and it is now buying a rival caster. A production-rate decision taken in Renton or Toulouse flows straight into Howmet's OE order book with no diversification available. The same concentration that makes qualification a moat makes the revenue line hostage to a handful of planning decisions, and it gives those customers real leverage in long-term agreement negotiations.
02
Valuation leaves no room for a build-rate stumble. At 39.1× trailing earnings the stock is still priced for several more years of fast growth. Boeing was cleared by the FAA to move from 38 to 42 737 MAX a month in October 2025 and passed the FAA's review for 47 in May 2026; Airbus expects 70 to 75 A320-family aircraft a month by the end of 2027. Those ramps have slipped repeatedly before. A slip does not immediately hit spares, but it hits OE, sentiment and the price investors will pay per dollar of earnings at once — the falls of 7.51% on August 31 and 10.70% on September 8, 2026, on news about competitors rather than about Howmet's own results, show how the stock trades when investors question the growth story.
03
Aftermarket depends on flight hours, not on contracts. Spares are durable relative to OE, but they are not contractual annuities. If global departures fall — a recession, a fuel shock, a pandemic-type event — airlines defer shop visits, park older aircraft and run engines on their remaining hours rather than overhaul them. Replacement-blade demand can fall faster than airline capacity does, and the segment carrying the highest margin is then the one whose profit falls hardest. On the Q3 call Plant said, "Naturally, the Middle East was lower given the Iran conflict and its fallout," and "Oil prices, notably jet fuel and diesel, are especially high," though the release says he does not expect a material impact on spares demand.
04
Capacity expansion and execution risk. Plant put 2026 capex at about half a billion dollars and said Howmet will "invest again at a higher level in 2027," and said Howmet is at seven new manufacturing plants, with three more under consideration. Bringing a new investment-casting facility to qualified production yield is slow and expensive, and the interim period consumes cash while depressing returns. Howmet is also putting more capital into industrial gas turbines, where its revenue grew 45% in Q3 on power demand, but which has its own history of sharp order cycles — and where SpaceX now plans to make its own blades and GE is buying a rival caster.

Notable One-Off Items

Three distortions are worth isolating. First, portfolio activity: Q3 2026 revenue grew 29% in total but 25% organically. Fastening Systems' 41% includes the CAM acquisition (10% without it), and Engineered Structures' 4% decline reflects the sale of the Savannah disc forging plant (8% growth without it). Second, buyback-driven EPS support: $500M of repurchases in Q3 at an average $264.77 retired about 1.9 million shares, and about $1.1B in 2026 through October 28 averaged $250.72; buybacks lift EPS and ROE by shrinking the share count and the equity base. On October 28 the board added $5B to the authorization, bringing it to $5.397B. Third, aluminium pass-through in Forged Wheels adds revenue without adding profit, which is why that segment's margin fell. Beyond these, note that adjusted EPS ($1.46, against GAAP EPS of $1.42) and adjusted EBITDA are non-GAAP measures and exclude special items by construction.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Howmet is growing earnings fast — nine-month diluted EPS up 50% — with nearly 23% ROIC in a business that is genuinely difficult to enter. Q3 answered the September scare: gas-turbine revenue grew 45%, Engine Products margin reached 39.1%, every 2026 guidance line went up for the second quarter running, and management put 2027 revenue at about $11.5B, up roughly 12%. Spares, the most profitable and most durable revenue, grew 30% to about 21% of sales. Narrowbody build rates are still below where Boeing and Airbus intend them to be, so the OE half has years of visible ramp ahead, and a newcomer such as SpaceX needs years to qualify its blades, so the programmes Howmet already holds are not at risk soon. Free cash flow of $1.93B over the last four quarters funds capacity expansion, the dividend and about $1.1B of buybacks this year, with $5.397B now authorized.

Bear Case

At 39.1× trailing and 36.5× forward (FY2026) earnings, the price still contains the aftermarket argument and several years of build-rate increases that have not happened yet. Arithmetic: to deliver a 10% annual return over five years while the multiple falls to 25× — still a premium for an industrial company — EPS must compound at roughly 18.6% a year for five straight years from the FY2026 consensus of $5.46. Consensus has 44.7% growth in 2026 and 21.2% in 2027, and management's first 2027 revenue figure is +12%, so the required path leaves little room for slowing after 2027. Alternatively, if EPS grows at 10% a year and the multiple holds at 36.5×, the return is about 10% a year — but multiples that high rarely hold. Meanwhile the gas-turbine growth that carried the 2026 rally now has two would-be new suppliers, six customers control the order book, and the falls of 7.5% on August 31 and 10.7% on September 8 show what happens to a richly valued stock when the story is questioned.

Top 3 Risks

1
A lower multiple. The dominant risk is not that the business deteriorates but that the multiple (the price investors pay per dollar of earnings) does. A move from 36.5× forward earnings to a still-generous 25× is a 31.5% price decline with no change to a single operating number.
2
Build-rate slippage at Boeing or Airbus. The OE half of the business depends on ramps that both airframers have publicly missed before. Boeing only regained FAA permission to exceed 38 737 MAX per month in October 2025, and Airbus expects 70 to 75 A320-family aircraft a month only by the end of 2027. Concentration means there is no offsetting customer.
3
Flight-hour shock to the aftermarket. The high-margin spares stream is durable relative to OE but is not contracted. A traffic downturn causes airlines to defer shop visits and park aircraft, and replacement-part demand falls faster than capacity does — which would hit the exact revenue line the current share price leans on most. Plant said on the Q3 call that Middle East traffic was lower because of the Iran conflict, though he expects no material impact on spares demand.
  Industry Commentary
Narrowbody build rates are the binding constraint on the whole supply chain
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Everything upstream of Boeing and Airbus final assembly is sized to a build rate those two companies set. Boeing won FAA clearance for 42 737 MAX a month on October 17, 2025, lifting the 38/month cap imposed in early 2024 after the door-plug incident, and in May 2026 passed the FAA's review for 47, with 52 the next step. Airbus expects to reach 70 to 75 A320-family aircraft a month by the end of 2027. Widebodies are moving too: on the Q3 call Plant said he expects volume increases for the 737, the A320, the 787 and the A350 to be healthy, and that "The current extraordinary aircraft OE order backlog will ensure that new aircraft production continues". For Howmet, every one of those units is engine airfoils, hundreds of thousands of fasteners and structural forgings. The constraint is real in both directions — it caps how fast OE revenue can grow, and it means any slip is felt immediately.
Industrial gas turbines have become a second growth engine
EMERGING TAILWIND
Gas turbine revenue grew 45% in Q3 2026 — faster than commercial aerospace at 32% and far faster than defence at 10% — after 38% in Q2. Bernstein puts Howmet's share of industrial gas turbine blade castings above 50%. On the Q2 call CEO John Plant described demand as "extraordinary," and on the Q3 call he said he now expects gas-turbine sales to double from their 2025 level within three years. The physical product is nearly the same as an aero turbine blade — single-crystal nickel superalloy castings — so the capability transfers directly, but the demand driver is electricity generation, not air travel. That is a genuine diversification of the cycle. It is also why Howmet expects about half a billion dollars of capex in 2026 and more in 2027, and why Plant counts seven new manufacturing plants, with three more under consideration. The boom is drawing rivals: SpaceX plans to cast blades for its own turbines, and GE Aerospace is buying CPP. And IGT order cycles have historically been sharp, so capacity added into a boom must be absorbed in a downturn.
The bull and bear cases both reduce to one number: aftermarket growth
FRAMING THE DEBATE
Strip away the narrative and the disagreement about HWM is arithmetic about a single variable. The bull says spares at about 21% of revenue and growing 30% is a mix shift that runs for years, because the installed fleet is young, flying more hours than ever, and every engine delivered in the last decade is a stream of replacement-blade sales for decades — and that mix is what produced the 580 basis point margin expansion in Engine Products in Q3. On that view, 36.5× forward earnings is defensible because forward earnings are understated relative to where mix takes them. The bear notes that in Q3 spares grew only in line with the company (30% against 29%), and says the same number is why the risk is lopsided: at 36.5× forward and 39.1× trailing, an investor needs roughly 18.6% compound EPS growth for five years just to earn 10% a year if the multiple falls to 25×. Aftermarket revenue is more durable than OE, but it is not contractual, and its profit falls hard in a traffic downturn. Both sides agree the aftermarket is the asset. They disagree only on what growth rate is already in the price — and at this multiple, quite a lot of it is.
