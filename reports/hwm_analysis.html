01 Company Overview

Howmet Aerospace makes the engineered metal parts that sit in the hottest, most highly stressed places on a commercial aircraft: investment-cast turbine blades and vanes for the hot section of a jet engine (the part downstream of the combustor, where gas temperatures are highest), aerospace fasteners and fluid fittings, forged titanium and aluminium airframe structures, and — in a completely unrelated end market — forged aluminium wheels for heavy trucks. Its customers are the engine makers (GE Aerospace, Safran, RTX/Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce), the airframers (Boeing, Airbus), and the industrial gas turbine builders. FY2025 revenue was $8.252B, up 11.1% year over year; the trailing-twelve-month figure has since accelerated to $9.117B, up 18.1%, and Q2 2026 alone grew 24% to a record $2.547B. The company ranks roughly #247 in the world by market capitalisation at $97.69B.

Two things separate Howmet from a generic aerospace supplier, and both are addressed in detail below. First, an unusually large and fast-growing share of revenue is aftermarket (spare parts sold to replace worn components on engines already flying) rather than OE (original equipment — parts sold into brand-new engines and airframes). Second, the single-crystal turbine blade is one of the genuinely hard things to manufacture in the industrial economy, and the qualification process on an engine programme makes supplier switching close to impossible once you are designed in.

OE (original equipment) revenue is the part Howmet sells into a new engine or a new airframe as it is being built. Aftermarket (or "spares") revenue is the same physical part sold years later as a replacement, when the original one has been consumed by heat and cyclic stress and comes out at an engine shop visit. The two are driven by completely different variables. OE tracks how many aircraft Boeing and Airbus assemble in a given month — a number set by two companies and their supply chains. Aftermarket tracks how many hours the global installed fleet actually flies, which is a much larger, much more diversified and much more persistent base: an engine delivered in 2016 will generate replacement-blade demand for decades regardless of what happens to next year's build rate.
Aftermarket also carries materially higher margin. The tooling, the ceramic cores, the casting moulds and the engine-programme qualification are all sunk costs already paid for during the OE phase; a spare blade sells at a replacement-part price into a customer with no qualified alternative source, so incremental margin is far above the OE margin on the same part. This is the mechanism behind Howmet's margin expansion: Engine Products adjusted EBITDA margin rose 470 basis points year over year to 37.7% in Q2 2026 on 32% revenue growth. Spares grew 37% against total company growth of 24% — meaning mix is actively shifting toward the higher-margin half, and consensus is capitalising that shift rather than the OE cycle.
Why the market pays up: spares growth outpacing total growth by ~13 points means margin expansion is structural, not cyclical — provided flight hours hold.

The core of the company. Engine Products makes airfoils — turbine blades and vanes — plus rolled rings and rotating and structural parts for jet engines and industrial gas turbines. The blades are produced by investment casting (also called lost-wax casting: a wax pattern of the part is coated in ceramic slurry to build a shell, the wax is melted out, and molten nickel superalloy is poured into the resulting cavity). 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Single-Crystal Casting Yield Engine-Programme Qualification >50% IGT Blade Share Aftermarket Sole-Source Decades of Process Know-How Capital & Capacity Scarcity Multi-Decade Programme Life

The moat here is worth explaining mechanically rather than asserting, because the mechanism is unusually literal. A high-pressure turbine blade sits immediately behind the combustor in a gas path whose temperature is above the melting point of the nickel superalloy the blade is made from. It survives because of three things layered together: the blade is hollow, with internal serpentine cooling passages formed by a fragile ceramic core placed inside the wax pattern before shelling; hundreds of laser-drilled film-cooling holes bleed compressor air out across the surface to form an insulating boundary layer; and a ceramic thermal barrier coating is applied on top. Then the casting itself is grown as a single crystal — the mould is withdrawn slowly from the furnace through a helical "pigtail" grain selector so that exactly one crystal orientation propagates up the entire part. There are no grain boundaries, because grain boundaries are precisely where creep and fatigue cracks nucleate when a part is held at 1,000°C-plus while spinning under tens of thousands of g of centrifugal load. Getting all of that right simultaneously, at scale, with acceptable scrap rates, is the constraint — the process is not secret, but the accumulated yield knowledge is, and yield is what separates a profitable caster from an unprofitable one.

Layered on top is qualification. A part on a certified engine programme is qualified to a specific supplier, a specific plant, a specific process route, and often a specific furnace. Changing any of those requires re-qualification with the engine OEM and, ultimately, the airworthiness authority — a multi-year exercise involving test castings, metallurgical validation, and in some cases engine testing. No engine maker undertakes that mid-programme to save a few points on a blade that represents a small fraction of engine cost but 100% of the risk of an uncontained failure. The practical effect is that once Howmet is designed into a programme, it holds that content for the life of the programme, which for a narrowbody engine is measured in decades — and it holds the spares revenue from that content too, which is where the profit is. The IGT position is the same mechanism in a different end market: over 50% global share in industrial gas turbine blades, at a moment when CEO John Plant describes demand as "extraordinary" and customers are queueing for capacity Howmet has not built yet.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Extreme customer concentration. The buyer set is effectively six names — GE Aerospace, Safran (jointly, via CFM), RTX/Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce on engines, Boeing and Airbus on airframes. A production-rate decision taken in Renton or Toulouse propagates directly into Howmet's OE order book with no diversification available. The same concentration that makes qualification a moat makes the revenue line hostage to a handful of planning decisions, and it gives those customers real leverage in long-term agreement negotiations.
02
Valuation leaves no room for a build-rate stumble. At 52.8× trailing earnings the stock is priced for the aftermarket mix shift to continue compounding. Boeing was only cleared by the FAA to move from 38 to 42 737 MAX per month in October 2025 and is targeting 47 during 2026; Airbus is pushing toward 75 A320-family aircraft a month by end-2027. Those ramps have slipped repeatedly before. A slip does not immediately hit spares, but it hits OE, sentiment and the multiple at once — the 7.5% single-day decline on August 31, 2026 is a demonstration of how the stock trades when the market re-prices growth expectations.
03
Aftermarket depends on flight hours, not on contracts. Spares are durable relative to OE, but they are not contractual annuities. If global departures fall — a recession, a fuel shock, a pandemic-type event — airlines defer shop visits, park older aircraft and green-time engines rather than overhaul them. Replacement-blade demand can fall faster than airline capacity does, and the segment carrying the highest margin is then the one that de-levers hardest.
04
Capacity expansion and execution risk. Capex exceeds $500M in 2026 with a "significant" further increase signalled for 2027, plus new plants in Japan and Europe. Bringing a new investment-casting facility to qualified production yield is slow and expensive, and the interim period consumes cash while depressing returns. Howmet is also concentrating more capital into industrial gas turbines, a market growing 38% today on data-centre and grid demand but with its own history of violent order cycles.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Howmet is compounding earnings at 35%+ with 23% ROIC in a business that is genuinely difficult to enter, and the mix is shifting toward the most profitable and most durable revenue it has: spares grew 37% versus 24% for the company overall and now represent 22% of first-half revenue. Every guidance line was raised in August. Narrowbody build rates are still well below where Boeing and Airbus intend them to be, so the OE half has years of visible ramp ahead, while industrial gas turbines — where Howmet holds over 50% global blade share — are growing 38% on power demand that has nothing to do with aviation. Free cash flow of $1.79B funds capacity expansion, a 17% dividend raise and $800M of buybacks simultaneously.

Bear Case

At 52.8× trailing and 46.0× forward earnings, the price already contains the aftermarket thesis and several years of build-rate increases that have not happened yet. Arithmetic: to deliver a 10% annual return over five years while the multiple normalises to 25× — still a premium industrial multiple — EPS must compound at roughly 24% a year for five straight years. Consensus has 41% in 2026 then 20% in 2027, so the required path assumes no deceleration at all thereafter. Alternatively, if EPS grows at 10% and the multiple holds at 46×, the return is 10% — but multiples that high rarely hold. Meanwhile six customers control the order book, the airframers have missed rate targets repeatedly, and the August 31 decline of 7.5% shows what happens to a crowded, richly valued name when the story is questioned.

Top 3 Risks

1
Valuation compression. The dominant risk is not that the business deteriorates but that the multiple does. A move from 46× forward earnings to a still-generous 30× is a 35% price decline with no change to a single operating number.
2
Build-rate slippage at Boeing or Airbus. The OE half of the business depends on ramps that both airframers have publicly missed before. Boeing only regained FAA permission to exceed 38 737 MAX per month in October 2025; Airbus's 75/month A320 target for end-2027 is described as urgent precisely because it is at risk. Concentration means there is no offsetting customer.
3
Flight-hour shock to the aftermarket. The high-margin spares stream is durable relative to OE but is not contracted. A traffic downturn causes airlines to defer shop visits and park aircraft, and replacement-part demand falls faster than capacity does — which would compress the exact revenue line the current multiple is capitalising.
  Industry Commentary
Narrowbody build rates are the binding constraint on the whole supply chain
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Everything upstream of Boeing and Airbus final assembly is sized to a build rate those two companies set. As of this snapshot, Boeing has FAA clearance for 42 737 MAX per month — granted October 17, 2025, lifting the 38/month cap imposed in early 2024 after the door-plug incident — and is targeting 47/month during 2026. Airbus is trying to reach 75 A320-family aircraft per month by the end of 2027, a rate at which it is already sold out through 2032, with 83/month under study; reporting suggests the company will be oversold as early as next year if that ramp slips. Widebodies are moving too: Boeing is pushing the 787 toward rate 10 and Airbus expects the A350 to go from 5–6 to 8–9 per month within a year. For Howmet, every one of those units is engine airfoils, hundreds of thousands of fasteners and structural forgings. The constraint is real in both directions — it caps how fast OE revenue can grow, and it means any slip is felt immediately.
Industrial gas turbines have become a second growth engine
EMERGING TAILWIND
Gas turbine revenue grew 38% in Q2 2026 — faster than commercial aerospace at 28% and far faster than defence at 11%. Howmet holds over 50% global share in industrial gas turbine blades, and CEO John Plant described demand as "extraordinary," disclosing that negotiations with seven major customers had been completed and that some are asking for capacity beyond what has been contracted. The physical product is nearly the same as an aero turbine blade — single-crystal nickel superalloy castings — so the capability transfers directly, but the demand driver is electricity generation, not air travel. That is a genuine diversification of the cycle. It is also why capex is rising above $500M in 2026 with a significant further increase in 2027, and why new plants are planned in Japan and Europe. The caveat is that IGT order cycles have historically been violent, and capacity added into a boom is capacity that must be absorbed in a downturn.
The bull and bear cases both reduce to one number: aftermarket growth
FRAMING THE DEBATE
Strip away the narrative and the disagreement about HWM is arithmetic about a single variable. The bull says spares at 22% of first-half revenue growing 37% while the total grows 24% is a mix shift that runs for years, because the installed fleet is young, flying more hours than ever, and every engine delivered in the last decade is an annuity of replacement blades — and that mix shift is what produced the 470 basis point margin expansion in Engine Products. On that view, 46× forward earnings is defensible because forward earnings are understated relative to where mix takes them. The bear says the same number is why the risk is asymmetric: at 46× forward and 52.8× trailing, an investor needs roughly 24% compound EPS growth for five years just to earn 10% a year if the multiple normalises to 25×. Aftermarket revenue is more durable than OE, but it is not contractual, and it de-levers hard in a traffic downturn. Both sides agree the aftermarket is the asset. They disagree only on what growth rate is already in the price — and at this multiple, quite a lot of it is.
