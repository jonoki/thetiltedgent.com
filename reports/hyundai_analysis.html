01 Company Overview

Hyundai sells about four million cars a year — Hyundai and Genesis badges, petrol, hybrid, electric and hydrogen — from plants in Korea, the US, India and elsewhere, and lends money to many of the people who buy them. In 2025 it booked ₩186.3 trillion of revenue and ₩11.5 trillion of operating profit, a 6.2% margin. In the third quarter of 2026 (July–September) revenue fell 3.4% to ₩45.11 trillion as strikes and fewer working days cut wholesale vehicle sales 11.3%, yet operating profit rose 5.5% to ₩2.68 trillion, a 5.9% margin, helped by favorable exchange rates, cost cuts and hybrids, which were 18.2% of sales. The gain was thin: group operating profit rose only ₩0.14 trillion, and on October 29 the company cut its 2026 guidance, lowering the operating-margin range to 5.5–6.5% from 6.3–7.3% and the wholesale target to 4.0 million cars from 4.16 million.

For a few months in 2026 the stock market stopped pricing Hyundai as a carmaker. Boston Dynamics, the Hyundai Motor Group robotics company, put its humanoid robot Atlas on stage at CES in January, and the shares rose 13.8% on January 7 alone (The Korea Times). By June 1 they had traded as high as ₩783,000, more than 2.6 times the 2025 year-end close of ₩296,500. Then the car business reasserted itself: falling sales, a weak second quarter, the first full-scale strike in a decade, a stronger won, a run of broker target cuts and, on October 29, a cut to the year's guidance. On November 9 the shares closed at ₩271,500 — 65.3% below that intraday high, 21.6% below the previous edition's October 2 close and 2.8% above a year earlier. The whole Korean market sold off in the same weeks: the KOSPI, the Korea Exchange's main index, fell 28.3% from October 2 to November 9 (7,003.74 to 5,018.85). The question for this report is how much of the robot story survives in a price that is now back near nine times trailing earnings.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hybrid + EV + ICE + hydrogen lineup US share in the 6% range, 6 straight quarters US plants (HMMA, HMGMA) Captive finance arm Group supply chain (Mobis, Kia) Boston Dynamics robotics

Carmaking is a scale business, and Hyundai's advantage is breadth. It sells every kind of powertrain, so when US buyers swung to hybrids — the company cites high fuel prices, and the CFO pointed on the call to what he called the abolition of the US IRA — it could ship more hybrids rather than rebuild its lineup; that is how US share reached a record 6.6% in the third quarter, in a market down 1.3% (company presentation). It builds cars inside the US at its Alabama (HMMA) and Georgia (HMGMA) plants, which matters more with a 15% tariff on imports, and the group has committed $26B of new US investment over 2025–2028, per the investor-day deck. Its own lender finances a large share of the cars it sells. The weakness is that none of this makes it unusually profitable: a 5–6% operating margin is the margin of a company that competes on price and volume.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Tariffs take a third of the profit. The ₩4.11T tariff cost in 2025 compares with ₩11.47T of operating profit that year. Local production cuts the cost only as fast as new US capacity comes on, and the CFO said on the Q3 call that he expects a similar bill this year (The Korea Herald).
02
Losing ground at home. Domestic sales fell 10.8% in the first half; in April Kia briefly overtook Hyundai as Korea's best-selling carmaker for the first time in nearly three decades, and Tesla's first-half Korean sales rose 192.2% to 56,139 against Hyundai's 39,575 EVs (The Korea Times). In September, domestic sales were 25.7% lower than a year earlier; third-quarter domestic wholesale fell 27.2% to 131,468 and January–October domestic sales were 15.6% lower (497,223 against 589,280). In October Kia's domestic sales (51,007) exceeded Hyundai's (49,042) (MoneyToday).
03
Labour. The union staged its first full-scale strike in a decade on August 21. Industry estimates put the disruption at 55,200 vehicles and more than ₩2.3T of lost sales (The Korea Times). The deal ratified on August 31 includes a bonus of 400% of monthly base pay plus ₩12.7M and 15 shares per worker, and the union had sought job guarantees as robots and AI come onto assembly lines (Korea Herald). The Q3 release names the strike among the causes of the 11.3% fall in wholesale volume.
04
The robot premium is fragile. NH Investment & Securities wrote in July that "progress is slower than the market had expected" on humanoids and autonomous driving (Seoul Economic Daily). Kiwoom's Shin Yoon-chul said foreign ownership had fallen from over 35% in January to below 25%, "a historically unusual level of foreign capital outflow" (The Korea Times). After the Q3 results he added that the group has now said it has no plans for overseas-subsidiary IPOs, a hope that had helped lift the shares in the first half (Asia Business Daily).

Notable One-Off Items

Four items shape the recent figures. First, the currency boost in Q3: in the automotive business favorable exchange rates added about ₩1.3T to profit and cost cuts about ₩0.5T, against ₩1.1T lost to lower volumes, ₩0.6T to a weaker sales mix and ₩0.5T to dearer raw materials (company presentation; Asia Business Daily). Group operating profit rose only ₩0.14T (₩2.68T against ₩2.54T), so the exchange-rate gain was larger than the whole increase. Second, the strike: Hanwha Investment & Securities put the output lost to the July and August strikes at about 55,000 vehicles (The Herald Business); the CFO said the disruption also cut shipments to overseas markets (The Korea Herald), and the company said domestic production began returning to normal in September (The Korea Times). Third, the second-quarter shocks: engine-valve supply to Hyundai's largest Korean plant was cut by a fire at a supplier — Korea Investment & Securities' Kim Chang-ho named it as the Ahn Safety Industry Daejeon plant (Seoul Economic Daily) — and a separate fire at a Hyundai Mobis plant in India briefly stopped Hyundai's Indian factory. Fourth, the Indian listing: Hyundai sold 17.5% of Hyundai Motor India in 2024, when the subsidiary listed in India (first trading day October 22, 2024); the sale added ₩3.07T to capital surplus rather than to profit, and Hyundai still owns 82.5%.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 9, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of ₩208,000 to the November 9, 2026 close of ₩271,500, the shares rose 30.5% in won, before dividends. For a dollar investor the won's fall from ₩1,167 to ₩1,342 per dollar over the same window cut that to about +13.6% (our calculation from Yahoo's KRW=X closes). For four years the stock was a ₩150,000–₩300,000 carmaker; then it became something else. January 2026 was the best month in the series (+68.6%), and March 2026 (−33.9%) and June 2026 (−31.5%) the two worst. The highest month-end, ₩723,000 in May 2026, is more than 2.6 times today's price.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The robot premium is mostly gone, so the price pays for the car business: 9.4 times trailing earnings, 7.3 times this year's consensus, a 3.7% dividend yield with a ₩10,000 floor. Third-quarter operating profit rose even though Hyundai sold 11% fewer cars, and the brokers that cut targets after the print kept their Buy ratings, pointing to hybrids, new models and robotics. The supplier fire and the strike are behind it, domestic production began returning to normal in September, and the company expects the fourth quarter to bring higher sales of the new Avante and Tucson, the Genesis GV90 and a GV80 hybrid. If the margin lands inside the cut guidance of 5.5–6.5% and Atlas production starts on time in 2028, today's price includes the robots almost for free.

Bear Case

The guidance has now been cut once: the margin range fell to 5.5–6.5% and the wholesale target to 4.0 million cars, and the Q3 profit rise came from the exchange rate (about ₩1.3T in the car business) rather than from selling more — group operating profit rose only ₩0.14T on 11% fewer cars. A stronger won and dearer raw materials threaten next year's earnings, and the company is reviewing its ₩4T buyback plan. The 15% US tariff, Chinese EVs in Europe and Tesla in Korea press on price. The average analyst target (₩529,682) is still 95% above the price and assumes a recovery the reported numbers do not yet show. The robot business is years from revenue, and NH says it is moving slower than the market expected. Foreign ownership fell from over 35% in January to below 25% by July (Kiwoom).

Top 3 Risks

01
Guidance cut again. On October 29 the company cut its 2026 operating-margin guidance to 5.5–6.5% from 6.3–7.3%, citing higher fixed costs per car from lower output and dearer raw materials, and said the change does not signal weaker competitiveness. The fourth quarter now has to earn roughly 4.8–8.7% for the year to land in the range (our calculation, Section 02), and Hyundai expects raw-material costs to stay high and US tariffs to keep weighing (The Korea Herald).
02
Currency turns from help to hurt. Favorable exchange rates added about ₩1.3T to Q3 profit in the car business, more than the whole ₩0.14T rise in group operating profit. At ₩1,341.83 per dollar on November 9 (Yahoo Finance KRW=X) a dollar buys about 5.7% fewer won than the Q3 average of ₩1,423 (The Korea Times), and Korea Investment & Securities' Kim Chang-ho warned in October that a stronger won could limit the fourth-quarter recovery (The Herald Business).
03
Losing share at home and in Europe. Domestic wholesale fell 27.2% in Q3 and domestic sales were down 15.6% for January–October; Kia outsold Hyundai in Korea in October (MoneyToday). Europe wholesale fell 18.0% in Q3 (company presentation), after 10.9% in Q2. The CFO said incentives were raised in Q2 in part because of the aggressive entry of Chinese EVs into Europe, and on the Q3 call that Hyundai answers Tesla and Chinese makers in Korea with financing offers and its service network rather than price cuts (The Korea Herald). Meritz Securities said in September that Hyundai's competitive position is deteriorating in Korea, the US and India (BusinessKorea).
Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the move to build in America
CURRENT HEADWIND

Korean cars entering the US now pay a 15% tariff (The Korea Times). Hyundai's answer is to build more where it sells: US plants in Alabama and Georgia, a group commitment of $26B of new US investment from 2025 to 2028, a new low-carbon Hyundai steel mill in Louisiana and an 80% parts-localisation target for 2030, per the August investor-day deck. The arithmetic is still daunting: 2025's ₩4.11T tariff bill was more than a third of operating profit, and the CFO said on the Q3 call that this year's should be similar (The Korea Herald).

Hybrids win, EVs stall, China competes
MIXED

With high fuel prices (company deck) and what the CFO called the abolition of the US IRA, buyers have moved to hybrids — Hyundai's global EV sales fell 12.7% in Q2 while hybrids rose 11.3%, according to the call; in Q3 EV sales fell 9.2% to 69,117 while hybrids rose 3.8% to 167,414 (company release). A carmaker that sells every powertrain can follow demand; one that bet only on EVs cannot. The competition has changed shape, though: Chinese EV makers are pushing into Europe, where Hyundai raised incentives, and Tesla, selling mostly the China-built Model Y, outsold Hyundai's whole EV range in Korea in the first half (The Korea Times). The bull framing: hybrids carry better margins and Hyundai has the lineup. The bear framing: price competition is spreading to every market Hyundai depends on.

Physical AI and the robot premium that didn't last
LONG-TERM BET

For half a year, investors valued Hyundai as a robotics company that happens to build cars. The company leans into the framing — its own profile now says it is "accelerating its transformation beyond mobility through advanced robotics and physical AI" — and its investor-day deck shows robotics, on-demand mobility and software-defined vehicles among the industry's main sources of profit in 2035. The market has since asked for proof: Kiwoom's Shin Yoon-chul said "the catalyst that brings foreign investors back is unlikely to be expectations for new businesses" (The Korea Times). Hana's Song Sun-jae lists what would bring the premium back: Boston Dynamics' robot application centre, a production entity, a parts supply chain and the SDV Pace Car (Seoul Economic Daily). Until then, the stock trades on cars.
