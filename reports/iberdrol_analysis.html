01 Company Overview

Iberdrola is a Spanish electricity group that owns and runs two very different kinds of asset. The first is power grids — the wires that carry electricity to homes in Spain, the UK (ScottishPower and Electricity North West), the US Northeast (Avangrid: New York, Maine, Connecticut) and Brazil (Neoenergia). Grids are regulated: a regulator lets the owner earn an agreed return on what it has invested. The second is Power & Customers — 56,599 MW of generating capacity at June 2026, 87% of it emission-free (wind, hydro, solar, nuclear), plus electricity and gas supply to millions of retail customers. In the first nine months of 2026 the group booked €34.41B of revenue, €11.89B of adjusted EBITDA (+7.5%) and €5.82B of reported net profit (+9.6%), which includes a €953M one-off gain on selling its Mexican business in the first half.

The story of the last two years is a deliberate change of mix. At its September 24, 2025 Capital Markets Day Executive Chairman Ignacio Galán said: "This plan aims to transform Iberdrola's profile into a more regulated company, with networks as a vector for growth." The moves since then line up with that sentence: Iberdrola bought out the 18.4% of Avangrid it did not own (merger completed December 23, 2024), consolidated Electricity North West from March 2025, raised €5B of new equity in July 2025, sold Mexico for an enterprise value (the price of the shares plus the debt taken on) of about $4.2B (closed April 24, 2026), took Neoenergia to 100% (buyout of the last minority shareholders completed May 15, 2026) and on July 21, 2026 agreed to buy 80% of Caruna, Finland's largest distribution grid, at about €5B enterprise value. The regulated asset base reached about €56B in September 2026, up 14%, and Iberdrola now targets more than €90B by 2030. On October 21, with the nine-month results, it raised its 2026 goal to at least 10% growth in adjusted net profit and set a new one of about 10% a year on average over the next five years. The market has rewarded the shift: the shares closed 2025 at €18.465 and stand at €21.97, up 28.4% over twelve months, above the €20.71 average target of the 27 analysts on Iberdrola's own table (read October 5).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Licensed grid monopolies in 4 countries €56B regulated asset base Pumped-storage hydro in Iberia Scale in offshore wind BBB+ / Baa1 funding access Asset-rotation partners (e.g. GIC)

A distribution grid cannot be competed away: nobody builds a second set of wires down the same street, so the owner holds a licensed monopoly and the regulator, not the market, sets its return. Iberdrola's plan cites a 9.5% average allowed ROE across frameworks already closed or advanced, Spain's 2026–2031 rate is 6.58%, and Caruna is expected to earn about 8%. The other edge is funding: Iberdrola describes itself as the world's leading private issuer of green bonds, its BBB+/Baa1 ratings keep it in investment-grade markets (cost of debt excluding Neoenergia, its Brazilian arm: 3.50% in H1 2026), and it sells minority stakes in finished assets to long-term investors (GIC in Brazilian transmission) to recycle capital. In generation, pumped-storage hydro in Spain is a hard-to-copy asset: it buys cheap midday solar power and sells it back in the evening. The vulnerability is the mirror image of the moat: a regulated return is a political decision, revisited every five years or so, and Iberdrola's growth depends on regulators in London, Albany, Brasília and Madrid keeping that return attractive while customer bills rise.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage rising with the build-out. Adjusted net debt rose from €50.18B at December 2025 to €53.97B at June, driven by investment, currency and repurchasing a hybrid bond (a very long-dated bond that rating agencies count partly as equity), and to €54.6B at September; FFO to net debt is 22.4%, down from 23.1% a year earlier. Caruna adds about €2B of equity outlay plus its own debt. Net debt equals about 3.4 years of EBITDA; there is room inside BBB+, but not much for an investment programme that keeps growing.
02
Regulatory resets. UK distribution moves to ED3, the regulator's next price control for local grids (negotiation starts this year), Spain has just set 6.58% for 2026–2031, and US returns are decided case by case. A lower allowed return on a €56B base flows straight into earnings, and affordability is a live political issue in every market Iberdrola serves.
03
Currency translation. About 83% of the regulated asset base sits in pounds, dollars and reais (UK 32%, US 28%, Brazil 23%; Spain 17%). In H1 2026 currencies cut €219M from adjusted net profit — the difference between +8% and +14% growth; over nine months, between +8.8% and +13%. Brazilian debt also costs more: the group's cost of debt including Neoenergia rose to 5.13% from 4.69%.
04
Generation is price-exposed. Power & Customers is still 45% of EBITDA and fell 2% in the nine months, after a 3% fall in the first quarter. Iberia margins depend on wholesale prices, hydro conditions and Spanish levies; the rest-of-world unit fell 18% in the half.

Notable One-Off Items

Iberdrola's reported and adjusted numbers diverge every period, so read both. 9M 2026: reported net profit of €5,816.1M against adjusted €5,140.5M; the gap is mostly the Mexico gain booked in the first half. H1 2026: reported net profit of €4,336.4M includes a €953.3M net gain on the Mexico sale and €74.8M of Mexico's contribution before the sale; adjusted net profit (€3,564.9M) removes the gain and adds back €181.8M of UK capital allowances (tax relief on investment). H1 2025: adjusted profit excluded €389.2M of US past costs recognised with no cash impact. The financial result also swung: net financial expense rose €508M to €1,113M, largely because 2025 had a €282M one-off gain on East Anglia 3 derivatives. Currency moves cut adjusted net profit by €218.6M. Finally, the share count moves every half-year: scrip dividends add shares (98.1M in July 2026) and capital reductions cancel them (to 6,575,000,000 on July 1, 2026), leaving 6,673,141,516 shares since July 2026. The proposed €0.275 interim payment will again be offered in shares or cash.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of €10.22 to the October 27, 2026 settled close of €21.97, Iberdrola's share price rose 115%, before dividends. The path had two halves. For almost three years the stock went sideways between roughly €9.6 and €12.0: the lowest month-end of the window was €9.582 in September 2022, and as late as February 2024 it closed at €10.62. Then it re-rated: from €12.11 in June 2024 it climbed to €18.465 at the end of 2025 and to a highest month-end of €21.84 at the end of June 2026. It eased to €20.08 by August, with the July 2026 supplementary dividend of €0.427 going ex during July, and the October 27 close of €21.97 is now the highest point on the chart.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how one-sided the climb was: it was above 70 at 21 of the 23 month-ends from August 2024 to June 2026 and peaked at 91.7 in February 2026, the highest reading in the series. It fell below 70 from July to September 2026 and is back above it at 72.9 on the October 27 close. The price sits above its 10-month moving average (about €20.33) and its 3-month average (about €20.89).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Iberdrola is turning into a grid company at the moment grids became the bottleneck of electrification. Its regulated asset base grew 14% in a year to about €56B, and Iberdrola now targets more than €90B by 2030, with an average allowed ROE of 9.5% on the frameworks cited in its 2025 plan. Networks EBITDA grew 15% in the nine months while generation fell 2%, so the mix is shifting toward earnings that do not depend on power prices. Management has raised 2026 guidance twice (to above 8% in April, to at least 10% in October) and now aims for about 10% a year on average over the next five years; Caruna adds a AA+ country at about 8% ROE, paid for with Mexico proceeds rather than new shares. A 3.12% dividend growing with earnings (the proposed interim is up 8.7%), 22.6x trailing earnings and a pipeline of 15.5 GW of new capacity priced against higher PPA prices make the case that the stock still compounds from here.

Bear Case

The re-rating has already happened. The stock more than doubled in five years and rose 28% in one, and it now trades about 6% above the €20.71 average target on Iberdrola's analyst table (read October 5), where two-thirds of brokers rate it Hold. Growth is being bought with balance sheet: adjusted net debt rose from €50.18B to €54.6B in the nine months, FFO/net debt fell to 22.4% from 23.1% a year earlier, the cost of debt including Brazil rose to 5.13%, and the company issued €5B of equity in 2025. Every euro of growth depends on regulators in four countries keeping returns high while bills rise, and the currency drag (−€219M of profit in one half) is structural for a group with most of its assets outside the euro. At about 22x adjusted earnings, a utility needs everything to go right.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory and political risk on returns. UK distribution enters the ED3 negotiation, US returns are set in rate cases, Spain's 6.58% runs to 2031 and Brazil's concessions were just renewed for 30 years. A cut in allowed returns, or a windfall-style levy (the UK Electricity Generator Levy and Spain's IVPEE are recent examples), hits a business that has committed €58B of investment on today's terms.
02
Balance-sheet stretch. Adjusted net debt of €54.6B, 3.4x EBITDA, with Caruna's ~€2B outlay and a growing investment plan ahead. The BBB+ rating is the funding advantage; slipping to the BBB band would raise the cost of every new grid euro.
03
Valuation leaves little room. At €21.97 the shares sit above the average and median targets on Iberdrola's analyst table and 0.5% below the 52-week high. A softer plan at the 2027 Capital Markets Day, or a slip from the new 10%-a-year goal, could matter more than the earnings themselves.
Industry Commentary
The grid build-out is now policy on both sides of the Atlantic
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Iberdrola's H1 2026 presentation lists the tailwinds by market: the EU Grids Package and an Electrification Action Plan targeting a 46% electricity share of final energy by 2040; in the UK, RIIO-T3 transmission from April 2026 and the system operator's estimate (quoted by Iberdrola) that UK grids need £89B of investment; in Brazil, 30-year concession renewals supporting €9B of Neoenergia investment to 2030; in New York, $2.2B of additional network investment approved for the next twelve months. For a regulated owner, every approved pound or dollar of investment becomes asset base that earns a return for decades.

An energy-price shock in 2026
MIXED

Iberdrola's half-year report describes a conflict between the US and Iran from the beginning of March 2026 that pushed interest rates higher; oil averaged $92.3 a barrel in H1 2026 against $71.9 a year earlier, and European gas (TTF) €42.9/MWh. The EU responded with a temporary state-aid framework for electricity-intensive industry (May 5, 2026) and Spain with Royal Decree-Law 18/2026, which phases the IVPEE generation tax down to 0% from 2028. For Iberdrola the effects pull both ways: higher power prices help unhedged renewable output and PPA pricing, while higher rates raise the cost of the debt that funds its grids and make a 3.1% dividend yield less distinctive.

Asset rotation: selling what is finished, buying what is regulated
STRATEGY

The 2025–2028 plan counts on €13B of asset rotation and partnerships, of which management said 75% was already done in September 2025. The pattern since is consistent: Mexico (thermal and renewables, ~$4.2B EV) sold; stakes in Brazilian transmission sold down to GIC while keeping 51%; businesses in Hungary and France sold. On the buying side: Electricity North West, the minorities in Avangrid and Neoenergia, and Caruna (agreed, to be paid for with the Mexico proceeds). The bull reading is that Iberdrola is swapping price-exposed earnings for regulated ones at sensible prices. The bear reading is that the remaining generation book becomes a smaller, more volatile tail on a utility that the market now prices like a grid owner.
