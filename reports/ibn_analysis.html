01 Company Overview

ICICI began in 1955 as a development lender set up at the initiative of the World Bank, the Government of India and Indian industry. ICICI Bank was incorporated in 1994 as part of the group, and in 2002 the parent merged into the bank. ICICI itself was the first Indian company to list on the New York Stock Exchange, in 1999; the bank's own ADS followed in March 2000, each worth two ₹2 shares. Today the bank has ₹24.3 trillion (≈$253B) of standalone assets, ₹18.3 trillion of deposits and 7,608 branches at June 30, 2026, and by market value it ranks behind HDFC Bank and ahead of State Bank of India at $97.6B. The home line trades on the NSE as ICICIBANK. The bank reports in rupees under Indian GAAP for a year ending March; the latest reported period is Q1 FY2027 (April–June 2026), released on Saturday, July 18, 2026.

The quarter was the kind a bank investor wants and rarely gets: loans +19.6% year on year while the net interest margin rose to 4.36% (4.34% a year earlier), and profit after tax grew 15.9% to ₹148.05bn — 11% above consensus estimates, Business Standard reported. Growth at that pace usually costs margin or credit quality; here the gross NPA ratio fell to 1.38% from 1.67%. Yet the ADR ended the window below its last close before the print — $27.44 against $29.76 on July 17 — and 19.9% under its May 2025 month-end peak, while the rupee's slide toward ₹96 per dollar has turned a strong rupee record into a middling dollar one. CEO Sandeep Bakhshi, in the job since October 2018, starts a new two-year term on October 4, 2026, approved by shareholders at the August 21 AGM.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

7,608-branch deposit franchise NIM 4.36% Net NPA 0.35% ₹131bn contingency buffer CET1 16.19% AMC, insurance & broking arms Digital platforms (iLens, InstaBIZ)

ICICI's edge is the combination of a cheap, granular deposit base and underwriting that has kept bad loans low while the book grew. Return on assets was 2.49% (annualised, Q1; 2.32% for FY2026), and it sits on top of a ₹131bn contingency provision — about 0.8% of advances — that is not tied to any loan and is there to absorb a bad year. Bakhshi frames the strategy as "growing profit before tax excluding treasury through the 360-degree customer centric approach" — selling every product to the same customer rather than chasing loan volume — and fee income (+23.5%) is where that shows. The vulnerabilities are the price of that growth and the rules it runs under.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Growth outrunning the cycle. Business banking +28.2%, rural +35.4%, overseas +52.5%. Banerjee says average loan growth "will be somewhat lower" than the 19.6% period-end figure and that part of it is base effect. Fast-growing small-business and rural books are where the next credit cycle would show first.
02
Regulatory friction. The RBI-directed ₹12.83bn provision on mis-classified farm loans is a reminder that India's supervisor reviews loan-by-loan. In late September 2026 the bank received two GST show-cause notices (disclosed September 25 and October 1) over services to customers maintaining minimum balances — ₹229.1 crore (about ₹2.29bn) plus interest and penalty, and ₹16.8 crore including interest and penalty — small against quarterly profit of ₹148bn, but the same theme of fees under scrutiny.
03
Margin squeeze ahead. About 57% of domestic loans reset with the RBI repo rate or other external benchmarks, so rate cuts reprice assets faster than deposits. Banerjee's guidance is that margins "should be range bound" only "assuming no policy rate movements", and that with the FCNR build-up "there could be some impact on the NIM" as overseas branches' balance sheets expand.
04
Accounting regime change. India's move to expected-credit-loss (ECL) provisioning will require provisions on Stage 2 loans that, in Banerjee's words, are "currently not made by any bank". On the one-off hit: "On the net worth, we will not really have any impact." The recurring cost was not quantified.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The numbers of a well-run bank: 2.49% ROA, 17.1% ROE, NIM 4.36% and up on the year, net NPAs 0.35%, CET1 16.19% and a ₹131bn buffer that belongs to no loan. Loan growth has re-accelerated to 19.6% without a credit-cost bill, fee income is up 23.5%, profit beat consensus by 11%, and the FCNR window gives it dollar funding below wholesale rates. At 17x trailing earnings and 2.6x book it is priced as a quality bank — JM Financial expects it to "continue to command a premium valuation among large banks" (as quoted by Business Standard) — and every one of 42 NSE analysts rates it a buy, the CEO's term is renewed, and LIC has permission to buy up to 9.99%.

Bear Case

Loans are growing 19.6% while deposits grow 14.0% — that gap closes either by slowing growth or by paying up for money. A majority of loans reprice with the repo rate, so rate cuts bite the margin first, and management itself expects FCNR balances to weigh on NIM. The fastest-growing books — business banking, rural, overseas — are the least seasoned, and the RBI has already found compliance problems in the farm book. Unanimous buy ratings leave little room for upgrades, and for a dollar holder the rupee's 22% five-year fall cut a +63.4% rupee gain to +29.7%.

Top 3 Risks

01
Credit cycle in the fast lanes. Business banking +28.2%, rural +35.4%, overseas +52.5%. Today's 0.32% credit cost was helped by a recovery; management's own normal is about 0.50%. A turn in small-business or farm conditions would show here first.
02
Margin. 57% of domestic loans are linked to external benchmarks; the "range bound" margin guidance assumes no rate moves, and management expects the FCNR build-up to weigh on NIM. ECL provisioning adds a recurring cost the bank has not sized.
03
Currency. Earnings and dividends are in rupees; the ADS is in dollars. ₹74.87 → ₹96.32 per USD over five years cost a US holder about 22% before any change in the business — the dollar dividend per ADS fell from $0.257 (August 2025) to $0.249 (August 2026) even as the rupee dividend rose from ₹11 to ₹12 per share.
Industry Commentary
Indian credit growth is back — and the banks are funding it from abroad
EMERGING TAILWIND

Banerjee describes system loan growth picking up "over the last 2-3 quarters as the various policy measures, both on the fiscal side and the monetary side have taken effect", with corporates shifting from bond markets to banks. The RBI's swap facility for FCNR(B) foreign-currency deposits lets banks gather dollar deposits at an all-in cost he puts below wholesale rates; ICICI alone had raised about $17.88B by August 31. The bull reading: a growth cycle with a funding valve, led by the banks with the cleanest books. The bear reading: funding growth with dollar deposits and dollar bonds — ICICI issued about $3.55B of US-dollar bonds in July–August alone — adds balance-sheet size faster than margin.

A supervisor that reads the fine print
ONGOING HEADWIND

The RBI's annual supervisory review led to a ₹12.83bn provision against farm loans classed as priority-sector lending on terms that were not fully compliant; tax authorities are pursuing GST on minimum-balance services; and the switch to expected-credit-loss provisioning will raise recurring provisions across the sector by requiring them on Stage 2 loans. For ICICI the sums are small against ₹148bn of quarterly profit. The pattern matters more than any one item: priority-sector farm lending and fee-earning retail accounts are both under regulatory and tax scrutiny.

Competition from state banks
STRUCTURAL

An analyst on the July call noted that public-sector banks have used up much of their liquidity and loan-to-deposit buffers. Banerjee's response was to point to ICICI's own franchise: "Our belief is that our franchise should deliver more," adding that some loans are being quoted at rates ICICI will not match. With State Bank of India at $91.4B of market value, close behind ICICI's $97.6B, the contest for corporate and deposit market share is between two models: the state bank's balance sheet against the private bank's pricing discipline.
