01 What It Measures

The policy interest rate is the rate the Bank of Canada wants Canada's big financial institutions to charge each other on one-day loans. It is a decision, not a measurement: the Bank's Governing Council sets it on eight fixed dates a year, and the Bank calls it "the starting point for setting many of the interest rates in the economy that matter for Canadians," including the prime rate banks use for mortgages and lines of credit, and the rates paid on deposits and GICs.

On October 7, 2026 the target was 2.25%, where it has been since October 30, 2025. Governing Council left it there at its seven decisions from December 10, 2025 to September 2, 2026.

More: the Bank's own definition

Valet series V39079: "Also called the policy interest rate, the average rate that the Bank of Canada wants to see in the market for overnight money market financing."

The Bank's explainer: "the primary tool we use to control inflation is our target for the overnight rate—also called our policy interest rate." The overnight market is where institutions lend to each other for one day to settle the day's payments: "The Bank sets a target for the interest rate we want financial institutions to charge each other when they make these overnight loans."

02 How It's Built

Who decides. Governing Council. For the September 2, 2026 decision its meetings began August 25 with Governor Tiff Macklem, Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers and Deputy Governors Toni Gravelle, Nicolas Vincent, Michelle Alexopoulos and Marc-André Gosselin (the Bank's summary of deliberations, published September 16). The decision goes out in a press release; four of the eight dates also bring the Monetary Policy Report (MPR), the Bank's projection for inflation and growth. Since 2021 a change takes effect the day after its announcement, which is why the Valet series shows the October 29, 2025 cut on October 30. The Bank can also move between dates, as it did on March 13 and March 27, 2020.

How the Bank holds the market to it. Two standing rates frame the target, an operating band: institutions can leave money with the Bank overnight at the deposit rate or borrow from it at the Bank Rate. The Bank runs a "floor system, because the target is at or just above the floor of the operating band." The band is now 30 basis points wide (a basis point is 0.01 percentage point).

The target is for a secured rate. In a September 29, 2026 speech Toni Gravelle said: "we target a secured rate: the overnight repo rate," and the Bank watches it through CORRA, the Canadian Overnight Repo Rate Average. When funding pressure pushes CORRA above the target, the Bank can add cash through overnight repo operations, offered case by case; since October 2025 it also runs weekly two-week term repo operations, and its Standing Liquidity Facility acts as a ceiling. He put the Bank's estimate of steady-state demand for reserves at $50 billion to $70 billion.

Revisions. None. The series records the target that was in force on each business day, so a past value never changes; the number moves only when Governing Council changes the setting. The deposit rate's place in the band changes only "when persistent or structural pressures warrant it" (Gravelle, September 29, 2026).

More: from corridor to floor

Before 2020 the Bank "generally kept the range one-half of a percentage point wide, with our policy interest rate sitting in the middle. This is called a corridor system." It began using a floor system during the COVID-19 pandemic: "Until January 2025, the deposit rate was set equal to the target rate. The Bank changed this to 5 basis points below the target rate to make its monetary policy implementation framework more effective."

The Bank Rate was the Bank's key rate from 1935; since February 1996 it has been set at the top of the operating band (Bank of Canada, "A history of the key interest rate").

01
A target, not a traded rate. CORRA, the market rate it aims at, ended July 2026 at 2.37%, 0.12 pp above the target; at the end of December 2021 it was 0.15 pp below (month-end Valet AVG.INTWO minus V39079). Gravelle: "Since QT ended, CORRA has often been modestly above target."
02
Moves in steps. Between decisions the series is flat; market yields move every day. On October 7, 2026 the 3-month treasury bill yielded 2.39%, 0.14 pp above the target (Valet TB.CDN.90D.MID).
03
Nominal, not real. Consumer prices rose 3.0% in the 12 months to August 2026 (CPI, not seasonally adjusted, Statistics Canada via Valet), so the target sat 0.75 pp below inflation (2.25 − 3.0).
04
Not every rate follows it. Fixed-rate loans and bonds price off longer-term yields, which can move the other way: the September 2 release noted "Long-term bond yields have moved up globally, including in Canada" while the target was held.
05
Canada only. It sets the cost of Canadian-dollar money; US-dollar rates follow the Federal Reserve (see the SOFR report).

05 What Moves It

The mandate. "Under the current agreement, the cornerstone of our framework is an inflation target of 2% within a range of 1% to 3%." The agreement with the Government of Canada is renewed every five years and is due to be renewed in 2026. In the Bank's words, it may lower the rate when a struggling economy "could pull inflation significantly below 2%," and raise it when growth is fast enough to lead to rising inflation.

At the September 2, 2026 hold. The release said CPI inflation "has been hovering around 3% in recent months, mainly because of persistently higher gasoline prices," while inflation excluding gasoline was 2.2% and core measures were "close to 2% in July." GDP was "up by 3.3%" in the second quarter and unemployment edged down to 6.4% in July, but "indicators point to continued excess supply in the economy." New US tariffs hit roughly 5% of Canadian goods exports to the United States (summary of deliberations). Members agreed that if higher energy prices "did spill over into other components of the CPI," it "could require a monetary policy response to prevent broad-based inflation from setting in."

08 Why It Matters

The target sets the price of overnight money, and other Canadian-dollar rates are priced from it. Prime, the base for many variable-rate mortgages and lines of credit, has moved one-for-one with it (2.20 pp above, Section 03). The Bank also lists "interest rates paid on deposits, guaranteed investment certificates and other savings" among the rates it moves. The 3-month treasury-bill yield has moved with it: 0.14% at the end of October 2021, 5.01% at the end of July 2023 when the target peaked, and 4.77% at the end of May 2024, days before the first cut, with the target still at 5.00% (month-end Valet TB.CDN.90D.MID). When yields rise, existing bonds lose price, and higher borrowing costs and discount rates weigh on share valuations. A cut works the other way.

When it rises

Prime and variable-rate loan payments go up; deposit and GIC rates follow. Short-term bond yields rise and bond prices fall. In the Bank's words, higher rates discourage borrowing, which "reduces their spending and puts the brakes on inflation."

When it falls

Variable-rate borrowers pay less; savers earn less on cash and new GICs. Short-term yields fall and existing bonds gain price. The Bank cuts when a struggling economy could pull inflation well below 2%: "With lower rates, people tend to spend more, boosting the economy."
