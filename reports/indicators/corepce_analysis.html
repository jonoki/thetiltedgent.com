01 What It Measures

How fast the prices of what Americans buy are rising, leaving out food and energy: in August 2026 they were 3.0% higher than in August 2025. The number is the 12-month change in the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE) excluding food and energy, the "core" PCE price index, published each month by the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) in its Personal Income and Outlays release.

BEA's definition: "A measure of prices that people living in the United States, or those buying on their behalf, pay for goods and services. It's sometimes called the core PCE price index, because two categories that can have price swings – food and energy – are left out to make underlying inflation easier to see." BEA adds that the core index "is closely watched by the Federal Reserve as it conducts monetary policy."

The Fed's goal is set on the full index, food and energy included. Its FAQ: the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) "judges that inflation of 2 percent over the longer run, as measured by the annual change in the price index for personal consumption expenditures, is most consistent with the Federal Reserve's mandate for maximum employment and price stability."

02 How It's Built

Built from other agencies' data. PCE covers the goods and services bought by, or on behalf of, households and the nonprofits that serve them, as BEA records them in the national accounts, mainly from Census Bureau surveys and other agencies' reports. BEA prices each spending category with the closest matching price index; its NIPA Handbook (chapter 5) says that for most categories this is a BLS consumer price index (for new cars, furniture, prescription drugs and motor fuel, among others), and it uses Producer Price Indexes for some services, such as hospitals and physicians' offices.

Weights that move with spending. BEA: "Quantity and price indexes are calculated using a Fisher chain-weighted formula that incorporates weights from two adjacent periods." The index is set to 100 in 2017. In BEA's words, the PCE price index is known for "reflecting changes in consumer behavior": when people switch from one product to another, the weights follow. Core removes food (7.0% of August 2026 spending) and energy goods and services (3.9%), leaving 89.1% of the basket (our calculation from BEA's nominal spending series on FRED).

Seasonal adjustment. The index is published seasonally adjusted (FRED: "Index 2017=100, Seasonally Adjusted"), and BEA's handbook says the monthly current-dollar estimates for most categories are extrapolated with seasonally adjusted indicator series. The 12-month change on this page is computed from the seasonally adjusted index; for August 2026 it matches the release's 3.0%.

03 Strengths & Limits
Spending paid on households' behalf Weights follow what people buy Monthly since 1959 The Fed's yardstick

Broad scope. PCE counts purchases made for households as well as by them: the NIPA Handbook says it "Includes purchases directly financed by government social benefits, such as Medicaid", and includes the imputed value of employer-paid medical care. Health care paid for by employers and government is therefore counted in this index.

Key Limitations

01
Revisions. July 2026 was first published at 3.3% over 12 months (August 26) and stood at 3.0% after the annual update on September 30. The month's change went from +0.2% to +0.1%.
02
Late. Each month arrives about four weeks after it ends, with the rest of Personal Income and Outlays.
03
Leaves out what households feel. Food and energy are real costs: energy goods and services prices were up 16.8% over the 12 months to August 2026, none of it in core.
04
Borrowed prices and imputed values. Most prices come from BLS and other price indexes, so their sampling error comes along. Some components have no market transaction at all: the space rent of owner-occupied housing and services that financial intermediaries provide without an explicit charge are "entirely imputed" (NIPA Handbook).
05
National only. One number for the whole country; it does not show what any one region or household pays.

05 What Moves It

Services are most of it. Services were about 69% of consumer spending in August 2026 and their prices rose 3.4% over 12 months, down from 3.5% a year earlier. Goods turned up: goods prices rose 3.6% over 12 months, against 0.9% in August 2025. Food (1.9%) and energy (16.8%) move the headline index but not core.

Policy. On September 16, 2026 the FOMC raised the federal funds target range by a quarter point to 3.75–4.00%. Its statement: "Inflation remains elevated. Today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal."

08 Why It Matters

Core PCE is the inflation number the Fed watches when it sets the policy rate, and the policy rate passes through to what savers earn and what borrowers pay. A cash rate minus inflation is the real return: on September 30, 2026 the top of the Fed's range (4.00%) was 1.0 percentage point above the latest core reading (3.0% over 12 months).

When it rises

The Fed is more likely to hold or raise its rate, as it did on September 16, 2026. Overnight rates, bill yields and money-market payouts follow the policy rate; mortgage and other longer rates tend to rise with bond yields, and existing bonds lose value as yields rise. Higher rates lower the present value of future profits, a weight on share prices.

When it falls

Room for rate cuts: savings and money-market yields drift down, borrowing gets cheaper, and existing bonds gain value as yields fall. A saver's real return depends on both numbers: a lower rate with lower inflation can leave it unchanged.

Inflation-linked Treasuries are tied to the other gauge: TreasuryDirect adjusts TIPS principal using a BLS Consumer Price Index, not the PCE index. Related reports: Effective Fed Funds Rate · SOFR · 2-year Treasury · 10-year TIPS · TIP (iShares TIPS Bond ETF).
