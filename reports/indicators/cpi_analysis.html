01 What It Measures

The CPI tracks what a fixed basket of goods and services costs US city households from one month to the next, and its 12-month change is the headline inflation figure in each BLS release. In August 2026 that basket cost 3.4% more than in August 2025.

BLS: "The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is a measure of the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services." The headline series is the CPI for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), U.S. city average, all items: BLS series CUUR0000SA0, carried on FRED as CPIAUCNS (an index level, 1982–84 = 100, not seasonally adjusted). The seasonally adjusted version is CUSR0000SA0 (FRED CPIAUCSL), which BLS uses for month-to-month changes.

It comes out once a month in the Consumer Price Index news release, at 8:30 a.m. ET. In 2026 the release dates fall between the 10th and the 14th of the following month (BLS release schedule).

02 How It's Built

The survey. BLS: "The CPI survey collects about 100,000 prices per month to compute indexes for commodities and services", plus "about 8,000 rental housing unit quotes each month" for housing. About two-thirds of prices are collected by data collectors visiting stores; the rest by telephone or on the seller's website and apps. Prices are what the shopper pays, "including sales and excise taxes".

Where and what. The sample covers 75 urban areas. The basket is split into 243 item strata (groups of similar items) priced in 32 index areas, giving 7,776 basic indexes. The CPI-U population "constitutes about 93 percent of the U.S. population".

Weights and calculation. Within most item strata, BLS averages price changes with a weighted geometric mean. The basic indexes are then combined using what households spend on each item, from the Consumer Expenditure Surveys; BLS: "these expenditure weights are updated annually." Each item's share of the basket is published as its relative importance (Section 05).

Housing. Renters' costs are measured from rents. Homeowners' costs enter as owners' equivalent rent, "the implicit rent that owner occupants would have to pay if they were renting their homes". House prices, mortgage interest and property taxes are not in the CPI: BLS treats buying a home as an investment, not consumption.

Seasonal adjustment. Some prices follow the calendar; BLS names "changing climatic conditions, production cycles, model changeovers, holidays, and sales". Seasonal adjustment removes that pattern so consecutive months can be compared. BLS derives its seasonal factors with the X-13ARIMA-SEATS software and updates them every year.

Revisions

The unadjusted CPI-U is never revised. BLS: "revisions can be costly for the users of these indexes, so these series are final when issued." The seasonally adjusted indexes are: "Each year in February, BLS recalculates and publishes revised seasonally adjusted indexes for the previous 5 years." Monthly changes on this page are computed from the latest seasonally adjusted indexes, so an older month can differ from the figure first published.

03 Strengths & Limits
~100,000 prices a month Unadjusted index never revised Published since 1919 Used to index TIPS and I bonds

Why it is the reference. The CPI is built from prices actually charged across 75 urban areas and is out within two weeks of the month's end. Because the unadjusted CPI-U is final when issued, contracts can be written against it: Treasury adjusts the principal of TIPS with it, and sets the inflation part of the I bond rate from "the non-seasonally adjusted Consumer Price Index for all Urban Consumers (CPI-U)" (TreasuryDirect).

Key Limitations

01
Not a full cost of living. In BLS's words, the CPI "only approximates a cost-of-living index." It leaves out income taxes and investment items such as stocks, bonds and real estate, so house prices and mortgage rates do not enter it directly.
02
Urban households only. The CPI-U covers about 93% of the population; people in rural nonmetropolitan areas, farm households, military installations and institutions are outside it.
03
Sampling error. It is a sample, not a census. BLS's own example: in 2016 the standard errors of the monthly all-items changes had a median of 0.03 percentage points, against a median monthly change of 0.15%.
04
Volatile parts. Food and energy can swing the headline: energy rose 16.3% over the 12 months to August 2026, against 2.4% for everything except food and energy (Section 04).
05
A gap in the record. There is no October 2025 index. The BLS series carries the footnote "Data unavailable due to the 2025 lapse in appropriations" (the federal government shutdown), so October 2025 has no 12-month change and November 2025 has no monthly change.
06
Seasonal revisions. Seasonally adjusted monthly changes are recalculated each February for five years back.

08 Why It Matters

Households. When the CPI rises faster than pay, the same income buys less. Savings lose purchasing power when the interest rate on them is below the inflation rate. Some payments are tied to the CPI directly: the principal of a TIPS "goes up with inflation and down with deflation", and the I bond's inflation rate is reset every May 1 and November 1 from the unadjusted CPI-U (TreasuryDirect).

Markets. Inflation above the Fed's 2 percent goal can lead it to raise its policy rate, as on September 16, 2026, when the FOMC said "Inflation remains elevated." The Fed describes how a rate change spreads: federal funds rate changes "are rapidly reflected in the interest rates that banks and other lenders charge on short-term loans" and in floating-rate loans; longer-term rates move with expectations of how policy and the economy will evolve; and changes in interest rates "tend to affect stock prices by changing the relative attractiveness of equity". When yields rise, existing bonds fall in price, because their fixed payments are worth less at the higher rate.

When it rises

Pay and savings buy less; bond yields tend to rise and existing bond prices fall; the Fed is more likely to raise or hold its rate, so borrowing costs stay high. TIPS principal and new I bond rates go up with it.

When it falls

Purchasing power holds up; bond yields tend to ease and existing bonds gain; the Fed has room to cut, which lowers borrowing costs and money market yields. Inflation-linked payments grow more slowly.
