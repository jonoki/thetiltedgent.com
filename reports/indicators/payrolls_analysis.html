01 What It Measures

Nonfarm payrolls is the count of jobs on US employers' books, and the headline number is how much that count changed from one month to the next. In September 2026 it rose by 29,000 jobs (monthly change, seasonally adjusted) to 159.04 million (level). The BLS publishes it at 8:30 a.m. ET, usually on a Friday early in the month, in the Employment Situation release, from its Current Employment Statistics (CES) survey of employers, which produces, in the BLS's words, "detailed industry estimates of nonfarm employment, hours, and earnings of workers on payrolls".

The BLS definition: "Employees on nonfarm payrolls are those who worked or received pay for any part of the reference pay period, including people on paid leave", and "the reference period is the pay period including the 12th". Left out, per the BLS: agricultural workers, self-employed workers whose businesses are unincorporated, unpaid family workers and private household workers. FRED's series notes say the measure "accounts for approximately 80 percent of the workers who contribute to Gross Domestic Product (GDP)".

02 How It's Built

Each month the BLS surveys about 119,000 businesses and government agencies, covering about 622,000 worksites and about 26% of all nonfarm payroll jobs. The worksites are drawn from a sampling frame of unemployment insurance (UI) tax accounts. Employers report how many people they paid in the pay period that includes the 12th of the month.

Why markets watch it. It is the broadest monthly count of US jobs, it arrives early in the following month, and it comes with hours and pay: in September 2026 average hourly earnings for private-sector employees were $37.81, up 5 cents on the month and 3.0% over 12 months (BLS).

Key Limitations

01
Sampling error. The BLS: the confidence interval for the monthly change in total nonfarm employment "is on the order of plus or minus 122,000" (90% level). September's +29K is well inside that band of zero; August's +133K only just clears it.
02
Revisions. Each month moves the two before it, and the benchmark moves a year more. Latest values against first prints: October 2025 −105K first, −140K now; January 2026 +130K first, +160K now; February 2026 −92K first, −156K now; July 2026 −23K first, then +21K, −10K now (monthly changes, seasonally adjusted).
03
New and closed businesses are modelled. The birth-death model estimates their net jobs until UI records catch up; the March 2025 level was cut by 898,000 at the February 2026 benchmark.
04
Not everyone who works. Farm workers, the unincorporated self-employed, unpaid family workers and private household workers are outside it; the household survey, which gives the unemployment rate, covers them.
05
Data interruptions. The October 2025 report was not published on its own "because of 2025 lapse in federal government appropriations"; October's payroll figure came out with November's on December 16, 2025.
04 Latest Reading

The BLS on October 2: "Both nonfarm payroll employment (+29,000) and the unemployment rate (4.2 percent) changed little in September". Health care (+17,000), construction (+11,000) and manufacturing (+9,000) added jobs; financial activities lost 7,000.

The Fed's projection

The Fed does not project payrolls; it projects the unemployment rate. Its Summary of Economic Projections of September 16, 2026 has a median of 4.1% for the fourth quarter of each year from 2026 to 2029 (June 2026: 4.3% for 2026 and 2027, 4.2% for 2028) and 4.2% in the longer run, against 4.2% in September.

08 Why It Matters

The Federal Reserve: "Congress has assigned the Fed to conduct the nation's monetary policy to support the goals of maximum employment and stable prices." Jobs data are half of that brief. More jobs mean more wages to spend; fewer mean less. Bond and stock prices react to the release as traders reset what they expect the Fed to do. Related reports: effective fed funds rate, SOFR, 2-year Treasury, S&P 500 (VOO).

When it rises

Strong job gains point to a growing economy and steady incomes. They make Fed rate cuts less likely, which tends to push short-term Treasury yields, and with them savings rates and new loan rates, up. For shares, more spending supports company sales, while higher rates weigh on what investors pay for future profits.

When it falls

Weak or negative months point to slowing hiring. They make rate cuts more likely, which tends to pull short-term yields, savings rates and variable loan rates down and lift bond prices. For households, fewer openings mean longer job searches; for shares, weaker spending can hit sales.
