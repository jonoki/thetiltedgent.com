01 What It Measures

The prime rate is the base rate that the largest US banks post for lending. Many loans, including small-business loans and credit cards, are priced from it, so when it moves, the interest on those loans moves too.

The Federal Reserve Board: "The prime rate is an interest rate determined by individual banks. It is often used as a reference rate (also called the base rate) for many types of loans, including loans to small businesses and credit card loans." The Board reports it every business day in its H.15 release, Selected Interest Rates, defined as the "Rate posted by a majority of top 25 (by assets in domestic offices) insured U.S.-chartered commercial banks."

02 How It's Built

Banks set it; the Fed reports it. Each bank posts its own prime rate. The H.15 shows the rate posted by a majority of the 25 largest insured US-chartered commercial banks, ranked by assets in their domestic offices. The Board: "Although the Federal Reserve has no direct role in setting the prime rate, many banks choose to set their prime rates based partly on the target level of the federal funds rate--the rate that banks charge each other for short-term loans--established by the Federal Open Market Committee."

The three-point rule in the data. Over the five years from October 1, 2021 to October 2, 2026, prime sat exactly 3.00 percentage points above the upper limit of the Fed's target range on 1,275 of the 1,276 business days in FRED's data. The exception was September 17, 2025: the H.15 shows prime already cut to 7.25% that day, the day the Fed announced its cut and a day before the new range took effect.

Basis, averages and timing. The rate is quoted per year on a 360-day basis (the H.15 note: "Annualized using a 360-day year or bank interest"). Weekly figures average the 7 calendar days ending on Wednesday; monthly figures (MPRIME) include each calendar day in the month and are posted on the first business day after the month ends. The release "is posted daily Monday through Friday at 4:15pm" and not on holidays.

A posted rate. The figure is the rate a majority of the 25 banks post, not an estimate from a survey, and FRED lists it as not seasonally adjusted. The H.15 note adds: "Prime is one of several base rates used by banks to price short-term business loans."

The Fed's rate that prime follows

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) sets a target range for the federal funds rate, the overnight rate banks charge each other, at eight scheduled meetings a year. Its trading desk is directed to keep the market rate inside the range, helped by rates the Fed sets itself. From September 17, 2026, per the Fed's implementation note of September 16: interest on reserve balances (IORB, what the Fed pays banks on their deposits with it) 3.90%, standing overnight repo operations (the Fed lending cash against securities) at 4.00%, overnight reverse repo (the Fed taking cash) at 3.75%, and the discount window's primary credit rate (the Fed's own loans to banks) at 4.00%.

What it captures well. The base cost of bank credit for loans written off prime, updated the day banks change it, with a daily history in FRED going back to 1955.

Key Limitations

01
A base, not the rate a borrower pays. It is a reference rate; what a given card or loan charges is set from it by the lender's own terms.
02
The majority's rate. The H.15 shows what most of the 25 largest banks post. Prime is "determined by individual banks", and other banks can post a different rate.
03
Moves in steps. It changed on only 18 days in the five years to October 7, 2026 (12 increases, 6 cuts; our count from DPRIME). Day-to-day moves in overnight market rates such as SOFR never show in it.
04
Large banks only. The panel is insured US-chartered commercial banks ranked by domestic assets; smaller banks, credit unions and non-bank lenders are outside it.

05 What Moves It

The Fed's decisions. Each of the 18 changes in prime in the five years to October 7, 2026 came with a change in the Fed's target range, on the day the new range took effect or, once (September 17, 2025), on the day it was announced (our comparison of DPRIME with DFEDTARU). The latest: on September 16, 2026 the FOMC statement said, "The Committee decided to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent, in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate." Prime rose from 6.75% to 7.00% the next day.

The Fed's projections. The Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) gives FOMC participants' median for the federal funds rate, as the midpoint of the target range at the end of each year. It has no projection for prime.

08 Why It Matters

For households and small firms, prime is the step between the Fed's decisions and their own interest bills: a loan priced from prime resets when prime moves. Each 0.25-point move changes the yearly interest on $10,000 of prime-linked debt by $25 (our arithmetic: $10,000 × 0.25%).

When it rises

Interest on loans priced from prime, such as many small-business loans and credit cards, goes up from the next reset. Lenders earn more on the same loans. It moves with the Fed's range: in the five years to October 7, 2026 it never changed without a change in the range.

When it falls

Prime-linked borrowers pay less from the next reset; lenders earn less on the same loans. The same Fed decision moves overnight market rates such as EFFR and SOFR down with the range.
