01 What It Measures

The share of people who want a job, are available to work and are looking for one, but don't have one. In September 2026 that was 4.2% of the labor force (seasonally adjusted), or 7.1 million people. The BLS publishes it once a month in the Employment Situation release, the same release as the payroll jobs count.

The BLS definitions, from the release's technical note: people are unemployed if "they had no employment during the reference week; they were available for work at that time; and they made specific active efforts to find employment sometime during the 4-week period ending with the reference week." The labor force is "the sum of the employed and unemployed", and the unemployment rate is "the number unemployed as a percent of the labor force". It is also called U-3, one of six measures of labor underutilization the BLS publishes (Section 04).

More: who counts, and who doesn't

FRED's series notes: labor force data "are restricted to people 16 years of age and older, who currently reside in 1 of the 50 states or the District of Columbia, who do not reside in institutions (e.g., penal and mental facilities, homes for the aged), and who are not on active duty in the Armed Forces."

Employed, per the BLS technical note: anyone who "did any work at all as paid employees during the reference week; worked in their own business, profession, or on their own farm; or worked without pay at least 15 hours in a family business or farm." One hour of paid work counts.

02 How It's Built

The rate comes from the Current Population Survey (CPS), which the BLS describes as "a sample survey of about 60,000 eligible households conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau for the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics". That is about 110,000 people a month (BLS, "How the Government Measures Unemployment"). Census Bureau interviewers ask about the reference week, "generally the calendar week that contains the 12th day of the month".

People are never asked whether they are unemployed. In the BLS's words, respondents "are never asked specifically if they are unemployed, nor are they given an opportunity to decide their own labor force status": the answers about work and job search sort them into employed, unemployed or not in the labor force. A household stays in the sample for a limited time: "Every month, one-fourth of the households in the sample are changed, so that no household is interviewed for more than 4 consecutive months."

The unemployment rate is not built from unemployment-insurance claims. The BLS says claims data "cannot be used as a source for complete information on the number of unemployed", since only about one-third of the unemployed received regular benefits on average over the past decade.

Seasonal adjustment and revisions

Seasonal adjustment removes the regular calendar swings: in September 2026 the not seasonally adjusted rate was 4.0%, the adjusted rate 4.2%. Per the technical note, "In the household survey, new seasonal factors are used to adjust only the current month's data", so the adjusted rate is not revised month to month; instead "5-year revisions to historical data are made once a year."

The survey's totals are scaled to Census Bureau population estimates, updated once a year ("Updated population controls are introduced annually with the release of January data", BLS Table A-15). In 2026 the update came a month late, with February data on March 6, 2026, and January was revised. The BLS put its effect at −1.4 million on the labor force and on employment, −0.4 pp on the participation rate and −0.5 pp on the employment-population ratio; the unemployment rate was unchanged. Comparisons of levels across January are affected.

There is no October 2025 reading. The BLS: "Household survey data were not collected for October 2025 due to the lapse in appropriations and were not collected retroactively."

03 Strengths & Limits
Asks people, not employers ~60,000 households a month Counts the self-employed Monthly since 1948 Not revised month to month

Because it surveys households, the rate counts people whatever their job type, including the self-employed and unpaid family workers, and it counts jobseekers whether or not they receive benefits. The same survey gives the breakdown by age, sex, race, reason for unemployment and duration, out on the same day.

Key Limitations

01
Sampling error. The BLS: "At an unemployment rate of around 6.0 percent, the 90-percent confidence interval for the monthly change in unemployment as measured by the household survey is about +/- 425,000, and for the monthly change in the unemployment rate it is about +/- 0.3 percentage point." A 0.1 pp move, like September's, is inside that band.
02
It leaves people out. Someone who wants a job but has stopped looking is not in the labor force, and someone working part time who wants full time counts as employed. The broader U-6 adds both: 7.6% in September 2026, against 4.2% for U-3 (both seasonally adjusted).
03
Participation can move it. The rate can fall because jobseekers give up, not because they find work. The labor force participation rate was 61.8% in September 2026 (seasonally adjusted).
04
Breaks in the series. Annual population controls shift levels each January (in 2026, the labor force by −1.4 million), and October 2025 is missing because of the 2025 lapse in federal appropriations.
05
Seasonal factors. The adjusted figures for the last five years change once a year when the BLS revises them; this page shows the values published as of October 2, 2026.

05 What Moves It

The rate moves when people lose jobs faster than others find them, and when people enter or leave the labor force. The BLS's Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS, released Sep 29, 2026) puts numbers on the first part for August 2026, seasonally adjusted: 7.1 million job openings (4.3% rate), 5.2 million hires (3.3%), 3.1 million quits (1.9%) and 1.6 million layoffs and discharges (1.0%).

The Fed's view

The Fed's own summary: "Congress has assigned the Fed to conduct the nation's monetary policy to support the goals of maximum employment and stable prices." It defines the first as "the highest level of employment or lowest level of unemployment that the economy can sustain in a context of price stability." On September 16, 2026 the FOMC raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% "in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate", and its statement said: "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little." 08 Why It Matters

Maximum employment is one of the two goals Congress gave the Fed, so the rate feeds into the policy rate, and from there into the overnight rates (effective fed funds rate, SOFR) and short Treasury yields (2-year Treasury). For households it is the odds of losing a paycheck, and of finding a new one. For companies, a tight labor market means higher wage bills; a loose one means weaker spending by their customers.

When it rises

More households lose income and cut spending. Markets tend to price lower policy rates, so short-term yields and savings rates can fall and bond prices rise (10-year Treasury). Share prices weigh weaker earnings against cheaper money.

When it falls

More people earn, and employers compete for workers, which can push wages up. If that adds to inflation, the Fed may hold or raise rates: borrowing costs and savings rates rise, and bond prices fall. Share prices gain from spending but can lose from higher rates (S&P 500, VOO).
