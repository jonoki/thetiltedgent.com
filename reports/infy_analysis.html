01 Company Overview

Infosys is a Bengaluru IT services firm founded in Pune in 1981 by seven founders, which in March 1999 became the first Indian company listed on Nasdaq. Its American shares now trade on the NYSE, one ADS for each rupee share listed on the NSE and BSE in India. In the fiscal year to March 31, 2026 it booked $20.16B of revenue, up 4.6% in dollars and 3.1% in constant currency, with a 20.3% operating margin, $3.31B of net profit and $3.73B of free cash flow. North America supplied 56.1% of revenue and Europe 32.1%; India itself only 2.9%.

The business is still growing, barely; the share price is not. The ADS closed 2025 at $17.82 and June 2026 at $10.49, a 41% fall in six months, and at $12.43 it sits 44.2% below its October 2021 close. On October 23, 2026 Infosys cut its FY2027 growth range for the second time in three months, to 1%–2% in constant currency from 1.5%–3.0% (it began the year at 1.5%–3.5%). Q2 revenue of $5,145M grew 1.5% in constant currency, and CFO Jayesh Sanghrajka gave the reason on the call: "Growth was below our expectations. Volume was softer, and price increases were lower than anticipated amid high competitive intensity." The same quarter brought $5.2B of large deals, up from $3.6B in Q1, including a single net-new contract worth more than $2B over seven years, and AI work reached 10.5% of revenue. The ADS fell 6.6% that day. The question for an owner is simple to state: is a 15.3x earnings multiple with a 4.2% dividend yield cheap for a business that still converts more than 100% of its profit to cash and has just signed a $2B+ contract, or fair for one whose existing business has stopped growing?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Offshore delivery scale (328,062 people) 41 clients above $100M a year Run-the-business switching costs Net cash balance sheet Large-deal engine ($14.9B FY26 TCV) Topaz AI platform and partnerships

The durable advantage is cost and embeddedness at scale. A bank or carmaker that has handed Infosys its application maintenance, testing and infrastructure for a decade would have to rebuild that knowledge to leave, and few clients want the operational risk. The financial result is a business that turned FY2026 net profit of $3.31B into $3.73B of free cash flow, returned over ₹37,500 crore to shareholders for FY2026 through dividends and a ₹18,000 crore buyback, and still held $3.98B of cash and current investments at September 30. Europe grew revenue 12.8% in dollars in FY2026 while North America grew 1.2%. The vulnerability is that embeddedness protects the relationship, not the rate card.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
AI-led deflation at every renewal. Clients have always asked for productivity savings when contracts renew; the CFO told the July call that AI adds a further cut on top and that it reaches beyond large deals: "That is not only on the large deal portion, that is also there on the non-large deal portion." In October CEO Salil Parekh described the same thing across the client base: "there is demand or request requirement which is focused on productivity benefits from the AI that are getting passed on." Revenue is people × hours × rate, and AI tools attack the hours.
02
Organic growth at zero. The CFO said in July that the Optimum and Stratus acquisitions add about 1.7 points to FY2027 growth. Taken from the new 1%–2% range, that leaves roughly −0.7% to +0.3% from the existing business (our arithmetic). The July breakdown also named a 0.75–1 point drag from work shifting offshore, where it bills at lower rates.
03
One client can move the year. The July range already carried slightly over 1 point of impact from a large European manufacturing client cutting spend, together with deals Infosys declined on returns, and in October the CFO again cited lower revenue from a large manufacturing client. With the top 10 clients at 19.9% of Q1 revenue, one client's budget shows up in the full-year number.
04
A leadership handover in a hard year. Salil Parekh hands over to Ashiss Kumar Dash on April 1, 2027, in the middle of the industry's pricing reset, and the FY2028 outlook will be the first under him.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the comparisons. First, India's new Labour Codes, notified on November 21, 2025, forced a one-time $143M increase in gratuity and leave liabilities in Q3 FY2026, cutting that quarter's reported operating margin by 2.8 points (18.4% reported against 21.2% adjusted); FY2026 operating margin was 20.3% reported and 21.0% adjusted. Second, the buyback: the board approved on September 11, 2025 a tender-offer repurchase of 10 crore shares (2.41% of the equity) at ₹1,800 each, ₹18,000 crore in all, and FY2026 cash flow shows $2,006M spent on it — at a price 54% above the October 26 close of ₹1,166. Third, acquisitions: Stratus Global ($74M, April 21) and Optimum Achieve ($365M, May 4, 2026) add roughly 1.7 points to FY2027 revenue growth, so reported growth this year flatters the organic trend.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $22.28 to the October 26, 2026 close of $12.43, the ADS lost 44.2% of its price. Adding back the $2.20 per ADS of dividends paid over the period (Yahoo's dividend record, without reinvestment) brings the loss to about 34%. Over the same window SPY rose 66.7% on price ($459.25 → $765.37), so the gap is about 111 percentage points. Part of the loss is the currency: the rupee weakened from ₹74.80 to ₹94.94 per dollar, and on the NSE the rupee share fell a smaller 30.1% (₹1,667.75 → ₹1,166.00) while the NIFTY 50 rose 28.8%. Either way, Infosys has badly lagged both its home market and Wall Street.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 13.0 at the June 2026 close, the most oversold reading in the five-year series, after five straight monthly readings under 25 (February–June). It recovered to 32 after July's rally, slipped to 25.6 at the September close and stands at 43.7 on the October 26 close, after a 15.5% rise in October so far — out of oversold territory without being stretched. The 10-month moving average, at $12.84, sits 3.3% above the price and has fallen every month since April 2025: the long trend is still down, though the gap has nearly closed.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 15.3x trailing and 14.5x forward earnings for a business with a 20%+ operating margin, a 30% return on equity, net cash, and free cash flow that ran at 113.3% of net profit last quarter. The 4.2% dividend yield, a 7.5% free-cash-flow yield and a 13% higher interim dividend pay you to wait. Q2 brought $5.2B of large deals, 62% net new, including a seven-year contract worth more than $2B that the CEO expects to add over 1 point to growth in FY2028; Investec, Metsä Group, Knorr-Bremse, ABN AMRO, Crocs and Telia have awarded or renewed work since July; and AI revenue reached 10.5% of the total, up 29% in a quarter. Accenture's October 1 report — 7% local-currency growth and a 3%–6% FY2027 guide — says the sector's clients are still spending. The ADS already fell 41% in six months to June; with the guidance reset low and the big contract starting in Q4, a stable FY2028 outlook could be enough for the market to pay a multiple closer to Accenture's 18.2x.

Bear Case

The guidance has been cut twice in three months, from 1.5%–3.5% to 1%–2%. Strip out the 1.7 points the CFO attributed to acquisitions in July and FY2027 implies organic growth of roughly −0.7% to +0.3% in constant currency. Both quarters fell short of the company's own plan, in each case on soft volumes and weak pricing under competition, and the CFO calls AI-led deflation a headwind that reaches every renewal, not just large deals. He also listed what weighs on second-half margins: pay rises, a soft season, AI savings passed to clients and the cost of ramping up large deals. J.P. Morgan, HSBC and CLSA all cut to Hold after the July print; eleven of fourteen analysts sit on Hold, and the average target is 2.9% below the price after an October rally. A low multiple on falling growth is how a structurally shrinking business looks on the way down, and a leadership change lands in April 2027 in the middle of it.

Top 3 Risks

01
Price deflation from AI. Management names it explicitly as a headwind on both large and smaller deals. If the productivity AI delivers is passed to clients faster than Infosys wins new AI work, revenue per client falls even while volumes hold.
02
Guidance and margin risk in the second half. The FY2027 range was trimmed in July and cut again in October; organic growth is now about −0.7% to +0.3% on our arithmetic. The CFO kept the 20%–22% margin range but said "H2 margins will be impacted by compensation hikes, softer revenue seasonality, continued sharing of AI-driven productivity benefits, and ramp-up of large deals", partly offset by a cost programme. A third cut in January would take reported growth toward zero.
03
Currency and the ADS premium. A US holder owns a rupee business: the rupee's fall from ₹75 to ₹95 per dollar since October 2021 cost ADS holders about 14 points more than rupee holders lost (−44.2% against −30.1%). The ADS also traded 1.2% above the NSE share at the October 26 exchange rate, a premium that can close.
Industry Commentary
AI-led deflation meets the effort-priced model
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The CFO's account on the July 23 call is the clearest statement of the problem from inside the industry: "whenever the deal comes up for renewal, we always used to have the additional productivity ask from the client, which is how traditionally this industry has been. On the back of AI, there is an additional deflation or the AI led deflation as we call it." Infosys's answer is to sell AI work itself — the Topaz platform and, in the CEO's words, "strategic partnerships established with all leading AI companies". The scoreboard is the gap between the two: AI revenue was 8.2% of total revenue in Q1 and 10.5% in Q2, up 29% in a quarter, against deflation on the legacy book. Read against Q2's total constant-currency growth of 1.5%, and a full-year range now cut to 1%–2%, the deflation is still winning. CEO Salil Parekh's outlook on the July call held both halves: "Overall, we see a good pipeline for AI services, and that gives us a good view for continued AI work with our clients. Outside of that, we continue to see the macro-environment remaining uncertain."

Accenture as the sector's weathervane
SECTOR SIGNAL

The largest one-day move in the Infosys ADS since February came on Accenture's results day, not its own: −9.7% on June 18, when Accenture cut its fiscal 2026 range to 3%–4%. Then came +5.5% on October 1, when it reported 7% local-currency growth and guided fiscal 2027 to 3%–6%, and +5.3% on October 13, when HCLTech's results lifted the Indian IT shares. Investors are treating the whole group as one trade on enterprise IT budgets; Infosys's own guidance cut on October 23 cost it 6.6% in a day. That cuts both ways: Infosys gets credit for a peer's recovery before showing one itself, and it will be marked down with the group if the recovery stalls.

Cash returns as the floor
SUPPORT

Infosys paid out more than ₹37,500 crore to shareholders for FY2026 through dividends and the buyback, and FY2026 cash flow shows $2,133M of dividends and $2,006M of buybacks against $3,733M of free cash flow. A 4.2% trailing yield, a 7.5% free-cash-flow yield and a 13% higher interim dividend (₹26, declared October 23) put a cash return under the shares that few growth stocks offer. The catch: the buyback was done at ₹1,800 a share, 54% above the October 26 NSE close — a reminder that capital returns support a price, they do not set it.
