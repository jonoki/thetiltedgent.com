01 Company Overview

Invitation Homes is one of America's largest landlords of single-family houses — not apartments, houses, with yards and garages, rented one lease at a time. At June 30, 2026 it owned or managed 109,217 homes: 85,509 wholly owned, 8,069 in joint ventures and 15,639 managed for other owners. It was founded in 2012 and grew up as a Blackstone-controlled company; it listed on February 1, 2017, and on November 16, 2017 completed an all-stock merger of equals with Starwood Waypoint Homes that created a portfolio of about 82,000 homes (Blackstone was described as the majority stockholder at the time of that deal). It joined the S&P 500 on September 19, 2022. It is a REIT, so the numbers that matter are Core FFO and AFFO, not GAAP earnings: FY2025 Core FFO was $1.91 per share (+1.7%) and AFFO $1.63 (+1.8%); the FY2026 guide, raised a cent in July, is $1.95 and $1.65. The company ranks #1,414 globally by market capitalisation at a $16.05B value.

The story of 2026 is that Washington changed the rules of the game mid-hand. The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law on July 11, 2026 and, from January 7, 2027, bars any for-profit owner controlling 350 or more single-family homes from buying existing houses — with exceptions for newly built homes, build-to-rent, substantial renovations, and purchases from other large investors — and with no requirement to sell what it already owns. That shuts the door Invitation Homes was born through, while leaving the house it built intact. Management's answer was already in motion: in January 2026 it bought ResiBuilt, a homebuilder, and it has been selling more homes than it buys (1,140 sold vs 457 bought in H1 2026) and using the proceeds to buy back its own stock — about 22.8 million shares for ~$600M at a $26.30 average since December 2025. The stock at $27.08 is 8.6% lower than a year ago and 29% below its September 2021 month-end close. The question is whether a landlord that can no longer buy its raw material in the open market is a melting ice cube, or a scarce, grandfathered portfolio whose moat just got a statute behind it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Scale: 109,217 homes owned or managed Grandfathered portfolio — no forced sales Build-to-rent capability (ResiBuilt) Dense local operating platform 92.4% fixed-rate debt ~90% of homes unencumbered Third-party management platform

The moat used to be the ability to buy: cheap capital, data, and a local crew in every metro that could acquire, renovate and lease scattered houses at a cost small landlords couldn't match. The ROAD Act removes most of that from January 2027. What remains is a moat of possession. The law caps new entrants at 350 homes before the purchase ban applies, sets no divestiture requirement, and allows purchases between large institutional investors — so the existing large portfolios become harder to replicate. A dense cluster of homes in one metro lowers the cost of every repair visit and every turn; that density can no longer be built by buying existing houses in the open market. The vulnerability is the mirror image: a REIT that cannot grow by acquisition must grow by rent, building, fees or buying back its own stock, and rent growth is currently being eaten by property taxes.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The acquisition door closes on January 7, 2027. Large institutional investors are prohibited from buying existing single-family homes except under statutory exceptions, with penalties of up to $1 million per violation or three times the purchase price, whichever is greater. The company acknowledges in its filings that such regulation could limit its ability to acquire homes and reduce the scale or efficiency of its operations.
02
Expenses are set by someone else. Property taxes are about 55% of operating expenses. Same-store core opex rose 5.7% in Q1 2026 and the FY2026 guide is +3.0–4.0% — above the 1.5–2.3% guide for core revenue growth. Management said preliminary valuations were expected within weeks of the July 30 call; the outcome is the swing factor for H2 NOI.
03
Occupancy is drifting and supply is local. 96.5% in July versus 97.1% in Q2, with supply pressure named in Florida, Texas and Phoenix. HousingWire counts institutional investor listings roughly doubling from 4,166 homes (Feb 1) to 9,447 (July 2026), with 54% of those listings carrying price cuts — more houses for sale in the same metros where these landlords rent.
04
Leverage in a rate-sensitive asset class. $8.59B of total debt, net debt at 5.4x TTM adjusted EBITDAre, interest coverage of 1.98x (StockAnalysis), and a $988M secured maturity in June 2027. The July 2026 issue of $500M of 4.950% notes due 2032 shows the market is open — at a coupon that is close to the stock's 4.43% dividend yield.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 13.9x guided Core FFO and 16.4x AFFO — a 6.1% AFFO yield with a 4.43% dividend covered about 1.4 times — for a portfolio of 85,509 wholly owned single-family rentals, at a time when the law has just made that portfolio impossible to reassemble by buying existing homes. Supply of new institutional competition is capped by statute, nothing has to be sold, and the one legal growth channel — building — is a capability the company bought in January. Management is selling homes at around $450,000 and buying its own stock at an average $26.30, prioritising buybacks because the shares trade at a discount to net asset value. Renewals are running 3.3–4.3% and new-lease growth turned positive. Every analyst target on the board, including the lowest at $28, sits above the price.

Bear Case

A landlord that can't buy houses has to grow by raising rent, and rent growth is being consumed by costs it can't control: property taxes are 55% of opex, opex is guided up 3–4% against revenue up 1.5–2.3%, and same-store NOI is guided at 0.4–1.9%. Occupancy slid to 96.5% in July, institutional listings doubled this year with most carrying price cuts, and supply is building in Florida, Texas and Phoenix. The portfolio is shrinking by design, $8.6B of debt at 5.4x EBITDAre must be refinanced at today's rates, and GAAP EPS is propped up by one-off sale gains — consensus has it falling to $0.71 by 2027. Five years of −29% on price says the market has consistently valued this model below what management says the homes are worth, and the new law removes the growth lever that was supposed to close that gap.

Top 3 Risks

01
Regulatory — the ROAD Act and whatever follows it. From January 7, 2027 the company cannot buy existing homes outside statutory exceptions, and penalties reach three times the purchase price. The law has no divestiture clause today; the risk is that a law which targeted the industry once becomes a template, federal or state, for tougher rules.
02
Expense inflation outrunning rent. With same-store core revenue guided +1.5–2.3% and opex +3.0–4.0%, a bad property-tax assessment cycle turns NOI growth negative, as it was in Q1 2026 (−0.3%).
03
Rates and refinancing. 5.4x net debt to adjusted EBITDAre, interest coverage under 2x on StockAnalysis's basis, a $988M maturity in June 2027 and new debt pricing near 5% — higher-for-longer rates hit both Core FFO and the valuation multiple on the portfolio.
Industry Commentary
The ROAD Act: Washington caps the institutional landlord
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law on July 11, 2026 when the President neither signed nor timely vetoed it. Any for-profit entity with investment control over 350 or more single-family homes is barred from purchasing single-family homes from January 7, 2027, unless the purchase is new construction, build-to-rent, a renovate-to-rent with at least 15% rehabilitation cost, part of a homeownership program, a foreclosure transaction, age-restricted senior housing, or a purchase from another large institutional investor. There is no divestiture requirement and the restrictions sunset 15 years after the effective date. The covered investors own about 589,000 homes — 3.9% of the roughly 14 million single-family rentals — and HousingWire reports that the named large operators (including Invitation Homes, Progress Residential, AMH, Tricon, FirstKey, Amherst and VineBrook) sold 3,180 more homes than they bought between January 1 and July 2026. The industry's growth model shifts from buying the existing stock to building new stock.

Property taxes, supply and the rent-versus-own gap
ONGOING HEADWIND

The single-family rental pitch rests on affordability: per John Burns data cited in the Q2 release, leasing costs more than $1,000 a month less than owning in Invitation Homes' markets, and in February Dallas Tanner said residents "save nearly $12,000 a year on average by renting their homes." That keeps demand deep and renewals sticky. The constraint is on the cost side and in local supply: property taxes are about 55% of operating expenses, new supply is pressuring Florida, Texas and Phoenix, and institutional sellers' listings roughly doubled between February and July 2026 with average markdowns widening from 3.1% to 4%. HousingWire's source at Compass, Mike Simonsen, cautioned that "we haven't seen a giant flood to move the needle nationally" — the effect is concentrated in the metros where these landlords cluster, with the top six (Atlanta, Dallas-Fort Worth, Phoenix, Charlotte, Houston, Tampa) holding 36.8% of institutionally owned homes.

Selling houses to buy back stock — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

Invitation Homes has turned into a capital recycler: sell individual homes at retail prices (about $450,000 average on the Q2 call; Q1 dispositions at "pro forma stabilized cap rates in the low-4%s"), and use the proceeds to buy the whole portfolio back through the stock market at a discount. The bull framing: if a home sells at a cap rate in the low 4% range while the stock implies a 6.1% AFFO yield, every such swap is accretive per share, and the law now guarantees the portfolio's scarcity. The bear framing: a shrinking portfolio spreads fixed corporate and operating costs over fewer homes, and buybacks at $26.30 have so far been followed by a $27.08 price — the arbitrage only pays if the market eventually closes the NAV gap, and the market has had five years to do it. The adjudicating numbers are reported every quarter: same-store NOI growth, the pace of dispositions, and whether Core FFO per share keeps rising as the share count falls.
