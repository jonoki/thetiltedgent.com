01 Company Overview

International Paper makes the brown box. It produces containerboard in its mills and converts it into corrugated boxes, bulk bins and shipping containers for food and beverage, agriculture, manufacturing and consumer-goods customers. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through September 30, 2026 is $24.18B, split between Packaging Solutions North America ($14.91B) and Packaging Solutions EMEA ($9.19B). Over the last twelve months the company has not made money on a GAAP basis: net loss of $3.46B, or −$6.56 per share, driven by two goodwill write-downs on the European business it bought in January 2025: $2.47B in December 2025 and another $1.38B at September 30, 2026.

This is a company in the middle of three transactions at once, and a reader of the five-year chart needs all three. October 1, 2021: IP spun off its printing-papers business as Sylvamo, handing shareholders one Sylvamo share for every eleven IP shares, just before this chart's five-year window opens at the October 2021 month-end. January 31, 2025: IP completed its all-share acquisition of the UK's DS Smith, issuing 0.1285 new IP shares per DS Smith share (178.1 million new shares, per IP’s 10-Q for the quarter to June 30, 2025) and adding a secondary London listing under "IPC"; average diluted shares were 529.3M in the first nine months of 2026 against 498.2M a year earlier. January 29, 2026: barely a year after closing that deal, management announced it will split the company again — into a North American packaging company that keeps the International Paper name and an EMEA packaging company built mostly from DS Smith — with completion expected in 12–15 months. In October the plan gained a second route: on October 15 IP confirmed it is also talking to potential buyers for the European business, while preparing the spin-off, as a London listing of a company to be called DS Smith plc, in parallel. Earlier, it sold its Global Cellulose Fibers pulp business for $1.5B (closed January 23, 2026). The stock closed October 29 at $36.03, 39% below its November 2024 month-end high of $58.83, after rising 7.9% over the two sessions that followed the Q3 report. The question for the reader: has North America turned (Q3 segment profit more than doubled from Q2), or is the market still marking down a European business that IP has now written down twice?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Mill network scale Integrated mill-to-box system Top-3 share of NA capacity High replacement cost of mills Local box-plant proximity Fiber sourcing & recycling

The moat in containerboard is physical and expensive to copy. Boxes are bulky and cheap, so they are made near customers; the mills that feed box plants cost billions and take years to build. Three companies — Smurfit Westrock, International Paper and Packaging Corporation of America — hold about 65% of North American containerboard capacity after the 2024–25 consolidation wave (TAPPI Paper360 via Mordor Intelligence). On the Q2 call, CEO Andy Silvernail said the replacement cost of mills has soared over the last five years and put the return that would make anyone seriously consider a major new mill at mid-to-high teens on invested capital. IP itself earns a 4.0% ROIC over the last twelve months (StockAnalysis). That gap is the whole argument: the moat keeps competitors out, but it does not, by itself, make IP's own capital earn its keep.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Europe is losing money and is still 38% of sales. EMEA posted segment losses of $236M in FY2025 and $204M in the first nine months of 2026, and has now carried two goodwill write-downs ($2.47B and $1.38B). A spin-off or sale removes it from IP's books eventually, but until then the losses, the restructuring cash and the separation costs ($119M before tax so far in 2026) are IP shareholders' problem, and no sale price is known.
02
Execution risk on the mill system. Q3 showed progress: Pine Hill restarted early and mill costs fell. But the full-year guide has now come down three times in 2026, in April, July and October, and the Riverdale ramp and NORPAC gains still have to arrive in 2027. Another cut would hit a management team whose credibility rests on 80/20 execution.
03
Input costs and freight. Inflation ran higher than management expected in Q3: diesel, energy and old corrugated containers had a $55M unfavorable effect on North American input costs, and the CFO counted about $145M of new headwinds since the Q2 call, led by diesel and distribution costs.
04
The dividend is not covered by free cash flow. $1.85 a share, a 5.14% yield (re-declared at $0.4625 a quarter on October 12, 2026), costs roughly $980M a year against $75M of TTM free cash flow and a 2026 free-cash-flow guide of about $100–200M. It is being paid partly from the balance sheet and asset sales while net debt sits at $8.88B. The consensus table on StockAnalysis shows a FY2026 dividend of $1.88 — a projection, not a company announcement.

Notable One-Off Items

Nearly every headline number is distorted. Q3 2026 net special items were a $1.43B pre-tax charge ($1.31B after tax, $2.46 a share): almost all of it the $1.38B non-cash goodwill write-down on PS EMEA, plus $65M of EMEA separation costs, partly offset by $116M of tax benefits from settled tax audits and capital losses and a $30M gain on selling assets of the closed Compton, California box plant. FY2025 loss from continuing operations of $2.84B includes a $2.47B pre-tax, non-cash goodwill impairment on PS EMEA (recorded December 31, 2025, alongside the separation review), about $960M of accelerated depreciation and $630M of restructuring charges, per the full-year results as reported by Paper Advance. The gap to the $3.52B total net loss (≈$0.68B, by subtraction) is discontinued operations — the GCF pulp business, sold to American Industrial Partners for $1.5B including $190M of preferred stock. On an adjusted basis Q3 2026 operating EPS was $0.37, against a $0.43 loss a year earlier and $0.04 in Q2. Finally, DS Smith was paid for in shares, not cash, so revenue grew 49% in FY2025 while revenue per share grew far less.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Strip out the write-downs and you are buying one of North America's three largest containerboard producers at 7.5x trailing EBITDA, with a 5.14% dividend, in an industry where those three control about 65% of capacity and new mills are too expensive to build. Q3 showed the North American turn: segment profit of $427M, more than double Q2, adjusted EBITDA above the company's own range and Pine Hill back early. The losses sit in one segment that is being separated within about a year, and IP is now also talking to potential buyers for it: a sale would turn Europe into cash rather than a spun-off stake. Consensus has adjusted EPS going from $0.98 in 2026 to $2.71 in 2027, and nine of eleven analysts rate it Buy.

Bear Case

IP issued about 178 million shares to buy DS Smith, has written off $3.85B of goodwill on it in two steps, and is now spinning it off or selling it, with the reported private-equity interest valuing it below $6B (FT, via Reuters). The per-share damage is done. The dividend costs about $980M a year against $75M of trailing free cash flow, net debt is $8.88B and interest coverage is 1.69x. ROIC of 4.0% is below the cost of capital. Guidance has been cut three times in 2026, inflation keeps eating into price increases, and North American box shipments fell 2% in Q3. Analysts cut targets through October: the average is down to $42.20 and UBS's $35 sits below the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Inflation versus price. The October guide cut came from about $145M of new cost and volume headwinds since July, and the September containerboard price increase only begins to flow through late in Q4, per the CFO. Another round of diesel, energy or fiber inflation before it lands would mean a fourth cut since January.
02
Separation route, cost and terms. A spin-off or sale of EMEA carries separation costs ($119M before tax in the first nine months of 2026), restructuring cash, the unknowns of how debt is split and, if it is sold, the price. The company says there is no certainty a sale will be agreed.
03
Dividend and balance sheet. $980M a year of dividends against $75M of TTM free cash flow, a 2026 free-cash-flow guide of about $100–200M, $8.88B of net debt and North American capex the CEO expects to stay elevated for a couple more years. If the 2027 ramp slips, something gives — capex, debt or the payout.
Industry Commentary
Input-cost inflation versus box pricing
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Containerboard is a spread business: the price of paper and boxes against the cost of fiber, energy and freight. In Q3 2026 inflation ran higher than IP expected: diesel, energy and old corrugated containers had a $55M unfavorable effect on North American input costs, and the CFO counted about $145M of new headwinds since the Q2 call, led by diesel and distribution costs and weaker volume. Price is the offset: price and mix added $141M in North America in Q3 as earlier published increases came through, and the September increase starts to flow late in Q4. In EMEA the squeeze ran the other way inside the company: higher paper prices had not yet reached published box prices, and energy costs rose. Management called the North American market generally stable but expects current conditions to persist through the fourth quarter and into 2027.

Consolidation and capacity discipline
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

The formation of Smurfit Westrock, IP's combination with DS Smith and Packaging Corporation of America's purchase of Greif's containerboard business left about 65% of North American capacity with three firms (TAPPI Paper360, via Mordor Intelligence). Supply has also been cut: Mordor cites AF&PA data showing North American containerboard production down 8% year over year in Q1 2026 after the 2025 round of capacity closures. Fewer, larger producers and less capacity is the setup that normally supports pricing — the basis of BofA's September upgrade — but it only helps the producers whose own mills run reliably.

Two companies, or a company and a sale
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: today the market values one company whose profitable North American business is blended with a loss-making European one. Separation lets each trade on its own numbers, and since October 15 there is a second route: IP confirmed it is talking to potential bidders for EMEA. North America, with Q3 segment profit of $427M, would no longer carry EMEA's losses and restructuring. The bear framing: a spin-off moves value around but does not create any unless the pieces perform, and a sale below what IP paid would lock in the loss that the two write-downs already admit. The 2021 Sylvamo spin is the local precedent — the chart has not rewarded shareholders since. The way to judge it is not the P/E; it is EMEA segment profit moving back toward zero (or the sale price, if there is one) and North American EBITDA hitting the $700–740M Q4 guide.
