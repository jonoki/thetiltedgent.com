01 Company Overview

International Paper makes the brown box. It produces containerboard in its mills and converts it into corrugated boxes, bulk bins and shipping containers for food and beverage, agriculture, manufacturing and consumer-goods customers. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $24.20B, split between Packaging Solutions North America ($14.93B) and Packaging Solutions EMEA ($9.22B). Over the last twelve months the company has not made money on a GAAP basis: net loss of $3.44B, or −$6.50 per share, driven by a $2.47B goodwill write-down on the European business it bought only a year earlier.

This is a company in the middle of three transactions at once, and a reader of the five-year chart needs all three. October 1, 2021: IP spun off its printing-papers business as Sylvamo, handing shareholders one Sylvamo share for every eleven IP shares — a chunk of value left the IP share price that day without any shareholder losing it. January 31, 2025: IP completed its all-share acquisition of the UK's DS Smith, issuing 0.1285 new IP shares per DS Smith share (roughly 179.8 million new shares at the time of announcement) and adding a secondary London listing under "IPC"; the share count is up 27.35% year over year. January 29, 2026: barely a year after closing that deal, management announced it will split the company again — into a North American packaging company that keeps the International Paper name and an EMEA packaging company built mostly from DS Smith — with completion expected in 12–15 months. In between, it sold its Global Cellulose Fibers pulp business for $1.5B (closed January 23, 2026). The stock sits at $35.33, 40% below its November 2024 month-end high of $58.83. The question for the reader: is this the bottom of a messy integration that 80/20 cost-cutting is about to fix, or a European bet that the market has already marked down for good?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Mill network scale Integrated mill-to-box system Top-3 share of NA capacity High replacement cost of mills Local box-plant proximity Fiber sourcing & recycling

The moat in containerboard is physical and expensive to copy. Boxes are bulky and cheap, so they are made near customers; the mills that feed box plants cost billions and take years to build. Three companies — Smurfit Westrock, International Paper and Packaging Corporation of America — hold about 65% of North American containerboard capacity after the 2024–25 consolidation wave (TAPPI Paper360 via Mordor Intelligence). On the Q2 call, CEO Andy Silvernail argued the bar for new capacity is far higher than five years ago because mill replacement costs have soared, suggesting new entrants would need mid-to-high-teens returns on invested capital. IP itself earns a 4.28% ROIC today. That gap is the whole argument: the moat keeps competitors out, but it does not, by itself, make IP's own capital earn its keep.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Europe is losing money and is still 38% of sales. EMEA posted segment losses of $236M in FY2025 and $131M in H1 2026, and carried a $2.47B goodwill impairment. The separation removes it from IP's books eventually, but until then the losses, the restructuring cash and the separation costs ($43M in Q2) are IP shareholders' problem.
02
Execution risk on the mill system. Q2 had heavy planned outages and Riverdale conversion costs; Q3 carries an unplanned Pine Hill closure worth an estimated $85M. The FY2026 guide already came down once, by $300M at both ends of the range. A second cut would hit a management team whose credibility rests on 80/20 execution.
03
Input costs and freight. Management said price increases have largely been offset by inflation in recovered fiber (old corrugated containers), energy and freight, and cited about $100M of added macro headwinds from the prolonged Middle East conflict via transport spot rates and diesel.
04
The dividend is not covered by free cash flow. $1.85 a share, a 5.24% yield, costs roughly $980M a year against $492M of TTM free cash flow. It is being funded partly by the balance sheet while net debt sits at $9.18B. The consensus table on StockAnalysis shows a FY2026 dividend of $1.80 — a projection, not a company announcement.

Notable One-Off Items

Nearly every headline number is distorted. FY2025 loss from continuing operations of $2.84B includes a $2.47B pre-tax, non-cash goodwill impairment on PS EMEA (recorded December 31, 2025, alongside the separation review), about $960M of accelerated depreciation and $630M of restructuring charges, per the full-year results as reported by Paper Advance. The gap to the $3.52B total net loss (≈$0.68B, by subtraction) is discontinued operations — the GCF pulp business, sold to American Industrial Partners for $1.5B including $190M of preferred stock. Q2 2026 net special items were a $42M after-tax charge ($0.08 per share), mostly EMEA separation costs. On an adjusted basis Q2 2026 operating EPS was $0.04, against $0.18 a year earlier. Finally, DS Smith was paid for in shares, not cash, so revenue grew 49% in FY2025 while revenue per share grew far less.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Strip out the write-downs and you are buying North America's largest containerboard producer at 7.1x trailing EBITDA, with a 5.24% dividend, in an industry where three players control about 65% of capacity and new mills are too expensive to build. The losses are concentrated in one segment that is being separated within about a year, and the $2.47B goodwill write-down means the bad news on Europe is already on the balance sheet. North America is getting cheaper and more focused: Riverdale is converted, NORPAC is bought, and management expects around $600M more EBITDA in the second half than the first. Consensus has adjusted EPS going from $1.33 in 2026 to $3.01 in 2027. Ten of twelve analysts rate it Buy, the lowest target is 10% above the price, and BofA's September upgrade was a bet on containerboard prices rising.

Bear Case

IP issued about 180 million shares to buy DS Smith, wrote off $2.47B of it within a year, and is now spinning it off. The per-share damage is done. The dividend costs about twice the trailing free cash flow while net debt sits at $9.18B and interest coverage is 1.87x. ROIC of 4.3% is below the cost of capital. Guidance has already been cut once in 2026 and the second half has to deliver about $2.0B of EBITDA against $1.26B in the first. Input costs are eating price increases. A $35 stock with a $47.82 average target has been "cheap" all the way down from $58.83, and the April low of $30.42 is only 14% below today's close.

Top 3 Risks

01
Second-half delivery. The FY2026 guide of $3.2–3.4B needs roughly $2.0B of EBITDA in H2 versus $1.26B in H1, with Pine Hill already costing ~$85M in Q3. A miss on October 28 would be the second downward revision in a year.
02
Separation cost and terms. The EMEA spin carries separation costs ($54M pre-tax in H1 2026), restructuring cash (31 closures, 3,000+ roles) and the unknowns of how debt is split between the two companies — none of which is disclosed in the sources fetched.
03
Dividend and balance sheet. $980M a year of dividends against $492M of TTM free cash flow, $9.18B of net debt and heavy capex. If the H2 ramp slips, something gives — capex, debt or the payout. Consensus already shows a slightly lower FY2026 dividend ($1.80) than the current run-rate.
Industry Commentary
Input-cost inflation versus box pricing
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Containerboard is a spread business: the price of paper and boxes against the cost of fiber, energy and freight. In Q2 2026 IP said recent price increases were largely offset by inflation in old corrugated containers, energy and freight, and it cited about $100M of added headwinds from the prolonged Middle East conflict through transport rates and diesel. In EMEA the squeeze ran the other way inside the company: higher paper prices had not yet reached box prices, hurting packaging margins. Second-half demand expectations were cut from a 1% uptick to "generally stable" as inflation and affordability weighed on consumer spending.

Consolidation and capacity discipline
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

The formation of Smurfit Westrock, IP's combination with DS Smith and Packaging Corporation of America's purchase of Greif's containerboard business left about 65% of North American capacity with three firms (TAPPI Paper360, via Mordor Intelligence). Supply has also been cut: Mordor cites AF&PA data showing North American containerboard production down 8% year over year in Q1 2026 after the 2025 round of capacity closures. Fewer, larger producers and less capacity is the setup that normally supports pricing — the basis of BofA's September upgrade — but it only helps the producers whose own mills run reliably.

Two companies, two valuations
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: today the market values one company whose profitable North American business is blended with a loss-making European one. Separation lets each trade on its own numbers; North America, with segment profit of $572M in FY2025 even in a year of heavy disruption, would no longer carry EMEA's losses and restructuring. The bear framing: a spin-off moves value around but does not create any unless the pieces perform, and IP shareholders will still own both. The 2021 Sylvamo spin is the local precedent — the chart has not rewarded shareholders since. The way to judge it is not the P/E; it is quarterly EMEA segment profit moving back toward zero and North American EBITDA hitting the second-half ramp. Both are reported every quarter.
