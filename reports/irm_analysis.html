01 Company Overview

Iron Mountain is a specialty REIT that stores things for other people: 740.5 million cubic feet of physical records as of Q2 2026, and, increasingly, megawatts of data-centre capacity. It serves more than 240,000 customers in 61 countries, including roughly 95% of the Fortune 1000. FY2025 revenue was $6.90B, up 12.2% from $6.15B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $7.56B, up 17.4%. At $33.61B of market capitalisation it ranks roughly #739 globally. Because this is a REIT, the page leads with AFFO and FFO per share rather than EPS; GAAP earnings for a business carrying $19.9B of debt and heavy depreciation are close to meaningless here.

The central debate is a funding question, not a demand question. Iron Mountain is using the cash thrown off by a slow-growing, extraordinarily sticky physical-storage annuity — organic storage rental growth of 11.3% in Q2 2026, of which the records business contributed about 5% organically — to build a data-centre platform that is now 528.5 leasable megawatts, 97.1% leased, and growing revenue at 38.8% year over year. That build is not self-funding: trailing-twelve-month capital expenditure of $2.15B against operating cash flow of $1.66B produces negative free cash flow of −$491M, and the gap is filled with debt. Total debt stands at $19.95B against a market capitalisation of $33.61B and negative book equity of −$892M. Everything on this page reduces to one question: does the annuity stay intact long enough, and grow fast enough, to carry that balance sheet through the build?

A second, equally important cut: storage rental revenue of $1,135M (55.9%) versus service revenue of $894M (44.1%). Storage rental is the annuity — recurring, contracted, inflation-linked, with retention measured in decades. Service revenue is transactional: retrieval, shredding, digitisation, ALM resale. Service grew 27.4% in Q2 against storage's 12.3%, which is why the revenue mix is drifting away from the annuity even as the annuity itself keeps compounding. Faster growth, lower quality of earnings — that trade-off is the single most underappreciated fact in the reported numbers.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SWITCHING COST OF PHYSICAL BOXES CHAIN-OF-CUSTODY TRUST 240,000 CUSTOMERS / 61 COUNTRIES 95% OF FORTUNE 1000 REGULATORY RETENTION MANDATES OWNED REAL ESTATE FOOTPRINT CROSS-SELL INTO DATA CENTRE & ALM

The moat is inertia backed by regulation. A bank, hospital or law firm must keep certain records for seven, ten or thirty years, and must be able to prove custody of them. Once 10,000 cubic feet of that material sits in an Iron Mountain facility under an audited chain of custody, the cost of moving it — physical transport, re-indexing, re-certification, the regulatory risk of a gap in the audit trail — dwarfs any plausible price saving. That is why records volume is still rising (740.5M cubic feet, +2.5M sequentially) two decades into the supposed death of paper, and why storage rental margins hold at a 43.3% segment adjusted EBITDA margin. The second-order moat is the one that actually matters for the next five years: that incumbency is a warm-call list. Iron Mountain already has a procurement relationship with 95% of the Fortune 1000, so selling those same CIOs data-centre capacity and hardware decommissioning is a cross-sell, not a cold start — and the growth businesses collectively grew more than 50% year over year in Q2 2026 as a result. What the moat does not do is protect the data-centre business, which competes head-on with Equinix, Digital Realty and well-funded private platforms on price, power availability and delivery date.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Leverage, plainly stated. Total debt is $19.95B against negative shareholders' equity of −$892M and an enterprise value of $53.99B — meaning roughly 37% of the enterprise is debt. Company-defined net total lease-adjusted leverage is 4.8×; on unadjusted measures stockanalysis.com computes debt/EBITDA at 6.09×. Either way this is a highly levered balance sheet by any standard other than "other levered REITs," and it is being levered further to fund data-centre construction. The honest counterpoint, which the premise of this report understated: management stated on the Q2 2026 call that 4.8× is the lowest since before the 2014 REIT conversion, i.e. leverage is high in level but falling in trend. Both things are true and both matter.
▸
Free cash flow is negative and the dividend is funded on top of it. TTM operating cash flow of $1.66B against capital expenditure of $2.15B gives free cash flow of −$491M. The dividend costs roughly $1.03B a year at $3.456 per share on 297.7M shares. The company's 60% AFFO payout ratio is real and is the right REIT framing — but AFFO deducts only recurring capex, not the growth capex building the data centres. The gap is bridged with debt and equity issuance. That is a perfectly normal REIT development model; it is also a model that stops working if credit markets close.
▸
Hyperscale concentration and lumpiness. Q2 leasing was 13 MW; July alone was 75 MW, of which a single Mumbai lease was 51 MW. When individual contracts are that large, a two-quarter drought is indistinguishable in the data from a structural slowdown until well after the fact. Management already trimmed the 24-month energisation pipeline from ~400 MW to ~325 MW. Counterparty concentration in a handful of hyperscalers also means the credit quality of the growth segment is the credit quality of a few names.
▸
Interest-rate sensitivity works on both sides. With $19.95B of debt, a REIT structure that requires distributing most taxable income, and a 3.06% dividend yield that competes directly with the front end of the curve, IRM is exposed to rates through refinancing cost, through the discount rate applied to a long-duration cash-flow stream, and through the relative attractiveness of its yield. A beta of 1.20 for what is nominally a storage company reflects exactly that.

Notable one-off items. Three things distort the reported numbers and should be handled explicitly. (1) The EPS swing is not an operating event. TTM EPS of $1.40 is up 898.7% and TTM net income of $418.66M is up 912.6% — but off a FY2025 base of $144.59M that itself included quarters of GAAP losses (−$45M in Q2 2025, −$34M in Q3 2024). A REIT whose GAAP net income oscillates around zero on $7.5B of revenue is telling you that depreciation and financing charges, not operations, drive the bottom line. (2) Free cash flow is negative for a reason that is not distress. The −$491M is operating cash flow of $1.66B less $2.15B of capex, the overwhelming majority of which is data-centre development. Strip growth capex and the business is strongly cash-generative, which is precisely what the 60% AFFO payout ratio encodes. (3) Data-centre leasing is not a run-rate. 13 MW in Q2, 110 MW in H1, 75 MW in July. Annualising any single period produces nonsense. (4) Segment reporting hides ALM profitability — corporate overhead is commingled into the same line, so the −$31.1M adjusted EBITDA loss is not the ALM business's own economics. No restatements, impairments or discontinued operations were identified on the sources fetched.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 monthly close of $45.64 to the September 10, 2026 close of $112.90, IRM returned +147.4% on price alone — a compound annual rate of about 19.9%, before a dividend that averaged roughly 3–5% a year across the window. That is an extraordinary outcome for a business that spent the 2010s being described as a melting ice cube, and it happened in two distinct phases. Phase one, late 2021 through 2023, was a grind: the stock went from $45.64 to $69.98 over twenty-six months, roughly tracking a high-yield REIT with modest organic growth. Phase two began in early 2024, when the market re-underwrote the name as a data-centre and AI-adjacent story: from the January 2024 close of $67.52 to the October 2024 close of $123.73, the stock gained 83% in nine months without the underlying storage business changing at all. Since then it has been range-bound and violent — down to $82.95 in December 2025, back to a monthly peak of $128.25 in May 2026, and now 12.0% below that peak.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A near-indestructible cash annuity — 740.5M cubic feet of records still growing, 43.3% segment EBITDA margins, 95% of the Fortune 1000 as customers — is funding entry into the highest-demand real-estate category on earth. The data-centre book is 97.1% leased at a 52.2% EBITDA margin, H1 leasing of 110 MW already beat the full-year target, and July alone added 75 MW. ALM is compounding at 80%+ organically toward $1B. FY2026 AFFO per share guidance of $5.87–5.93 puts the stock at 19.1× forward AFFO with a covered 3.06% yield at a 60% payout — and leverage is at its lowest since before the 2014 REIT conversion while it all happens.

Bear Case

This is a $19.95B debt stack with negative book equity building capital-intensive assets into a category where every well-funded competitor is building the same thing. Free cash flow is −$491M; the $1.03B dividend and the construction programme are both funded from outside the business. ROIC is 5.32% — roughly a cost of capital, not a spread over it. Leasing is lumpy enough (13 MW, then 75 MW) that a bad quarter is unreadable, the energisation pipeline was already cut from ~400 MW to ~325 MW, and 10 of 11 analysts rate it Buy with a low target 24% above spot, which means positioning is crowded and expectations are set high.

Top 3 Risks

01
Balance-sheet fragility under a credit shock. $19.95B of debt, negative book equity, negative free cash flow and a development programme that must keep being financed. At 4.8× net lease-adjusted leverage (6.09× on unadjusted debt/EBITDA) the company is dependent on continuous access to debt and equity markets. A sustained widening of REIT credit spreads would raise refinancing cost, slow the build, and pressure the multiple simultaneously.
02
Data-centre leasing disappointing after an exceptional month. The stock's re-rating from $67.52 (Jan 2024) to $128.25 (May 2026) was a multiple event predicated on megawatts, not on the storage business. If Q3 or Q4 2026 leasing normalises toward the Q2 pace of 13 MW rather than the July pace of 75 MW, the market will re-test how much of the 19.1× forward AFFO multiple was really the annuity.
03
Rate and yield-competition risk. A 3.06% dividend yield is thin for a levered REIT and depends on the growth story holding. If long rates rise, the discount rate on a long-duration cash-flow stream rises, the refinancing cost on $19.95B rises, and the yield becomes less competitive against risk-free alternatives — three headwinds from a single variable. Beta of 1.20 means the market already prices IRM as rate- and risk-sensitive, not defensive.
Industry Commentary
AI inference demand is pulling hyperscale leasing forward
EMERGING TAILWIND

The July 2026 signings — 51 MW in Mumbai and 25 MW at London 3 — were attributed by the CEO directly to "the large and expanding pipeline we have around AI inference." That distinction matters. Training capacity has been the story for three years and clusters in a handful of enormous US campuses; inference capacity has to sit near the users, which favours a global footprint across 61 countries over raw scale in Northern Virginia. Iron Mountain's data-centre segment grew 38.8% year over year to $262.9M with capacity expanding from 450.2 MW to 528.5 MW and utilisation at 97.1%. Across the listed comparables the same demand is visible: Equinix TTM revenue of $9.90B (+10.9%), Digital Realty $6.76B (+18.8%) — IRM is growing this segment roughly twice as fast as either, off a base a fraction of their size.

Capital intensity meets a levered balance sheet
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Data centres cost roughly an order of magnitude more per square foot than records warehouses, and Iron Mountain is building them while already carrying $19.95B of total debt against negative shareholders' equity of −$891.77M. TTM capex of $2.15B exceeded operating cash flow of $1.66B, and the ~$1.03B annual dividend sits on top of that. The company's answer — 4.8× net lease-adjusted leverage, its lowest since before the 2014 REIT conversion, and a 60% AFFO payout ratio — is genuinely better than the raw numbers suggest, and deserves to be said plainly alongside the debt figure. But the constraint is arithmetic: every incremental megawatt requires capital the operating business does not currently produce, and the cost of that capital is set by markets IRM does not control. This is the reason the stock trades at 19.1× forward AFFO rather than the multiple a 38%-growth data-centre platform would otherwise command.

A REIT wrapper on a business the market no longer values as a REIT
ONGOING HEADWIND

IRM is classified Real Estate / REIT — Specialty, sits in REIT indices, and is held in size by a dedicated real-estate manager (Cohen & Steers, $1.57B). Yet it yields 3.06% against a specialty-REIT cohort that generally yields more, carries a beta of 1.20 where REITs typically run below 1.0, and grew revenue 17.4% where the sector grows mid-single-digit. It is owned by two constituencies with incompatible requirements: income investors who want coverage, distribution growth and falling leverage, and growth investors who want megawatts, ALM scale and reinvestment. Bull framing: that dual ownership is why the stock re-rated 83% in nine months during 2024 — when growth buyers arrive, they pay multiples income buyers never would. Bear framing: it is also why the stock fell 33% into December 2025 while fundamentals improved — when growth buyers leave, the income buyers who remain will only pay a REIT price for a 3% yield, and that price is a long way below $112.90. Both sets of buyers have to stay for the current multiple to hold.
