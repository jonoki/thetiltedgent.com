01 Company Overview

Iron Mountain is a specialty REIT that stores things for other people: 743.3 million cubic feet of physical records at September 30, 2026, and, increasingly, megawatts of data-centre capacity. It serves more than 240,000 customers in 61 countries, including roughly 95% of the Fortune 1000. FY2025 revenue was $6.90B, up 12.2% from $6.15B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $7.80B, up 17.5%. At $31.72B of market capitalisation it ranks #306 in the S&P 500. Because this is a REIT, the page leads with AFFO and FFO per share rather than EPS; GAAP earnings (profit under standard accounting rules) for a business carrying $20.5B of debt and heavy depreciation are close to meaningless here.

The central debate is a funding question, not a demand question. Iron Mountain is using the cash thrown off by a slow-growing, extraordinarily sticky physical-storage business — organic storage rental growth (excluding acquisitions and currency) of 11.1% across the company in Q3 2026 and about 5% in the records segment — to build a data-centre platform that is now 544.6 leasable megawatts, 97.2% leased, and growing revenue at 34.0% year over year. That build is not self-funding: trailing-twelve-month capital expenditure of $2.24B against operating cash flow of $1.70B produces negative free cash flow of −$537M, and the gap is filled with debt. Total debt including lease liabilities stands at $20.49B against a market capitalisation of $31.72B and total equity of −$1.06B. The third quarter settled the demand question for now, with a record 102.5 MW of leasing. What it raised is the growth question: total revenue grew 21.6% in Q1, 18.5% in Q2 and 13.5% in Q3, and management guided to about 10% for Q4. Everything on this page reduces to one question: does the storage business stay intact long enough, and grow fast enough, to carry that balance sheet through the build?

The oldest bear case on this stock — that paper dies and the boxes come home — was wrong again this quarter. The bucket the market watches least and the one that moved most this quarter. ALM revenue was $241M in Q3 2026, up $72M or 43% year over year, down from $288M in Q2. The CFO put about $30M of that swing down to decommissioning work for hyperscalers (the giant cloud companies) that moved from Q3 forward into Q2, and said that "adjusting for the shift, our third quarter decommissioning growth would have been in excess of 50%." He said enterprise ALM grew "greater than 50% organically," and that memory prices "remained elevated as compared to last year." Management now expects ALM revenue "modestly ahead of $1 billion" for 2026 and is planning for "25-plus percent growth next year." The logic: hyperscalers refreshing GPU and server fleets for AI need someone to wipe, certify and resell the retired kit, and Iron Mountain already holds the chain-of-custody trust relationship from the records business. The caution is that resale revenue moves with used-hardware prices as well as volume, and the segment as reported still runs an adjusted EBITDA loss of −$36.0M, narrowed from −$45.5M a year ago. Corporate overhead sits in this line too, so the loss is not purely operating; the company does not disclose ALM-only profitability, and specialist IT-disposal competitors do not report comparable figures.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SWITCHING COST OF PHYSICAL BOXES CHAIN-OF-CUSTODY TRUST 240,000 CUSTOMERS / 61 COUNTRIES 95% OF FORTUNE 1000 REGULATORY RETENTION MANDATES OWNED REAL ESTATE FOOTPRINT CROSS-SELL INTO DATA CENTRE & ALM

The moat is inertia backed by regulation. A bank, hospital or law firm must keep certain records for seven, ten or thirty years, and must be able to prove custody of them. Once 10,000 cubic feet of that material sits in an Iron Mountain facility under an audited chain of custody, the cost of moving it — physical transport, re-indexing, re-certification, the regulatory risk of a gap in the audit trail — dwarfs any plausible price saving. That is why records volume is still rising (743.3M cubic feet, +2.8M sequentially) two decades into the supposed death of paper, and why the records segment held a 44.0% adjusted EBITDA margin in Q3. The second advantage is the one that matters for the next five years: those existing customers are people Iron Mountain can already call. Iron Mountain already sells to 95% of the Fortune 1000, so selling those same CIOs data-centre capacity and hardware decommissioning starts from an existing relationship, and the growth businesses collectively grew more than 30% year over year in Q3 2026 (more than 50% in Q2). What the moat does not do is protect the data-centre business, which competes head-on with Equinix, Digital Realty and well-funded private platforms on price, power availability and delivery date.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Leverage, plainly stated. Total debt including lease liabilities is $20.49B (StockAnalysis; long-term debt alone, current portion included, is $17.97B on the September 30 balance sheet) against total equity of −$1.06B and an enterprise value of about $52.02B, market value plus net debt at the November 6 close. Roughly 39% of the enterprise is debt. Company-defined net lease-adjusted leverage is 4.8×, down from 5.0× a year earlier; on unadjusted measures StockAnalysis computes debt/EBITDA at 6.08×. The CFO called 4.8× the lowest the company has had on this measure since before its 2014 conversion to a REIT, and said borrowings and interest will rise as the build continues. The level is high and the direction has been down. Both matter.
▸
Free cash flow is negative and the dividend is funded on top of it. TTM operating cash flow of $1.70B against capital expenditure of $2.24B gives free cash flow of −$537M. The dividend now costs roughly $1.18B a year at the new $3.972 per share on 297.83M shares. The company's 59.2% TTM AFFO payout ratio is real and is the right REIT framing, but AFFO deducts only recurring capex, not the growth capex building the data centres. The gap is bridged with debt and equity issuance. That is a normal REIT development model; it is also a model that stops working if credit markets close.
▸
Hyperscale concentration and lumpiness. Q2 leasing was 13 MW; Q3 was a record 102.5 MW, of which 51 MW was in Mumbai alone. When signings are that large and that concentrated, a two-quarter drought is indistinguishable in the data from a real slowdown until well after the fact. Counterparty concentration in a handful of hyperscalers also means the credit quality of the growth segment is the credit quality of a few names.
▸
Interest-rate sensitivity works on both sides. With $20.49B of debt, a REIT structure that requires distributing most taxable income, and a 3.73% dividend yield that competes directly with short-term Treasury yields, IRM is exposed to rates through refinancing cost, through the rate investors use to value cash flows many years out, and through the relative attractiveness of its yield. A beta of 1.20 for what is nominally a storage company reflects exactly that.

Notable one-off items. Four things distort the reported numbers. (1) GAAP EPS is noisy. TTM EPS of $1.53 sits on TTM net income of about $458M, against $7.80B of revenue; depreciation and financing charges, not operations, drive the bottom line. In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 month-end close of $45.44 to the November 6, 2026 close of $106.52, IRM returned +134.4% on price alone — a compound annual rate of about 18.9%, before dividends, against +61.4% for SPY and +66.6% for QQQ over the same dates. That is an extraordinary outcome for a business once described as a melting ice cube, and it happened in two phases. Phase one, late 2021 through 2023, was a grind: the stock went from $45.44 to $69.98 over twenty-five months, roughly tracking a high-yield REIT with modest organic growth. Phase two began in early 2024, when investors started valuing it as a data-centre and AI-adjacent story: from the January 2024 close of $67.52 to the October 2024 close of $123.73, the stock gained 83% in nine months. Since then it has been range-bound and violent — down to $82.95 in December 2025, back to a monthly peak of $128.25 in May 2026, and now 16.9% below that peak.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A near-indestructible cash business — 743.3M cubic feet of records still growing, 44.0% segment EBITDA margins, 95% of the Fortune 1000 as customers — is funding entry into one of the highest-demand real-estate categories. The data-centre book is 97.2% leased at a 56.3% EBITDA margin, Q3 leasing of 102.5 MW was a record, and management expects 2027 data-centre revenue of more than $1.3B. ALM should finish 2026 modestly ahead of $1B. FY2026 AFFO per share guidance of $5.94–6.00 puts the stock at 17.8× forward AFFO with a covered 3.73% yield at a 59.2% payout, after a 15% dividend raise, and net lease-adjusted leverage is 4.8×, the lowest since before its 2014 REIT conversion.

Bear Case

This is a $20.49B pile of debt with negative book equity building capital-intensive assets into a category where every well-funded competitor is building the same thing. Free cash flow is −$537M; the $1.18B dividend and the construction programme are both funded from outside the business. ROIC is 5.37%, a thin return on the money tied up in the business. Revenue growth has slowed from 21.6% in Q1 to 13.5% in Q3 and about 10% guided for Q4. Leasing is lumpy enough (13 MW, then 102.5 MW) that a bad quarter is unreadable, and 11 of 12 analysts rate it Buy with the lowest target 16.4% above the price, so expectations are set high.

Top 3 Risks

01
Balance-sheet fragility under a credit shock. $20.49B of debt including leases, negative book equity, negative free cash flow and a development programme that must keep being financed. At 4.8× net lease-adjusted leverage (6.08× on unadjusted debt/EBITDA) the company depends on continuous access to debt and equity markets. A lasting rise in the extra interest lenders charge REITs over government bonds would raise refinancing cost, slow the build, and pressure the price investors will pay for the shares at the same time.
02
Revenue growth and data-centre leasing both disappointing after an exceptional quarter. The rise from $67.52 (Jan 2024) to $128.25 (May 2026) came from investors paying up for megawatts, not from the storage business. The November 5 fall came as revenue growth slowed and Q4 revenue was guided below forecasts. If leasing also returns to Q2's 13 MW pace rather than Q3's 102.5 MW, investors will re-test how much of the 17.8× forward AFFO multiple was really the storage business.
03
Rate and yield-competition risk. A 3.73% dividend yield is thin for a REIT carrying this debt and depends on the growth story holding. If long rates rise, the rate investors use to value far-off cash flows rises, the refinancing cost on $20.49B rises, and the yield becomes less competitive against risk-free alternatives — three headwinds from a single variable. Beta of 1.20 means the market already treats IRM as sensitive to rates and risk, not defensive.
Industry Commentary
AI inference demand is pulling hyperscale leasing forward
EMERGING TAILWIND

Q3's signings — 74 MW in India and 25 MW at London 3 — came with a CFO comment that hyperscale demand for "cloud and inference capacity" is outpacing supply, and the CEO said: "Industry demand remains incredibly strong." That distinction matters. Training capacity has been the story for three years and clusters in a handful of enormous US campuses; inference capacity has to sit near the users, which favours a footprint spread across major markets over raw scale in Northern Virginia. Iron Mountain's data-centre segment grew 34.0% year over year to $273.6M with capacity expanding from 452.2 MW to 544.6 MW and 97.2% leased. Across the listed comparables the same demand is visible: Equinix TTM revenue of $10.15B (+11.8%), Digital Realty $7.08B (+21.2%). IRM is growing this segment faster than either, off a base a fraction of their size.

Capital intensity meets a debt-heavy balance sheet
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Iron Mountain is building data centres while already carrying $20.49B of total debt including leases against total equity of −$1.06B. TTM capex of $2.24B exceeded operating cash flow of $1.70B, and the roughly $1.18B annual dividend at the new rate sits on top of that. The company's answer — 4.8× net lease-adjusted leverage, the lowest since before its 2014 REIT conversion, and a 59.2% AFFO payout ratio — is better than the raw numbers suggest, and deserves to be said plainly alongside the debt figure. But the constraint is arithmetic: every extra megawatt requires capital the operating business does not currently produce, and the cost of that capital is set by markets IRM does not control. The stock trades at 17.8× forward AFFO even with a data-centre segment growing 34%.

A REIT by structure, valued by the market as something else
ONGOING HEADWIND

IRM is classified Real Estate / REIT — Specialty, sits in REIT indices, and counts a dedicated real-estate manager among its holders (Cohen & Steers, 4.89% at April 30, 2026). Yet it yields 3.73%, carries a beta of 1.20, meaning it has moved more than the market, and grew revenue 17.5% over the last twelve months. It is owned by two groups with incompatible requirements: income investors who want coverage, distribution growth and falling leverage, and growth investors who want megawatts, ALM scale and reinvestment. Bull framing: that dual ownership is why the stock rose 83% in nine months during 2024 — when growth buyers arrive, they pay prices income buyers never would. Bear framing: it is also why the stock fell 33% into December 2025 while fundamentals improved, and fell 11.1% on November 5 on a day of record leasing — when growth buyers leave, the income buyers who remain will only pay a REIT price for a 3.7% yield, and that price is a long way below $106.52. Both sets of buyers have to stay for the current valuation to hold.
