01 Company Overview

Invesco is a global asset manager: it runs other people's money — for retail investors, institutions, pension funds and sovereign wealth funds — and charges a fee on the balance. The company was AMVESCAP PLC until May 2007, moved its primary listing to the New York Stock Exchange and redomiciled from the UK to Bermuda in December 2007, joined the S&P 500 on August 21, 2008, and bought MassMutual's OppenheimerFunds in May 2019. Preliminary AUM reached $2,562.1 billion on August 31, 2026, up 4.7% in a single month. For the business it runs, the market value is small: $13.85B, about 0.54% of the assets it manages.

An asset manager's earnings come down to one equation: average AUM × fee rate − costs. For most of the last decade, Invesco's AUM grew while its fee rate shrank, as money moved from expensive active funds into cheap index funds. What changed in the past year is that Invesco found a way to earn more from the cheap side. On December 20, 2025 the Invesco QQQ Trust — a Nasdaq-100 tracker with $489B of assets at August 31, 2026 — converted from a unit investment trust into an open-end fund ETF. Before that, the fund generated pass-through service revenue; after it, Invesco earns a management fee on it. Management put the gain at $130 million of extra net revenue in the first half of 2026 (per the Q2 call transcript). Add record inflows ($45.1B of net long-term inflows in Q2 2026) and flat quarter-on-quarter expenses, and the adjusted operating margin rose to 37.5%. The stock went from a $13.93 close in April 2025 to $31.38. The central question now is whether Invesco can keep growing faster than its fees shrink. Assets are flowing into its lowest-fee products, and the fee rate is going down: 22.4 bps in Q2 2026, with a 22.0 bps exit rate.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

QQQ brand & liquidity Low-tax-basis holder lock-in $2.56T scale Global distribution (Americas, EMEA, APAC) China JV access Operating leverage

The strongest asset is QQQ itself. On the call, Schlossberg described its advantage as "tight bid-ask spreads, deep on-the-screen liquidity." Its long-time holders face a tax bill if they switch, because they have low tax basis positions. A cheaper Nasdaq-100 tracker can win new money, but it cannot pull the existing $489B across without those holders paying capital-gains tax. That is real switching cost in a business that usually has almost none. Everywhere else, the moat is scale: a platform that grew net revenue 5% quarter-on-quarter in Q2 2026 on flat expenses. The weakness is the other side of scale. Invesco's growth is concentrated in the products where it has the least pricing power.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Fee compression. The net revenue yield fell to 22.4 bps with a 22.0 bps exit rate because flows go to QQQ, QQQM and RSP. If Invesco has to cut the QQQ fee to defend share, the conversion gain ($130M of extra net revenue in H1 2026) shrinks with it. Management says it is resisting fee cuts. That is a stated choice, not a guarantee.
02
Market beta is the business model. Fees are charged on AUM, and AUM moves with markets: August 2026 alone added $49B from market returns. A 20% equity drawdown takes revenue down with it, while costs are largely fixed. StockAnalysis puts the 5-year beta at 1.61, a leveraged bet on the market.
03
Active equity is shrinking. Fundamental Equities lost $7.7B in Q2 2026, and the US retail fund contracts behind it were written down by $1.79B in Q4 2025. That book still earns the highest fees in the firm, so every dollar that leaves it costs more revenue than a dollar entering an ETF adds.
04
The MassMutual preferred. $2.5B of Series A preferred ranks ahead of common shareholders and costs about $148M a year in dividends (the Q2 2026 $37.0M payment × 4, an estimate). Repurchasing it would lift common EPS, but the CFO said doing so requires a negotiation with MassMutual over "rate environment and required premiums." The 2025 repurchase came with a $159.3M cost-of-repurchase charge in Q2 2025.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the Q4 2025 impairment: a $1,794.9M non-cash charge on US retail mutual-fund management contracts, which cut Q4 diluted EPS by $3.01 and swung FY2025 to a GAAP net loss of $726.3M (EPS −$1.60) against adjusted EPS of $2.03. Second, the preferred stock repurchases: $1.5B of MassMutual preferred was bought back in 2025 ($1.0B in Q2, $500M on December 15). The Q2 2025 deal carried a $159.3M cost that turned that quarter's GAAP EPS negative (−$0.03). Third, the QQQ conversion on December 20, 2025 changes how the fund shows up in revenue, from pass-through service fees to management fees. Year-over-year comparisons of individual revenue lines through Q4 2026 are therefore not like-for-like.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 11.2x FY2026 consensus earnings and 9.6x FY2027, you get a manager that just reported record inflows ($45.1B in Q2, $67B in H1, 7% annualized organic growth), a new fee stream from QQQ that did not exist before December 2025, an adjusted margin that rose 6.3 points in a year toward a stated "high 30s" target, and a headcount that fell 11.9% while revenue rose 20.5%. That is operating leverage working as it should. Net debt fell by more than $450M in one quarter. The next step is the $2.5B preferred, and every dollar of it retired goes to common shareholders. The stock is 125% off its April 2025 low, but the earnings are growing too: consensus EPS is up 38% this year.

Bear Case

Invesco is growing where it earns the least and shrinking where it earns the most. The fee rate is 22.4 bps and falling (22.0 exit), active equity lost $7.7B in its best quarter ever, and the US retail fund book was written down by $1.79B. The QQQ gain was a one-time step-up, not a growth rate, and competition on fees can take it back. With a 1.61 beta and AUM that rose $49B in August on market returns alone, a large part of 2026's earnings is simply the market going up. Nine of fourteen analysts rate the stock Hold, the monthly RSI spent eight straight months above 70, and the consensus target implies only 8% upside.

Top 3 Risks

01
Market drawdown. Fees are charged on AUM, so revenue falls with markets while costs do not. With a 5-year beta of 1.61 and half the book in equity index products (QQQ alone is $489B), a broad equity sell-off hits revenue and margin at the same time. 2022 is the precedent: revenue −12.3% and operating margin down 4 points.
02
Fee compression on the growth products. The 22.0 bps exit rate is below the Q2 average. If QQQ's fee has to fall to meet cheaper Nasdaq-100 trackers, the $130M-per-half-year conversion benefit shrinks. Management's defence is "total cost of ownership," which holds only as long as clients agree.
03
Active equity outflows and the preferred overhang. Fundamental Equities (−$7.7B in Q2) is the highest-fee book, and it is shrinking. Meanwhile $2.5B of MassMutual preferred still ranks ahead of common holders, and its retirement depends on a negotiation over "rate environment and required premiums" that has no date.
Industry Commentary
Passive vs. active: the fee-rate squeeze
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Every traditional asset manager faces the same squeeze: money keeps moving from active funds to index funds, and index funds charge a fraction of the fee. Invesco's Q2 2026 is a clean example. The firm took in $45.1B of long-term money, a record, yet its fee rate slipped, because $43.9B of it went to ETFs, index strategies and QQQ while active equity lost $7.7B. The $1.79B impairment on US retail mutual-fund contracts is what the squeeze looks like on a balance sheet. Invesco's answer has been to be on the winning side of the shift rather than to fight it, and on the evidence of 2026 that has worked. Revenue can grow even as the fee rate falls, as long as AUM grows faster.

Market levels do most of the work
CYCLICAL EXPOSURE

In August 2026, market returns added $49B to Invesco's AUM, nearly twice the month's $27.6B of net long-term inflows. That is the normal state of an asset manager: in any given year, market movement tends to matter more than flows, and costs do not adjust quickly when markets fall. The 2022 record makes the point. Revenue fell 12.3% and operating margin fell from 25.0% to 21.0% in one year. The 2026 margin gains are real operating leverage, and operating leverage works in both directions.

Scale and structure as the new moat
EMERGING TAILWIND

The QQQ conversion shows how an old product structure can hold back earnings, and how changing it can release them. A fund Invesco had sponsored for years became a management-fee earner overnight and added $130M of net revenue in six months. The bull framing: Invesco owns one of the most liquid ETFs in the world, holders cannot leave without a tax bill, and scale lets it grow revenue on flat costs, which is why the margin reached 37.5%. The bear framing: the conversion was a single step-up, and it is already in the numbers. From here, earnings growth has to come from AUM and flows, in a business where the price of the product falls every year. The measure to watch is the net revenue yield against average AUM growth: if the yield falls faster than AUM grows, the margin gains stop.
