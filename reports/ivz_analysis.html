01 Company Overview

Invesco is a global asset manager: it runs other people's money — for retail investors, institutions, pension funds and sovereign wealth funds — and charges a fee on the balance. The company was AMVESCAP PLC until May 2007, moved its primary listing to the New York Stock Exchange and redomiciled from the UK to Bermuda in December 2007, joined the S&P 500 on August 21, 2008, and bought MassMutual's OppenheimerFunds in May 2019. AUM stood at $2,537.8 billion on September 30, 2026, up 19.4% from a year earlier and 2.7% from June (Q3 2026 earnings release, sec.gov). For the business it runs, the market value is small: $12.20B, about 0.48% of the assets it manages.

An asset manager's earnings come down to one equation: average AUM × fee rate − costs. For most of the last decade, Invesco's AUM grew while its fee rate shrank, as money moved from expensive active funds into cheap index funds. What changed in the past year is that Invesco found a way to earn more from the cheap side. On December 20, 2025 the Invesco QQQ Trust — a Nasdaq-100 tracker with $498.9B of assets at September 30, 2026 — converted from a unit investment trust into an open-end fund ETF. Before that, the fund generated pass-through service revenue; after it, Invesco earns a management fee on it. Management put the gain at $130 million of extra net revenue in the first half of 2026 (per the Q2 call transcript). Add inflows ($45.1B of net long-term inflows in Q2 2026, a record, and $35.1B in Q3, the 13th straight quarter of inflows) and expenses that stayed flat or fell, and the adjusted operating margin rose from 37.5% in Q2 to 40.7% in Q3, above the high-30s level management had aimed for on the Q2 call. The stock went from a $13.93 close in April 2025 to $27.76, after a month-end peak of $32.78 in August 2026. The central question now is whether Invesco can keep growing faster than its fees shrink. Assets are flowing into its lowest-fee products, and the fee rate is going down: 22.4 bps in Q2 2026 and about 22.0 in Q3 (our calculation from the release, which does not state it). One day before the Q3 results, on October 26, 2026, Invesco repurchased another $1.5 billion of the MassMutual preferred stock, at a 12.5% premium over face value, paid for with a new $1.5 billion five-year floating-rate bank term loan. About $1 billion of that preferred stock remains outstanding.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

QQQ brand & liquidity Holders face a tax bill to switch $2.54T scale Global distribution (Americas, EMEA, APAC) China JV access Costs flat as revenue grows

The strongest asset is QQQ itself. On the Q2 call, Schlossberg described its advantage as "tight bid-ask spreads, deep on-the-screen liquidity." Its long-time holders face a tax bill if they switch, because many bought at far lower prices and would owe capital-gains tax on selling (the CEO cited "the low relative tax base of so many in the QQQ"). A cheaper Nasdaq-100 tracker can win new money, but it cannot pull the existing $498.9B across without those holders paying capital-gains tax. That is real switching cost in a business that usually has almost none. Everywhere else, the moat is scale: a platform that grew net revenue 4% quarter-on-quarter in Q3 2026 while adjusted operating expenses fell 1%. The weakness is the other side of scale. Invesco's growth is concentrated in the products where it has the least pricing power.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Fee compression. The net revenue yield fell from 22.4 bps in Q2 to about 22.0 in Q3 (our calculation) because flows go to QQQ, QQQM and RSP. If Invesco has to cut the QQQ fee to defend share, the conversion gain ($130M of extra net revenue in H1 2026) shrinks with it. Management says it is resisting fee cuts. That is a stated choice, not a guarantee.
02
The business rides the market. Fees are charged on AUM, and AUM moves with markets: August 2026 alone added $49B from market returns, while market moves cut AUM by $16B in September and $26.4B over Q3 as a whole. A 20% equity drawdown takes revenue down with it, while costs are largely fixed. StockAnalysis puts the 5-year beta at 1.63: over five years the stock has tended to move about 1.6 times as much as the market.
03
Active equity is shrinking. Fundamental Equities lost $1.9B in Q3 2026 and $7.7B in Q2, and the US retail fund contracts behind it were written down by $1.79B in Q4 2025. That book still earns the highest fees in the firm, so every dollar that leaves it costs more revenue than a dollar entering an ETF adds.
04
The MassMutual preferred and the new loan. About $1.0B of Series A preferred still ranks ahead of common shareholders after the October 26 repurchase of $1.5B. The preferred pays a fixed 5.9% a year (the October 27, 2026 8-K names it the 5.9% Fixed Rate Non-Cumulative Perpetual Series A Preference Shares), so the remaining $1.0B costs about $59M a year in dividends. Invesco paid MassMutual a 12.5% premium over the shares' $1,000 face value, and paid for the repurchase with a $1.5B five-year floating-rate bank term loan: SOFR, a benchmark overnight lending rate, plus 1.35 percentage points at its current credit ratings (same 8-K). So Invesco swapped a fixed preferred dividend for debt whose interest cost moves with rates. Debt was $1,281.3M at September 30; adding the $1.5B loan gives about $2.78B (our arithmetic).

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the Q4 2025 impairment: a $1,794.9M non-cash charge on US retail mutual-fund management contracts, which cut Q4 diluted EPS by $3.01 and swung FY2025 to a GAAP net loss of $726.3M (EPS −$1.60) against adjusted EPS of $2.03. Second, the preferred stock repurchases: $1.5B of MassMutual preferred was bought back in 2025 ($1.0B in Q2, $500M on December 15), and a further $1.5B on October 26, 2026, after the Q3 quarter-end, so it is not in the Q3 figures. The Q2 2025 deal carried a $159.3M cost that turned that quarter's GAAP EPS negative (−$0.03). Third, the QQQ conversion on December 20, 2025 changes how the fund shows up in revenue, from pass-through service fees to management fees. Year-over-year comparisons of individual revenue lines through Q4 2026 are therefore not like-for-like.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"). Readings below 30 mean it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value: an expensive stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the October 2021 close of $25.41 to the October 30, 2026 settled close of $27.76, IVZ gained 9.2% in price, before dividends. The route matters more than the endpoints. For three and a half years the stock went nowhere, closing between $12.97 (October 2023) and $19.34 (May 2022) every month from April 2022 to June 2025. As late as April 2025 it closed at $13.93, 45% below where the window began. Then it more than doubled in sixteen months, to a five-year-high month-end of $32.78 in August 2026, and has since given back 15.3% of that.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how one-sided that rally was. It crossed 70 in July 2025 and stayed above it for eight straight month-ends, peaking at 86.8 in January 2026, the most overbought reading in the series. It cooled to 57.9 in March 2026, topped 70 again in May, July and August 2026, and now reads 51.5, near the neutral midpoint. The 10-month moving average, about $27.92, sits almost exactly at the price, so the rally's momentum has faded. The question is how much of the good news is already priced.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 9.9x FY2026 consensus earnings and 8.5x FY2027, you get a manager that just reported its 13th straight quarter of net inflows ($35.1B in Q3, after a record $45.1B in Q2), a new fee stream from QQQ that did not exist before December 2025, an adjusted margin that rose 6.5 points in a year to 40.7%, and a headcount that fell 11.4% while revenue rose 14.2%. That is the cost side working as it should: costs grow slower than revenue. Net debt fell by $393M in Q3. The next step was the $2.5B preferred: $1.5B of it was retired on October 26 and every dollar of the remainder retired goes to common shareholders. The stock is still 99% above its April 2025 low, and the earnings are growing too: the consensus expects adjusted EPS to rise 37% this year ($2.79 against 2025's $2.03).

Bear Case

Invesco is growing where it earns the least and shrinking where it earns the most. The fee rate is about 22 bps and falling, active equity lost $1.9B in Q3 and $7.7B in Q2, fixed income lost $2.6B in Q3, institutional clients withdrew $6.6B, and the US retail fund book was written down by $1.79B. The QQQ gain was a one-time step-up, not a growth rate, and competition on fees can take it back. With a 1.63 beta and AUM that rose $49B in August on market returns alone, a large part of 2026's earnings is simply the market going up. The $1.5B preferred repurchase was paid for with floating-rate bank debt. Nine of fifteen analysts rate the stock Hold, the monthly RSI spent eight straight months above 70, and the stock is 15% below its August close.

Top 3 Risks

01
Market drawdown. Fees are charged on AUM, so revenue falls with markets while costs do not. With a 5-year beta of 1.63 and half the book in equity index products (QQQ alone is $498.9B), a broad equity sell-off hits revenue and margin at the same time. 2022 is the precedent: revenue −12.3% and operating margin down 4 points.
02
Fee compression on the growth products. The net revenue yield slipped from 22.4 bps in Q2 to about 22.0 in Q3 (our calculation). If QQQ's fee has to fall to meet cheaper Nasdaq-100 trackers, the $130M-per-half-year conversion benefit shrinks. Management's defence is "total cost of ownership," which holds only as long as clients agree.
03
Active equity outflows and the debt-funded preferred. Fundamental Equities (−$1.9B in Q3, −$7.7B in Q2) is the highest-fee book, and it is still shrinking. Meanwhile about $1.0B of MassMutual preferred still ranks ahead of common holders, and $1.5B of floating-rate bank debt now stands behind the repurchase of the rest.
Industry Commentary
Passive vs. active: the fee-rate squeeze
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Every traditional asset manager faces the same squeeze: money keeps moving from active funds to index funds, and index funds charge a fraction of the fee. Invesco's Q3 2026 is a clean example. The firm took in $35.1B of long-term money, its 13th straight quarter of inflows, yet its fee rate slipped, because $35.2B went to ETFs, index strategies and QQQ while active equity lost $1.9B and fixed income lost $2.6B. The $1.79B impairment on US retail mutual-fund contracts is what the squeeze looks like on a balance sheet. Invesco's answer has been to be on the winning side of the shift rather than to fight it, and on the evidence of 2026 that has worked. Revenue can grow even as the fee rate falls, as long as AUM grows faster.

Market levels do most of the work
CYCLICAL EXPOSURE

In August 2026, market returns added $49B to Invesco's AUM, nearly twice the month's $27.6B of net long-term inflows; in September they took $16B off. That is the normal state of an asset manager: in any given year, market movement tends to matter more than flows, and costs do not adjust quickly when markets fall. The 2022 record makes the point. Revenue fell 12.3% and operating margin fell from 25.0% to 21.0% in one year. The 2026 margin gains are real, and because costs are largely fixed they work in both directions: margins widen when markets rise and narrow when they fall.

Scale and structure as the new moat
EMERGING TAILWIND

The QQQ conversion shows how an old product structure can hold back earnings, and how changing it can release them. A fund Invesco had sponsored for years became a management-fee earner overnight and added $130M of net revenue in six months. The bull framing: Invesco owns one of the most liquid ETFs in the world, holders cannot leave without a tax bill, and scale lets it grow revenue on flat costs, which is why the margin reached 40.7% in Q3. The bear framing: the conversion was a single step-up, and it is already in the numbers. From here, earnings growth has to come from AUM and flows, in a business where the price of the product falls every year. The measure to watch is the net revenue yield against average AUM growth: if the yield falls faster than AUM grows, the margin gains stop.
