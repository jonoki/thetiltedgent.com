01 Company Overview

J.B. Hunt started in 1961 in Arkansas, founded by Johnnie Bryan and Johnelle Hunt, and went public in 1983. What it became is not really a trucking company. Its biggest business is intermodal — J.B. Hunt owns the containers and the trucks at each end, and rents the long middle of the trip from the railroads, a partnership model that began with the Santa Fe railway in 1989. Around that sit four more segments: dedicated fleets run for single customers, a freight brokerage, home delivery of big and bulky goods, and a trailer-pool truckload business. TTM revenue is $13.33B, and TTM diluted EPS is $7.10.

The story of the last year is a stock that ran on a thesis and then got hit by the same thesis from the other side. From a September 2025 month-end of $134.17, JBHT more than doubled to an all-time closing high of $298.41 on July 16, 2026, the day after a Q2 in which EPS rose 45% to $1.91. The engine was fuel and drivers: higher fuel prices and a tight truck-driver market pushed shippers off the highway and onto the rail (the Q2 release cites both), and intermodal loads rose 10%. Then on the evening of September 15, 2026 J.B. Hunt told a Morgan Stanley investor conference that Q3 EPS would come in 5–10% below Q2 — because the same high diesel prices, plus about $25M of extra driver-related cost such as recruiting and bonuses, were hitting J.B. Hunt's own trucks. The stock fell 13.3% the next day, September 16, its worst one-day fall since March 2020. Then came the Q3 report. On October 15, 2026, after the close, J.B. Hunt reported revenue of $3.68B (+20%), operating income of $247.1M (+2%) and EPS of $1.82 against $1.76 a year earlier: a cent above the $1.81 consensus of 22 analysts reported by Talk Business & Politics, and 4.7% below Q2's $1.91, a touch better than the 5–10% drop the company had flagged. Volume and revenue grew; the CFO put cost headwinds versus Q2 at more than $50M, and operating income grew only 2%. The stock fell 2.8% on the next session (October 16, $235.31 to $228.78) and another 2.4% on October 19, then closed October 21 at $228.59. The question now: is fuel a tailwind that happens to have a cost, or a cost that happens to have a tailwind?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Owned intermodal container fleet Long-standing rail partnerships Dedicated contracts (~96% retention) Density in the Eastern network Five-segment cross-selling Pre-funded capacity (low capex need)

The moat is capacity bought in the trough. An intermodal operator needs containers, chassis, drayage trucks and a rail contract before it can win a single conversion from the highway, and J.B. Hunt carried roughly 20% spare container capacity into this upcycle (per the Q2 call). When fuel and driver scarcity push freight toward rail, it can absorb the volume without buying equipment at peak prices — which is why intermodal operating income grew 58% on 22% more revenue in Q2 (18% on 20% in Q3). The vulnerability is that J.B. Hunt is not only a rail customer; through DCS, Truckload and drayage it is also one of the country's larger employers of truck drivers and buyers of diesel. The same shock that sends it freight also raises its costs.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Fuel cuts both ways. Fuel surcharges reset with a lag. When diesel spikes, costs arrive before surcharge revenue catches up — close to 100bps of DCS margin in Q2, and in September the company flagged at least $10M of extra fuel cost in Q3 versus Q2. A fall in diesel would reverse the timing drag but also remove part of the reason shippers are choosing rail.
02
Drivers are the binding constraint. The September warning flagged roughly $25M of extra driver-related cost (recruiting, bonuses and the like) in Q3 versus Q2; on the Q3 call the CFO put total cost headwinds versus Q2 at more than $50M, including more than $16M of group medical cost. A driver shortage raises truckload rates (good for intermodal conversion) but J.B. Hunt still needs drivers for drayage at both ends of every intermodal move and for every dedicated fleet.
03
It rents the railroad. The long haul runs on track J.B. Hunt does not own. On the Q2 call Field said rail service "has moderated slightly as volumes accelerated" while adding he is "not at all concerned." Service quality and the rail contracts' economics are outside the company's direct control.
04
Pricing has not shown up yet. Intermodal revenue per load excluding fuel was up 1% in Q2 and 2% in Q3; repricing phases in from Q4. The bull case needs the 2027 bid season to deliver real price. If the fuel spike fades before contracts reset, the volume gains may arrive at today's rates.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

J.B. Hunt built its container fleet in the downturn and is now filling it: intermodal loads up 8% in Q3, net capex down 41% over nine months ($291.1M against $490.9M) and $1.10B of trailing free cash flow. Freight is converting from truck to rail in the East, and the price lever has barely been pulled: management says 10% of intermodal volume reprices in Q4 and 30% in each of the first three quarters of 2027. The Q3 results landed at the low bar the September warning set: EPS of $1.82 beat the $1.81 consensus and revenue grew 20%. Consensus FY2027 EPS of $9.87 would put the stock at 23.2x, and none of the five broker actions listed after the print was a downgrade.

Bear Case

You are paying 32.2x trailing earnings for a trucking-and-rail business at what may be the top of a fuel-driven cycle. Revenue grew 20% in Q3 but operating income only 2%: the CFO put cost headwinds versus Q2 at more than $50M, and on the Q2 call the CEO said some driver markets are as tight as the company has ever seen. The profit growth depends on intermodal pricing that arrives gradually through 2027. If diesel falls, the conversion tailwind fades before the contracts reset. If it stays high, the cost squeeze continues. Dedicated operating income fell 22%, brokerage and truckload together earned $16.6M on $695M of quarterly revenue, and TTM EPS of $7.10 is still well below the $9.21 of 2022.

Top 3 Risks

01
Driver and fuel cost inflation outrunning surcharges and contracts. The CFO put Q3 cost headwinds versus Q2 at more than $50M, and operating income grew 2% on 20% more revenue. If driver cost proves structural rather than one-off onboarding, 2027 margin assumptions are too high.
02
Intermodal pricing fails to reset in 2027. Volume without price leaves the multiple exposed. The bull case at 31x FY2026 earnings relies on the FY2027 EPS jump to $9.87.
03
Freight-cycle reversal. Beta of 1.31, a +35% 52-week move and three consecutive down revenue years before this one: the history says this stock moves with the freight cycle, and cycles turn.
Industry Commentary
Record diesel and a tight driver market
COST SHOCK

The US average retail diesel price passed $6 a gallon for the first time in the EIA's weekly survey in September and reached $6.53 in the week of September 21. For a road carrier that is pure cost, recovered through fuel surcharges only with a lag. For J.B. Hunt it is both: its rail-based intermodal network becomes more competitive against trucks, while its truck-based segments pay more. On the day after the warning, peers Old Dominion, ArcBest and Knight-Swift fell 3.6% to 4.0% (close to close), against J.B. Hunt's 13.3%.

Road-to-rail conversion
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Management frames the Eastern US as the largest conversion opportunity. Shorter hauls in the East have historically favoured trucks, but higher fuel and fewer drivers change that trade-off. Eastern loads grew 16% in Q2 and 31% over two years. With roughly 20% spare container capacity going into the cycle, J.B. Hunt can take that volume without a new round of heavy capex.

DEBATE
