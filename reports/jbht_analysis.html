01 Company Overview

J.B. Hunt started in 1961 in Arkansas, founded by Johnnie Bryan and Johnelle Hunt, and went public in 1983. What it became is not really a trucking company. Its biggest business is intermodal — J.B. Hunt owns the containers and the trucks at each end, and rents the long middle of the trip from the railroads, a partnership model that began with the Santa Fe railway in 1989. Around that sit four more segments: dedicated fleets run for single customers, a freight brokerage, home delivery of big and bulky goods, and a trailer-pool truckload business. TTM revenue is $12.70B, up 5.3%, and TTM diluted EPS is $7.05, up 27.9%.

The story of the last year is a stock that ran on a thesis and then got hit by the same thesis from the other side. From a September 2025 month-end of $134.17, JBHT more than doubled to an all-time closing high of $298.41 on July 16, 2026, the day after a Q2 in which EPS rose 45% to $1.91. The engine was diesel: record fuel prices and a tight truck-driver market pushed shippers off the highway and onto the rail, and intermodal loads hit a quarterly record. Then on September 16, 2026 CFO Brad Delco told a Morgan Stanley conference that Q3 EPS would come in 5–10% below Q2 — because the same record diesel, plus about $25M of extra driver recruiting and onboarding cost, was hitting J.B. Hunt's own trucks. The stock fell 13.3% in one session, one of the worst days in its four decades as a public company. The question now: is fuel a tailwind that happens to have a cost, or a cost that happens to have a tailwind?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Owned intermodal container fleet Long-standing rail partnerships Dedicated contracts (~96% retention) Density in the Eastern network Five-segment cross-selling Pre-funded capacity (low capex need)

The moat is capacity bought in the trough. An intermodal operator needs containers, chassis, drayage trucks and a rail contract before it can win a single conversion from the highway, and J.B. Hunt carried roughly 20% spare container capacity into this upcycle (per the Q2 call). When fuel and driver scarcity push freight toward rail, it can absorb the volume without buying equipment at peak prices — which is why intermodal operating income grew 58% on 22% revenue. The vulnerability is that J.B. Hunt is not only a rail customer; through DCS, Truckload and drayage it is also one of the country's larger employers of truck drivers and buyers of diesel. The same shock that sends it freight also raises its costs.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Fuel cuts both ways. Fuel surcharges reset with a lag. When diesel spikes, costs arrive before surcharge revenue catches up — about 100bps of DCS margin in Q2 and at least a $10M headwind guided for Q3. A fall in diesel would reverse the timing drag but also remove part of the reason shippers are choosing rail.
02
Drivers are the binding constraint. Roughly $25M of extra recruiting, advertising, training and onboarding cost in Q3 versus Q2. A driver shortage raises truckload rates (good for intermodal conversion) but J.B. Hunt still needs drivers for drayage at both ends of every intermodal move and for every dedicated fleet.
03
It rents the railroad. The long haul runs on track J.B. Hunt does not own. Field said rail service "has moderated slightly as volumes accelerated" while adding he is "not at all concerned." Service quality and the rail contracts' economics are outside the company's direct control.
04
Pricing has not shown up yet. Intermodal revenue per load ex-fuel +1%. The bull case needs the 2027 bid season to deliver real price. If the fuel spike fades before contracts reset, the volume gains may arrive at today's rates.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

J.B. Hunt built its container fleet in the downturn and is now filling it: record intermodal loads, 58% intermodal profit growth, capex cut by nearly two-thirds and $1.10B of free cash flow. Freight is converting from truck to rail in the East, bids are "structurally larger," and the price lever has barely been pulled — revenue per load ex-fuel is up only 1%, with the 2027 bid season ahead. The Q3 warning takes EPS back to about last year's level for one quarter; it does not change the volume trend. Consensus FY2027 EPS of $10.04 would put the stock at 23.5x, and none of the five broker actions listed after the warning was a downgrade.

Bear Case

You are paying 33.5x trailing earnings for a trucking-and-rail business at what may be the top of a fuel-driven cycle. The same diesel spike that drove the rally is now raising J.B. Hunt's own costs, and drivers are as scarce as management has ever seen. The profit growth depends on intermodal pricing that has not arrived yet. If diesel falls, the conversion tailwind fades before the 2027 contracts reset. If it stays high, the cost squeeze continues. Brokerage and truckload together barely break even on $628M of quarterly revenue, and TTM EPS of $7.05 is still well below the $9.21 of 2022.

Top 3 Risks

01
Driver and fuel cost inflation outrunning surcharges and contracts. About $35M+ of combined Q3 headwind was flagged in one quarter. If driver cost proves structural rather than one-off onboarding, 2027 margin assumptions are too high.
02
Intermodal pricing fails to reset in 2027. Volume without price leaves the multiple exposed. The bull case at 31x FY2026 earnings relies on the FY2027 EPS jump to $10.04.
03
Freight-cycle reversal. Beta of 1.29, a +78% 52-week move and three consecutive down revenue years before this one: the history says this stock moves with the freight cycle, and cycles turn.
Industry Commentary
Record diesel and a tight driver market
COST SHOCK

Press coverage of the warning put the national average diesel price at a record $6.31 a gallon, per AAA. For a road carrier that is pure cost, recovered through fuel surcharges only with a lag. For J.B. Hunt it is both: its rail-based intermodal network becomes more competitive against trucks, while its truck-based segments pay more. Peers Old Dominion, ArcBest and Knight-Swift fell in sympathy with the warning — the market treated it as an industry cost signal, not a J.B. Hunt problem.

Road-to-rail conversion
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Management frames the Eastern US as the largest conversion opportunity. Shorter hauls in the East have historically favoured trucks, but higher fuel and fewer drivers change that trade-off. Eastern loads grew 16% in Q2 and 31% over two years. With roughly 20% spare container capacity going into the cycle, J.B. Hunt can take that volume without a new round of heavy capex.

Valuation — bull and bear framing
DEBATE

Bull framing: earnings are still recovering from a three-year freight recession, so the trailing multiple overstates the price — on consensus FY2027 EPS it is 23.5x, with a 5% FCF yield and falling capex. Bear framing: consensus already assumes a 31.8% EPS jump in 2027, and the stock trades at 33.5x a TTM figure that includes a fuel-boosted peak quarter. The Q3 print on October 15 and the first 2027 bid results will show which is right.
