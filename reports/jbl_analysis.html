01 Company Overview

Jabil builds other companies' hardware. It is an EMS contractor — founded in Detroit in 1966 by James Golden and William E. Morean (the name joins their first names), headquartered in St. Petersburg, Florida since 1982 — that designs, sources, assembles and ships products whose logos belong to someone else: server racks and networking gear for cloud operators, medical devices and drug-delivery systems for healthcare companies, automotive electronics, and consumer products. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through May 31, 2026 was $33.59B, up 17.8%, on a gross margin of just 9.23%. That margin is the whole business model in one number: Jabil keeps about nine cents of every revenue dollar before overhead, and about five cents after it.

What changed is who is buying. Management now expects AI-related revenue of about $13.6B in fiscal 2026 (year ending August 2026), up roughly 50% from about $9B in fiscal 2025 and $500M above its own March outlook — roughly 39% of the ~$35B full-year revenue guide ($13.6B ÷ $35B). The market paid for that story in full: the stock went from a $58.37 month-end close in September 2021 to a $385.48 close in June 2026, printing an intraday high of $428.93 on June 17, 2026, the day of the Q3 beat and raise. Then it gave back 30% in three months without the guidance being cut. The central question for a low-margin assembler that suddenly trades like a growth company: is AI data-center demand a durable mix shift that lifts Jabil's margins above 6% as management targets for fiscal 2027, or a hyperscaler capex cycle that Jabil rides up and rides back down — with the multiple doing more damage than the earnings on the way?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Global factory footprint at scale Regulated-product qualification lock-in Hyperscaler rack-integration know-how Supply-chain purchasing power Photonics & power capabilities (Intel, Hanley) Diversified end markets

A contract manufacturer does not own a brand, a patent portfolio that matters to the end user, or pricing power in the usual sense. Its moat is operational trust: the customer hands over its product, its launch schedule and its supply chain, and switching mid-program is expensive and risky. In regulated work — a drug-delivery device, an automotive safety module — that switching cost is written into the regulatory file. In AI infrastructure it is written into the calendar: a hyperscaler bringing a new rack generation online cannot afford a vendor who slips. The vulnerability is that trust protects the relationship, not the margin. Every contract is re-bid, customers are enormous and concentrated, and the 9.23% gross margin says plainly who holds the pricing power.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Hyperscaler capex is the growth rate. Roughly 39% of fiscal 2026 revenue is AI-related (computed: $13.6B ÷ $35B guide), and UBS's own bull case names the customers — Amazon, Meta and likely Google. When a few buyers set the budget, a single capex pause at one of them shows up in Jabil's revenue within quarters, not years.
02
Thin margins magnify small misses. On a 5.06% operating margin, a one-point swing in gross margin is roughly a fifth of operating profit. Pricing concessions on a big program, a ramp inefficiency or a component shortage do not dent earnings — they dominate them.
03
The balance sheet is lean by design. Buybacks have shrunk book equity to $1.33B, so debt-to-equity reads 2.97 and the current ratio 0.98. Debt/EBITDA of 1.57x and interest coverage of 10.4x say the leverage is manageable, but there is less cushion than the headline revenue suggests if a downturn traps cash in inventory.
04
The multiple, not the business, is where most of the risk sits. The stock has fallen 30% from its June high with no cut to guidance. A company priced at 37x trailing GAAP earnings has to keep beating; the June–September slide shows what "only meeting" costs.

Third, the gap between GAAP and core earnings is wide — Q3 FY26 GAAP operating income was $445M against $504M core, and GAAP EPS $2.59 against $3.16 core — so check which basis any multiple is quoted on. RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $58.37 to the September 18, 2026 settled close of $299.57, JBL gained 413% on price — the stock is worth 5.1 times what it was. Dividends ($0.32 a year) barely register in that. The path had three stages: a flat 2021–22 between roughly $51 and $72; a 2023 re-rating as the market started to see Jabil as more than a phone-parts assembler, ending with its return to the S&P 500 in December 2023; and then, from mid-2025, an AI-infrastructure run that took the month-end close from $168.01 in May 2025 to $385.48 in June 2026.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how stretched that last leg was. It hit 94.3 at the June 2026 close — the highest reading in the five-year series — after three straight months above 90. The July–September pullback has brought it back to 53.0, dead neutral. The 10-month moving average is $300.32, within a dollar of the price: the long-term uptrend has not broken, but the stock is sitting on the line that decides whether it does.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 18x consensus fiscal-2027 earnings for a company whose AI revenue is growing ~50% a year, whose margin is rising in the direction management has promised (above 6% core in FY2027), and which retires roughly 4–5% of its shares a year. Consensus has revenue growing 22% to $42.75B in fiscal 2027 and EPS 32% to $16.84. The 30% drawdown came with no guidance cut, the Street's lowest target is $365, and Regulated Industries — over a third of revenue — gives a floor that a pure AI assembler does not have. If September 30 confirms the FY2027 trajectory, the stock is priced for a slowdown that is not in the numbers.

Bear Case

The bear case does not dispute this year's numbers; it disputes how long they last. Jabil keeps 2.57 cents of every revenue dollar, a large and growing share of that revenue comes from a few hyperscalers, and the stock is priced at 37x trailing GAAP earnings. Contract manufacturers have been here before: revenue follows the customer's capex cycle, margins do not expand much at the top, and multiples collapse at the turn. The July–September slide — worst month in five years, sector-wide sell-offs, Goldman cutting its target 22% — is what the market looks like when it starts to price the cycle rather than the trend.

Top 3 Risks

01
Hyperscaler capex slowdown. About 39% of FY2026 revenue is AI-related and the named incremental customers are the largest cloud operators. A pause at one of them would hit the fastest-growing, highest-margin segment first.
02
Multiple compression. The stock fell 30% from its high while estimates held. At 37x trailing GAAP earnings, the price depends on the market continuing to treat an EMS company as a growth company; the September 14 sector sell-off shows how quickly that can change.
03
Thin-margin execution. A 5.06% operating margin leaves little room for a mispriced program, a slow ramp or a component shortage, and a current ratio of 0.98 with $2.58B of net debt means working-capital swings show up quickly in cash flow.
Industry Commentary
AI infrastructure turns EMS into a growth sector
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

For decades contract manufacturing was a low-multiple, low-margin business valued on its cycle. The build-out of AI data centers changed the customer list: rack-scale integration, liquid cooling, power and optical interconnects are complex enough that hyperscalers pay for execution, and a small group of EMS firms — Jabil, Celestica, Flex, Sanmina — is doing that work. Jabil's AI revenue (~$9B in FY2025 → ~$13.6B in FY2026) and UBS's ~$20.3B FY2027 estimate describe that shift. The bull framing: this is a multi-year mix shift that raises margins and deserves a higher multiple. The bear framing: it is the same cyclical business with a new customer, and the multiple is borrowed from the customer's enthusiasm.

The group trades as one
ONGOING HEADWIND

On September 14, 2026 Jabil fell 6.1%, Benchmark Electronics 7.1%, Flex 6.4% and Sanmina 9.24%, while the S&P 500 fell 0.5%. When a whole peer group moves twelve to eighteen times the index on the same day, the stocks are being traded as one AI-hardware position. That cuts both ways: Jabil's diversification (Regulated Industries, consumer and automation) gets little credit on the way up, and little protection on the way down.
