01 Company Overview

Jabil builds other companies' hardware. It is an EMS contractor. James Golden and William Morean started it in 1966 at the Morean family's kitchen table outside Detroit; the name joins their first names. It has been headquartered in St. Petersburg, Florida since 1982. Jabil designs, sources, assembles and ships products whose logos belong to someone else: server racks, power and networking gear for cloud operators, medical devices and drug-delivery systems, automotive electronics, and consumer and automation products. Fiscal 2026 (the year to August 31, 2026) brought revenue of $35.95B, up 20.6%, on a gross margin of just 9.23%. That margin is the whole business model in one number: Jabil keeps about nine cents of every revenue dollar before overhead, and under five cents after it (GAAP operating margin 4.74%).

What changed is who is buying. AI-related revenue reached about $14.4B in fiscal 2026, up $5.4B. That is roughly 40% of revenue ($14.4B ÷ $35.95B). Management expects about $22.1B in fiscal 2027, up 54%, or about half of its $44.5B revenue guide. The market paid for that story early. The stock went from a $58.37 month-end close in September 2021 to a $385.48 close in June 2026, and hit an intraday high of $428.93 on June 17, 2026, the day of the Q3 beat and raise. By the eve of the Q4 print it had slid to $318.84, 26% below that high. On September 30 Jabil beat and guided above consensus, and the stock fell another 10.0% that day. The central question for a low-margin assembler that now trades like a growth company: does AI work lift margins for years, or is this a hyperscaler capex cycle that Jabil rides up and rides back down? Management guides a 6.1% core margin for fiscal 2027.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Global factory footprint at scale Regulated-product qualification lock-in Hyperscaler rack-integration know-how Supply-chain purchasing power Photonics & power capabilities (Intel, Hanley) Diversified end markets

A contract manufacturer does not own a brand, a patent portfolio that matters to the end user, or pricing power in the usual sense. Its moat is operational trust: the customer hands over its product, its launch schedule and its supply chain, and switching mid-program is expensive and risky. In regulated work — a drug-delivery device, an automotive safety module — that switching cost is written into the regulatory file. In AI infrastructure it is written into the calendar: a hyperscaler bringing a new rack generation online cannot afford a vendor who slips. The vulnerability is that trust protects the relationship, not the margin. Every contract is re-bid, customers are enormous and concentrated, and the 9.23% gross margin says plainly who holds the pricing power.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Hyperscaler capex is the growth rate. About 40% of fiscal 2026 revenue was AI-related ($14.4B ÷ $35.95B). The fiscal 2027 guide takes that to about half ($22.1B ÷ $44.5B). Management expects six Intelligent Infrastructure customers to bring in more than $1B each. When a few buyers set the budget, a capex pause at one of them shows up in Jabil's revenue within quarters, not years.
02
Thin margins magnify small misses. On a 4.74% GAAP operating margin, a one-point swing in gross margin is roughly a fifth of operating profit. Pricing concessions on a big program, a slow ramp or a component shortage do not dent earnings — they dominate them. Management has already said fiscal 2027 margins come late in the year, after the new capacity fills.
03
The balance sheet is lean by design, and the build-out is getting bigger. Buybacks keep book equity small ($1.62B), so debt-to-equity reads 2.49 (debt including leases) and the current ratio 0.99. Debt/EBITDA is 1.59x. Inventory rose to $7.41B at August 31 from $4.68B a year earlier, and the CFO said inventory days are still above the 55–60 day target. Net capex is guided at 1.5–2% of revenue for fiscal 2027, about $0.67–0.89B on the $44.5B guide (our calculation), against $470M in fiscal 2026.
04
The multiple, not the business, is where most of the risk sits. On September 30 the stock fell 10% on a quarter that beat and a guide that topped consensus. Flex, Sanmina and Celestica fell between 1.4% and 3.5% that day. At 30.7x trailing GAAP earnings and 17.1x management's fiscal 2027 core EPS guide, the price needs the guide to be beaten, not just met.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the long-run series. First, the mobility divestiture. Jabil sold its China mobility business to BYD Electronic, closing December 29, 2023, and booked a $944M pre-tax gain on it in the quarter to February 2024 (10-Q). Fiscal 2024 revenue fell 16.8% to $28.88B, yet GAAP EPS jumped to $11.17 on $1,388M of net income, lifted by that gain. Fiscal 2024 EPS is not a clean base, and fiscal 2025's $5.92 is not a collapse. Second, buybacks: diluted shares fell 3.6% in fiscal 2026, so per-share figures grow faster than the business. Third, the gap between GAAP and core earnings is wide. Q4 GAAP operating income was $602M against $675M core, and GAAP EPS $3.76 against $4.40 core, so check which basis any multiple is quoted on. The Hanley Energy acquisition (closed January 2, 2026; about $725M plus up to $58M contingent) adds acquired revenue to fiscal 2026 growth.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $58.37 to the October 1, 2026 settled close of $299.79, JBL gained 414% on price, so the stock is worth 5.1 times what it was. Dividends ($0.32 a year) barely register in that. The path had three stages. First a flat 2021–22, with month-end closes between $51.21 and $72.19. Then a 2023 re-rating as the market started to see Jabil as more than a phone-parts assembler, ending with its return to the S&P 500 in December 2023. Then, from mid-2025, an AI-infrastructure run that took the month-end close from $168.01 in May 2025 to $385.48 in June 2026.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how stretched that last leg was. It hit 94.3 at the June 2026 close, the highest reading in the five-year series, after three straight months above 90. The pullback since has brought it to 48.7 at the September month-end and 53.3 at the October 1 point: neutral. The 10-month moving average is $306.23 at the last point, and the price sits 2.1% below it. The stock dipped under that line in September for the first time since the AI run began, and has not yet won it back.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 17x management's own fiscal 2027 core EPS guide. That guide calls for 24% revenue growth to $44.5B and 34% core EPS growth to $17.55, and it sits above what the Street expected before the print. AI revenue is guided up 54% to about $22.1B. Margin keeps rising in the direction management promised: 5.8% core in fiscal 2026, 6.1% guided for 2027. Jabil shrank its diluted share count 3.6% last year. Fiscal 2026 beat the June outlook on revenue, core EPS and free cash flow, and matched it on margin. The September 30 sell-off came with no cut to anything, the Street's lowest target is $365, and Regulated Industries (close to a third of revenue, guided +7%) gives a floor that a pure AI assembler does not have. On this reading the stock is priced for a slowdown the company is not guiding to.

Bear Case

The bear case does not dispute this year's numbers; it disputes how long they last and what they are worth. Jabil keeps 2.90 cents of every revenue dollar, and about half of next year's revenue is guided to be AI-related, concentrated in a handful of hyperscalers. The fiscal 2027 guide adds 24% more revenue but only 0.3 points of margin and about 4% more adjusted free cash flow, while capex and inventory rise. Contract manufacturers have been here before: revenue follows the customer's capex cycle, margins do not expand much at the top, and multiples collapse at the turn. Three straight down months, including the worst month in five years and a 10% fall on a beat-and-raise, look like a market starting to price the cycle rather than the trend.

Top 3 Risks

01
Hyperscaler capex slowdown. About 40% of fiscal 2026 revenue was AI-related, and the fiscal 2027 guide takes it to about half. A pause at one of the six customers expected to pass $1B each would hit Intelligent Infrastructure, the largest and fastest-growing segment, first.
02
Multiple compression. The stock is 30% below its high while guidance went up. At 30.7x trailing GAAP earnings, the price depends on the market continuing to treat an EMS company as a growth company. September 14 (a sector sell-off) and September 30 (a beat that still fell 10%) show how quickly that can change.
03
Ramp execution. The fiscal 2027 margin gain is back-loaded behind about 4 million square feet of new capacity. A 4.74% GAAP operating margin leaves little room for a slow ramp, a mispriced program or a component shortage. A current ratio of 0.99 with $7.41B of inventory means working-capital swings show up quickly in cash flow.
Industry Commentary
AI infrastructure turns EMS into a growth sector
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

For decades contract manufacturing was a low-multiple, low-margin business valued on its cycle. The build-out of AI data centers changed the customer list: rack-scale integration, liquid cooling, power and optical interconnects are complex enough that hyperscalers pay for execution, and a small group of EMS firms — Jabil, Celestica, Flex, Sanmina — is doing that work. Jabil's AI revenue (about $9.0B in FY2025 → $14.4B in FY2026 → about $22.1B guided for FY2027) describes that shift. So does Hanley Energy, bought in January 2026, which designs, installs and commissions power systems from the grid to the data-center rack. The bull framing: this is a multi-year mix shift that raises margins and deserves a higher multiple. The bear framing: it is the same cyclical business with a new customer, and the multiple is borrowed from the customer's enthusiasm.

The group trades as one — until a print
ONGOING HEADWIND

On September 14, 2026 Jabil fell 6.1%, Benchmark Electronics 7.1%, Flex 6.4%, Celestica 8.8% and Sanmina 9.2%, while the S&P 500 fell 0.48%. When a whole peer group moves roughly thirteen to nineteen times the index on the same day, the stocks are being traded as one AI-hardware position. That cuts both ways: Jabil's diversification (Regulated Industries, consumer and automation) gets little credit on the way up, and little protection on the way down. September 30 was the exception. Jabil fell 10.03% on its own print while Flex, Sanmina and Celestica fell 1.4–3.5%.
