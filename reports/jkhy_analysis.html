01 Company Overview

Jack Henry started in 1976 in southwest Missouri, when Jack Henry and Jerry Hall rented space in an engine repair shop for $40 a month and began writing software for small banks. Fifty years later it is based in Monett, Missouri, and still sells to the same kind of customer: roughly 7,200 banks and credit unions, most of them community institutions too small to build their own technology. It runs their core (SilverLake and CIF 20/20 for banks, Symitar for credit unions), moves their payments, and sells the digital-banking, lending and fraud tools that bolt on top. Fiscal 2026 (year to June 30) revenue was $2.544B, up 7.1%, with GAAP diluted EPS of $6.98, up 11.9%. In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 (to September 30), revenue rose 8.8% to $701.5M and GAAP diluted EPS rose 15.6% to $2.28.

This is a business built on the most boring promise in finance: the ledger will post tonight, the same as every night. Banks switch core providers about as often as people move house, and when they do it is a multi-year project. That inertia is Jack Henry's whole edge, and the numbers show it working — management reported a record 58 competitive core wins in fiscal 2026, 14 of them institutions above $1 billion in assets, and nine more in the September quarter against four a year earlier. On November 3 the company beat expectations and raised its full-year guide, and the next session the stock jumped 10.8%. It closed November 6, 2026 at $173.23, 14.2% above where it stood at the end of November 2021 ($151.63), and 5.1% below its December 2025 close of $182.48. The question this report keeps asking has changed shape: is the jump the start of the faster growth management has promised for fiscal 2028 and 2029, or a good quarter flattered by one-off exit fees, with the slow-then-faster plan from its September 15 investor day unchanged?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Core-conversion switching cost Cross-sell into 7,200+ institutions Recurring processing revenue Community-bank focus Low debt (D/E 0.06) 22 straight years of dividend increases

The moat is the conversion. Moving a bank off one core and onto another means migrating every account, every loan and every integration, retraining every teller, and betting the institution's reputation on one weekend cutover. Clients do it rarely, which means a core provider's revenue behaves more like an annuity than like software sales. Jack Henry's differentiation inside that is who it serves: community banks and credit unions, where its service reputation has let it take share from larger rivals — the CEO said on the August 2026 call, per the call highlights, that the company is taking share from all of its competitors rather than from one, and on the November 4 call that it has "never been the lowest cost provider." The vulnerability sits at the other end of the same annuity: the client base shrinks every time a small bank is bought by a bigger one, and a good year of wins can be offset by a wave of mergers.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Growth is steady, not accelerating — and management said so. Going into the September 15, 2026 investor day, management had already said fiscal 2027 revenue growth should be similar to the 7% of fiscal 2026, with a step-up only in fiscal 2028 and 2029 as signed deals are implemented (per Quartr's summary of management's remarks at the Goldman Sachs Communacopia + Technology conference in September 2026). The stock fell 6.37% on the investor day. November's raise was modest on the company's own non-GAAP measure: revenue growth of 6.6–7.4%, from 6.3–7.3%, with the adjusted operating-margin guide left at 24.1–24.3%. At about 24x trailing earnings the stock now needs that later growth to arrive.
02
Margins stall in fiscal 2027. First-quarter GAAP operating margin was 29.5% (28.5% a year earlier), but the full-year GAAP guide, raised to 24.7–24.9% from 24.5–24.7%, is still below fiscal 2026's 25.0%, which implies lower margins in the remaining quarters. The non-GAAP margin is guided up only 0.2 to 0.4 of a percentage point. On the November 4 call the CFO said cyber and infrastructure spending, including the move from company-owned data centres to shared facilities and public cloud, will pressure fiscal 2027 margins.
03
Trust is the product, and it was tested. On August 31, 2026 the company disclosed a cybersecurity incident: a voice-phishing ("vishing") attack attributed to ShinyHunters reached "a limited portion of our internal, non-production corporate environment," and personal data for "fewer than 10 clients" was impacted. The company said the incident "is not financially material" and that no client-facing or core systems were accessed. For a vendor whose pitch is reliability, reputational cost can exceed financial cost — and it landed in the middle of the core-win sales cycle. There is no sign of it in the sales numbers so far: the first quarter produced nine competitive core wins, against four a year earlier.
04
Client consolidation. Every acquisition of a Jack Henry client by a bank on another platform is a lost relationship. Deconversion fees soften the blow in the year they are paid and then disappear, as the drop from $42.8M to a guided ~$30M shows. On the November 4 call the CFO called consolidation "largely neutral to slightly positive for our business."

Notable One-Off Items

Two items distort the reported figures. First, deconversion revenue: $42.8M in fiscal 2026, mostly high-margin, $17.9M in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 (against $8.6M a year earlier), and now guided to about $30M for the year, up from about $23M — the main reason the company reports a non-GAAP revenue line ($682.2M in the quarter) alongside GAAP ($701.5M), and a swing in reported growth that has nothing to do with client demand. Second, buybacks: $448M of repurchases in fiscal 2026 and $71M in the September quarter cut the average diluted count to 70.3M from 72.9M a year earlier, so EPS growth in the quarter (+15.6%) ran ahead of net income growth (+11.4%). The raised FY27 GAAP EPS guide of $7.47–$7.51 is growth of 7.1–7.6% by the company's count, well below the 11.9% of fiscal 2026.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 close of $151.63 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $173.23, JKHY gained 14.2% on price over five years — while SPY, the S&P 500 fund, gained 61.4% over the same dates, and QQQ, the Nasdaq-100 fund, 66.6%. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 23.8x trailing and 23.8x forward earnings (the forward figure uses a non-GAAP consensus) for a company with 22 consecutive years of dividend increases, very little debt (D/E 0.06), ROIC above 23%, a record 58 core wins in fiscal 2026, nine more in the first quarter against four a year earlier, and a sticky client base whose switching cost is a multi-year conversion. The first quarter beat expectations and the full-year guide went up, with cloud revenue up 11%; the company sold 367 Gladiator security solutions, triple the year-earlier volume. Deconversion fees will still fall from fiscal 2026, so fiscal 2027 GAAP growth looks slower than the business is, and cloud conversions and signed-but-not-yet-live deals are what management says will lift growth in FY28 and FY29. The average price target of $200 is 15.5% above the price, and management bought $71M of stock in the quarter at an average of $153.47. Trailing EPS of $7.28 is already above fiscal 2026's $6.98, and if the core wins convert on schedule it has further to go.

Bear Case

The company told you what it is: non-GAAP revenue growth of 6.6–7.4% this year, acceleration deferred to FY28–FY29, and a non-GAAP margin guide that moves up only 0.2 to 0.4 of a percentage point. Part of the quarter's GAAP beat was one-off deconversion fees of $17.9M, more than double the $8.6M of a year earlier, and Core segment non-GAAP revenue grew only 5%. At about 24x trailing earnings that is not cheap for an EPS guide of 7–8% growth, and the stock has already risen 10.83% on the results. With 6.38% of the freely traded shares sold short, a real minority of investors is betting against it, the August cybersecurity incident hands competitors a talking point in exactly the sales cycles that produce core wins, and bank mergers keep shrinking the number of institutions it can sell to. A 14.2% price gain in five years against 61.4% for SPY, while EPS went from $4.94 in fiscal 2022 to $7.28 over the last four quarters, shows investors paying less for each dollar of profit, and nothing in the guide says that has reversed for good.

Industry Commentary
The core-processing annuity — and its leak
STRUCTURAL

Core processors sell the ledger a bank cannot run without, on long contracts, with conversion costs that make leaving a board-level decision. That makes the revenue durable. The leak is consolidation: when a client bank is acquired, its accounts usually move to the acquirer's platform, and the processor collects a one-time deconversion fee on the way out. Jack Henry's $42.8M of fiscal 2026 deconversion revenue, guided to about $30M in fiscal 2027, is the visible trace of that process. Growth for a processor in this market is therefore wins minus losses — which is why the company leads with competitive core wins, and why 58 of them in a year is the number that matters most.

AI: a cost now, a velocity gain later
ONGOING HEADWIND

On the November 4 call the CEO said Jack Henry has 30 AI-enabled products in market, up from 22 in August, and is launching AI consulting services for clients; per the August call highlights, it has hired nine "AI coaches" to train over 2,000 associates. On the same call the CEO tied Gladiator security sales, triple the year-earlier volume in the quarter (367 solutions, per MarketBeat's call highlights), to institutions prioritising protection as the use of frontier AI models spreads. The near-term effect is spend: management says cyber and infrastructure work will pressure fiscal 2027 margins. For a vendor to community banks, AI is less a threat to the product than a feature race — the risk is falling behind larger rivals with bigger research budgets, not being replaced.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
OPEN QUESTION

JKHY trades at about 16.7x EV/EBITDA and 23.8x trailing earnings (our calculations at $173.23), with a 1.41% dividend yield and a free-cash-flow yield of 4.6–5.9% depending on the definition. The bull framing: that is a fair price for a business with almost no debt and a 23% return on invested capital, whose five-year price gain has badly lagged its earnings; the average analyst target is above the price, and the company keeps buying its own stock. The bear framing: the price was set when the market expected acceleration, and the company has said acceleration is gradual and years away. A steady 7% grower with near-flat near-term margins may simply be worth what it trades at. What settles it is not the P/E; it is whether core wins turn into faster non-GAAP revenue growth in FY28, as management says it expects.
