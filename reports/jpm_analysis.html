Company Overview

JPMorgan Chase is the largest bank in the United States by assets (~$5.0T as of Q2 2026) and the largest Western bank by market capitalization, operating a genuinely universal model: retail and card banking for ~94 million US customers, the world's #1 investment bank and #1 markets franchise by revenue share, and a $5.1T asset and wealth management arm. Full-year 2025 managed revenue was approximately $186B (reported net revenue $182.5B, roughly +3% YoY against a 2024 base inflated by a one-time $7.9B Visa share exchange gain) with net income of $57.0B and $20.02 in diluted EPS. Momentum has since accelerated sharply: on a TTM basis the firm has now earned $186.3B of revenue (+13.8%) and $63.6B of net income (+15.1%), capped by a record $21.2B quarterly profit in Q2 2026 — the largest quarterly profit ever posted by a US bank.

Has run JPMorgan since 2005–06 and is the longest-tenured CEO of any major US bank; personally holds roughly 6.3 million shares. In June 2026 the board named Doug Petno and Troy Rohrbaugh co-presidents — the clearest succession signal yet — after Marianne Lake, long the presumed heir, announced her retirement.

Institutions own roughly 75.8% of shares outstanding, led by Vanguard Group (largest holder, high single-digit %), BlackRock and State Street. Ownership is overwhelmingly passive-index driven; there is no activist or control block.

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat
Scale & Cost of Funds Deposit Franchise / Switching Costs Brand Power (Chase & J.P. Morgan) Regulatory Barriers Network Effects in Payments Fortress Balance Sheet Technology & Data Spend ($19.8B/yr)

The core advantage is a self-reinforcing loop between scale and cost of funds. A $2.6T+ deposit base — 11.1% of all US retail deposits, built from 5,000+ branches and 94 million customers — funds the balance sheet more cheaply and more stably than any competitor can match, which in turn lets JPMorgan underwrite larger loans, warehouse more trading risk and win more lead-left mandates than rivals with the same capital. That scale then pays for a $19.8B annual technology budget, roughly the entire revenue of a mid-cap bank, spent largely on products, platforms and AI. Regulation, ironically, deepens the moat: G-SIB (Global Systemically Important Bank) capital surcharges, resolution planning and stress-test infrastructure impose fixed costs that only a handful of firms can absorb, which is why the top five US banks keep taking share. Layer on genuine network effects in Payments and securities services — where the value of the rails rises with the number of counterparties on them — and the result is the widest moat in Western banking. The evidence is in the share data: deposits 8.1%→11.1%, card sales 20.9%→23.6%, IB fees 7.9%→8.4% and markets revenue 9.3%→11.8%, all gained over the decade to 2025.

Key Vulnerabilities
Cyclicality dressed up as compounding

Roughly 42% of revenue now comes from the Commercial & Investment Bank, and Q2 2026's record was driven by equities trading up 86% and IB fees up 30% — levels that are exceptional, not structural. Consensus 2026 EPS of $24.07 implies +22% growth off a base that already embeds a trading boom and a $4.6B Visa gain. If capital-markets volumes normalize, the earnings base resets lower and the current 15.1x trailing multiple looks a good deal less cheap.

Consumer credit and the expense line moving the wrong way together

Card net charge-offs are running at 3.34% with guidance to ~3.4%, well above the post-pandemic trough, while management raised 2026 adjusted expense guidance to ~$107.5B from $106B at Q2 — the third consecutive quarter of expense growth outpacing some revenue lines (Q1 2026 expenses +14% YoY). That combination is exactly what triggered the ~15% drawdown into March 2026 and the negative Q2 earnings-day reaction despite a 9.8% EPS beat.

Regulatory and political whiplash

The June 2026 capital re-proposal is a large tailwind — but it is a proposal, and the previous Basel III Endgame framework was written, attacked, and rescinded across a single political cycle. A future administration or Fed vice chair for supervision can re-tighten G-SIB surcharges and stress-capital buffers as quickly as they were loosened. JPMorgan carries the highest surcharge in the system, so it has the most to lose from a reversal.

Key-man risk and the succession overhang

Jamie Dimon has been CEO for twenty years and a meaningful part of the valuation premium to peers — JPM trades near 3.1x tangible book versus roughly 1.5–2.0x for most large-cap peers — is attributable to him. The June 2026 elevation of Doug Petno and Troy Rohrbaugh to co-presidents, and Marianne Lake's departure, formalize a race but do not resolve it. A co-president structure also creates its own retention risk; the firm has already become the first stop for outside CEO recruiters.

Disintermediation from private credit and tokenized money

Private credit funds now compete directly for the leveraged-lending and middle-market assets that used to sit on bank balance sheets, at lower regulatory cost. Meanwhile stablecoins threaten the deposit base at the margin — JPMorgan's answer is the permissioned JPMD deposit token and a multi-bank shared token consortium, but that is a defensive move to protect deposit economics, not an obvious new profit pool.

Notable One-Off Items
Q2 2026 gains materially distort the headline — adjust before you model

Reported Q2 2026 net income of $21.2B ($7.70 EPS) includes a $4.6B gain on Visa shares (+$1.27 EPS) and roughly $1.0B of other equity investment gains (+$0.29 EPS). On an adjusted basis net income was $16.9B ($6.14 EPS) and ROTCE 23% rather than 29%. The prior-year comparison is similarly distorted: FY2024 included a $7.9B Visa share-exchange gain, which is why FY2025 net income of $57.0B looks flat-to-down against FY2024 despite better underlying performance. Q4 2025 also carried a significant item that took net income from $14.7B adjusted to $13.0B reported, alongside a $2.1B reserve build. On the expense side, 2026 adjusted expense guidance has been walked up twice, from $105B at the January outlook to $106B and then $107.5B at Q2 — volume-related, but a genuine change in the cost base rather than a one-off. No material litigation charge, write-down or acquisition accounting item is currently distorting results.

Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The most dominant franchise in Western banking is still gaining share in every business it competes in, earns 23% ROTCE against a 17% through-the-cycle target, and is about to have its regulatory capital requirement cut — the June 2026 proposal would reduce G-SIB CET1 requirements by roughly 4.8%, freeing capital directly into buybacks or a $20B acquisition. At 14.6x forward earnings for mid-to-high-teens ROE and 22% consensus EPS growth, an investor is paying roughly half the market multiple for above-market returns.

Bear Case

Peak everything. Q2's record leaned on equities trading up 86%, IB fees up 30% and a $4.6B Visa gain; strip the cycle out and growth is far more ordinary. Expenses have been guided up twice this year to $107.5B, card charge-offs are running at 3.34%, and the stock trades near 3.1x tangible book — a premium to peers that assumes both the cycle and Jamie Dimon persist. Twelve of 23 analysts are at hold and the average target implies only ~5% upside.

Top 3 Risks
  1. Capital-markets normalization. The Commercial & Investment Bank is 42% of revenue and produced Q2 growth of 27%. Trading and underwriting revenue is the most mean-reverting line in finance; a return to a normal fee pool would knock high-single-digit percentages off firmwide revenue with almost no offsetting cost relief, and the multiple would compress on top of it.
  2. Consumer credit deterioration alongside rising costs. Card net charge-offs of 3.34% heading to ~3.4% are manageable, but the combination of a slowing consumer, a Fed that markets now expect to hike rather than cut, and adjusted expenses guided to $107.5B creates negative operating leverage precisely when provisions would be rising. Q4 2025 already required a $2.1B reserve build.
  3. Regulatory reversal and succession risk. The capital relief now in the price is a proposal, not a rule, and the last framework was written and rescinded inside one political cycle. Layered on top, Dimon's exit is now a formal process rather than a hypothetical — and a meaningful part of JPM's premium to tangible book is a bet on management that a co-president handover does not obviously preserve.

The rate path has flipped from cuts to possible hikes

Critical constraint

The Fed held at 3.50%–3.75% in July 2026 and, under Chair Kevin Warsh, has taken an explicitly hawkish line — three officials dissented in favour of an immediate 25bp increase, and markets now price high odds of one to two hikes by year-end on the back of energy-driven inflation. The 30-year Treasury yield is at its highest since 2007. For banks this cuts both ways with unusual force: higher-for-longer supports net interest income (JPM raised 2026 NII guidance to ~$105.5B, with NII ex-Markets at ~$96.5B), but it pressures loan demand, raises deposit costs as competition for funding intensifies, marks down securities portfolios, and increases the odds of consumer and commercial-real-estate credit deterioration. The 2026 consensus that banks would coast on falling funding costs is now dead.

The most bank-friendly capital regime in fifteen years

Emerging tailwind

The 2023 Basel III Endgame proposal — which would have raised large-bank capital by double digits — has been rescinded and replaced. The June 2026 re-proposal introduces the Expanded Risk-Based Approach, removes the punitive dual-calculation requirement, and recalibrates the G-SIB surcharge with a one-time 1.2x reduction in Method 2 coefficients and narrower 20bp bands to soften cliff effects. The Fed's own estimate is an average surcharge reduction of 40bps and roughly $23B of aggregate surcharge relief, with CET1 requirements down ~4.8% for Category I–II G-SIBs and ~5.2% for regionals. JPMorgan carries the system's highest surcharge, so it captures the largest absolute benefit — meaningful against a CET1 ratio already at 14.1% and buybacks running at $6–8B a quarter. The caveat is that this is a proposal in comment, and it is reversible.

The AI and technology arms race — and what it means for the bull/bear debate

Ongoing headwind

Every large bank is now running an AI capex cycle simultaneously. JPMorgan spends about $19.8B a year on technology, largely on products, platforms and AI, and Dimon says the technology has already removed 30–40% of roles in some operations units — yet total headcount is flat near 318,500 and adjusted expenses have been guided up twice this year, to $107.5B. The bull framing: a firm with $186B of revenue can absorb an AI build-out that mid-size competitors cannot fund at all, so the efficiency gain shows up as widening share and a structurally lower overhead ratio from 2027 — JPM already ran a 47% overhead ratio in Q2 2026 versus ~51% for FY2025. The bear framing: if every competitor spends the same and the savings are competed away in pricing, this is a permanent increase in the industry's fixed cost base with no durable return, and Dimon's own warning that "sharp cost reductions are an illusion" is the tell. Which framing is right will be visible in the 2027 expense guide, not before.
