01 Company Overview

KeyCorp is the holding company for KeyBank, a Cleveland-based regional bank with roughly $193B of assets, about 950 branches and about 1,100 ATMs in 15 states. Its roots run to the Commercial Bank of Albany (1825) and Cleveland's Society for Savings (1849); the modern company was assembled by the March 1, 1994 merger of Society Corporation and KeyCorp — the same day it entered the S&P 500 — and widened in 2016 with the $4.1B purchase of First Niagara. Key also runs an investment bank alongside the branches: KeyBanc Capital Markets, which in August 2026 added a UK advisory arm by closing the acquisition of Clearwater Corporate Finance.

The story of the last five years is one hand played badly and then played back. Key went into the 2022 rate shock holding a large book of low-yielding fixed-rate securities. When rates rose and the March 2023 regional-bank panic hit, the stock went from a $25.07 month-end in February 2022 to $9.24 in June 2023 — a 63% drawdown. The repair was expensive and public: in August 2024 Scotiabank agreed to buy up to 14.9% of the company for about $2.8B (the first tranche at $17.17 a share), and Key used the capital to sell underwater bonds and reinvest at higher yields — booking a net loss of $(447)M in Q3 2024 and $(279)M in Q4 2024, the latter including a $915M securities loss. That is why FY2024 shows a loss of $(0.32) a share and why growth rates measured against 2024 look outsized: StockAnalysis shows trailing-twelve-month EPS up 109%. The payoff is now visible: Q3 2026 EPS of $0.51, up 24% year-over-year, record revenue of $2.05B, NIM up 20 basis points to 2.95%, and a ROTCE of 15.11%, above the 15% management had promised for the end of 2027. The debate has moved: can Key hold 15%+ once credit costs and deal fees normalise (the quarter carried a provision for credit losses, the money set aside for expected bad loans, of just $61M, against $107M a year earlier), and with capital thinner than it looks, after falling bond values cut tangible book value per share 9% in the quarter?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Sticky core deposits (about 950 branches) Middle-market relationship lending In-house investment bank (KeyBanc) Treasury & payments switching costs $74.5B wealth AUM Strategic anchor holder (Scotiabank)

A regional bank's moat is not a product; it is the cost and stickiness of its funding and the depth of its client relationships. Key's edge relative to a plain branch bank is that it can serve a mid-sized company from its operating account to its M&A exit — the treasury-management plumbing makes the deposits sticky, and the investment bank captures fees that pure lenders never see. The honest caveat is that this is a franchise, not a fortress: 2023 showed that when depositors panic and rates move against the bond book, a regional bank's moat can drain in a quarter. The Scotiabank investment is both a moat and a signal — it recapitalised the bank at the bottom, and it gives a strategic owner a seat at a table where consolidation is being discussed.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Margin is being traded for growth. C&I loans grew 13.9% YoY and NIM rose 6bps to 2.95% in Q3, but the Q4 guide of about 3.0% is the bottom of the 3.00–3.05% exit rate given in July. Management says most of the climb to 3.25%+ by late 2027 comes from old low-yield securities and swaps maturing and being reinvested higher; thin spreads on new corporate loans pull the other way. Holding 15%+ ROTCE depends on that path, and the share price is paying for it.
02
Deposits have one good quarter. Average deposits rose $3.6B in Q3 against $1.5B of loan growth, but over a year they are up 0.5% against loans up 5.0%. Lending growth funded by a flat deposit base eventually means paying up for funding (compressing NIM) or slowing the loan book.
03
The capital is thinner than the headline. CET1 of 11.1% becomes 9.0% once unrealised securities losses are counted, down from 9.8% a quarter earlier, and tangible book value per share fell to $12.35 from $13.62. Key is running a $3.0B buyback authorisation and says it is on pace for $1.3B of 2026 repurchases ($320M in Q3), so any credit or rate surprise lands on a buffer that is being actively returned.
04
Fee income is cyclical. Noninterest income rose 5.4% QoQ in Q3 2026 to $744M, led by investment-banking and debt-placement fees of $214M. That line, the piece that sets Key apart, is the most market-sensitive one on the income statement, and the Clearwater acquisition adds more of it: a strong deal quarter can reverse as quickly as it came.

Notable One-Off Items

The big one is history rather than this year: the 2024 securities repositioning. Key sold low-yielding bonds at a loss in two steps — Q3 2024 closed with a net loss of $(447)M and Q4 2024 with $(279)M, the latter including a $915M loss on securities sales ($(657)M, or $(0.66) per share, after tax). FY2024 EPS was −$0.32 as a result, and every trailing comparison that reaches into 2024 is distorted. Second, share count: the Scotiabank investment was new-issue equity, while the buyback now runs the other way — about $341M repurchased in Q2 2026 and $320M in Q3, a new $3.0B authorisation approved May 13, 2026 (replacing a $1.0B programme), and $1.3B of 2026 repurchases guided; shares outstanding fell 1.3% in Q3 to 1.058B. Third, Clearwater closed August 4, 2026. Fourth, AOCI: unrealised losses widened to $(3.87)B at September 30 from $(2.35)B at June 30, which is why book value per share fell to $15.01 from $16.19 in a quarter of record revenue.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The expensive repair is done and paying off. Key took its securities losses in 2024, has a strategic shareholder holding about 14.9% of the shares and cleared by the Fed in June to go to 19.99%, and in Q3 2026 showed what the rebuilt balance sheet earns: EPS +24% YoY, record revenue, NIM 2.95% and ROTCE of 15.11%, the end-2027 target reached more than a year early. Deposits grew faster than loans in the quarter, and guidance went up for loans and down for charge-offs. If 15%+ holds, 1.70x tangible book and 11.4x 2026 earnings are not demanding for a bank also retiring stock under a $3.0B authorisation and paying a 3.90% yield. Every published analyst target sits above today's price.

Bear Case

One quarter is not a run-rate. Q3's 15% return came with a $61M provision for credit losses (the Commercial Bank's was $8M, against $68M a year earlier) and a strong quarter for deal fees, and both can turn. The Q4 NIM guide of about 3.0% is the bottom of the range given in July. Marked CET1 fell to 9.0% and tangible book value per share dropped 9% in a quarter as bond values fell, while $1.3B goes back out in buybacks this year. Key has been here before — the 2023 drawdown was 63% — and a beta of 1.09 does not describe the tail. Eight of twenty analysts are at Hold.

Industry Commentary
The regional-bank repair cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND

Regional banks went into 2022 loaded with bonds bought at near-zero yields. The 2023 panic exposed the mark-to-market losses; the years since have been about grinding them out — letting bonds mature, or selling them and eating the loss up front, as Key did in 2024. Each maturing low-yield asset reprices higher, which is why Key's net interest income is guided up about 10% in 2026 while deposits are up only 0.5% over the year. The gap between reported and marked CET1, 210bp at September 30 (11.1% vs 9.0%) against 140bp in June, is the part of that repair still outstanding.

Consolidation and the Scotiabank option
WATCH

Key sits in the middle of the regional pack by size — well below PNC and Truist, below Fifth Third — and has a foreign strategic holder that the Federal Reserve cleared in June 2026 to own up to 19.99%. None of that is a deal, and no transaction has been announced. It does mean the stock carries a strategic dimension, in both directions: support from an anchor owner, and a shareholder list where one holder can shape outcomes.

Rates cut both ways
KEY SENSITIVITY

Key raised its prime rate to 7.00% from 6.75% effective September 17, 2026. Higher short rates lift yields on floating-rate loans; they also raise deposit costs and push down the value of the remaining fixed-rate securities, which is exactly what widened the marked-CET1 gap in 2022–23. The September 16 sell-off, which hit the big banks as well, shows the market is still trading the whole group on the rate path rather than on individual results. The third quarter showed the balance-sheet side of the same move: unrealised losses in AOCI widened by about $1.5B, and tangible book value per share fell 9%.
