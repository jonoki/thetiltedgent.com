01 Company Overview

KeyCorp is the holding company for KeyBank, a Cleveland-based regional bank with roughly $191B of assets, 939 branches and about 1,100 ATMs across 15 states. Its roots run to the Commercial Bank of Albany (1825) and Cleveland's Society for Savings (1849); the modern company was assembled by the March 1, 1994 merger of Society Corporation and KeyCorp — the same day it entered the S&P 500 — and widened in 2016 with the $4.1B purchase of First Niagara. Key also runs an investment bank alongside the branches: KeyBanc Capital Markets, which in August 2026 added a UK advisory arm by closing the acquisition of Clearwater Corporate Finance.

The story of the last five years is one hand played badly and then played back. Key went into the 2022 rate shock holding a large book of low-yielding fixed-rate securities. When rates rose and the March 2023 regional-bank panic hit, the stock went from a $25.07 month-end in February 2022 to $9.24 in June 2023 — a 63% drawdown. The repair was expensive and public: in August 2024 Scotiabank agreed to buy up to 14.9% of the company for about $2.8B (the first tranche at $17.17 a share), and Key used the capital to sell underwater bonds and reinvest at higher yields — booking a net loss of $(447)M in Q3 2024 and $(279)M in Q4 2024, the latter including a $915M securities loss. That is why FY2024 shows a loss and why every trailing growth rate on the screen looks absurd (TTM EPS "+5,258%"). The payoff is now visible: Q2 2026 EPS of $0.44, up 26% year-over-year, NIM up 23 basis points to 2.89%, and management guiding to a 3.00–3.05% exit rate. The debate now is simple: does the rebuilt margin reach the 15%+ ROTCE management has promised for 2027, or does a bank that is trading margin for loan growth stall around 13%?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Sticky core deposits (939 branches) Middle-market relationship lending In-house investment bank (KeyBanc) Treasury & payments switching costs $74.2B wealth AUM Strategic anchor holder (Scotiabank)

A regional bank's moat is not a product; it is the cost and stickiness of its funding and the depth of its client relationships. Key's edge relative to a plain branch bank is that it can serve a mid-sized company from its operating account to its M&A exit — the treasury-management plumbing makes the deposits sticky, and the investment bank captures fees that pure lenders never see. The honest caveat is that this is a franchise, not a fortress: 2023 showed that when depositors panic and rates move against the bond book, a regional bank's moat can drain in a quarter. The Scotiabank investment is both a moat and a signal — it recapitalised the bank at the bottom, and it gives a strategic owner a seat at a table where consolidation is being discussed.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Margin is being traded for growth. C&I loans grew 11.7% YoY, but NIM rose just 2bps in Q2 and the CFO admitted it rose less than expected. If the 3.00–3.05% exit rate slips, the ROTCE path to 15%+ by end-2027 slips with it — and that path is what the multiple is paying for.
02
Deposits aren't keeping up. Average deposits +0.1% YoY against average loans +4.1%. Lending growth funded by a flat deposit base eventually means paying up for funding (compressing NIM) or slowing the loan book.
03
The capital is thinner than the headline. CET1 of 11.2% becomes 9.8% once unrealised securities losses are counted. Key is simultaneously running a $3.0B buyback authorisation and guiding to at least $1.3B of 2026 repurchases, so any credit or rate surprise lands on a buffer that is being actively returned.
04
Fee income is cyclical. Noninterest income fell 2.4% QoQ in Q2 2026 to $706M. Investment banking — the piece that differentiates Key — is the most market-sensitive line on the income statement, and the Clearwater acquisition adds more of it.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Five years, almost no net movement: from the September 2021 month-end of $21.62 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $20.87, KEY is down 3.5% on price (dividends excluded; the current rate is $0.82 a year). The flat endpoints hide the round trip. The stock peaked at a $25.07 month-end in February 2022, fell 63% to $9.24 in June 2023, and has since climbed 126% off that low — with the $23.05 June 2026 close the best month-end since early 2022.

The 6-period monthly RSI bottomed at 19.1 in June 2023, the most oversold reading in the series, and ran as high as 80.7 in November 2024. It now reads 49.4 — neutral — after three straight down months from June's high. The price sits just below the 10-month moving average of about $21.45: the uptrend that began in 2023 has stalled rather than broken.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The expensive repair is done and paid for. Key took its securities losses in 2024, has a strategic shareholder anchoring 14.9% of the register, and is now showing what the rebuilt balance sheet earns: EPS +26% YoY, NIM +23bps, ROTCE 12.89% and rising, guidance raised across revenue, NII and loans. If NIM reaches the 3.00–3.05% exit rate and ROTCE clears 15% by end-2027 as promised, 1.53x tangible book and 11.4x 2026 earnings are not demanding for a bank also retiring stock under a $3.0B authorisation and paying a 3.93% yield. Every published analyst target sits above today's price.

Bear Case

The recovery is in the price and the next leg depends on the hardest things to deliver. NIM rose 2bps in a quarter the CFO called slower than expected, loans are growing on flat deposits, and the loan growth that is coming is lower-spread investment-grade C&I. Marked CET1 of 9.8% is a thinner cushion than the 11.2% headline while billions go back out in buybacks. Key has been here before — the 2023 drawdown was 63% — and a regional bank's beta of 1.02 does not describe its tail. Nine of nineteen analysts are at Hold.

Top 3 Risks

01
NIM falls short of the 3.00–3.05% exit rate. The ROTCE target, the earnings estimates and the buyback capacity all rest on it. Management has already said it will trade margin for relationship loans.
02
Funding. Average deposits grew 0.1% YoY while loans grew 4.1%. If the operating deposits management expects do not arrive, Key pays more for funding or slows the commercial book it is betting on.
03
Credit and capital. Net charge-offs are guided at 40–45bps with nonperforming loans at 0.73% — benign today. A credit turn would hit a balance sheet whose marked CET1 is 9.8% while capital is being returned at pace.
Industry Commentary
The regional-bank repair cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND

Regional banks went into 2022 loaded with bonds bought at near-zero yields. The 2023 panic exposed the mark-to-market losses; the years since have been about grinding them out — letting bonds mature, or selling them and eating the loss up front, as Key did in 2024. Each maturing low-yield asset reprices higher, which is why Key's net interest income is guided up 9–11% in 2026 while deposits are flat. The 140bp gap between reported and marked CET1 is the part of that repair still outstanding.

Consolidation and the Scotiabank option
WATCH

Gorman told analysts that "higher rates may actually be a better climate to get deals done." Key sits in the middle of the regional pack by size — well below PNC and Truist, below Fifth Third — and has a foreign strategic holder asking permission to own up to 19.99%. None of that is a deal, and no transaction has been announced. It does mean the stock carries a strategic dimension, in both directions: support from an anchor owner, and a register where one holder can shape outcomes.

Rates cut both ways
KEY SENSITIVITY

Key raised its prime rate to 7.00% from 6.75% effective September 17, 2026. Higher short rates lift yields on floating-rate loans; they also raise deposit costs and push down the value of the remaining fixed-rate securities, which is exactly what widened the marked-CET1 gap in 2022–23. The September 16 sell-off, which hit the big banks as well, shows the market is still trading the whole group on the rate path rather than on individual results.
