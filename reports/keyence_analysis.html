01 Company Overview

Keyence makes the eyes and the measuring tape of the modern factory: photoelectric, fiber-optic, laser and proximity sensors that tell a machine where a part is; machine-vision cameras that inspect it; measuring instruments, microscopes and barcode readers; PLCs; and, most recently, 3D printers built for production floors. Its annual report puts the customer base at around 400,000 customers in some 110 countries, with no single customer above 10% of sales. In the fiscal year ended March 20, 2026, net sales rose 10.4% to ¥1,169.3B, operating income rose 8.4% to ¥595.8B — an operating margin of 51.0% — and net income rose 11.7% to ¥445.2B, or EPS of ¥1,835.63. Then the first quarter of the new year (March 21 – June 20, 2026) accelerated hard: sales +32.8% to ¥346.6B, operating income +44.7% to ¥187.1B, a 54.0% operating margin, with net income up 51.0% to ¥139.1B (EPS ¥573.69). Keyence gives no sales or profit forecast; its only guidance is the dividend, ¥550 a share for the year to March 2027 (¥275 interim, ¥275 year-end), left unchanged in the July 28 release.

Half of every yen of sales ends up as operating profit, at a company that does not own most of its production — its own risk disclosures describe products made at "cooperating factories" under a fabless system — and that sells through its own direct sales force. That margin is more than double each of the three listed automation peers compared in section 04. The surprise is the five-year record: from the September 2021 month-end close of ¥67,000 to the October 2, 2026 close of ¥80,270, Keyence gained just 19.8% while the Nikkei 225 gained 131.9%. All of that gain, and more, came in 2026: the stock was down 15.4% from September 2021 to December 2025, then rose 41.6% to October 2, with its two biggest daily gains of the year on the sessions after the April and July results. The question this report turns on: is the first-quarter acceleration the start of a new factory-investment upcycle, or a peak flattered by a weak yen? The company says currency added roughly ¥26.1B to sales and ¥18.9B to operating income in that quarter alone.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

51% operating margin Fabless production Direct sales force ~400,000 customers, ~110 countries No customer >10% of sales Steady new-product cadence ¥3.0T cash & securities, no debt

The moat shows up in one number before any other: an 83.7% gross margin over the last twelve months on products that are boxes of optics and electronics. Customers are paying for what the sensor does on their line — fewer defects, faster changeovers, less downtime — not for its parts. Three habits keep it that way. Production is mostly outsourced to "cooperating factories" under Keyence's own quality control, so capital goes into design and selling rather than plants (capital spending was ¥28.4B last year against ¥430.7B of operating cash flow). It sells through its own direct sales force, which the annual report names as a growth priority, keeping the company close to the engineers who specify its products. And the product list keeps turning over: the 2026 annual report highlights a new PLC, an all-in-one fluorescence microscope, 3D printers for production sites, linear cylinder sensors and smart valves. With some 400,000 customers and none above 10% of sales, no single buyer has the leverage to push prices down. The weakness is the mirror image: a business this concentrated on factory capital spending rises and falls with it, and a share price that already assumes excellence has little room for an ordinary year.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The price already assumes the best. At ¥80,270 the stock trades at 39.6× trailing earnings and 5.5× book value, against a Nikkei 225 at 22.7× Nikkei's own earnings estimates and 2.8× book. The five-year chart shows what a high starting multiple can cost: the stock fell 15.4% from September 2021 to December 2025 while the Nikkei 225 rose 70.9%.
02
The yen flatters the latest numbers. About ¥18.9B of Q1's ¥57.8B operating-income increase came from currency, by the company's estimate. Keyence's own sensitivity table says each ¥1 move in the dollar shifts annual operating income by about ¥1.1B, each ¥1 in the euro by about ¥0.5B, and each ¥0.1 in the yuan by about ¥1.0B. A stronger yen would turn this tailwind around.
03
It is a factory-capex business. Every sale depends on manufacturers deciding to automate, inspect or expand. When that slows, so does Keyence: Japan grew just 0.3% in the quarter to December 20, 2025 and Europe 0.2% in the quarter to September 20, 2025. Asia's 33.9% local-currency surge in the latest quarter is the kind of number that cycles reverse.
04
A balance sheet that is almost all cash. Cash, deposits and securities totalled about ¥3.05T at June 20, 2026 — roughly 16% of the market value — against total equity of ¥3.55T. Keeping that much capital idle drags return on equity down to 13.5% despite 50%-plus operating margins. The 57% dividend increase (¥350 → ¥550 a share) is a step; buybacks have been negligible (treasury purchases of ¥15M last year).

Notable One-Off Items

Two items below the operating line move the profit figures. First, currency gains: the year to March 2026 booked a foreign-exchange gain of ¥16.3B against a ¥4.2B loss the year before, which is why pre-tax income grew 13.3% while operating income grew 8.4%; the June 2026 quarter added a ¥2.2B gain against a ¥2.1B loss a year earlier. Second, interest and dividend income on the cash pile rose to ¥16.4B for the year (from ¥9.1B). Neither is part of the core business, and both can reverse. At the operating level there were no unusual items: Keyence's income statements show no restructuring or impairment lines in either period.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Keyence just posted 32.8% sales growth with every region accelerating at once: Japan +19.9%, the Americas +18.4%, Asia +33.9% and Europe +12.8% in local currency. Even after stripping out the weak yen, operating income grew about 30%. The margin structure — 85% gross, 54% operating — is unmatched among listed automation peers, the balance sheet holds about ¥3T of cash and securities with no debt, and the dividend has gone from ¥350 to ¥550. President Tetsuya Nakano's June 2026 letter to shareholders expects "growing demand driven by ongoing technological innovation as well as increasing needs for automation, quality improvement, and energy efficiency." Thirteen of fifteen analysts rate it a buy, with an average target of ¥97,867 (+21.9%). If the new president's overseas push keeps the growth rate in the high teens, today's 39.6× P/E compresses quickly.

Bear Case

The stock spent 2021–2025 proving that quality does not protect a stock bought at a peak multiple: +19.8% in five years against a Nikkei up 131.9%. Today it trades at 39.6× trailing and 35× forward earnings, with a 0.7% dividend yield and a 2.1% free-cash-flow yield, after two exceptional quarters. Part of the growth is currency — ¥18.9B of Q1 operating profit by the company's own estimate — and the strongest region, Asia, is the most cyclical. Nakano's own letter flags the backdrop: "the global economy requires continued close monitoring of policy trends in various countries, in addition to concerns over a potential economic downturn driven by rising geopolitical risks." A strong yen, a turn in electronics capital spending or simply a return to 10% growth would leave the multiple with nothing to stand on. Meanwhile about ¥3T of capital sits idle and buybacks are negligible, holding ROE at 13.5%.

Top 3 Risks

01
Valuation de-rating. At 39.6× trailing earnings and 5.5× book, the stock prices in sustained high growth. This chart shows the cost of a high starting multiple: −15.4% from September 2021 to December 2025.
02
A stronger yen. The dollar averaged ¥159 in Q1 against ¥146 a year earlier. Reverse that and, on the company's sensitivity of about ¥1.1B of annual operating income per yen, the dollar alone would take roughly ¥14B off operating income — before the euro and yuan.
03
The factory-capex cycle. Keyence has no recurring software or service base to cushion a downturn in factory investment. The quarters to September 20 and December 20, 2025 showed how quickly Japan and Europe can slow to near zero; Asia's latest 33.9% is the most exposed to a reversal.
Industry Commentary
Every region accelerating at once
EMERGING TAILWIND

Keyence's local-currency growth table is a clean read on global factory spending because the company sells to so many manufacturers in so many industries. Over the five quarters to June 2026, overseas growth went 11.4% → 10.4% → 13.3% → 16.4% → 24.0%, and Japan went 5.2% → 3.8% → 0.3% → 8.6% → 19.9%. The annual report describes "continued capital investment, particularly in the manufacturing sector," and points to demand from "automation, quality improvement, and energy efficiency." When a supplier to some 400,000 manufacturers sees every region speed up together, it is a signal about the cycle, not only about Keyence. The company's own account of the June quarter, in the Japanese text of its July 28, 2026 earnings release, says the same thing region by region: manufacturing-led capital spending continued; the Americas stayed firm across a wide range of industries; Asia kept growing, led by the semiconductor and electronics industries; Europe showed signs of recovery; and capital spending in Japan was on a recovering trend.

The weak yen — tailwind with an expiry date
ONGOING HEADWIND RISK

With 70% of sales overseas, Keyence gains when the yen weakens, and its own sensitivity table puts numbers on it. In Q1 the yen averaged 159 to the dollar (146 a year earlier), 185 to the euro (162) and 23.2 to the yuan (20.1). The company puts the effect at roughly ¥26.1B of sales and ¥18.9B of operating income for the quarter. That is real cash in yen, but it is not demand. Over the full year to March 2026 currency barely mattered (12.9% local-currency overseas growth against 13.5% reported); in Q1 it was 15 points of the overseas growth rate.

Quality at a price — how Keyence compares
VALUATION

Against three listed automation peers, Keyence's operating margin (52.1%) is more than double Fanuc's (21.8%), SMC's (24.1%) and Cognex's (21.9%), and its gross margin (83.7%) leads all three. Its trailing P/E of 39.6 sits above Fanuc (31.6) and SMC (23.2) and below Cognex (62.5); on Nikkei's forward basis the index trades at 22.7×. The bull framing: a business that converts half of sales into operating profit with no debt deserves a large premium, and growth this fast shrinks it within a year or two. The bear framing: the stock returned less than a fifth of the Nikkei 225's gain over five years while running the same business model — the margins were never the problem; the price paid was.
