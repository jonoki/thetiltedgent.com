01 Company Overview

Keysight builds the instruments engineers use to prove that electronics work before anyone ships them: oscilloscopes, signal generators and analysers, bit-error-rate testers, network and channel emulators, protocol test systems, and the design software that sits upstream of all of it. It does not sell into end-unit demand — it sells into other people's R&D and validation budgets. Fiscal 2026 (ended 31 October 2026) revenue was $7.157B, up 33.2% from $5.375B in fiscal 2025, and GAAP earnings per share rose 78% to $8.75. On the call the CEO said "Fiscal 2026 was the strongest year in Keysight's history." The stock market values the company at $49.21B.

The central debate on this page is about the shape of the current boom, not its existence. Keysight is a bookings business: it reports orders separately from revenue, and orders come before the revenue they turn into. Fiscal Q4 2026 orders were $2.339B against $1.994B of revenue — a book-to-bill of about 1.17 (our arithmetic from the release), with orders up 53% year over year; full-year orders were $8.126B, up 49%. What is inside them: AI and data-centre interconnect test — 800G and 1.6T validation, high-speed digital, pre-silicon design through data-centre-scale emulation. The CEO said "our bookings in the AI business has been roughly 2.5 times what it was the previous year", and the CFO said commercial growth was "led by triple-digit growth in wireline and robust double-digit growth in wireless." Management's base case for fiscal 2027 is 20% revenue growth. Sections 02, 04 and 07 take apart whether that is a durable platform shift or an unusually sharp, temporary surge in equipment spending, now priced at 33× trailing earnings.

This is the growth engine: $1,145M in Q4, up 73%. The demand is AI interconnect validation: as datacentre networks move from 400G to 800G and 1.6T (the data rates of the optical and electrical links that connect AI chips, switches and racks), the physics gets harder faster than the volume grows. At those rates signal quality, timing noise and signal loss become the binding constraint, which means more instruments per design, more expensive instruments, and test at more stages — pre-silicon design simulation, transceiver and co-packaged optics characterisation, then live data-centre-scale network emulation. On the Q4 call the CEO said "the industry is in early innings of 1.6T ramp, with 3.2T already in active R&D," and put the bigger turn for near- and co-packaged optics in 2028. The CFO said "demand continues to stay ahead of our ability to ship," with the shortfall concentrated in a dozen to two dozen products aimed at AI. Wireless grew too: the CFO called it "robust double-digit growth," and Kailash Narayanan, president of the group, said "Our 6G business doubled year-over-year." Margin profile: CSG gross margin 71% (66% a year earlier), operating margin 37% (27%). The other long-standing rivals are Rohde & Schwarz (private, German) and Anritsu (listed in Tokyo).

Mix Direction

The long-run bull argument for Keysight's valuation is that it is moving from selling boxes to selling design software, test automation, and multi-year subscriptions — recurring revenue at software margins, attached to installed hardware. Spirent adds directly here with automated test-and-assurance and network automation software. On the Q4 call the CFO said "Software and services grew double digits, representing 33% of Keysight revenue" — the same share as in Q3, so hardware is still growing at least as fast. Q4 GAAP gross margin was 66.3%, up from 61.2% a year earlier (our arithmetic from the release); on the company's non-GAAP basis the CFO put it at 69%. Part of fiscal 2026's margin was a one-time 1.1-point benefit from refunds of tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which the CFO said the outlook excludes. So the margin gain is still coming mostly from volume and price on instruments, not from a shift toward software — and those two behave very differently when orders roll over: software revenue persists, instrument volume does not.

Disclosure gap: Keysight's Q4 FY2026 results release reports no software revenue, ARR (annual recurring revenue) or subscription renewal rates. On the call the CFO gave only shares: annual recurring revenue was 25% of the total mix (24% in Q3), with no dollar figure.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
MEASUREMENT SCIENCE IP STANDARDS-BODY POSITION DESIGNED-IN AT SPEC STAGE CALIBRATION & SERVICE NETWORK ENGINEER WORKFLOW LOCK-IN FULL-STACK: EDA → LAB → PRODUCTION DEFENCE ACCREDITATION

The moat is being early to the standard. Test equipment for a new interface has to exist before the interface does, because the people writing the specification need something to measure against. Keysight sits inside the standards bodies for high-speed digital, optical interconnect and wireless, so its instruments become the de facto reference against which a 1.6T link is declared compliant. Once a chip designer, a transceiver vendor and a hyperscaler have all correlated their measurements to the same box, switching vendors mid-programme means re-correlating the whole chain — a cost measured in engineer-months, not in the price difference between two oscilloscopes. That is why fiscal 2026 gross margin was 65.8% and why the incumbent set (Keysight, Rohde & Schwarz, Anritsu, Viavi, Tektronix) has been stable for decades rather than churning.

The economics that follow are visible in the numbers. ROIC of 22.7% against ROE of 23.6%, on a balance sheet with $2.51B of debt against $2.92B of cash at 31 October 2026, says the returns are coming from the business rather than from leverage. The lock-in is real. What it is not is protection against the customer simply not buying this year — and that is the vulnerability list below.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Orders are a budget line, and budget lines get cut first. Keysight sells into R&D and capital budgets, which are the most discretionary spending a customer has. When the cycle turns, test equipment is deferred before headcount is. FY2024 is the proof: revenue fell 8.9% to $4.979B and EPS fell 40.6% without anything breaking in the business — customers simply stopped ordering for four quarters. A book-to-bill of about 1.17 in Q4 FY2026 is the same gauge that read below 1.0 in 2024.
▸
Customer concentration inside the AI pool. The hyperscalers, the large network-silicon vendors and the optical-module makers driving the wireline doubling are a short list. Their capex plans are correlated with each other and reviewed annually. A single deferral cycle among two or three of them would show up in orders long before it showed up in revenue — which cuts both ways, since orders are the reported number the market now watches most. Management also says the tightest supply is in a dozen to two dozen AI-focused products, so the hottest demand is concentrated in a narrow slice of the catalogue.
▸
Wireless is growing again, but it is the smaller engine. The 5G build-out that drove the 2019–2021 cycle is over. In Q4 the CFO described wireless growth as robust and double-digit, and the head of the communications group said the 6G business doubled, but wireline — the AI links — grew at triple digits. If AI interconnect spending stalls, wireless alone would not carry the growth the stock now prices in.
▸
Spirent integration and the divested crown jewels. The deal closed only after a contested global review, and the price of clearance was giving up Spirent's high-speed Ethernet, network security and channel emulation lines — arguably the parts most adjacent to the AI datacentre boom. Keysight kept satellite emulation, positioning and network automation. On the Q4 call the CFO said Keysight has "now realized the full $100 million of cost synergies" on a run-rate basis, a target set for its recent acquisitions together, but what was bought is materially narrower than what was announced.
▸
Export controls and China. High-end measurement instruments sit close to the line on semiconductor and defence export restrictions, and China is both a large end market and a jurisdiction whose antitrust regulator (SAMR) had to sign off on Spirent with undertakings. Policy risk here is bidirectional and outside management's control.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a stock can stay above 70 for a long time in a strong, lasting rally.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 month-end close of $194.48 to the November 23, 2026 close of $289.63, KEYS returned +48.9% — about 8.3% a year compounded, price only, with no dividend. Over the same dates SPY returned +58.6% and QQQ +65.3% (price only, Yahoo). Almost none of Keysight's return came in the first four years: as late as the August 2025 monthly close the stock was $163.43, about 16% below where the window starts. The whole five-year gain — and then some — came in the last twelve months, during which the stock rose +67.7%. This is the classic shape of a picks-and-shovels business: nothing happens for years, then the customers' spending cycle turns and everything happens at once.

The 2022–2024 dead zone. From the December 2021 close of $206.51 the stock fell to an October 2023 monthly close of $122.05, a drawdown of −40.9% (month-end closes), and its 2024 month-end closes stayed between $136.75 and $170.84. Fundamentally that was the 5G hangover: the wireless build-out that had funded the 2019–2021 order book was finished, and fiscal 2024 revenue fell 8.9% with EPS down 40.6%. Nothing was wrong with the franchise. The customers had simply stopped spending — which is precisely the risk the current order book inverts.

Momentum read. Monthly 6-period RSI is about 47 — back to neutral, down from 75.5 at the September close and from a reading near 95 in the February 2026 spike. The price sits below both its 3-month ($320.29) and 10-month ($323.07) averages of month-end closes; the 3-month average is now falling while the 10-month is still rising. On daily data the close is 12.1% below its 50-day average ($329.38) and 10.1% below its 200-day ($322.21), with a 14-day RSI of 45.2. The stock is 25.7% below the 52-week high of $389.93, set intraday on 6 October 2026. In plain terms: the uptrend that ran from late 2025 has stalled, and the stock is back near its March 2026 close ($282.37) while the business kept growing.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Keysight is the toll booth on AI interconnect. Every step from 400G to 800G to 1.6T makes validation harder and more instrument-intensive, and Keysight is designed into the standards those links are measured against. The evidence is not a narrative — it is $8.126B of fiscal 2026 orders, +49%, a Q4 book-to-bill of about 1.17, AI bookings about 2.5 times the year before, and triple-digit wireline growth, with management setting a 20% revenue-growth base case for fiscal 2027. Segment operating margins rose 7–10 points year over year in Q4, fiscal 2026 gross margin was 65.8%, and non-GAAP EPS grew 64% to $11.72. Wireless is growing again, the $100M of cost savings targeted from its recent acquisitions is fully in on a run-rate basis, and the balance sheet (net cash, $1.75B of free cash flow) funds buybacks without strain. After the November fall the stock trades at 19× next year's consensus earnings.

Bear Case

This is a capital-equipment company trading at 33× trailing GAAP earnings near the visible peak of an order cycle, with a consensus in which the lowest of twelve price targets sits above the current price. Two years ago the same business printed −8.9% revenue and −40.6% EPS because customers deferred budgets for four quarters — that mechanism has not been repaired, only inverted. Demand is concentrated in a short list of cloud companies and optical vendors whose spending moves together and is set once a year, and the supply crunch sits in a dozen to two dozen AI products. The margin gain is coming from hardware volume and pricing, plus a one-time tariff refund, not from software mix — software and services are still a third of revenue — so it is the reversible kind. The Spirent deal that was supposed to strengthen the datacentre position surrendered its high-speed Ethernet and network-security lines to win clearance. And the CFO of 13 years hands over the job in December.

Top 3 Risks

01
The order cycle turns. Book-to-bill is the whole thesis and it can move fast. A single quarter below 1.0 would invert the argument, and because orders come before revenue, the share price would move long before the income statement showed anything. FY2024 is the template: nothing broke, and the shares still spent two years going nowhere.
02
A lower price per dollar of earnings, without an earnings miss. November showed how this works: a quarter that beat guidance was met by seven target cuts and a 7% first-day fall. At 33× trailing GAAP and 24.7× fiscal 2026 non-GAAP earnings, if the market decides a cyclical test business deserves 20× instead of 25×, the stock falls a fifth with every operating number intact. The absence of any Sell rating means there is little room left for upgrades.
03
Concentration and policy. The AI-test demand pool is a handful of customers; wireless is growing again but is the smaller engine, so it would not carry the growth if AI spending paused; and high-end measurement instruments sit near the boundary of semiconductor and defence export controls, with China both a material end market and the jurisdiction whose regulator imposed undertakings on the Spirent deal. Any of the three can move independently of how well Keysight executes.
Industry Commentary
AI interconnect is a test-intensity story, not a volume story
EMERGING TAILWIND

The usual way to think about a component supplier is units × price. That framing badly understates what is happening to Keysight. Moving a datacentre fabric from 400G to 800G to 1.6T does not double the number of links — it makes each link far harder to validate. Signal integrity margins shrink, jitter budgets tighten, channel loss dominates, and co-packaged optics removes the ability to unplug the optical module and measure it in isolation. The consequence is more test points per design, more expensive instruments per test point, and testing at more stages of the flow — pre-silicon simulation, transceiver characterisation, system-level and then live network emulation. Keysight quantified the effect rather than asserting it: in Q4 commercial communications revenue rose 73% to $1.145B, led by triple-digit wireline growth; AI bookings for the year were about 2.5 times the year before; and the CFO said demand is still ahead of what the company can ship. The CEO's framing — "the industry is in early innings of 1.6T ramp, with 3.2T already in active R&D" — is the bull case in a sentence, and also the hardest statement on the call to test.

Wireless is back to growth, but it is not the engine
RECOVERING

Keysight's 2019–2021 order book was built on 5G: carriers, base-station vendors and handset chipset designers all buying radio test at once. That spend finished, and the fiscal 2024 collapse — revenue −8.9%, EPS −40.6% — is what its absence looks like on the income statement. In Q4 fiscal 2026 the CFO described wireless growth as "robust double-digit," and Kailash Narayanan, president of the Communications Solutions Group, said "Our 6G business doubled year-over-year." That is a recovery from a low base, and it is still dwarfed by wireline, which grew at triple digits. The practical implication: the diversification benefit of owning a broad test franchise instead of a pure AI-spending name is improving, but most of the growth still rests on one leg. Aerospace and defence, at +15% for the year and with orders up strong double digits in Q4, is the leg that steadies the cycle.

The bull/bear frame: is the market pricing a cycle as if it were permanent?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Everything on this page reduces to one disagreement. The bulls read the order book as evidence of a structural step-change in test intensity that persists as long as interconnect speeds keep doubling — a lasting shift that deserves a permanently higher price per dollar of earnings, with software and Spirent hardening it into recurring revenue. The bears read it as a textbook boom in equipment spending: customers over-order into a supply-constrained window, book-to-bill runs above 1.0 for six to eight quarters, then normalises hard — and paying 33× trailing GAAP earnings at that point is how investors historically lose money in this sector, without the company doing anything wrong. Two facts sit awkwardly for the bulls. First, software and services are still 33% of revenue as hardware keeps pace, so the margin gain is the cyclical kind rather than the mix kind. Second, demand is still ahead of what Keysight can ship — which means part of today's backlog is a queue, and queues can be cancelled. The falsifiable test is public and quarterly: book-to-bill. Above 1.0, the platform reading holds. Below 1.0 for two consecutive quarters, it does not.
