01 Company Overview

Keysight builds the instruments engineers use to prove that electronics work before anyone ships them: oscilloscopes, signal generators and analysers, bit-error-rate testers, network and channel emulators, protocol test systems, and the design software that sits upstream of all of it. It does not sell into end-unit demand — it sells into other people's R&D and validation budgets. Fiscal 2025 (ended 31 October 2025) revenue was $5.375B, up 7.95% from $4.979B in FY2024; the trailing-twelve-month figure through the July 2026 quarter is $6.582B, up 25.5%. At $55.71B the company ranks roughly #461 globally by market capitalisation.

The central debate on this page is about the shape of the current boom, not its existence. Keysight is a bookings business: it reports orders separately from revenue, and orders lead revenue by roughly two to four quarters. Fiscal Q3 2026 orders were $2.091B against $1.846B of revenue — a book-to-bill of 1.13, the second consecutive record order quarter, with orders up 56% year over year. That is an unusually clean forward signal. The question is what is inside it: AI and datacentre interconnect test — 800G and 1.6T validation, high-speed digital, pre-silicon through data-centre-scale emulation — is doing the heavy lifting, and management said wireline revenue more than doubled and surpassed wireless for the first time. Sections 02, 04 and 07 take apart whether that is a durable platform shift or an unusually violent capital-equipment upcycle being capitalised at 45× trailing earnings.

The long-run bull argument for Keysight's multiple is that it is migrating from selling boxes to selling design software, test automation, and multi-year subscriptions — recurring revenue at software gross margins, attached to installed hardware. Spirent adds directly here with automated test-and-assurance and network automation software. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
MEASUREMENT SCIENCE IP STANDARDS-BODY POSITION DESIGNED-IN AT SPEC STAGE CALIBRATION & SERVICE NETWORK ENGINEER WORKFLOW LOCK-IN FULL-STACK: EDA → LAB → PRODUCTION DEFENCE ACCREDITATION

The moat is being early to the standard. Test equipment for a new interface has to exist before the interface does, because the people writing the specification need something to measure against. Keysight sits inside the standards bodies for high-speed digital, optical interconnect and wireless, so its instruments become the de facto reference against which a 1.6T link is declared compliant. Once a chip designer, a transceiver vendor and a hyperscaler have all correlated their measurements to the same box, switching vendors mid-programme means re-correlating the whole chain — a cost measured in engineer-months, not in the price difference between two oscilloscopes. That is why gross margin is 65.9% and why the incumbent set (Keysight, Rohde & Schwarz, Anritsu, Viavi, Tektronix) has been stable for decades rather than churning.

The economics that follow are visible in the numbers. ROIC of 17.6% against ROE of 20.9%, on a balance sheet with $2.77B of debt against $2.61B of cash, says the returns are coming from the business rather than from leverage. The lock-in is real. What it is not is protection against the customer simply not buying this year — and that is the vulnerability list below.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Orders are a budget line, and budget lines get cut first. Keysight sells into R&D and capital budgets, which are the most discretionary spending a customer has. When the cycle turns, test equipment is deferred before headcount is. FY2024 is the proof: revenue fell 8.9% to $4.979B and EPS fell 40.6% without anything breaking in the business — customers simply stopped ordering for four quarters. A book-to-bill of 1.13 today is the same instrument that read below 1.0 in 2024.
▸
Customer concentration inside the AI pool. The hyperscalers, the large network-silicon vendors and the optical-module makers driving the wireline doubling are a short list. Their capex plans are correlated with each other and reviewed annually. A single deferral cycle among two or three of them would show up in orders long before it showed up in revenue — which cuts both ways, since orders are the reported number the market now watches most.
▸
Wireless has been dead weight for years, and 6G is not close. The 5G build-out that drove the 2019–2021 cycle is over; carrier and handset-chipset test spending has been soft since. Management's near-term answer is a product refresh, not a new standard. If AI interconnect is the only growth engine, the diversification argument for owning Keysight over a pure AI-capex name gets thinner.
▸
Spirent integration and the divested crown jewels. The deal closed only after a contested global review, and the price of clearance was giving up Spirent's high-speed Ethernet, network security and channel emulation lines — arguably the parts most adjacent to the AI datacentre boom. Keysight kept satellite emulation, positioning and network automation. The $100M synergy target is described as substantially de-risked, but what was bought is materially narrower than what was announced.
▸
Export controls and China. High-end measurement instruments sit close to the line on semiconductor and defence export restrictions, and China is both a large end market and a jurisdiction whose antitrust regulator (SAMR) had to sign off on Spirent with undertakings. Policy risk here is bidirectional and outside management's control.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $180.02 to the September 4, 2026 close of $327.21, KEYS returned +81.8% over five years — about 12.7% compounded annually, with no dividend. But almost none of that return was earned in the first four years. As late as the August 2025 monthly close the stock was $163.43, roughly 9% below where it started the window. The entire five-year return — and then some — was generated in the last twelve months, during which the stock rose +97.93%. This is the classic shape of a picks-and-shovels business: nothing happens for years, then the customers' capital cycle turns and everything happens at once.

The 2022–2024 dead zone. From the December 2021 close of $206.51 the stock fell to an October 2023 monthly close of $122.05, a drawdown of −40.9%, and then spent all of 2024 oscillating in a $136–$178 band. Fundamentally that was the 5G hangover: the wireless build-out that had funded the 2019–2021 order book was finished, and FY2024 revenue fell 8.9% with EPS down 40.6%. Nothing was wrong with the franchise. The customers had simply stopped spending — which is precisely the risk the current order book inverts.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Keysight is the toll booth on AI interconnect. Every step from 400G to 800G to 1.6T makes validation harder and more instrument-intensive, and Keysight is designed into the standards those links are measured against. The evidence is not a narrative — it is $2.091B of Q3 orders, +56% YoY, a 1.13 book-to-bill, and wireline revenue doubling past wireless for the first time, with a second $2B+ order quarter already banked and a third guided. Operating margins expanded 8–9 points year over year in both segments simultaneously, gross margin hit 65.9%, and FY2026 non-GAAP EPS guidance moved to ~$11.46 against a $10.24 street. Wireless refresh and the Spirent software stack are optionality on top, and the balance sheet (net-debt-neutral, $1.47B FCF) funds buybacks without strain.

Bear Case

This is a capital-equipment company trading at 44× trailing GAAP earnings at the visible peak of an order cycle, with a consensus in which the lowest of twelve price targets sits above the current price. Two years ago the same business printed −8.9% revenue and −40.6% EPS because customers deferred budgets for four quarters — that mechanism has not been repaired, only inverted. Demand is concentrated in a short list of hyperscalers and optical vendors whose capex is correlated and annually reviewed. The margin expansion is coming from hardware volume and pricing, not from software mix — software and services actually fell to 33% of revenue — so it is the reversible kind. And the Spirent deal that was supposed to strengthen the datacentre position surrendered its high-speed Ethernet and network-security lines to win clearance.

Top 3 Risks

01
The order cycle turns. Book-to-bill is the whole thesis and it is a monthly-resolution instrument that can move fast. A single quarter below 1.0 would invert the argument, and because orders lead revenue by two to four quarters, the stock would reprice long before the income statement showed anything. FY2024 is the template: nothing broke, and the shares still spent two years going nowhere.
02
Multiple compression without an earnings miss. At 44× trailing and ~28.6× the company's own FY2026 non-GAAP guidance, a large share of the last twelve months' 98% gain is re-rating rather than earnings. If the market simply decides a cyclical test business deserves 25× instead of 40×, the stock falls a third with every operating metric intact. The absence of any Sell rating and the clustering of eight same-day target raises are both signs that positioning is one-directional.
03
Concentration and policy. The AI-test demand pool is a handful of customers; the wireless leg is still dormant with no 6G cycle to spend into; and high-end measurement instruments sit near the boundary of semiconductor and defence export controls, with China both a material end market and the jurisdiction whose regulator imposed undertakings on the Spirent deal. Any of the three can move independently of how well Keysight executes.
Industry Commentary
AI interconnect is a test-intensity story, not a volume story
EMERGING TAILWIND

The usual way to think about a component supplier is units × price. That framing badly understates what is happening to Keysight. Moving a datacentre fabric from 400G to 800G to 1.6T does not double the number of links — it makes each link far harder to validate. Signal integrity margins shrink, jitter budgets tighten, channel loss dominates, and co-packaged optics removes the ability to unplug the optical module and measure it in isolation. The consequence is more test points per design, more expensive instruments per test point, and testing at more stages of the flow — pre-silicon simulation, transceiver characterisation, system-level and then live network emulation. Keysight quantified the effect rather than asserting it: wireline revenue more than doubled year over year and overtook wireless for the first time, commercial communications crossed $1B in a quarter for the first time at +56%, and management said demand currently exceeds its supply capacity. The CEO's own framing — "early stages of a long AI adoption cycle" — is the bull case in six words, and also the least falsifiable statement in the release.

Wireless has no cycle to spend into, and won't for years
ONGOING HEADWIND

Keysight's 2019–2021 order book was built on 5G: carriers, base-station vendors and handset chipset designers all buying RF test at once. That spend is finished, and the FY2024 collapse — revenue −8.9%, EPS −40.6% — is what its absence looks like on the income statement. There is no 6G standard to test against yet, and management's stated near-term wireless catalyst is a product refresh — "the biggest refresh of core RF microwave and high-speed digital products since the company's formation" — which is a company-driven event, not a customer-budget event. Refreshes pull forward replacement demand; they do not create a new spending pool. The practical implication for anyone underwriting this stock: the diversification benefit of owning a broad test franchise instead of a pure AI-capex name is currently smaller than it looks, because one of the three legs is not contributing growth. Aerospace and defence, at +14% and on a multi-year modernisation cycle, is the leg genuinely doing that job.

The bull/bear frame: is this a cycle being capitalised as a platform?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Everything on this page reduces to one disagreement. The bulls read the order book as evidence of a structural step-change in test intensity that persists as long as interconnect speeds keep doubling — a platform shift deserving a platform multiple, with software and Spirent hardening it into recurring revenue. The bears read it as a textbook capital-equipment upcycle: customers over-order into a supply-constrained window, book-to-bill runs above 1.0 for six to eight quarters, then normalises hard — and paying 44× trailing GAAP earnings at that point is how investors historically lose money in this sector, without the company doing anything wrong. Two verified facts sit awkwardly for the bulls. First, software and services fell to 33% of revenue as hardware outgrew it, so the margin expansion is currently the cyclical kind rather than the mix kind. Second, demand exceeds supply capacity — which means part of today's backlog is a queue, and queues can be cancelled. The falsifiable test is public and quarterly: book-to-bill. Above 1.0, the platform reading holds. Below 1.0 for two consecutive quarters, it does not.
