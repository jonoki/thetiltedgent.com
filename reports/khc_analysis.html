01 Company Overview

Kraft Heinz makes and sells packaged food under a portfolio of legacy American and international brands — Heinz ketchup, Kraft cheese and macaroni, Oscar Mayer, Philadelphia, Capri Sun, Lunchables, Ore-Ida, Jell-O, Velveeta, Kool-Aid — through grocery retail, foodservice and emerging-market channels. FY2025 revenue was $24.94B, down 3.5% from $25.85B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through the June 27, 2026 quarter is $24.90B, down 1.6%. The company ranks roughly #861 globally by market capitalisation at $29.47B — a number worth sitting with, because in 2017 this company launched a $143B takeover bid for Unilever. It is now worth about a fifth of that bid.

This is the canonical "cheap or broken?" name in US large-cap staples, and it is the epilogue to the 3G Capital experiment — the thesis that a food conglomerate could be run permanently on zero-based budgeting, that brand equity was a fixed asset you could harvest rather than a flow you had to feed. The stock trades at 0.82× book value, yields 6.44%, and generated $3.82B of free cash flow in the last twelve months. It also lost $3.40B on a GAAP basis over the same twelve months, because it wrote off $7.35B of goodwill and intangibles in the June 2026 quarter alone. The central question on this page is whether a 6.4% yield backed by real cash flow compensates you for owning a portfolio whose accounting value the company itself keeps marking down, in categories whose volumes keep shrinking. Two premises in the original brief for this report were wrong and are corrected below: the split has been paused, not completed, and the CEO has changed.

Direct correction to the brief for this report. Kraft Heinz announced in September 2025 a plan to separate into two companies — provisionally "Global Taste Elevation Co." (sauces, spreads, condiments, international) and "North American Grocery Co." On February 11, 2026, alongside Q4 2025 results, new CEO Steve Cahillane paused the separation and redirected the company toward a $600M investment programme in marketing, sales, R&D and price. The Q2 2026 results release still refers to "the current pause on work related to the separation" as a risk factor affecting the share price. No separation has completed. No new company names have been finalised. No new tickers exist. Anyone modelling a sum-of-the-parts on two listed entities is modelling something that does not exist and, on current management commentary, has no announced timetable. The initial February guidance that came with the pause was for organic net sales down 1.5% to 3.5% and adjusted EPS of $1.98–$2.10, both materially below the consensus of the time; the August update improved this to organic −0.5% to −2.0% and adjusted EPS $2.03–$2.09.

Analytical read: a split is the standard way to get a shrinking business out of a growing one's multiple. Pausing it says management believes the operating problem must be fixed before the financial engineering — a defensible view, and also a removal of the main near-term re-rating catalyst.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
HEINZ CONDIMENT FRANCHISE SHELF SPACE INCUMBENCY FOODSERVICE SACHET LOCK-IN MANUFACTURING SCALE DISTRIBUTION DENSITY EMERGING-MARKET HEINZ BUILD CASH-GENERATIVE MATURITY

The real moat here is narrower than the brand list suggests, and it is worth separating the two halves of the portfolio. Heinz — specifically ketchup and the wider condiments and sauces franchise — is a genuine brand asset: it holds category-defining mental availability, it is a low-ticket purchase where consumers do not comparison-shop hard, and in foodservice it enjoys the closest thing to a switching cost in packaged food, because a restaurant that puts Heinz on the table is signalling something to its customer and cannot swap it silently. That is why the proposed break-up put condiments in the "Taste Elevation" bucket and why that bucket was expected to carry the higher multiple. The Kraft half is different. Processed cheese, cold cuts, boxed dinners and lunch kits are categories where private label has closed most of the quality gap, where the retailer sets the price ladder, and where the consumer trend — less processed, higher protein, fewer additives, smaller portions — runs directly against the product. The balance sheet has been telling this story for years: goodwill has fallen from $31.30B at FY2021 to $19.71B at the June 2026 quarter, and other intangibles from $43.54B to $32.37B. That is $22.8B of brand and goodwill value written off in under five years — not lost to competitors in a single battle, but conceded in an annual impairment test, one brand at a time. Goodwill plus intangibles still stand at $52.09B against total assets of $73.07B — 71% of the balance sheet, and 1.77× the entire $29.47B market capitalisation. The market is already pricing further write-downs; the accountants are catching up.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Volume is falling and price is the only offset. Q2 2026 organic net sales decomposed to price +1.3pp, volume/mix −2.6pp. A packaged-food company that grows price faster than it loses volume is managing a decline; one that loses volume faster than it prices is shrinking. Kraft Heinz is currently the second. Every year of this trade thins the consumer franchise a little more, and every price increase widens the gap to private label — the mechanism is self-reinforcing, which is precisely why it is hard to arrest.
▸
The impairment treadmill. The $15.4B write-down of February 2019 (Kraft and Oscar Mayer brand values, widely reported at the time — not re-verified from the sources fetched today) was supposed to be the reset. It was not. Kraft Heinz took a $9.3B non-cash impairment in Q2 2025, driving a quarterly loss of more than $7.8B and an FY2025 net loss of $5.85B, then a further $7.35B in Q2 2026 — $2.44B goodwill plus $4.91B intangibles — driving a $5.46B quarterly loss. Impairments are non-cash and backward-looking, but a company that has to reduce its own estimate of its brands' future cash flows three times in seven years is telling you those cash flows keep coming in below plan.
▸
A departing anchor shareholder. Berkshire Hathaway's ~325M shares (~28%) were registered for sale in January 2026. Until that block clears, every rally faces a known seller, and the exit itself functions as a public verdict on the merger Berkshire co-engineered in 2015. There is a second-order effect: a widely-held income name loses part of its shareholder base when the most credible long-term holder leaves.
▸
Operating leverage runs both ways. Q2 2026 North American sales fell 2.7% while segment adjusted operating income fell 15.8%. Full-year 2026 guidance is for constant-currency adjusted operating income down 16% to 18% on organic sales down only 0.5% to 2.0%. Fixed-cost-heavy manufacturing plus reinvestment means small revenue declines produce large profit declines — and the reinvestment is discretionary spending management could cut, which would flatter next year's earnings while doing nothing for the brands.

Notable One-Off Items. This company's GAAP income statement is currently unusable without adjustment, and saying so is not an apology for management. Three items dominate. (1) The Q2 2026 impairment of $7,352M — $2,441M goodwill and $4,911M other intangibles — turned a quarter with $6,262M of sales and $34.1% adjusted gross margin into a $5,460M net loss and $(4.60) diluted EPS, against $0.56 of adjusted EPS. (2) The Q2 2025 impairment of $9.3B, which produced a quarterly loss of more than $7.8B and drove the FY2025 net loss of $5,846M. (3) The February 2019 write-down of $15.4B against the Kraft and Oscar Mayer brands (widely reported at the time; not re-verified from the sources fetched today). None of these consumed cash — free cash flow rose in each of the last four fiscal years, from $1,553M in FY2022 to $3,816M TTM. But cumulative non-cash write-offs of this magnitude are the balance-sheet record of a merger thesis that did not work, and the remaining $52.09B of goodwill and intangibles is the size of the exposure that has not yet been marked.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the security has risen persistently and is conventionally called "overbought"; below 30 it has fallen persistently and is called "oversold". This is a six-month version, so it describes multi-month momentum, not day-trading signals — and in a stock that has spent five years in a downtrend, sub-30 readings have marked pauses in the decline rather than bottoms.

The five-year picture is a straight line down with two shelves. From the October 2021 close of $35.89 to $24.85 on September 4, 2026, the price fell 30.8%. Add roughly $8.00 of dividends collected over the period at the $1.60 annual rate and the total return is approximately −8% (approximate: dividends summed at the current annual rate, not reconstructed payment by payment). Over the same five years the NASDAQ-100 and S&P 500 both compounded strongly — a precise index return over the identical window was not verified from the sources fetched and is therefore not quoted, but the direction is not in doubt: this has been a very large relative underperformance, and the dividend has done nearly all of the work of keeping shareholders whole in nominal terms.

The shape matters more than the total. The stock actually rose through the 2022 inflation shock, peaking at $42.63 in April 2022, because packaged food was the classic pricing-power trade: pass through cost inflation, defend margin, get paid for defensiveness. It held a $33–$40 band all the way to December 2023 ($36.98). The break came in 2024–25, and it was not a macro break — it was the market re-rating the terminal value of processed food. From $35.11 in September 2024 the stock fell to $25.82 by June 2025, a 26% decline in nine months with no recession and no earnings collapse. That is the GLP-1 and better-for-you repricing, and it is the single most important move on this chart.

Relative performance. Against the NASDAQ-100 — the benchmark for its listing venue — KHC has been a very poor holding over five years, and the comparison is close to meaningless in composition terms: an index dominated by megacap technology against a shrinking condiment and cheese business. The more useful comparison is the staples peer group, where KHC's problem is visibly its own. Mondelez, with $39.68B of revenue, carries a $78.21B market cap and a 22.5× P/E — 2.0× revenue. Kraft Heinz, with $24.90B of revenue, carries $29.47B — 1.18× revenue. Campbell's trades at 16.3×. Conagra, at $7.41B market cap on $11.28B of revenue, trades at a similar discount to KHC and shares the same North American shelf-stable problem. The market is not derating packaged food uniformly; it is separating global snacking and condiments from North American centre-store grocery, and paying a premium for the former. That distinction is precisely the one the September 2025 split was designed to monetise — and precisely the reason pausing it left the stock stuck.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 0.82× book and ~12× guided adjusted earnings for a business that threw off $3.82B of free cash flow in the last twelve months and grew H1 free cash flow 10.3% at 123% conversion. The 6.44% dividend is covered 2.0× by cash, adjusted gross margin was flat in Q2, guidance was raised in August, consumption improved from −2.5% to about −1% by July, Emerging Markets grew 10.4%, and $2.9B of debt has been retired in and around the quarter. Once the Berkshire block clears, the marginal seller is gone. If organic sales merely stabilise near zero, a mid-teens multiple on $2.05 of earnings plus a 6% yield is a perfectly respectable total return from here — and a restarted separation is free optionality on top.

Bear Case

Volume/mix was −2.6pp in Q2 2026 and price is the only thing holding the top line up — a trade that gets harder every year against private label. Segment operating leverage is brutal: North American sales −2.7% produced adjusted operating income −15.8%, and FY2026 guidance is for constant-currency adjusted operating income down 16–18%. The company has written off $22.8B of goodwill and intangibles since FY2021 and still carries $52.09B, or 1.77× its market cap. The anchor shareholder of ten years is selling ~28% of the company, 10% of the float is short, zero of the named recent analyst calls is a Buy, and management has removed the one structural catalyst by pausing the split. A 6.4% yield on a business whose earnings fall 16–18% a year is not a value stock; it is a liquidation in slow motion.

Top 3 Risks

01
Dividend sustainability, on earnings rather than cash. The $1.60 rate costs ~$1.90B a year and is comfortably covered by $3.82B of free cash flow — that part is real. But it consumes 77–79% of guided adjusted EPS of $2.03–$2.09, and adjusted operating income is guided down 16–18%. If earnings decline another year at that rate, the payout crosses 100% of adjusted EPS and the board faces a choice between the dividend, the $600M reinvestment programme and continued deleveraging. Income holders should treat the yield as adequate compensation, not as a guarantee.
02
Further impairment of the remaining $52.09B. Goodwill and intangibles are 71% of total assets and 1.77× market capitalisation. Impairment tests key off forecast brand cash flows; those forecasts have been cut in 2019, 2025 and 2026. Each write-down reduces book equity (already down from $49.45B at FY2021 to $36.13B), which mechanically worsens the reported leverage ratios and can pressure the credit rating even though no cash moves. The 0.53 debt-to-equity that looks reassuring today is the most fragile number in Section 04.
03
Structural demand decline in centre-store processed food. This is the risk that makes the other two matter. GLP-1 adoption, protein-forward eating and ingredient-label scrutiny all reduce the calories consumed from exactly the categories Kraft Heinz over-indexes to — boxed dinners, processed cheese, cold cuts, lunch kits, sweetened drinks. Volume/mix has been negative for multiple quarters. If this is a permanent change in category consumption rather than a cyclical trade-down, no amount of marketing spend, cost cutting or corporate restructuring fixes it — and the terminal value of the North American grocery portfolio is materially lower than any multiple currently in the price.
Industry Commentary
GLP-1 and the Re-Rating of Processed Calories
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

GLP-1 receptor agonists (the class of injectable weight-loss and diabetes drugs including semaglutide) reduce appetite, and in doing so reduce the number of calories a user buys. The categories most exposed are precisely the ones where Kraft Heinz has its highest share of the household: snacking, sweetened beverages, boxed convenience dinners, processed meat and cheese. The market has already acted on this — the stock fell from $35.11 in September 2024 to $25.82 in June 2025, 26% in nine months, in a period with no recession and no earnings collapse. What the market has not yet resolved is magnitude: whether GLP-1s remove one or two percent of category volume permanently, or reset the whole demand curve for centre-store grocery. Kraft Heinz's own Q2 2026 numbers show volume/mix at −2.6pp with price at +1.3pp, which is consistent with either interpretation. Note that GLP-1 was not mentioned on the Q2 2026 earnings call — management is framing the problem as fixable execution, not structural demand. That framing is itself the thing to test at Q3.

Private Label and the Erosion of the Price Ladder
ONGOING HEADWIND

Four years of packaged-food price increases have widened the gap between branded and retailer-owned products at exactly the moment when retailer-brand quality has become hard to distinguish in commodity categories — shredded cheese, mayonnaise, frozen potatoes, cold cuts. The company's own decomposition tells the story: it took +1.3pp of price in Q2 2026 and gave up 2.6pp of volume, meaning it is buying revenue stability with market share. The $600M reinvestment programme announced in February 2026 is explicitly aimed at reversing this — part marketing, part price investment — and the honest read of that programme is that it is a margin sacrifice to defend volume, which is why constant-currency adjusted operating income is guided down 16–18% on sales down only 0.5–2.0%. This is also the reason the 3G playbook has run out: you cannot cost-cut your way through a share loss caused by the price gap your cost-cutting funded.

The Break-Up Trade — Paused, Not Cancelled
EMERGING TAILWIND

Food conglomerates have spent five years unbundling: Kellogg into Kellanova and WK Kellogg in 2023 — run by the man now running Kraft Heinz — and Kraft Heinz's own September 2025 plan to separate "Global Taste Elevation Co." from "North American Grocery Co." The logic is visible in the peer multiples fetched for this report: Mondelez at 2.0× revenue and 22.5× earnings versus Kraft Heinz at 1.18× revenue. A condiments-and-international entity would plausibly clear a Mondelez-adjacent multiple; the grocery entity would trade nearer Conagra. That arbitrage is the entire basis of the $41 high price target on the street.

Bull vs bear, framed honestly: the bull owns a 6.44% yield covered 2.0× by free cash flow, at 0.82× book, in a business whose guidance was raised in August and whose July consumption trend improved — with a paused-not-cancelled break-up as free optionality and a departing 28% holder as a clearing event rather than a verdict. The bear owns a business losing 2.6 points of volume a year, guiding operating income down 16–18%, carrying $52.09B of goodwill and intangibles against a $29.47B market cap after already writing off $22.8B, with fourteen holds and four sells against a single buy. Both readings use the same numbers. The difference between them is entirely whether you believe the volume decline is cyclical or terminal — and Q3 2026, with its annual impairment test and its read on whether July's −1% consumption held, is where that question gets its next answer.
