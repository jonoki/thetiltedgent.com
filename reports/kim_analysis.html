01 Company Overview

Kimco is the landlord of the weekly errand. It owns 564 open-air shopping centers and mixed-use assets totalling about 100 million square feet of leasable space across the US, concentrated in the first-ring suburbs of major metro areas: Sun Belt cities and high-barrier coastal markets. The tenants are the grocery store, the pharmacy, the dry cleaner and the fast-casual lunch spot. They sell what the company calls "essential, necessity-based goods and services that drive multiple shopping trips per week." It is a REIT, and the company says it has run shopping centers for more than 65 years.

Start with the number that matters for a REIT. GAAP earnings per share of $0.85 put the stock on a 26.5x trailing P/E and a dividend payout ratio of 131%. Both figures look alarming and both mislead, because GAAP depreciation treats shopping centers as if they wear out like delivery trucks. The industry measure is FFO. Kimco earned $1.76 of FFO per diluted share in FY2025, up 6.7% from $1.65. Management has twice raised its 2026 guidance, which now stands at $1.83–$1.84. At $22.54 the stock trades at about 12.3x this year's FFO and yields 4.97% on a dividend that uses about 61% of that FFO. The operating record is strong: 96.4% occupancy, matching the all-time high, and a record 92.9% small-shop occupancy. The market has not rewarded it lately. The stock closed July at $25.48 and has since fallen 11.5% to $22.54, which leaves it only 8.6% above where it stood five years ago.

This part of the business sells assets that other buyers value more highly than Kimco does, and puts the cash back into the core. On the Q2 call management cited the sale of The Milton at a 4.9% cap rate, alongside sales of Costco ground leases with the proceeds recycled into grocery-anchored centers. Through July 31, 2026, dispositions totalled $261M against about $109M of acquisitions (5.1% vs 5.7% weighted-average cap rates, per the release). The logic of the trade: sell assets the market values richly, at a 4.9% cap rate, and buy centers that yield more. The catch is that each sale shrinks the income base, and gains on sale flow into GAAP net income, not FFO.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Limited new supply Grocery-anchored traffic Scale: 564 centers, ~100M sq ft First-ring suburb locations A-level ratings at all three agencies $2.7B liquidity

The moat is scarcity. Very few new shopping centers are being built, a point Flynn made himself in the Q2 release, so the existing ones in dense suburbs gain pricing power as retailers compete for a fixed stock of space. The 40.4% new-lease spread measures that power. The balance sheet adds a second, quieter advantage. Kimco says it has A-level ratings from all three major agencies, after Moody's upgraded it to A3 on December 3, 2025, so it can borrow more cheaply than lower-rated landlords. It holds $2.7B of liquidity ($700M cash plus a $2.0B credit facility), and net debt runs at 5.2x EBITDA on a consolidated basis.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
It trades like a bond with a roof. A REIT yielding 4.97% competes with Treasuries and investment-grade credit for the same income buyer, so rates matter as much as rents. Total debt is $8.86B against a $15.16B market cap. Debt/EBITDA is 6.57x on StockAnalysis's gross measure and 5.2x on management's net measure, with interest coverage of 2.25x. The August slide from $25.48 to $23.71 came despite a beat-and-raise quarter. The cause was not established from the sources fetched.
02
Small-shop tenants fail first. The record 92.9% small-shop occupancy is the most cyclical number in the portfolio. Painted Tree's bankruptcy already cost 16bps of occupancy this year. Credit loss has fallen to 57bps, but that is a good-times reading. In a consumer downturn the small tenants would go well before the grocers.
03
Growth is low single digits. The 2026 FFO guidance midpoint of $1.835 is 4.3% above FY2025's $1.76, and same-property NOI guidance is 3.0–3.5%. That is a steady compounder, not a grower. The return case rests on the 4.97% yield plus about 4% growth. It does not rest on the multiple rising.
04
Capital recycling can shrink the base. Selling at a 4.9% cap rate works only if the proceeds earn more. Year-to-date dispositions of $261M exceed acquisitions of about $109M. If buyers stop paying low cap rates, or if reinvestment yields fall, the arbitrage closes.

Notable One-Off Items

GAAP earnings at Kimco are noisy because property-sale gains flow through them. Diluted EPS ran $1.60 (FY2021), $0.16 (FY2022), $1.02 (FY2023), $0.55 (FY2024) and $0.82 (FY2025). Per-share FFO, the steadier series, rose from $1.65 to $1.76 over the last year. The per-share history also spans two all-stock mergers. Weingarten Realty merged into Kimco under an agreement announced April 15, 2021, and revenue rose 28.99% in FY2021 and 26.61% in FY2022. RPT Realty (56 centers, 13.3M sq ft) closed on January 2, 2024. The company has also been buying back stock: 6.1M shares at an average $19.79 in 2025, and 4.1M shares at $25.38 ($104.7M) reported with Q2 2026 results, plus 516,750 preferred shares at $64.50. The share count is down 0.23% YoY. Net income guidance was raised much more (to $1.00–$1.03 from $0.83–$0.87) than FFO guidance. The release does not say why, but because FFO excludes gains on sale, a gap that size points to expected gains. That is this report's inference, not a company statement. The last stock split was 2-for-1 on August 24, 2005, before the chart window.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over five years, from the September 2021 close of $20.75 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $22.54, KIM's price rose 8.6%. For a REIT the dividend is most of the return. Adding the dividends from FY2022 through September 2026, about $4.55 a share by this report's estimate (StockAnalysis annual DPS for FY2022–FY2025 plus the three 2026 payments listed by Yahoo; Q4 2021 excluded; no reinvestment), gives a total return of roughly +31%. The path was a five-year range. The stock traded between a $17.59 low (September 2023) and a $25.57 high (November 2024) and was never far from where it began.

It is now 43.3, neutral, after two down months. The 10-month moving average is about $23.22, above the current price, so the stock has slipped below its medium-term trend. The low-30s troughs of September 2022, September 2023 and April 2025 (34.1, 33.5 and 33.1) each marked a turn higher. The current reading is still some way above them.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay about 12.3x this year's FFO for the largest open-air shopping-center REIT in its peer group. It has occupancy at its all-time high, record small-shop occupancy, 40.4% spreads on new leases, $95M of signed rent not yet paying, A-level credit ratings and a 4.97% yield covered about 1.6 times by FFO. Guidance has gone up twice this year and the dividend rose 12%. Very little new retail space is being built, so every point of occupancy gets harder to lose and easier to reprice. If rates stop rising, the 11.5% fall since July is a gift to income buyers. The Street's lowest target, $24, is above today's price.

Bear Case

Occupancy at a record high has nowhere to go but sideways or down, and the small-shop tenants who drove the record are the first to fail in a consumer slowdown. Painted Tree already has. Growth is about 4% a year in FFO. The stock is a rate instrument with $8.86B of debt and 2.25x interest coverage. The market sold it through a beat-and-raise quarter, which says the price is set by the bond market, not by leasing. Fifteen of 23 analysts say Hold, and five years of owning it produced 8.6% of price gain.

Top 3 Risks

01
Interest rates. A 4.97%-yielding REIT is valued against bond yields, and the stock fell 11.5% from July through a quarter of better numbers. Higher rates raise refinancing cost on $8.86B of debt, with coverage at 2.25x, and lower the price investors will pay for the dividend.
02
Tenant credit at the top of the cycle. Credit loss is 57bps against 89bps a year ago, and small-shop occupancy is at a record 92.9%. Both are peak-cycle readings. A consumer downturn would hit the small tenants that pay the highest rents first.
03
Low growth with shrinking recycling gains. FFO growth guided around 4%, same-property NOI 3.0–3.5%. Dispositions ($261M) exceed acquisitions (~$109M) year to date. If the market for low-cap-rate asset sales cools, one of the levers behind per-share growth weakens.
Industry Commentary
Limited new supply in open-air retail
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

The whole open-air sector is reporting the same pattern. Kimco's CEO cites "limited new shopping center supply." Brixmor reported "record small shop occupancy and record signed but not yet commenced rent" for Q2 2026, with same-property NOI up 5.8%. Regency raised full-year guidance. When few new centers are built and grocers keep opening stores, landlords with existing, well-located centers gain pricing power. Kimco's 40.4% new-lease spread is that pricing power made visible.

The rate channel
ONGOING HEADWIND

All four open-air names yield between 4.14% and 4.97% at their September 21, 2026 closes (StockAnalysis dividends, this report's arithmetic). At those yields they compete for the same income buyer as investment-grade bonds. Kimco's August and September decline came with no negative company news in the sources fetched. That is consistent with a rate-driven or sector-wide move, but it is not proven here. Current Treasury yields were not sourced for this report.
