01 Company Overview
KKR & Co. Inc. is one of the world's largest alternative asset managers, investing across private equity, real assets (infrastructure, energy and real estate), credit and liquid strategies, while also owning a life and annuity balance sheet (Global Atlantic) and a portfolio of consolidated operating companies. As of September 30, 2026 the firm managed $804 billion in total AUM, up 11% YoY, of which $648 billion was fee-paying AUM (+11%). Reported GAAP revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was $14.61 billion (+6.4% on the same period of 2025), while third-quarter revenue fell 17.4% to $4.56 billion — swings driven largely by consolidation and mark-to-market accounting rather than by underlying fee generation.
Ranked #148 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization and one of the largest US-listed asset managers by market value, KKR sits inside a small group of scaled alternative managers that has drawn a disproportionate share of institutional and, increasingly, individual capital. Its economics are best read through three non-GAAP measures the company itself emphasizes: fee-related earnings, total operating earnings and adjusted net income — all three of which set per-share records in the third quarter of 2026, reported on November 9.
Co-Chief Executive Officers
Joseph Y. Bae & Scott C. Nuttall
Both joined KKR in 1996, were named Co-Presidents/Co-COOs in 2017 and Co-CEOs in 2021. Bae built KKR's Asia franchise; Nuttall built the credit, capital-markets and balance-sheet businesses. Founders Henry R. Kravis and George R. Roberts remain Co-Executive Chairmen.

The clearest structural difference between KKR and its peers. Rather than run an asset-light manager, KKR holds long-duration majority stakes in a portfolio of operating businesses on its own balance sheet — mostly core private-equity holdings acquired at low leverage and held indefinitely — and reports the dividends they pay up as a separate earnings stream. KKR's moat is built on duration of capital rather than on any product. Roughly $344 billion — 43% of AUM — is perpetual capital (+11% YoY at September 30, 2026), meaning it has no contractual end date: Global Atlantic's policyholder liabilities, core private-equity and core infrastructure vehicles, listed and semi-liquid vehicles, and the balance sheet itself. Traditional drawdown funds add a further layer of stickiness with ten-year-plus lock-ups and $146 billion of uncalled commitments (money clients have promised but KKR has not yet drawn) that will convert to fee-paying assets on KKR's schedule, not the market's. That structure means a bad quarter in markets does not produce redemptions the way it does for a mutual-fund family. On top of it sits a genuine scale advantage: KKR raised $25 billion in Q3'26 and $116 billion over the trailing twelve months, and with its Q2 results in July it said it had passed its three-year, $300 billion fundraising target six months early, at $305 billion raised since the start of 2024. On the November 9 call Co-CEO Scott Nuttall called it a "K-shaped industry": in his words, "the happy part of the K is taking market share" — capital concentrating in a handful of scaled platforms while sub-scale managers struggle to raise. The owned insurer closes the loop: KKR originates credit assets, places them on Global Atlantic's balance sheet, and earns a spread plus a fee on capital it does not have to re-raise. Finally, the in-house capital-markets business monetizes syndication and financing on KKR's own deals, converting deal flow into fee income that a pure LP-funded manager cannot capture.
KEY VULNERABILITIES

Private-credit cycle exposure. Credit and liquid strategies are KKR's single largest AUM bucket at $337B, and Global Atlantic held a further $170B of credit AUM at September 30, 2026. Industry-wide fundraising has already cracked: global private-credit fundraising was $49.9B in Q1 2026 and direct-lending fundraising fell to $10.7B, the lowest quarterly figure in nearly three years. Over $620 billion of high-yield bonds and leveraged loans mature in 2026–2027. Spread compression, rising defaults or a marks-driven confidence shock would hit fee growth, spread income and carried interest simultaneously — the classic correlated-exposure problem for a manager that has diversified into adjacent forms of the same credit risk.

Realization dependence and a blocked exit window. Roughly $9.7 billion of gross unrealized carried interest (KKR's share of fund profits not yet cashed) sits on the balance sheet at September 30, 2026, but it converts to cash only through IPOs, sales to other buyout firms or sales to companies. Industry-wide there are approximately 29,000 buyout-owned portfolio companies awaiting exits. Realized performance income fell to $602 million in Q3 from $931 million a year earlier. On the November 9 call Lewin put visibility on gains and fees over the next couple of quarters at more than $3.5 billion, but about $600 million of it without the USI sale. If the exit window stays narrow outside one-off sales like USI, performance fees stay deferred, distributable earnings stay depressed, and — more damaging — clients who receive no distributions have less money to commit to the next flagship fund.

Retail channel fragility. K-Series has grown to $45B and is central to the growth story, but the wealth channel is semi-liquid by design. Peers have already been tested: retail assets are roughly 24% of Blackstone's AUM and about 40% of Blue Owl's, and record redemption requests in Q1 2026 came predominantly from high-net-worth investors.

GAAP opacity and key-person concentration. KKR's reported financials consolidate funds and an insurance company, so GAAP revenue swung from $5.7B (2022) to $14.5B (2023) to $21.9B (2024) to $19.5B (2025) — and fell 17.4% year on year in Q3'26 while fee-related earnings rose 27% — with no comparable change in the underlying business — an investor base that cannot model the company reliably will discount it. Separately, the firm is still culturally and commercially tied to a small group of individuals: two co-CEOs, two co-executive chairmen who are the founders, and a partnership whose deal-level relationships are not fully institutional.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

Unrealized carried-interest marks. KKR carries roughly $9.7 billion of gross unrealized carried interest that has not been realized in cash. Movements in this balance flow through GAAP results without any cash changing hands, and they reverse. Investors should treat any quarter's GAAP swing that traces to carry marks as noise; the cash event is the realization. Q3'26 produced $602 million of realized performance income (versus $931 million a year earlier) — that is the number that matters.

Global Atlantic consolidation effects. Full consolidation of a life and annuity balance sheet added tens of billions of assets, liabilities and debt to KKR's GAAP statements and is the single biggest reason revenue, margins, ROE, ROIC and debt-to-equity are not comparable to a pure-play manager or to KKR's own pre-2024 history. Q3'26 insurance operating earnings of $230M included a $26M charge from GA's annual review of its actuarial assumptions — a reminder that reported insurance earnings move with accounting judgments as well as with the business.

USI sale. On August 31, 2026 KKR agreed to sell USI Insurance Services, an insurance broker it has owned since 2017, to Aon for $17 billion; the deal is expected to close in the fourth quarter of 2026, subject to approvals. On the November 9 call CFO Robert Lewin put the return at about 6x KKR's cost on the original 2017 investment and 3.4x all KKR balance-sheet capital, with after-tax proceeds of about $3.3 billion. The gain is a one-time item: it will lift realized investment income and adjusted net income in the quarter it closes, not the recurring fee base.

Antitrust settlement. On August 26, 2026 KKR settled the Justice Department's January 2025 civil suit over premerger-notification filings for 2021–2022 deals: a KKR subsidiary will pay $250 million once the court approves the final judgment, and the Antitrust Division closed its related investigations. KKR said in its 8-K that outside law firms will reimburse the penalty, so it expects no financial impact, and that it disagrees with the Division's description of the matter.

Equity-based compensation. KKR excludes equity-based comp from adjusted net income. This is standard for the peer group, but it is a real economic cost borne by shareholders through dilution; investors comparing KKR's ANI multiple to a conventional company's P/E should mentally haircut it.

M&A. The Arctos Partners acquisition added $16 billion of AUM at closing (Q3 release), concentrated in private equity, and therefore inflates the private-equity AUM growth rate (+15% YoY at Q3'26) relative to organic growth. Adjust for it when extrapolating.

Buybacks. From June 30 through November 4, 2026 KKR spent $567 million buying back and retiring 6.2 million shares at an average of $92; on November 6 the board added $500 million to the program, leaving about $533 million available.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hot and often pauses; below 30 it has been beaten up and often bounces. It says nothing about whether the business is good.

Five-year trajectory. KKR closed November 2021 at $74.45 and November 13, 2026 at $98.05 — a price return of +31.7%, dividends excluded. Over the same window the S&P 500 (via SPY) went from $455.56 to $754.37, a price return of +65.6%. In other words, after one of the biggest round trips among large US financial stocks, KKR has returned about half what the market did, with more volatility. The path was anything but linear: a 2022 credit-and-rate drawdown to $43.00 (September 2022, -42% from the November 2021 close), a 2023–2024 surge that took the stock to an all-time monthly closing high of $167.07 in January 2025 (a +289% advance from the 2022 low), and then a 13-month slide to $87.68 in February 2026, -47.5% from the peak, as investors paid less for each dollar of earnings. The recovery since has been partial: +11.8% off that trough at the Nov 13 close, but still -41.3% below the January 2025 high (all monthly closes) and 32.3% below the 52-week intraday high of $144.84.

What actually changed. Almost nothing in the fundamentals explains the 2025–26 fall. FRE per share went from $4.13 (FY2025) to $4.96 over the twelve months to September 2026; AUM went from $743.9B at December 2025 to $804B at September 2026; the firm beat a three-year fundraising target six months early. What changed was the price the market is willing to pay for those earnings. CFO Robert Lewin said on the November 9 call that management sees "a very big disconnect between perception of our industry and our share price" — and KKR has been buying back stock on that view.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operating business keeps growing while the price the market pays for it has roughly halved. LTM FRE of $4.5B (+26%), TOE of $5.9B (+23%) and ANI of $5.4B (+18%), with per-share records on all three in Q3; AUM is $804B (+11%), 43% of it perpetual; $146B of uncalled commitments converts to fees on KKR's schedule; and the $300B three-year fundraising target was beaten six months early. At $98.05 the stock trades at ~13.6x consensus FY2027 earnings with a 0.58 PEG (StockAnalysis) — cheaper than Blackstone and Ares on StockAnalysis's forward multiples. The USI sale, expected to close in Q4, brings a large one-time gain; management is buying back stock below every analyst target, and three options are not in the price: Strategic Holdings scaling toward $1.1B by 2030, K-Series compounding from a $45B base, and Global Atlantic's growing annuity sales.

Bear Case

KKR has concentrated its growth in the one asset class the market has decided to distrust. Credit is the largest AUM bucket at $337B, and Global Atlantic holds $170B of credit AUM of its own — so a private-credit downturn hits fees, spread income and carry simultaneously. The evidence is already visible: direct-lending fundraising at a near-three-year low in Q1, $620B+ of leveraged maturities in 2026–27, record retail redemptions across the peer group. Meanwhile, outside the one-off USI sale, only about $600M of gains and fees are in view against $9.7B of unrealized carry; GAAP earnings are so noisy that many investors cannot value them; and a 1.71 beta means the stock tends to fall harder than the market in any credit-driven sell-off. Over five years the stock has returned about half the S&P 500's price gain for more than market risk.

01
Private-credit deterioration is a triple-count exposure. Unlike a pure-play manager, KKR earns fees on credit AUM, spread income on Global Atlantic's credit book ($170B of credit AUM at September 30, 2026), and carry on credit funds. A rise in defaults or a spread-widening event does not diversify across those three lines — it correlates them. Watch direct-lending fundraising (already at a near-three-year low of $10.7B in Q1'26) and the $620B+ 2026–27 maturity wall.
02
The realization window may stay shut longer than the balance sheet can wait. $9.7B of gross unrealized carry is an asset only if it converts. Excluding the USI sale, KKR's visibility on gains and fees over the next couple of quarters is about $600M, against ~29,000 buyout-owned companies industry-wide queued for exit. A long stretch without exits holds down distributable earnings, starves fund investors of the distributions that pay for their next commitments, and keeps the market paying little for performance fees.
03
The retail/wealth growth story is unproven under stress. K-Series is up ~55% YoY to $45B and is a core plank of the growth narrative, but semi-liquid vehicles have never been tested through a genuine credit cycle. Peers with larger retail books have already seen record redemption requests. If the channel proves procyclical — inflows in calm markets, gates in stressed ones — a meaningful share of KKR's projected FRE growth does not arrive.
  Industry Commentary
The Migration of Capital from Public to Private Markets
EMERGING TAILWIND
This is the structural thesis underneath every alternative manager, and it has not broken. Preqin projects global alternatives AUM reaching $32 trillion by 2030, with infrastructure alone approaching $3 trillion. The drivers are durable: fewer companies choosing to be public, pension and sovereign funds needing returns that public beta cannot deliver, banks retreating from balance-sheet lending under capital rules, and — newest and most important — the opening of the individual-investor channel through semi-liquid vehicles. KKR's own numbers show the pull-through: $116B raised in the twelve months to September 2026, $305B from the start of 2024 to mid-2026 against a $300B three-year goal, and K-Series at $45B. The caution is that a lasting tailwind and a good entry price are different things; the same thesis was used to justify 40x FRE in January 2025.
Private Credit: Fastest-Growing Asset Class, Loudest Alarm Bell
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Private credit is forecast to more than double to $4.5 trillion by 2030, with semi-liquid and evergreen structures accounting for roughly $1.4 trillion of that. But the near-term data has turned: global private-credit fundraising was $49.9B in Q1 2026 and direct lending collapsed to $10.7B, the weakest quarter in nearly three years. Regulators are increasing scrutiny of lending exposure and systemic risk, and more than $620 billion of high-yield bonds and leveraged loans mature in 2026–2027 into a higher-rate environment. For KKR specifically this is not a side business: credit and liquid strategies are the largest AUM bucket at $337B, and Global Atlantic held a further $170B of credit AUM at September 30, 2026. This is the single dynamic most likely to determine the next 24 months of the share price.
The Exit Drought and the Two Multiples — Bull vs Bear
ONGOING HEADWIND
The market does not value an alternative manager with one multiple; it values two earnings streams separately. Fee-related earnings — recurring, contracted, high-margin (70% at KKR over the last twelve months) — are treated like a subscription business and historically command a premium multiple. Performance fees and carried interest — lumpy, market-dependent, and currently held as $9.7B of unrealized marks — are valued at a fraction of that. The 2025–26 fall was essentially the market valuing the second bucket at close to nothing while paying less for the first. At $98.05 KKR trades at roughly 19.8x LTM FRE per share ($4.96), 15.2x LTM TOE per share ($6.46) and 16.6x LTM ANI per share ($5.92) — against a January 2025 peak that priced 40.5x FY2025 FRE per share and an asset-management industry weighted-average P/E of 25.9x (StockAnalysis). Bull implication: you are buying a recurring earnings stream that grew 26% over the last twelve months at a discount to the index, with the carry thrown in free. Bear implication: if the exit window stays shut beyond one-off sales like USI, that carry never converts, FRE growth slows as fundraising gets harder to repeat, and about 20x FRE turns out to be the fair price for a business that rises and falls with the deal cycle rather than one that grows steadily. Roughly 29,000 sponsor-owned companies are waiting to be sold; the reopening of that queue is the event both cases hinge on.
