01 Company Overview

Kimberly-Clark makes disposable paper and personal-care staples — Huggies, Pull-Ups, Kotex, Depend, Poise, Kleenex, Scott, Cottonelle — and sells them through mass retailers, grocers and e-commerce. Until November 2, 2026 it was that company alone: fiscal 2025 net sales from continuing operations were $16.45 billion, down 2.1% YoY, on a base reshaped when the International Family Care and Professional business went into a joint venture with Suzano. Since the close it also owns Kenvue — the Johnson & Johnson consumer-health spin-off behind Tylenol, Band-Aid, Neutrogena, Listerine and Aveeno. At a $66.5 billion market capitalization (613.7 million shares × $108.43, our calculation) the company ranks #184 in the S&P 500.

The company announced the deal on November 3, 2025, in cash and stock at an enterprise value (shares plus debt) of about $48.7 billion, and it closed on November 2, 2026. Each Kenvue share became $3.50 in cash plus 0.14625 Kimberly-Clark shares. The 8-K filed that day says about 281.1 million Kimberly-Clark shares were delivered or owed to Kenvue holders; the 10-Q puts the cash at roughly $6.7 billion, funded from cash on hand, new bond issues ($4.9 billion of notes sold October 29) and commercial paper (short-term borrowing). Kenvue's own $7.0 billion of notes came with it; $6.38 billion of them were swapped for new Kimberly-Clark notes on November 4 (8-Ks of October 29 and November 4). Kenvue holders now own about 46% of the company (281.1M of 613.7M shares, our arithmetic), so the share count has nearly doubled from 332.6 million. The stock has gone from $143.76 in May 2025 to $108.43 — it fell after the announcement, reached its lowest month-end close of the window, $96.47, in March 2026 (the lowest daily close was $93.05 on April 7) and has recovered part of the way since. Two questions sit in every line below: is the legacy tissue-and-diaper business, whose China diaper sales were hit this year, worth this price, and can management make the Kenvue integration earn its cost?

The durable advantage is mundane and real: converting pulp and polymer into branded absorbent products at a cost per unit that a private-label challenger cannot easily match, then defending shelf position with brands parents and caregivers reach for without thinking. The profit lines show it better than the revenue lines: through nine months adjusted operating profit rose 3.0% to $2,166M while net sales rose 1.0%, and adjusted gross margin was 37.4% (flat). The third quarter shows the limit: adjusted gross margin slipped to 35.5%, 1.3 percentage points lower, on what the company calls unfavorable pricing net of cost inflation and supply-chain investments, and adjusted operating profit fell 0.9%. A business that can grow nine-month profit through a Chinese reputational shock and retailers trimming inventories has a real cost advantage; what it lacks is pricing power (net price, meaning prices after promotions, fell 1.1% company-wide in Q3 and 1.8% in North America). The moat protects margin, not growth.

Integration and debt
The deal is done, so the risk has moved from "will it close" to "will it pay". The cash portion of about $6.7 billion came partly from $4.9 billion of new notes and commercial paper, Kenvue's $7.0 billion of notes came too, and the company expects $3.1 billion of integration charges. The share price, up from $98.48 on September 10 to $108.43, shows the market's view has improved but not reversed: it is still 24.6% below the May 2025 high of $143.76.
Tylenol litigation and regulatory overhang
The acquired company's flagship brand sits inside an acetaminophen-safety controversy. Kenvue's 10-Q says it cannot yet reasonably estimate the likelihood or size of any liability; that exposure now belongs to Kimberly-Clark shareholders.
China and retailer power
The China diaper allegations cost about 2.7 percentage points of organic sales in Q3 and about 1 point for the full year (company outlook). The company says independent testing by a government-certified third party confirmed the products' quality and safety. At home, Kimberly-Clark exited its own U.S. private-label diaper business, a deliberate sales sacrifice.
Input-cost and energy exposure
The Q3 release blames the lower adjusted gross margin on pricing net of cost inflation and on supply-chain investments, partly offset by productivity savings, and cites higher input costs in International Personal Care. Raw materials and energy are named among the company's risk factors.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paid a 4.72% dividend yield by a company with 54 consecutive years of dividend increases, 28.6% return on invested capital before Kenvue and a productivity engine that grew nine-month adjusted operating profit 3.0% on 1.0% sales growth. The deal is done and cleared, management attributes the China disruption to false allegations and cites independent testing that backed its products, and the combined company owns Huggies, Kleenex, Tylenol, Band-Aid and Neutrogena with a $2.1 billion synergy target set when the deal was announced. Piper Sandler raised its target to $137 on November 6 and RBC holds $162; the average target is $118.53.

Bear Case

The legacy business is shrinking in the quarter that mattered (Q3 sales −0.4%, International Personal Care operating profit −32.7%), and management's own outlook calls for adjusted EPS attributable to Kimberly-Clark to decline by a low single-digit percentage in 2026 at constant currency, before Kenvue. Into that, the company has issued about 281 million shares, paid about $6.7 billion in cash and taken on a $3.1 billion integration bill, plus the Tylenol lawsuits and a forced sale of European tampon brands. The share price is still 24.6% below its May 2025 high, and 8.2% of shares (about 15% of the pre-deal count) are sold short.

01
Kenvue integration and Tylenol liability. A $48.7 billion transformation executed while the core business is shrinking, with an inherited acetaminophen-safety controversy: a federal appeals court sent the cases back to the lower court in July 2026 (Kenvue 10-Q) and Texas has sued over Tylenol marketing. If the synergies slip or the lawsuits turn into large payments, the dilution is permanent and the benefit is not.
02
Dividend coverage on a bigger share count. Through nine months the company paid $1.27 billion of dividends against $1.37 billion of operating cash flow less capital spending (our arithmetic from the release; operating cash flow includes discontinued operations). Paying $5.12 a share on about 613.7 million shares implies roughly $3.1 billion a year (our arithmetic), before $2.5 billion of cash integration charges. A 54-year streak is an asset management will defend hard, which is exactly what makes a cut, if it came, so damaging.
03
China and input costs together. The China diaper disruption is guided to cost about a point of organic sales for 2026, while input-cost inflation and promotional pressure on net price (down 1.8% in North America in Q3) continue. Productivity has covered the gap so far; it is not guaranteed to keep doing so.

Industry Commentary

Consumer trade-down and retailer power
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Net price, meaning prices after promotions, was −1.8% in North America and −1.1% company-wide in Q3 2026. North America still grew organic sales 0.4% on 2.0% higher volume, but company-wide organic sales fell 1.3%. Kimberly-Clark's response — exiting its own U.S. private-label diaper business — is a bet that the branded premium survives the trade-down cycle. The margin data supports it so far; the revenue data does not.
Pulp, resin and energy costs
ONGOING HEADWIND
Absorbent products turn commodities into branded goods: fluff pulp, superabsorbent polymer, resin, energy and freight. Adjusted gross margin fell 1.3 percentage points to 35.5% in Q3, which the company puts down to pricing net of cost inflation and to supply-chain investments. The 2022 experience is the cautionary case: gross margin fell to 30.8% (on the larger company before the Suzano deal) when costs ran ahead of price. Today's 37.3% trailing margin is measured on the smaller continuing business, so the two are not directly comparable.
Staples consolidation into health & wellness — bull vs bear
NOW IN PROGRESS
The strategic logic of Kenvue is that low-growth household paper needs a higher-margin, higher-growth adjacency, and that scale in consumer health buys shelf leverage against Procter & Gamble and Unilever. Bull framing: a $2.1 billion synergy target against a $48.7 billion price and a combined portfolio spanning care from birth to old age. Bear framing: large consumer mergers have a mixed record, this one issued about 46% of the company, required the sale of tampon brands in Europe and imports a product-safety controversy into a business that shareholders valued for predictability. The share price — $143.76 in May 2025, $108.43 today — records which framing the market holds.
