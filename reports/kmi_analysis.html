01 Company Overview

Kinder Morgan owns and operates energy infrastructure across North America — natural gas transmission and gathering pipelines, refined-products and crude pipelines, liquids and bulk terminals, and a carbon-dioxide business that both floods mature oilfields and, increasingly, sells CO₂ handling as a service. FY2025 revenue was $16.94B, up 12.2% from $15.10B in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through Q2 2026 is $17.96B, up 12.5%. At a $69.92B market capitalisation the company ranks roughly #370 globally. It employs 11,028 people — a striking number against $17.96B of revenue, and a fair first clue about what kind of business this is.

The central point of this page is that KMI is not an earnings story and should not be read as one. It is a contracted-cash-flow instrument with a dividend attached. Almost the entire income statement is the output of long-term, largely fee-based transportation and storage contracts on assets that cost decades and billions to replicate. So the numbers that matter are Adjusted EBITDA, the leverage ratio against it, the dividend and its coverage, and the size of the project backlog. The live debate is whether the two demand shocks now hitting natural gas — LNG (liquefied natural gas — gas chilled to liquid for seaborne export, which requires enormous volumes of pipeline "feedgas" delivered to Gulf Coast terminals) and data-centre power load — convert into contracted backlog fast enough to matter. Section 02 and the Industry Commentary quantify exactly what the company does and does not disclose about that.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
IRREPLACEABLE RIGHTS-OF-WAY FERC-REGULATED TARIFFS LONG-TERM FEE CONTRACTS LNG FEEDGAS POSITION HOUSTON SHIP CHANNEL HUB PERMIT & SITING BARRIERS SELF-FUNDED BACKLOG

The moat is the right-of-way, not the pipe. Steel is a commodity; a continuous, permitted corridor from the Appalachian or Permian producing basins to a Gulf Coast liquefaction terminal or a power plant is not, and in most of the United States it can no longer be assembled at any price. That is why a pipeline network built over decades throws off returns that look like a utility's and a balance sheet that looks like a utility's. Layered on top are long-term, largely fee-based contracts — Executive Chairman Richard Kinder's framing in the Q2 2026 release is that a "fee-based business model" and "long-term contracts with financially strong customers" produce cash flows stable enough that KMI can "fund virtually all project backlog internally." The numbers support that claim: $978M of free cash flow in Q2 2026 after $982M of capital expenditure, and $1,665M in the first six months against $1,315M of dividends paid. The company is building a $9.6B backlog out of operating cash while covering the dividend — no equity issuance required, which is the single most important structural difference between the KMI of 2026 and the KMI of 2015.

What the moat is not is protection against the demand mix changing. A pipeline is a fixed corridor between two fixed points. If the gas wants to go somewhere else, the moat becomes a stranded asset.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Leverage is the governing constraint. Net debt of $32,027M against Adjusted EBITDA gives 3.6× net debt / Adjusted EBITDA at Q2 2026 — inside the company's stated ~3.5–4.0× comfort zone, and better than the 3.8× carried in the 2026 budget. But it means total debt of $32.43B sits against $91M of cash. There is no cash cushion; the cushion is the contract book. Any sustained EBITDA shortfall raises the ratio mechanically, and the ratio is what disciplines both the dividend and the backlog.
▸
Dividend growth is 2%, not a growth rate. The Q2 2026 declared dividend of $0.2975 per share ($1.19 annualised) was a 2% increase year over year, and per-share dividends have crept from $1.08 (FY2021) to $1.17 (FY2025) — roughly 2% a year for five years, below most measures of inflation over that span. The yield is 3.79%. An investor buying this for income is buying a high current yield with a real-terms flat coupon, not a compounding one.
▸
Commodity exposure is small but real, and it is currently flattering the numbers. CO₂/ETV grew 42.8% on SACROC oil production and higher prices, and Products Pipelines grew 17.3% partly on higher commodity prices while its volumes fell. Together those two segments are 23.5% of segment EBDA. The "pure toll road" description of KMI is directionally right and quantitatively incomplete.
▸
Backlog concentration in one demand story. 92% of the $9.6B backlog is natural gas and more than 60% supports power generation and local distribution company demand. That is excellent if the electricity-load forecasts hold. It also means the company has aimed nearly its entire growth capital at a single thesis, at a moment when that thesis is the consensus of every midstream operator, utility and hyperscaler simultaneously. Consensus theses attract capital, and capital compresses returns.

Notable One-Off Items

There is no single large non-recurring charge distorting this period. There are, however, three items that make the reported growth look better than the underlying run-rate.

Commodity price lift in two segments. CO₂/ETV EBDA rose 42.8% with SACROC oil production up 15% "driven by higher commodity prices and volumes," and Products Pipelines EBDA rose 17.3% helped by "higher commodity prices" despite refined-product volumes falling 5% and crude/condensate volumes falling 16%. Strip commodity assistance out and the quarter is a Natural Gas Pipelines quarter — which grew a solid but less dramatic 8.5%.

$650–660M of projects entering service in Q2 2026. The Cumberland Project ($235M on TGP, 245,000 Dth/d to a TVA gas-fired power plant in Tennessee), the Hiland Express / Double H NGL conversion ($165M) and a Gulf Coast Express expansion ($160M KM-share, 570 MMcf/d from the Permian to South Texas). These are additive to EBITDA going forward and are the reason the backlog fell $0.5B sequentially — backlog declining because projects finished is a good decline, not a bad one.

Guidance is being beaten, and the company said so mid-year. Against a 2026 budget of $3.1B net income, $1.36 Adjusted EPS and $8.6B Adjusted EBITDA, KMI stated at Q2 that it expects to come in more than 12% favourable on Adjusted EPS and more than 5% favourable on Adjusted EBITDA, with year-end leverage at 3.6× rather than the budgeted 3.8×. A mid-year guidance raise of that size in a contracted business is unusual and is most of the reason the stock is where it is.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $16.75 to $31.40 on September 4, 2026, KMI returned +87.5% on price alone — a compound annual rate of about 13.4%. Add dividends and the picture improves substantially: declared dividends per share ran $1.08, $1.11, $1.13, $1.15 and $1.17 across FY2021–FY2025 plus $1.19 annualised now, so an investor who bought at $16.75 has collected roughly $5.80 per share of cash — about 35 percentage points of additional return on the entry price, for an approximate five-year total return in the +120% area (est., simple, no dividend reinvestment). That is a very good five years for an asset with a 0.55 beta.

The shape matters as much as the number. The first three years of the window went almost nowhere: from $16.75 in October 2021 the stock churned between roughly $16 and $19 through the whole of 2022 and 2023, bottoming at a monthly close of $16.11 in May 2023 — below where it started nineteen months earlier. Then something changed. From September 2024's $22.09, the stock went to $24.51 in October and $28.27 in November 2024 — a 28% move in two months. Every subsequent gain builds on that re-rating: the market stopped valuing KMI as a declining-demand toll road and started valuing it as infrastructure with a growth call option attached, as electricity-load and LNG forecasts were rewritten upward across the sector. The peak monthly close of the window is $33.53 in March 2026; the 52-week intraday high of $34.81 sits just above it. The last six months have been a consolidation, not a reversal: $33.53 → $31.08 in May → $31.40 now, with the July and August closes ($32.18 and $32.24) essentially flat.

Relative performance. KMI's 52-week price change is +17.47%. Over what appears to be the same window, sector peer Williams (WMB) shows a +28.7% change in market capitalisation, and ONEOK +29.3%, Enbridge's market cap and Energy Transfer's +22.7% — on those readings KMI has been a laggard within its own sector over the past year despite record operating results, which is consistent with a stock that re-rated early (late 2024) and is now waiting for the backlog to convert into EBITDA. A verified S&P 500 total return over the identical five-year window was not obtained from the sources fetched and is deliberately not stated; nor was a NASDAQ-100 comparison. What can be said without estimating anything: with a beta of 0.55, KMI is structurally a low-participation asset in an index rally and a low-drawdown asset in a selloff, and the flat 2022–2023 stretch followed by a demand-driven 2024–2026 re-rating is a pattern that owes far more to natural gas demand forecasts than to the equity market.

Q2 2026 delivered record net income, a record Q2 Adjusted EBITDA, +7% transport volumes and a mid-year guidance raise — and the stock is 6.4% below its March close. The market has already paid for the demand story; it now wants the backlog converted into EBITDA before it pays again.
Waiting on conversion
08 Summary & Key Risks

Read the business in the right order. Start with Adjusted EBITDA: $2,199M in Q2 2026, up 12% — a Q2 record — against a full-year budget of $8.6B that the company now says it will beat by more than 5%. Subtract cash interest and maintenance capital and you get free cash flow: $978M in the quarter after $982M of total capital expenditure, and $1,665M across the first six months. Against that, the company paid $1,315M of dividends in the same six months, at $0.2975 per share per quarter ($1.19 annualised). That is 1.27× coverage on free cash flow after all capex, and 2.6× on operating cash flow. Then the leverage test: net debt $32,027M, 3.6× Adjusted EBITDA, inside the ~3.5–4.0× target band and guided to stay at 3.6× at year-end against a 3.8× budget. And finally the growth engine: a $9.6B project backlog, 92% natural gas, expected to earn roughly 5.6× first-full-year project EBITDA — an implied first-year return near 18% on invested project capital.

That last number is the crux, and it needs to be set against the ROIC line. Kinder Morgan's ROIC on its whole asset base is 6.24%. New projects are being underwritten at roughly 18%. Both can be true: the legacy base carries goodwill and the accumulated cost of a decade-old acquisition spree, while new steel is being laid into a demand environment where shippers will sign long-term contracts at attractive rates because nobody can permit an alternative. The investment question reduces to a mix problem — how many billions of 18% capital can KMI deploy, and how fast, against a $100B+ enterprise value earning 6%? At $9.6B of backlog against a $102.26B enterprise value, the answer today is: enough to matter at the margin, not enough to transform the return profile. That is the honest ceiling on the bull case.

What is actually contracted, and what is not. Kinder Morgan describes itself as fee-based and long-term contracted, and Executive Chairman Richard Kinder's Q2 language — "long-term contracts with financially strong customers" funding "virtually all" of the backlog internally — is the company's own framing. A specific percentage of cash flow that is take-or-pay or fee-based was not disclosed in any source fetched for this report, and none is estimated here. What the segment data does show is the shape of it: Natural Gas Pipelines, 63.1% of segment EBDA, grew 8.5% on volumes (transport +7%, gathering +26%). Products Pipelines and CO₂/ETV, together 23.5% of segment EBDA, grew 17.3% and 42.8% respectively with the company explicitly crediting higher commodity prices in both. Terminals, 13.3%, grew 3.0% on rates. So the practical answer is that roughly two-thirds of the profit behaved like a contracted toll road last quarter and roughly a quarter behaved like a commodity business having a good year. That is why KMI does not trade like an E&P — but it is also why "zero commodity exposure" overstates the case.

The demand debate, quantified as far as disclosure allows. On LNG and data-centre power the company gives three verifiable facts and withholds a fourth. Verified: (i) >60% of the $9.6B backlog supports power generation and local distribution company demand; (ii) LNG deliveries on Tennessee Gas Pipeline were the first-named driver of the 7% transport volume increase, alongside Texas Intrastate demand, Mexico exports and Arizona power generation; (iii) two named projects tie directly to the thesis — the Cumberland Project (245,000 Dth/d to a new TVA gas-fired power plant, $235M, in service in Q2 2026) and the NGPL Amarillo Expansion (~550,000 Dth/d, fully subscribed, explicitly supporting "additional data center development" in the Texas Panhandle). Withheld: there is no separate disclosure of backlog dollars attributable to data centres, and no disclosed LNG feedgas volume or LNG share of backlog. The ">60% power generation and LDC" figure bundles data-centre load in with ordinary utility and residential distribution demand, and an investor cannot separate them from the published figures. Anyone quoting a data-centre backlog number for KMI is estimating; this page will not.

Valuation. On GAAP earnings the stock trades at 20.25× trailing and 21.37× forward — the forward multiple is higher because consensus 2026 EPS of $1.56 is essentially flat against $1.55 TTM. On enterprise value the arithmetic is more useful: $102.26B EV against a 2026 Adjusted EBITDA now tracking above $9.0B (the $8.6B budget plus the company's stated >5% beat) gives roughly 11.3× EV/Adjusted EBITDA (est., using management's own guidance revision). On yield, 3.79% with 1.27× free-cash-flow coverage and 2% annual growth. Pick your frame and the answer is consistent: this is priced as sound, well-covered, slowly-growing infrastructure — not cheap, not expensive, and dependent on the backlog converting for the next leg. The Street agrees, which is why eleven of twenty-two analysts sit on Hold and the lowest target on the board ($31) is the current price.

Bull Case

The demand step-up is real and showing up in volumes, not just in slides: transport +7%, gathering +26%, with LNG and power generation named as the drivers. A $9.6B backlog that is 92% natural gas and >60% power-and-LDC gets built at roughly 5.6× first-year project EBITDA — an ~18% return — and is funded entirely from internal cash flow at 3.6× leverage with no equity issuance. Management raised full-year guidance mid-year by more than 5% on EBITDA and more than 12% on Adjusted EPS. The rights-of-way cannot be replicated at any price, the beta is 0.55, and the dividend is covered 1.27× after every dollar of growth capex.

Bear Case

ROIC on the whole asset base is 6.24% — plausibly at or below the cost of capital — and $9.6B of high-return backlog against a $102B enterprise value moves that needle slowly. Dividend growth has been 2% a year for five years, below inflation, on a 76.75% payout of GAAP earnings. Net debt of $32.0B sits against $91M of cash. A quarter of segment EBDA grew on commodity prices while the underlying volumes in those segments fell. The stock has been flat for six months through a record operating year, and half the analyst panel is on Hold with the low target sitting at today's price — the demand re-rating already happened in late 2024, and it has to be earned back now.

Top 3 Risks

01
The power-demand thesis is priced in and concentrated. More than 60% of the backlog is aimed at power generation and LDC demand, and the stock re-rated 28% in two months in late 2024 on exactly that forecast. If electricity-load growth is revised down — or if data-centre buildouts shift toward on-site generation, nuclear, or regions KMI does not serve — the backlog's contracted portion still gets built, but the pipeline of future $10B-plus opportunities that supports the multiple thins out fast.
02
Leverage leaves no room for an EBITDA miss. At 3.6× net debt to Adjusted EBITDA with $91M of cash on a $32.4B debt load, the ratio is managed by the numerator staying put and the denominator growing. A demand shock, a major asset outage, or a sustained commodity downdraft in the CO₂ and Products segments pushes the ratio toward the top of the 3.5–4.0× band, and the response menu is short: slow the backlog, or freeze the dividend.
03
Return on capital, not growth, is the structural problem. A 6.24% ROIC on the installed base means the existing assets are earning something close to their cost of capital and not obviously more. The equity story depends on new projects at ~18% first-year returns diluting that average upward over many years. Any compression in project economics — competition for the same power and LNG corridors from Williams, Energy Transfer, TC Energy and ONEOK, all of whom announced expansions in this cycle — attacks the only part of the business that creates value at the margin.
  Industry Commentary
Electricity Load Growth Rewrote the Gas Thesis
EMERGING TAILWIND
For most of the 2010s the market priced US natural gas infrastructure for terminal decline. That assumption broke in late 2024, and KMI's chart shows exactly when: $22.09 in September 2024 to $28.27 in November, a 28% move in two months, on nothing the company itself did. What changed was the load forecast — data centres, electrification and industrial reshoring turned flat power demand into growing power demand, and gas-fired generation is what fills that gap on a five-year horizon. KMI's response is visible in the backlog: $9.6B, 92% natural gas, more than 60% supporting power generation and local distribution, at roughly 5.6× first-full-year project EBITDA. The Cumberland Project (245,000 Dth/d to a TVA plant) and the fully-subscribed NGPL Amarillo Expansion (~550,000 Dth/d, explicitly tied to data-centre development) are the two named, verified instances.
Every Midstream Operator Is Building the Same Thing
ONGOING HEADWIND
The scarcity that makes pipeline corridors valuable does not extend to the projects being sanctioned against this demand wave, and the whole sector is sanctioning them at once. Williams ($90.70B market cap, revenue +8.4%) closed a $5.5B Momentum Midstream acquisition; ONEOK ($60.16B, revenue +40.8%) bought Brazos Midland Basin assets for $4.425B and took a $9B minority equity investment from Apollo; Energy Transfer ($74.03B) and Enbridge ($109.43B) are expanding in parallel. KMI's own opportunity set is "more than $10 billion." When five well-capitalised operators chase the same power and LNG corridors, shippers gain negotiating leverage and the 5.6× project multiple — the ~18% first-year return the entire growth case rests on — is the first thing to compress. Watch project returns, not project counts.
The Disclosure Gap Is the Investable Question
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The two demand stories driving this stock — LNG export feedgas and data-centre power — are the two things Kinder Morgan quantifies least. The company gives one bundled figure (>60% of backlog for power generation and LDC demand), names LNG as a volume driver without a feedgas number, and discloses no data-centre-specific backlog dollars at all. Bull framing: the operating data corroborates the story anyway — transport volumes +7%, gathering +26%, a mid-year guidance raise of >5% on EBITDA, 3.6× leverage and 1.27× dividend coverage after all capex. You do not need the breakout when volumes and cash are both moving. Bear framing: the market has already paid for the demand thesis — that is what the late-2024 re-rating was — and is now being asked to pay again for a growth story whose most important components are not separately reported. Six months of flat price action through a record operating year suggests investors have noticed. The next real information event is the year-end backlog figure: if roughly $1B rolls into service in H2 2026 and the backlog still stands at or above $9.6B, the opportunity set is converting. If it drops toward $8.5B, it is not.
