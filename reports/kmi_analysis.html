01 Company Overview

Kinder Morgan owns and operates energy infrastructure across North America — natural gas transmission and gathering pipelines, refined-products and crude pipelines, liquids and bulk terminals, and a carbon-dioxide business that both floods mature oilfields and, increasingly, sells CO₂ handling as a service. FY2025 revenue was $16.94B, up 12.2% from $15.10B in FY2024; nine-month 2026 revenue was $13.84B, up 11.3% on the same nine months of 2025, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through Q3 2026 is $18.34B. At a $75.58B market capitalisation the company ranks #167 in the S&P 500. It employs 11,028 people — a striking number against $18.34B of revenue, and a fair first clue about what kind of business this is.

The central point of this page is that KMI is not an earnings story and should not be read as one. It is a business that collects fees under long contracts, with a dividend attached. Almost the entire income statement is the output of long-term, largely fee-based transportation and storage contracts on assets that cost decades and billions to replicate. So the numbers that matter are Adjusted EBITDA, the leverage ratio against it, the dividend and its coverage, and the size of the project backlog. The live debate is whether the two demand shocks now hitting natural gas — LNG (liquefied natural gas — gas chilled to liquid for seaborne export, which requires enormous volumes of pipeline "feedgas" delivered to Gulf Coast terminals) and data-centre power load — convert into contracted backlog fast enough to matter. The Q3 2026 print gave a large part of the answer: the backlog rose from $9.6B to $14.4B. Section 02 and the Industry Commentary set out what the company does and does not disclose about the demand behind it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
IRREPLACEABLE RIGHTS-OF-WAY FERC-REGULATED TARIFFS LONG-TERM FEE CONTRACTS LNG FEEDGAS POSITION HOUSTON SHIP CHANNEL HUB PERMIT & SITING BARRIERS $14.4B BACKLOG

The moat is the right-of-way, not the pipe. Steel is a commodity; a continuous, permitted corridor from the Appalachian or Permian producing basins to a Gulf Coast liquefaction terminal or a power plant is not, and in most of the United States it can no longer be assembled at any price. That is why a pipeline network built over decades throws off returns that look like a utility's and a balance sheet that looks like a utility's. Layered on top are long-term, largely fee-based contracts. In the Q3 2026 release Executive Chairman Richard Kinder credits the company's long-standing business model for the record results, and CEO Kim Dang highlights the backlog: $14.4B, including the largest natural gas project backlog in company history at $13.2B.

The cash numbers are tighter than the backlog headline. Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital spending) was $545M in Q3 2026 after $1,045M of capital expenditure, and $2,210M in the first nine months against $1,984M of dividends paid. In the quarter alone, free cash flow fell $120M short of the $665M of dividends paid. In August the company issued $1.15B of 5.55% senior notes due 2036 and $600M of 6.15% senior notes due 2056, with the proceeds going to repay commercial paper and maturing debt and for general purposes. So the backlog is being built out of operating cash and the bond market together, while the dividend is covered over nine months but not in every quarter.

What the moat is not is protection against the demand mix changing. A pipeline is a fixed corridor between two fixed points. If the gas wants to go somewhere else, the moat becomes a stranded asset.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Leverage is the governing constraint. Net debt of $32,229M against Adjusted EBITDA gives 3.5× net debt / Adjusted EBITDA at September 30, 2026, which the company describes as the low end of its targeted range (3.5–4.5×, per its December 2025 budget release), and below the 3.8× it carried at the end of 2025. Cash is $918M against about $33.2B of debt ($1,726M short-term and $31,445M long-term). A $14.4B backlog to build means that ratio will be tested as spending peaks; it is what disciplines both the dividend and the backlog.
▸
Dividend growth is 2%, not a growth rate. The Q3 2026 declared dividend of $0.2975 per share ($1.19 annualised) was a 2% increase on the $0.2925 of Q3 2025, and per-share dividends have crept from $1.08 (FY2021) to $1.17 (FY2025) — roughly 2% a year for five years, below most measures of inflation over that span. The yield is 3.51%. An investor buying this for income is buying a high current yield with a real-terms flat coupon, not a compounding one.
▸
Commodity exposure is small but real. The CO₂ segment grew 35.3% on adjusted segment EBDA, on oil and NGL volumes and, for NGLs, a higher price, and Products Pipelines grew 8.7% while its volumes fell. Together those two segments are 21.6% of adjusted segment EBDA. The "pure toll road" description of KMI is directionally right and quantitatively incomplete.
▸
Backlog concentration in one demand story. $13.2B of the $14.4B backlog is natural gas (92%) and more than 75% supports power generation and local distribution company demand, up from more than 60% of a smaller backlog a quarter ago. That is excellent if the electricity-load forecasts hold. It also means the company has aimed nearly its entire growth capital at a single thesis, at a moment when that thesis is the consensus of every midstream operator, utility and hyperscaler simultaneously. Consensus theses attract capital, and capital compresses returns.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 close of $15.46 to $33.94 on October 26, 2026, KMI returned +119.5% on price alone (closing prices, dividends not included) — a compound annual rate of about 17.4% over 4.9 years. Dividends come on top: the declared dividend per share has run $1.08, $1.11, $1.13, $1.15 and $1.17 across FY2021–FY2025 and is $1.19 annualised now. That is a very good five years for an asset with a 0.58 beta.

The shape matters as much as the number. The first three years of the window went almost nowhere: after the November 2021 low of $15.46, the stock churned between roughly $16 and $20 through the whole of 2022 and 2023, with a monthly close of $16.11 in May 2023. Then something changed. From September 2024's $22.09, the stock went to $24.51 in October and $28.27 in November 2024 — a 28% move in two months. Every subsequent gain builds on that re-rating. The usual reading is that the market stopped valuing KMI as a declining-demand toll road and started valuing it as infrastructure with a growth option attached, as electricity-load and LNG forecasts were rewritten upward across the sector. The highest month-end close in the window is $33.53 in March 2026; the 52-week intraday high of $34.81 sits above it. The stock then fell to $31.08 in May, rose to $32.18 and $32.24 in July and August, and closed September at $30.15, 10.1% below March's close.

October 2026. The rebound came mostly before the print. From the September 30 close of $30.15 the stock reached $33.57 by October 20, a gain of 11.3%, the day before the Q3 release. After the release it closed at $33.97 on October 21 (the release came after the close), $33.72 on October 22 (−0.74%), $34.43 on October 23 (+2.1%) and $33.94 on October 26. KMI closed October 26 about 2.5% below its 52-week intraday high of $34.81 and 32.6% above the 52-week low of $25.60. With a beta of 0.58 (a measure of how much a stock moves with the market), KMI tends to rise less than the market in rallies and fall less in selloffs.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Read the business in the right order. Start with Adjusted EBITDA: $2.18B in Q3 2026, up 9%, and $6.918B for nine months (+13%), against a full-year budget of $8.6B that the company now says it will beat by more than 6%. Operating cash flow minus all capital spending gives free cash flow: $545M in the quarter after $1,045M of capital expenditure, and $2,210M across the first nine months. Against that, the company paid $1,984M of dividends in the same nine months, at $0.2975 per share per quarter ($1.19 annualised). That is 1.11× coverage on free cash flow after all capex, and 2.5× on operating cash flow; in Q3 alone dividends ($665M) exceeded free cash flow ($545M). Then the leverage test: net debt $32,229M, 3.5× Adjusted EBITDA, at the low end of the company's 3.5–4.5× target range and guided to 3.6× at year-end against a 3.8× budget. And finally the growth engine: a $14.4B project backlog, $13.2B of it natural gas; the $13.7B of it backed by primarily take-or-pay contracts is expected to earn roughly 5.7× first-full-year project EBITDA, an implied first-year EBITDA return near 17.5% on project capital (1 ÷ 5.7).

That last number is the crux, and it needs to be set against the ROIC line. Kinder Morgan's ROIC on its whole asset base is 6.28%. New projects are being underwritten at roughly 17.5%. Both can be true: the legacy base carries goodwill and the accumulated cost of a decade-old acquisition spree, while new steel is being laid into a demand environment where shippers will sign long-term contracts at attractive rates because nobody can permit an alternative. The investment question reduces to a mix problem — how many billions of 17.5% capital can KMI deploy, and how fast, against an existing asset base earning about 6% on invested capital? At $14.4B of backlog the answer is now: a larger slice than in July ($9.6B), still not enough to transform the return profile on its own. That is the honest ceiling on the bull case.

What is actually contracted, and what is not. Kinder Morgan describes itself as fee-based and long-term contracted. What the segment data does show is the shape of it: Natural Gas Pipelines, 64.7% of adjusted segment EBDA, grew 6.1% on volumes (transport +3%, gathering +14%). Products Pipelines and CO₂, together 21.6% of adjusted segment EBDA, grew 8.7% and 35.3% respectively, the first with volumes falling and the second on higher oil and NGL volumes and a higher NGL price. Terminals, 13.7%, grew 14.6%. So the practical answer is that roughly two-thirds of the profit behaved like a contracted toll road last quarter and roughly a fifth behaved like a commodity business having a good year. That is why KMI does not trade like an E&P — but it is also why "zero commodity exposure" overstates the case.

The demand debate, quantified as far as disclosure allows. On LNG and data-centre power the company gives verifiable facts and no more. Verified: (i) more than 75% of the $14.4B backlog supports power generation and local distribution company demand; (ii) LNG deliveries on Tennessee Gas Pipeline and Texas Intrastate demand were the primary drivers of the 3% transport volume increase; (iii) new large projects in the backlog include the Southeast Connector and the Permian Link, and the earlier Cumberland Project (245,000 Dth/d to a new TVA gas-fired power plant, in service in Q2 2026) and NGPL Amarillo Expansion (~550,000 Dth/d, fully subscribed, tied in the Q2 release to "additional data center development" in the Texas Panhandle). The Q3 release says one new project will serve power generation for an A-rated hyperscaler, but gives no separate disclosure of backlog dollars attributable to data centres, and no LNG feedgas volume or LNG share of backlog. The ">75% power generation and LDC" figure bundles data-centre load in with ordinary utility and residential distribution demand. Anyone quoting a data-centre backlog number for KMI is estimating; this page will not.

Valuation. The stock trades at 21.08× trailing GAAP earnings and 21.76× forward on analysts' consensus 2026 EPS (stockanalysis.com) — the forward multiple is higher because consensus 2026 EPS of $1.56 is below the $1.61 TTM figure. On enterprise value the arithmetic is more useful: $108.06B EV (market cap of $75.58B plus $32.48B of net debt per stockanalysis.com) against last-twelve-months Adjusted EBITDA of $9.189B gives roughly 11.8× EV/Adjusted EBITDA (calc.). On yield, 3.51% with 1.11× free-cash-flow coverage over nine months and 2% annual growth. Pick your frame and the answer is consistent: this is priced as sound, slowly-growing infrastructure with a growing backlog — not cheap, not expensive, and dependent on the backlog converting for the next leg. The Street is split: 13 of 24 analysts rate it Buy, 11 Hold, and the average target ($37.27, read October 27) is 9.8% above the price.

Bull Case

The demand step-up is real and showing up in the backlog: $14.4B, up from $9.6B in one quarter, $13.2B of it natural gas and more than 75% of the total supporting power generation and distribution-company demand, with the $13.7B take-or-pay part expected at roughly 5.7× first-year project EBITDA (about a 17.5% first-year EBITDA return). Management raised full-year guidance again, to more than 6% above budget on EBITDA and more than 13% on Adjusted EPS, with leverage at the low end of its range at 3.5×. The rights-of-way cannot be replicated at any price, the beta is 0.58, and the dividend was covered 1.11× over nine months after every dollar of growth capex.

Bear Case

ROIC on the whole asset base is 6.28% — plausibly at or below the cost of capital — and a bigger backlog means a larger bill: Q3 free cash flow ($545M) did not cover the quarter's dividends ($665M). Dividend growth has been 2% a year for five years, below inflation, on a 73.86% payout of GAAP earnings. Net debt is $32.2B. Transport volumes grew only 3% in the quarter, down from 7% in Q2. The stock is back near its 52-week high at 21× trailing earnings, 11 of 24 analysts are on Hold, and consensus 2027 EPS ($1.55) is below the TTM figure ($1.61): the demand re-rating has been paid for, and the backlog now has to earn it.

Top 3 Risks

01
The power-demand thesis is priced in and concentrated. More than 75% of the backlog is aimed at power generation and LDC demand, and the stock re-rated 28% in two months in late 2024 on exactly that forecast. If electricity-load growth is revised down — or if data-centre buildouts shift toward on-site generation, nuclear, or regions KMI does not serve — the backlog's contracted portion still gets built, but the pipeline of future opportunities that supports the multiple thins out fast.
02
Leverage and funding leave little room for an EBITDA miss. At 3.5× net debt to Adjusted EBITDA with $32.2B of net debt, the ratio is managed by the numerator growing while a $14.4B backlog is built. Q3 free cash flow did not cover the dividend, and the company issued $1.75B of bonds in August. A demand shock, a major asset outage, or a sustained commodity downdraft in the CO₂ and Products segments pushes the ratio up through the company's 3.5–4.5× target range, and the response menu is short: slow the backlog, or freeze the dividend.
03
Return on capital, not growth, is the structural problem. A 6.28% ROIC on the installed base means the existing assets are earning something close to their cost of capital and not obviously more. The equity story depends on new projects at ~17.5% first-year returns diluting that average upward over many years. Any compression in project economics — competition for the same power and LNG corridors from other pipeline companies, such as Williams and ONEOK, both of which struck multibillion-dollar gas-asset deals in August–September 2026 — attacks the only part of the business that creates value at the margin.
  Industry Commentary
Electricity Load Growth Rewrote the Gas Thesis
EMERGING TAILWIND
For most of the 2010s the market priced US natural gas infrastructure for terminal decline. That assumption broke in late 2024, and KMI's chart shows exactly when: $22.09 in September 2024 to $28.27 in November, a 28% move in two months. The usual reading is that what changed was the load forecast — data centres, electrification and industrial reshoring turned flat power demand into growing power demand, and gas-fired generation is what fills that gap on a five-year horizon. KMI's response is visible in the backlog: $14.4B, $13.2B of it natural gas, more than 75% supporting power generation and local distribution, with the $13.7B take-or-pay part expected at roughly 5.7× first-full-year project EBITDA. The Cumberland Project (245,000 Dth/d to a TVA plant) and the fully-subscribed NGPL Amarillo Expansion (~550,000 Dth/d, tied to data-centre development) are named instances from the Q2 2026 release.
Every Midstream Operator Is Building the Same Thing
ONGOING HEADWIND
The scarcity that makes pipeline corridors valuable does not extend to the projects being sanctioned against this demand wave, and the whole sector is sanctioning them at once. Williams closed its roughly $5.5B purchase of Momentum Midstream's Haynesville business in September 2026 (LNG Industry); ONEOK agreed on August 31, 2026 to buy Brazos Midstream's Midland Basin assets for $4.425B, alongside a $9B minority equity investment from Apollo (Oil & Gas Journal). KMI's own backlog is now $14.4B. When several well-capitalised operators chase the same power and LNG corridors, shippers gain negotiating leverage and the ~5.7× project multiple — the ~17.5% first-year return the entire growth case rests on — is the first thing to compress. Watch project returns, not project counts.
The Disclosure Gap Is the Investable Question
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The two demand stories driving this stock — LNG export feedgas and data-centre power — are the two things Kinder Morgan quantifies least. The company gives one bundled figure (>75% of backlog for power generation and LDC demand), names LNG as a volume driver without a feedgas number, and the Q3 release gives no data-centre-specific backlog dollars. Bull framing: the operating data and the backlog corroborate the story anyway — a backlog up $4.7B in a quarter, a guidance raise of >6% on EBITDA, 3.5× leverage. You do not need the breakout when the backlog is moving. Bear framing: the market has already paid for the demand thesis — that is what the late-2024 re-rating was — and transport volume growth slowed from 7% to 3% between the second and third quarters. The next real information events are the year-end backlog figure and the 2027 budget: if the backlog holds at or above $14.4B after projects roll into service, the opportunity set is converting.
