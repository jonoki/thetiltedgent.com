01 Company Overview

Leidos is the company that runs a surprising amount of the US government's plumbing: intelligence-community software and cyber operations, the medical disability exams the Department of Veterans Affairs relies on, border-screening systems, missile-warning satellites and, since March 2026, power-grid engineering. It began in 1969 as Bob Beyster's employee-owned Science Applications Inc. in La Jolla, listed as SAIC in October 2006, renamed itself Leidos in 2013 when it spun off a smaller company that kept the SAIC name, and more than doubled in 2016 by merging with Lockheed Martin's Information Systems & Global Solutions business. The listed entity is the legal successor to the pre-2013 SAIC, so its price history runs unbroken — with a 1-for-4 reverse split on September 30, 2013, well outside this report's five-year window.

FY2025 revenue was $17.17B, up 3.07%, with GAAP diluted EPS of $11.14. Then 2026 happened to the share price, not to the income statement. The stock closed 2025 at $180.40 and June 2026 at $102.97 — a 43% fall in six months — as Jefferies (June 3) and Bank of America (June 17) downgraded it, the Defense Health Agency moved to buy electronic-health-record software directly rather than through Leidos as lead integrator, and management warned that Q2 would be the year's low point. Then the Q2 print on August 4 beat, guidance went up, and the stock rose 10.01% in a day. Q3, reported on November 3, beat again on profit (non-GAAP EPS $3.62, up 19%) and the EPS guidance went up a third time, but revenue of $4.63B grew only 0.6% without acquisitions and missed forecasts, and the CFO said 2027 is "not a bottom line growth year" — the stock fell 6.55% that day. At $135.68 it trades at 12.1x trailing earnings with a $49.6B backlog — 2.8 years of revenue already under contract. The debate is simple to state: is the Health business the start of a pattern of the government cutting out the integrator, or a one-segment problem inside a portfolio that is growing everywhere else?

Two big franchises. The first is VA medical disability exams through QTC, which, the CFO said on the Q2 call, "has a network of more than 15,000 providers, 90 clinics" and does 2.8 million examinations a year. This segment is the bear case in one card — the most profitable business Leidos owns, with its two anchors both being re-bid or re-shaped.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$49.6B contracted backlog Security clearances at scale Incumbency on mission systems QTC exam network (15,000 providers) Defense hardware IP (Dynetics) Fixed-price delivery track record

The moat in government services is incumbency. Nobody else holds the clearances, knows the undocumented systems, or can take over a live intelligence or health program without risking an outage the agency will be blamed for. That is why the backlog is 2.8 years deep. But incumbency protects you only until the customer decides the job should be done differently — and 2026 showed exactly that. The DHA did not fire Leidos from MHS GENESIS; it finished the contract "on time and under budget" and then decided to buy the software itself. The VA did not drop QTC; it added a fourth vendor and suspended incentive payments. Neither is a failure of delivery. Both are the customer changing the shape of the contract.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The government is cutting out the integrator in Health. Bank of America's June 2026 downgrade cited building pressure on a healthcare portfolio that had been a strength, as the DHA prioritises direct supplier relationships. Health is 23.1% of revenue at a 22.5% operating margin (26.8% a year earlier), and about 42% of segment operating income in Q3 ($240M of $578M), with the CFO guiding Q4 Health margins to the high teens. Its shrinkage hits profit far harder than revenue.
02
Fixed-price development risk. The administration wants "fixed-price outcome-based results," and management says it is "very comfortable" with that. But a schedule slip on a fixed-price Defense program is what caused the Q2 soft patch. On fixed-price work the contractor, not the customer, eats the overrun.
03
Budget timing. "The guidance assumes that the government is operating under a continuing resolution through the end of the year," the CFO said on the Q3 call; on the Q2 call he named Intelligence & Digital as the most exposed segment. Continuing resolutions delay new programs; they do not cancel them, but they push revenue into later quarters. The CFO also flagged "some delays in some decisions that didn't go our way" as a drag on Q4 and 2027 growth.
04
More leverage than it used to carry. After the ~$2.4B ENTRUST deal Leidos had $6.0B of debt against $630M of cash at October 2, 2026 (Q3 release); StockAnalysis, which counts leases, shows $6.56B and debt-to-equity of 1.26. Comfortable for a business with steady government cash flows — and Leidos still spent $504M on buybacks in Q3 — but it narrows the room for more while the Health question plays out.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported figures. Deals: ENTRUST (closed March 27, 2026) is why reported Q3 growth (+3.6%) runs ahead of organic (+0.6%), and Homeland's GAAP margin (7.9%) trails its non-GAAP margin (11.3%). From October 5 the SES businesses sit in a joint venture Leidos owns 41.5% of, which is why the FY2026 revenue range came down while the EPS range went up. FY2023: net income collapsed to $199M (EPS $1.44) before recovering to $1.25B in FY2024 — the five-year EPS line is not a smooth series, and the "+540%" FY2024 EPS growth figure is a rebound, not a trend. Cash conversion: Q2 free cash flow of $761M was 214% of net income ($356M), Q3's $476M was 115% ($413M) — lumpy quarters, not a run-rate; full-year operating cash flow guidance is about $1.90B. Buybacks: $504M in Q3, after only $66–72M in Q2 (the Q2 release and call used different figures).

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 4, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $87.91 to the November 4, 2026 settled close of $135.68, LDOS gained 54.3% on price, before dividends; the S&P 500 fund SPY gained 68.3% over the same dates. The endpoints hide the round trip. For about two years the stock went nowhere — it closed May 2023 at $78.06, below where it started. Then it ran: from $110.47 in January 2024 to $183.16 in October 2024, and after a pullback, to a month-end peak of $191.10 in November 2025. 2026 took almost all of that back — $102.97 by June, 46% below the peak — before the Q2 print recovered part of it. September fell 13.1%, October rose 17.4% into the Q3 print, and the print day took 6.55% off.

The 6-period monthly RSI fell to 15.2 in June 2026, the most oversold reading in the five-year series and well below the 27.0 of May 2023. It now reads 47.1 — neutral. The 10-month moving average is $136.69, about level with the price. On daily closes the 200-day average is $140.34 and the 50-day $129.31: the price is back above its 50-day average but still below its 200-day, so the longer trend has not turned.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 12.1x trailing earnings and ~10.7x FY2026 consensus earnings for a business with a $49.6B backlog (2.8 years of revenue), $28B of proposals awaiting decision and a ~12% free-cash-flow yield. The 2026 collapse was a Health problem priced as a company problem: three of four segments grew in Q3, Homeland by 32%. Management raised its EPS guidance for the third time this year and bought back $504M of stock in the quarter, saying it sees the shares as worth more. The Defense Tech pillar — munitions, missile-tracking payloads — is where the growth is; the CEO sees a path to a $1 billion-a-year munitions business by 2030. If Health merely stabilises after the VBA re-bid, the P/E has room to recover.

Bear Case

The bear case is that Health is not a one-off but a template. The customer paying the highest margins just decided to buy the software itself and added a fourth exam vendor; Health's margin fell to 22.5% in Q3 from 26.8% and is guided to the high teens in Q4. If other agencies follow the DHA and deal with suppliers directly, the integrator's margin is the thing that gets competed away. Meanwhile organic growth was 0.6% in Q3, growth is shifting toward fixed-price Defense work that has already slipped once, the first-round loss on the Navy's unmanned surface vessel shows the Defense Tech wins are not automatic, the CFO says 2027 is "not a bottom line growth year," and consensus EPS for FY2027 is 3.9% below FY2026. A low multiple on flat-to-falling earnings is not obviously cheap. Eleven of seventeen analysts say Hold.

Top 3 Risks

01
Health segment erosion. Down 11.7% in Q3 at a 22.5% operating margin (26.8% a year earlier), guided to the high teens in Q4, with the VBA exam contract heading to re-competition (current contracts likely extended through the first half of 2027, per the CEO) and MHS GENESIS on a bridge contract. The most profitable revenue in the company is the least secure.
02
Fixed-price execution. The growth businesses — munitions, vessels, satellites — increasingly run on fixed-price terms the administration favours. One schedule slip already made Q2 the guided low point of the year. A cost overrun lands directly on Leidos's margin.
03
Federal budget timing. Guidance assumes a continuing resolution through year-end; a longer one or a shutdown delays new programs and pushes revenue into later quarters, with Intelligence & Digital most exposed. Backlog does not turn into revenue until money is appropriated — funded backlog is $11.1B of the $49.6B.
Industry Commentary
Agencies buying direct — the integrator squeeze
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The MHS GENESIS decision is the clearest example this year of a federal customer deciding it no longer needs a prime integrator between itself and the commercial software vendor. Leidos delivered the program; the DHA then chose to procure software directly and keep Leidos on a sole-source bridge. Whether this spreads is the question for every services contractor with high-margin integration work. It is not yet visible in the other three segments — Intelligence & Digital, Homeland and Defense all grew in Q2 and Q3 — but it is precisely what BofA's downgrade priced in.

Fixed-price, outcome-based contracting
ONGOING SHIFT

Tom Bell says the administration is "very keen to have a fixed-price outcome-based results" model. For a contractor that can deliver, fixed-price work carries better margins than cost-plus; for one that slips, it carries the overrun. Leidos is leaning in — it is rolling out software tools aimed at fixed-price sales and pitching open-interface work ("Operation Jailbreak") that runs on non-Leidos hardware. The Jefferies downgrade framed the same shift from the other side: as StockStory reported it, Jefferies worried that margins could be hard to hold with US defense spending uncertain.

Defense tech and energy infrastructure
EMERGING TAILWIND

The growth pillars management names — Defense Tech, Energy Infrastructure and Cyber — are where the order book is filling: a Q2 Defense book-to-bill of 2.2x, $272M of missile-tracking payloads for the Space Development Agency in Q3, a munitions framework agreement, and grid engineering via ENTRUST. The bull framing is that Leidos is turning from a services company that happens to build some hardware into a defense-technology company with a services base. The bear framing is that this is a shift from a high-margin, low-risk Health business to lower-margin, higher-risk fixed-price hardware. Both can be true; the Q4 margins and the 2027 outlook due in February will show which dominates.
