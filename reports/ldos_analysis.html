01 Company Overview

Leidos is the company that runs a surprising amount of the US government's plumbing: intelligence-community software and cyber operations, the medical disability exams the Department of Veterans Affairs relies on, border-screening systems, missile-warning satellites and, since March 2026, power-grid engineering. It began in 1969 as Bob Beyster's employee-owned Science Applications Inc. in La Jolla, listed as SAIC in October 2006, renamed itself Leidos in 2013 when it spun off a smaller company that kept the SAIC name, and more than doubled in 2016 by merging with Lockheed Martin's Information Systems & Global Solutions business. The listed entity is the legal successor to the pre-2013 SAIC, so its price history runs unbroken — with a 1-for-4 reverse split on September 30, 2013, well outside this report's five-year window.

FY2025 revenue was $17.17B, up 3.07%, with GAAP diluted EPS of $11.14. Then 2026 happened to the share price, not to the income statement. The stock closed 2025 at $180.40 and June 2026 at $102.97 — a 43% fall in six months — as Jefferies (June 3) and Bank of America (June 17) downgraded it, the Defense Health Agency moved to buy electronic-health-record software directly rather than through Leidos as lead integrator, and management warned of a "soft patch" in Q2. Then the Q2 print on August 4 beat, guidance went up, and the stock rose 10.01% in a day. At $128.23 it trades at 12.0x trailing earnings with a $48.7B backlog — 2.8 years of revenue already under contract. The debate is simple to state: is the Health business the start of a pattern of the government cutting out the integrator, or a one-segment problem inside a portfolio that is growing everywhere else?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

$48.7B contracted backlog Security clearances at scale Incumbency on mission systems QTC exam network (15,000 providers) Defense hardware IP (Dynetics) Fixed-price delivery track record

The moat in government services is incumbency. Nobody else holds the clearances, knows the undocumented systems, or can take over a live intelligence or health program without risking an outage the agency will be blamed for. That is why the backlog is 2.8 years deep. But incumbency protects you only until the customer decides the job should be done differently — and 2026 showed exactly that. The DHA did not fire Leidos from MHS GENESIS; it finished the contract "on time and under budget" and then decided to buy the software itself. The VA did not drop QTC; it added a fourth vendor and suspended incentive payments. Neither is a failure of delivery. Both are the customer changing the shape of the contract.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The government is cutting out the integrator in Health. Bank of America's June 2026 downgrade cited pressure on the "once blooming" healthcare portfolio as the DHA prioritises direct supplier relationships. Health is 23.8% of revenue at a 23.4% operating margin — roughly 44% of segment operating income in Q2 ($254M of $572M). Its shrinkage hits profit far harder than revenue.
02
Fixed-price development risk. The administration wants "fixed-price outcome-based results," and management says it is "very comfortable" with that. But a schedule slip on a fixed-price Defense program is what caused the Q2 soft patch. On fixed-price work the contractor, not the customer, eats the overrun.
03
Budget timing. Management expects "a continuing resolution of some kind" at the October 1 fiscal year-end rather than a shutdown, and names Intelligence & Digital as the most exposed segment. Continuing resolutions delay new starts; they do not cancel them, but they push revenue right.
04
More leverage than it used to carry. The ~$2.4B ENTRUST deal took gross leverage to 2.5x and debt-to-equity to 1.23, with $6.58B of total debt against $748M of cash. Comfortable for a business with steady government cash flows — but it narrows the room for buybacks while the Health question plays out.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $96.13 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $128.23, LDOS gained 33.4% on price, before dividends. The endpoints hide the round trip. For two and a half years the stock went nowhere — it closed May 2023 at $78.06, below where it started. Then it ran: from $110.47 in January 2024 to $183.16 in October 2024, and after a pullback, to a month-end peak of $191.10 in November 2025. 2026 took almost all of that back — $102.97 by June, 46% below the peak — before the Q2 print recovered part of it.

The 10-month moving average sits at roughly $146.33, well above the price, and the 200-day average per StockAnalysis is $150.19 against a 50-day of $127.75. The short-term bounce is real; the longer trend has not turned.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 12.0x trailing earnings and ~10.6x forward for a business with a $48.7B backlog (2.8 years of revenue), $23B of proposals awaiting decision, a 2.2x book-to-bill in Defense, and a ~13% free-cash-flow yield. The 2026 collapse was a Health problem priced as a company problem: three of four segments grew in Q2, one by 32%. Management raised guidance twice. The Defense Tech pillar — munitions, unmanned vessels, missile-warning satellites — is where the growth is, and a Q4 production award on the medium USV would prove it. If Health merely stabilises after the VA re-bid, the multiple has room to move back toward its own five-year average.

Bear Case

The bear case is that Health is not a one-off but a template. The customer paying the highest margins just decided to buy the software itself and added a fourth exam vendor. If other agencies follow — "direct supplier relationships," in BofA's framing — the integrator's margin is the thing that gets competed away. Meanwhile growth is shifting toward fixed-price Defense work that has already slipped once, leverage has risen to 2.5x gross after ENTRUST, and consensus EPS growth is only 3.4% this year and 2.8% next. A low multiple on low growth is not obviously cheap. Ten of seventeen analysts say Hold.

Top 3 Risks

01
Health segment erosion. Down 7.6% in Q2 at a 23.4% operating margin, with the VA exam contract heading to re-competition (decisions expected early 2027) and MHS GENESIS on a bridge contract. The most profitable quarter of revenue in the company is the least secure.
02
Fixed-price execution. The growth businesses — munitions, vessels, satellites — increasingly run on fixed-price terms the administration favours. One schedule slip already produced a guided "soft patch." A cost overrun lands directly on Leidos's margin.
03
Federal budget timing. A continuing resolution (management's base case) or a shutdown at October 1 delays new starts and pushes revenue right, with Intelligence & Digital most exposed. Backlog does not turn into revenue until money is appropriated — funded backlog is $10.2B of the $48.7B.
Industry Commentary
Agencies buying direct — the integrator squeeze
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The MHS GENESIS decision is the clearest example this year of a federal customer deciding it no longer needs a prime integrator between itself and the commercial software vendor. Leidos delivered the program; the DHA then chose to procure software directly and keep Leidos on a sole-source bridge. Whether this spreads is the question for every services contractor with high-margin integration work. It is not yet visible in the other three segments — Intelligence & Digital, Homeland and Defense all grew in Q2 — but it is precisely what BofA's downgrade priced in.

Fixed-price, outcome-based contracting
ONGOING SHIFT

Tom Bell says the administration is "very keen to have a fixed-price outcome-based results" model. For a contractor that can deliver, fixed-price work carries better margins than cost-plus; for one that slips, it carries the overrun. Leidos is leaning in — it is rolling out software tools aimed at fixed-price sales and pitching open-interface work ("Operation Jailbreak") that runs on non-Leidos hardware. The Jefferies downgrade framed the same shift from the other side: margins "could be difficult to maintain due to uncertainty around U.S. defense spending."

Defense tech and energy infrastructure
EMERGING TAILWIND

The growth pillars management names — Defense Tech, Energy Infrastructure and Cyber — are where the order book is filling: Defense book-to-bill of 2.2x, missile-warning satellites under Golden Dome, a $1B munitions framework, and grid engineering via ENTRUST. The bull framing is that Leidos is turning from a services company that happens to build some hardware into a defense-technology company with a services base. The bear framing is that this is a shift from a high-margin, low-risk Health business to lower-margin, higher-risk fixed-price hardware. Both can be true; the Q3 and Q4 margin prints will show which dominates.
