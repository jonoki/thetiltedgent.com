01 Company Overview

Lennar is the second-largest US homebuilder by homes sold. It builds entry-level and move-up single-family homes across four regions, and runs a mortgage, title and insurance arm that finances most of its own buyers. It delivered 82,583 homes in fiscal 2025 (year to November 30) and is guiding to 80,000–81,000 in fiscal 2026. FY2025 revenue was $34.19B, down 3.54%, and net income was $2.06B, down 47.14%. Revenue barely moved while profit halved, and the explanation is one line of the income statement.

That line is gross margin on home sales. Lennar's strategy is to keep the factory running and price to whatever the market will pay. On the Q3 call management described it as "compromising margin in order to maintain volume." When mortgage rates stay high, "pricing to market" means incentives, mostly paying to buy the buyer's mortgage rate down. Incentives were about 14% of the sale price in Q4 FY2025 and were about 12% in Q3 FY2026. Gross margin on home sales went from 22.51% (total company, FY2024) to 15.8% in Q3 FY2026. The share price made the same trip, from a $186.10 month-end close in September 2024 to $78.08. Part of that drop is not a loss: in February 2025 Lennar spun off its land into Millrose Properties and handed shareholders half a Millrose share for every Lennar share (see Section 07). The live question is whether ~16% is the floor for margins, or just a pause before rates push them lower again.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

National scale (~80K homes/yr) Land-light: 98% of homesites controlled, not owned $80/sq ft direct cost 116-day cycle time In-house mortgage (83% capture) Low leverage (16.6% debt/capital)

A homebuilder has no brand premium to speak of. Its edge is cost and capital turns. Lennar's version is to own almost no land. On the Q3 call management said it controls about 476,000 homesites and owns about 11,800, roughly 2%. 86% of Q3 homes were delivered on land-bank land, meaning lots held by a third party and bought just in time. That turns a land-heavy business into something closer to a manufacturer: inventory turned 2.4 times, and homebuilding debt-to-total-capital is 16.6%. The weakness is that land-light is also margin-light. Option fees to land bankers are a carrying cost that owners of cheap, older land do not pay. A builder that prices "to market" to keep the factory full has chosen to absorb the whole downturn through price.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Volume over margin is a stated choice. Management said on the Q3 call that it is "compromising margin in order to maintain volume." That is a coherent strategy for a fixed-cost manufacturer. It also means that every rise in mortgage rates comes straight off gross margin. The CEO said the 30-year rate was about 6.8% at quarter end "and even higher since."
02
Demand is softening under the guide. Q3 new orders were 20,879, down 9% and just below the 21,000–22,000 range management had guided to. Full-year delivery guidance has come down from about 85,000 (December 2025) to 82,000–83,000 and now to 80,000–81,000.
03
Earnings keep missing. Q1 FY2026 revenue of $6.6B came in under the Street's $6.84B. On Q3 the CEO opened by saying EPS of $1.19 was "below expectations." Q4 FY2025, Q1 FY2026 and Q3 FY2026 each missed on profit or revenue, which undermines the forward guide.
04
Rate buydowns are pro-cyclical. The main incentive tool is buying down the buyer's mortgage rate. It gets more expensive exactly when rates rise, which is also when demand is weakest. The CFO acknowledged the cost is rising but did not quantify it.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"). Below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value: a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

How the Millrose spin-off affects this chart. On February 7, 2025 Lennar distributed about 80% of Millrose Properties (NYSE: MRP) to its shareholders: one Millrose share for every two Lennar shares. Millrose received $5.5B of Lennar's land and $1.0B of cash. The line above is the unadjusted price, so from February 2025 onward each point is the price of a Lennar share without that land. The fall from January 2025 ($131.24) to February 2025 ($119.63), −8.8%, is therefore partly value handed to shareholders as Millrose stock, not value lost. The Millrose price at distribution was not retrieved, so the two parts are not separated here. Anyone who received and kept the Millrose shares did better than this line shows. Digrin's "adjusted" column tries to correct for this, but its adjustment factor changes between the December 2024 and January 2025 rows, before the February distribution, and it also folds in dividends, so the unadjusted closes are used instead. Every change, return and RSI reading below that spans February 2025 includes this distortion.

With that caveat, from the September 2021 close of $92.99 to the September 21, 2026 close of $78.08, the share price is down 16.0%. From the September 2024 peak of $186.10 it is down 58.0%. The shape has two halves. From the June 2022 low of $70.05 to September 2024 the stock rose 2.7x while FY2022–FY2024 EPS held between $13.73 and $15.72. Since then, the rate-buydown economics have caught up with it. The 6-period monthly RSI is 25.8, deep in oversold territory. The 10-month moving average is about $92.85, 19% above the price. The downtrend is intact, and the stock is not bouncing off it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the second-largest US homebuilder at 0.86x book, with 16.6% homebuilding debt-to-capital, a 2.56% dividend and a share count shrinking about 7% a year. Margin looks like it bottomed at 15.2% in Q1 and has risen two quarters in a row as incentives fell from about 14% to about 12%. Direct construction costs are down 14% since late 2023 and cycle times are at a record low, so the cost base is lean. If mortgage rates ease even modestly, buydowns get cheaper, and the volume Lennar refused to give up turns into operating leverage. Housing is still undersupplied, and the CEO says demand from both owner-buyers and build-to-rent buyers is still there. With about 2% of homesites owned, there is less land on the balance sheet to write down.

Bear Case

"Volume over margin" is a strategy that makes the shareholder absorb the whole housing cycle. EPS fell from $14.31 to $5.28 in two years, ROE is 6% and FCF was $28M in FY2025. Orders fell 9% in Q3, full-year delivery guidance has been cut twice, and management says rates are "even higher" after quarter end, so the incentive bill is heading back up just as margins seemed to stabilise. 8 of 19 analysts rate it Sell, the median target is the current price, and the best-known peer is valued at twice Lennar per dollar of revenue. Stocks trading below book with a 6% ROE can stay there for years.

Top 3 Risks

01
Mortgage rates. Management put the 30-year rate at about 7% on the Q3 call, up from 6.4–6.5%. Rate buydowns are the main incentive tool, and their cost rises with rates, so this feeds straight into the gross-margin guide of 15.5–16.0%.
02
Guidance credibility. The EPS miss in Q3, the revenue miss in Q1, orders below the guided range and two cuts to full-year deliveries. Another reset in December would tell the market the "trough" call was early.
03
Returns below the cost of capital. 5.96% ROE and 5.23% ROIC. Until margins recover, buying back stock and holding volume do not create value per share as fast as the headline figures suggest, and a discount to book is the market's way of saying so.
Industry Commentary
Affordability and the rate buydown
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A new home competes with an existing one mostly on the monthly payment. When rates rise, a builder can cut the price, which marks every comparable home down, or keep the price and subsidise the buyer's rate, which keeps the comparables intact and puts the cost into incentives. Lennar has used both: the average price fell from $408K to $374K year on year in Q1, and incentives reached about 14% in Q4 FY2025. Stuart Miller described the setting in Q3: "Mortgage rates increased through the quarter... Rates are responding as inflation remains above the Fed's target, driven by geopolitical tension and higher oil prices." Management also noted recent legislative moves that constrain institutional and build-to-rent buyers, one of the demand sources it had been leaning on.

Land-light and the Millrose structure
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

With the Millrose spin-off, Lennar moved $5.5B of land and $1.0B of cash off its balance sheet into a separately listed homesite option platform. That platform sells lots back to Lennar on a set schedule in exchange for monthly option payments. Lennar then gave shareholders the 80% (February 2025) and swapped the last 20% for its own stock (November 2025). The result is a builder that owns about 2% of the homesites it controls and delivered 86% of Q3 homes on land-bank land. That lifts asset turns and lowers balance-sheet risk. The cost is the option fee inside every home's margin, at a time when that margin has little room left.

The cost side is working
EMERGING TAILWIND

Direct construction costs of $80 per square foot are down 6% year on year and 14% since Q4 2023. Cycle time is 116 days, a record. Q3 gross margin rose to 15.8% as incentives came down. The mechanical upside is clear: every point of incentive given back is roughly a point of gross margin. For scale only, on the $7.7B of Q3 home revenue one point is about $77M a quarter before tax (our arithmetic). The market is not pricing that in, because it is not yet persuaded that rates will allow it.
