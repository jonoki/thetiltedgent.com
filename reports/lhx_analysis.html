01 Company Overview

L3Harris builds the parts of the defense stack that other primes buy: missile-warning and missile-tracking satellites, tactical radios and electronic-warfare systems, and — since the 2023 Aerojet Rocketdyne acquisition — solid rocket motors and propulsion. FY2025 revenue was $21.87B, up 2.5% from $21.33B; the TTM figure through the October 2, 2026 quarter is $23.33B, up 7.3% YoY. In October management raised full-year 2026 guidance again, to $23.5–23.7B of revenue and $12.25–12.35 of GAAP EPS, with about $3.0B of free cash flow. Backlog, the value of signed orders not yet delivered, is a record $53.2B after a quarter in which the company booked 3.2× as much in new orders as it billed (book-to-bill).

The stock spent most of 2026 going the other way. From a February monthly close of $364.54 it fell for seven straight months to $236.78 at the end of September, −35.0%, and touched a 52-week low of $232.34 on October 8 — while the company kept raising guidance. Two events stand out on the way down: the second-quarter beat, reported after the close on July 29 together with a delay of the planned Missile Solutions listing, after which the shares fell 8.6% the next day; and the August 17 departure of CEO Christopher Kubasik, who left under a separation agreement after the board, with independent counsel, found conduct not consistent with the company's Code of Conduct — conduct the company said did not involve its financial reporting, controls, customer relationships or operational performance; the shares fell 4.6% that day. October reversed part of it: the stock closed the month at $277.61, up 17.2% from the September close, including a 6.84% gain on the day of the third-quarter print. It is still 23.8% below the February close. Sections 02 and 04 look at whether the business got worse, or only the story around it did.

Tactical radios, waveforms and software, satellite terminals, battlefield networking, electronic warfare and public-safety communications. Economically this is a catalogue product business wearing a defense-contractor badge: hardware sold into a large base of radios already in the field, with software and waveform licences layered on top. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOLID ROCKET MOTOR DUOPOLY TACTICAL RADIO INSTALLED BASE PROGRAM-OF-RECORD LOCK-IN CLEARED FACILITIES & WORKFORCE $53.2B RECORD BACKLOG SDA / GOLDEN DOME PRIME POSITION 24 YEARS OF DIVIDEND GROWTH

The core advantage is that L3Harris sells into two positions almost nobody can contest. In solid rocket motors there is essentially one competitor: in 2022 the FTC said Aerojet and only one other company, Northrop Grumman, compete to provide propulsion for U.S. missile systems. Entry takes years of capital, qualification testing and security clearances, and management said in July that its talks with the Pentagon had moved from whether it can deliver to how many it can produce. Tactical radios are the same idea in software form: waveforms and encryption are certified for each platform, so a customer switching suppliers has to recertify its radios, which costs time rather than just price. That is the mechanism behind CSD's 25.2% Q3 margin. Backlog of $53.2B against guided 2026 revenue of about $23.6B (the midpoint) is roughly 2.3 years of revenue, after a quarter with a 3.2× book-to-bill — orders are coming in faster than the company can deliver them.

Vulnerability 01
Mix Is Diluting Margin
The fastest-growing segment is the least profitable one. SMS grew nine-month revenue 13% at a 10.4% margin while CSD, at 25.7%, grew 3%. Every extra dollar of proliferated-space revenue pulls the company's average margin down. The 2028 targets now reflect it: management lowered its segment margin goal to about 16% from 16.3%. Consolidated ROIC of 8.76% and ROE of 10.03% (stockanalysis) say this business earns close to its cost of capital, the return investors require, not comfortably above it.
Vulnerability 02
Capital Intensity & Cash Timing
Quadrupling THAAD and tripling PAC-3 output requires plant that does not generate revenue until late 2027. The bill is now in the long-range plan: management cut its 2028 free cash flow target to $3B from $3.5B, citing about $250M of company 401(k) contributions to be paid in cash instead of new shares, a lower segment margin, missile capital spending brought forward, and dropping any assumed benefit from product-line sales or investment income. Free cash flow is also very uneven through the year: −$194M in Q1 2026, +$771M in Q2, +$1,260M in Q3. CFO Ken Sharp said the company expects about $1B in the fourth quarter and will repay $800M of debt.
Vulnerability 03
Governance Discontinuity
A CEO stepping down under a separation agreement following a board investigation into Code of Conduct matters, mid-transformation, is a real risk even though the company stated the matter was unrelated to its financial reporting, controls, customer relationships or operational performance. The successor is an internal operator who ran more than 80% of revenue, and the board split the Chair role — both mitigants. But an abrupt exit means every multi-year promise the prior CEO made has to be earned again. The first test went reasonably: Mehta's first quarter raised the 2026 guide, but it also lowered the 2028 targets.
Vulnerability 04
Single-Customer Concentration
The U.S. government is the dominant customer, and the appropriations calendar, continuing resolutions and supplemental funding decide the revenue line. In July management said it expected the largest U.S. defense budget on record plus a supplemental budget for munitions — but that is an expectation about a political process, not a contract. International sales, about a quarter of revenue in Q3 and growing faster than the total, are the diversification, from a low base.

Notable one-off items. Four things distort a naïve read of these numbers. First, the segment structure changed: the four-segment reporting many screens still carry (Space & Airborne Systems, Integrated Mission Systems, Communication Systems, Aerojet Rocketdyne) is no longer what the company publishes, so any multi-year segment series built on the old labels is broken. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard enough that momentum traders call it "overbought"; below 30, sold hard enough to be "oversold." It describes the pace of the move, not whether the price is right — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a year.

Over the five years from the October 29, 2021 close of $230.54 to the October 30, 2026 close of $277.61, LHX returned +20.4% in price — roughly 3.8% a year before dividends — against +64.6% for SPY and +78.8% for QQQ over the same dates (price only). That single number hides three different stocks. From late 2021 through September 2023 this was a dead-money defense name grinding from $230 to a cycle low of $174.12, a −24.5% drawdown, while the market questioned whether the Aerojet deal was an expensive distraction. From that low it compounded almost without interruption to a February 2026 monthly close of $364.54 — a +109.4% run that coincided with European rearmament, the Golden Dome missile-defense plan and munitions restocking. Then it fell for seven straight months to a September 2026 close of $236.78, −35.0%, with the price below its 10-month moving average from June onward. October was the third stock: +17.2% in a month, the last 6.84% of it on the day of the Q3 print. The price is now back above its 3-month average ($260.21) but still below the 10-month average ($303.66); on daily closes it is 11.1% above its 50-day average and 8.9% below its 200-day average, and the 14-day daily RSI is 76.2, above the 70 line traders call overbought. The monthly 6-period RSI on the chart went from 24.9 in September, deep in oversold territory, to 47.4.

What makes the fall interesting is that it did not line up with the business. Over those seven months the company raised guidance, reported a then-record $42B backlog at the end of June and won all five AMDT-3 constellation contracts; the quarter that followed brought a $53.2B backlog and free cash flow nearly three times the year before. Most of the drop came from the price investors would pay per dollar of earnings, not from the earnings: at the February peak the stock carried roughly 30× the current FY2026 EPS guide midpoint of $12.30; at the September close it carried 19.3×, and after October's rebound 22.6×. Nothing in the operating numbers fell by a third.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operating business keeps delivering and the stock is still 23.8% below its February close. A record $53.2B backlog is about 2.3 years of revenue; Q3 orders were 3.2× billings, with about $11B of PAC-3 and THAAD motor contracts now signed; 2026 guidance was raised again; Q3 free cash flow was nearly three times the year before; and the radio business's 25% margin held up despite a supply problem. At 22.6× the FY2026 EPS guide midpoint, with a 5.8% free cash flow yield on the guided $3.0B, a 1.80% dividend yield backed by 24 years of increases and short interest of 2.32%, investors are paying a middling price for orders that are already contracted. The missile plants, at the 15–18% margins management is aiming for, would lift the company's average margin, and none of that is in reported results yet.

Bear Case

Growth is coming from the wrong places. SMS — half of revenue — grew 13% in the first nine months at a 10.4% margin, while the 25.7%-margin radio business grew 3% and now has a supply problem. That mix pulls the company's average margin down even when every segment executes, and it shows up in 8.76% ROIC and 10.03% ROE: close to the cost of capital, not above it. Management has now said as much in its own numbers, cutting the 2028 free cash flow target to $3B from $3.5B and the segment margin goal to about 16%. The plant that is supposed to fix the mix opens in late 2027, the missile-business listing will not be reconsidered until the middle of 2027, and the $1B Department of War preferred stock carries a 7% dividend that compounds, booked as a deemed dividend that is subtracted before L3Harris's earnings per share. The CEO change is still recent. And the analysts who raised targets after the print still rate the stock Hold, with targets of $293–295.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
Free cash flow misses the ~$3.0B guide, or the 2028 target slips again. Q3 delivered $1,260M and management expects about $1B in Q4. Free cash flow is the number this sector's valuation is anchored to, and the 2028 target has already been cut once, to $3B. Another cut would confirm the fear that the missile build-out eats the cash, and would hit the price harder than an EPS miss.
02
SMS stays the low-margin growth engine. At 10.9% in Q3 (up 0.2 points) on half the revenue base, proliferated-space fixed-price work earns less than anything else L3Harris does. If it keeps growing at double digits, the company's average margin falls even with flawless execution — and fixed-price contracts mean any cost overrun is absorbed, not billed.
03
Appropriations, not orders, set the ceiling. The backlog is real, but management's growth outlook leans on a record U.S. defense budget plus a munitions supplemental — the outcome of a political process. A continuing resolution delays new starts and pushes the munitions ramp right, into a year when the capacity has already been paid for.
Industry Commentary
Munitions Capacity Is the Scarce Asset
EMERGING TAILWIND
The defense industry spent thirty years optimising for low-rate production of exquisite systems and is now being asked for volume. L3Harris said in July that its talks with the Pentagon were no longer about whether it could deliver but how many it could produce. The commercial expression is a seven-year framework agreement worth $12B of future production revenue, under which THAAD motor output quadruples and PAC-3 nearly triples, with an automated facility opening late 2027 and a 15–18% framework margin that management has called a target, not guidance. In Q3 large orders followed: about $11B of PAC-3 and THAAD contracts, an 11.8× book-to-bill for Missile Solutions and a segment backlog of $20.0B, up from $10.5B a quarter earlier. Solid rocket motors for U.S. missiles have essentially two suppliers and multi-year qualification barriers, so this capacity cannot be competed away quickly. The government is helping pay for it: the Department of War put $1B into the missile subsidiary in April. Missile Solutions grew 12% in the first nine months (17% in Q3 excluding a business it sold) and is the smallest segment at 17.3% of revenue — most of the tailwind is still ahead of the reported numbers.
Proliferated Space Is Commoditising the Satellite
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The Space Development Agency's model — many cheap satellites on fixed-price contracts rather than a few expensive ones on cost-plus — is deliberately designed to squeeze contractor margin, and it is working. L3Harris won all five AMDT-3 constellation contracts, an unambiguous competitive win, and the segment that books them earned 10.9% in Q3 and 10.4% over nine months while growing 13%. Winning more of this work makes the company bigger and less profitable per dollar. That tension — winning share while earning thin margins — is the central issue for every prime with a space business right now, and for L3Harris it applies to half the revenue base. It is also part of why consolidated ROIC sits at 8.76%.
Investors Cooled on Defense Before the Spending Cycle Ended
ONGOING HEADWIND
From September 2023 to February 2026 investors paid steadily more for defense earnings on European rearmament and missile defense; LHX went from $174.12 to $364.54. By the end of September it was $236.78 despite raised guidance and a growing backlog. The price investors paid per dollar of earnings, not the earnings, did the damage: roughly 30× the FY2026 guide midpoint at the peak versus 19.3× at the September close and 22.6× after October's rebound. Bull framing: Q3 brought about $11B of signed PAC-3 and THAAD orders, the 15–18% framework margins are still ahead, and at a 5.8% free cash flow yield with 2.32% short interest you are buying contracted revenue at a middling price. Bear framing: October's 17.2% bounce came after seven straight down months and still leaves the stock below its 10-month average; the most recent target raises came from analysts rating it Hold; and the basic objection — 8.76% ROIC with the fastest growth in the thinnest-margin segment, and a 2028 cash target just cut — is about business quality, which a lower price does not answer.
