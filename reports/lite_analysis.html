01 Company Overview

Lumentum designs and manufactures the photonics that move data with light: indium phosphide (InP) lasers — EMLs (electro-absorption modulated lasers, the transmit chip inside a data-center transceiver), CW (continuous-wave) lasers for silicon photonics, pump lasers for amplifiers and ultra-high-power lasers for co-packaged optics — plus finished 800G and 1.6T optical transceivers, optical circuit switches (OCS), telecom line systems and industrial/3D-sensing lasers. Fiscal 2026 (ended June 27, 2026) revenue was $3.014B, up 83.2%, and growth has kept speeding up: the September quarter (Q1 FY2027) printed $1.263B, up 136.5% year over year, and the company has guided the December quarter to $1.575–1.625B. On November 1, 2026 it agreed to be bought by Marvell Technology in an all-stock deal: each Lumentum share is to become 4.500 Marvell shares at closing, which the companies expect in the first half of 2028. It ranks #213 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $56.85B.

This page is about one question, and the deal changes how to ask it. For most of its life Lumentum was a cyclical component supplier — a telecom-spending and Apple-3D-sensing name that traded between $40 and $100. In two years it became a laser and switch vendor behind the optical links that connect AI chip clusters, and the stock rose about 7× over the chart's five years and 3.3× in twelve months. The bull case said the shift from electrical to optical links inside data centers is lasting and Lumentum's InP fabs are a scarce asset; Marvell is now paying in its own shares to own them. The bear case said this is the same component supplier on the same cloud-spending cycle. Because the exchange ratio is fixed, a LITE share is now mostly a claim on Marvell stock plus the odds that the deal closes: at the November 4 close, 4.5 Marvell shares were worth $719.10 and LITE traded 12.8% below that. Sections 02, 04 and 08 take the business and the deal apart with the numbers the companies report.

Three options on the future sit inside the two reported lines. Ultra-high-power lasers for CPO (co-packaged optics — the laser sits beside the switch ASIC and light is piped in from an external source) are guided to their first triple-digit-million quarter in fiscal Q3 FY27; management says it is "way behind" on shipments against demand. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
INP WAFER FABS (JAPAN ×2, GREENSBORO) EML YIELD & RELIABILITY ~1-YEAR CUSTOMER QUALIFICATION PUMP-LASER SHARE 70–80% LONG-TERM SUPPLY AGREEMENTS CHIP-TO-MODULE VERTICAL STACK OCS FIRST-MOVER SCALE

The moat is a scarce physical asset plus a slow qualification cycle. InP lasers cannot be made in a standard silicon fab; the world has a handful of high-volume InP lines, and Lumentum operates two in Japan and is bringing up a third in Greensboro, North Carolina (acquired from Qorvo, announced March 26, 2026; first revenue expected in early 2028). Even the raw substrate is tight — management said it had signed a large substrate deal with a Japanese supplier and another with AXT. On top of the fab sits yield: at 200G per lane the company says it and Broadcom face "very little competition", and the price premium it charges on CW lasers is explicitly attributed to yield and reliability. Then the customers themselves harden the position — management says it took about a year to get qualified at Nvidia, and says it has "secured multiple long-term customer agreements that help offset our planned capital expenditures", meaning hyperscalers are pre-funding capacity. Finally, owning both the chip (Components) and the module and switch (Systems) gives Lumentum a seat at the architecture table when CPO, NPO and OCS designs are being decided. What the moat is not: a guarantee of price. The 53.2% gross margin is a function of demand exceeding supply, and the company says so. Marvell's offer puts a price on the asset: the companies valued the deal at about $115B of enterprise value (debt plus equity, less cash) at the October 28 prices.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer concentration. The company did not name its largest customers on the call or at the Deutsche Bank conference, but said its "largest customer has taken up their orders very significantly" and that this underpins the fiscal 2028 earnings-power claim; it also referenced a ~one-year Nvidia qualification cycle. When one account's forecast moves the company's three-year target, that customer's capex (capital spending) plan is the single biggest risk factor on this page.
▸
Architecture transitions can strand a product. Management's own numbers: EML-based transceivers' share of the market drops from 70–80% at 800G to 40–50% at 1.6T as silicon photonics with CW lasers takes lanes. Further out, CPO removes the pluggable transceiver entirely — good for Lumentum's laser business, bad for its Cloud Light module business. Every generation re-opens the socket.
▸
Chinese InP capacity. Chinese transceiver leaders (Innolight, Eoptolink) are both customers and, via domestic laser fabs, potential competitors. Management's stance — Chinese vendors are a future risk rather than an immediate one, entering at 70–100 mW CW parts first, and "we obviously haven't seen any impact on our numbers as yet" — is honest, and also exactly what an incumbent says two years before pricing changes.
▸
Cyclicality dressed as structure. Lumentum's 2021–2023 history is the template: telecom inventory digestion and an Apple share loss took revenue down and the stock from $106 to $39. Hyperscaler optics is a bigger, faster cycle, not a non-cycle. At 15.2× trailing sales, the price still assumes the shortage lasts.
▸
The deal now sets the price. With 4.500 Marvell shares fixed per LITE share, Marvell's share price moves LITE more than Lumentum's own results: Marvell fell 36.5% from October 28 to November 4 and LITE fell 31.4%. The deal needs both companies' stockholder votes and regulatory approvals; Bank of America, downgrading Marvell in a November 2 note, cited an extended regulatory process including China (CNBC, Nov 3).

Notable One-Off Items

One item still dominates every trailing GAAP figure on this page, and it is important to understand what it is and is not. It stays in the trailing numbers until the June 2027 quarter replaces it.

$7.8B non-cash loss on extinguishment of convertible notes (Q4 FY2026). After the stock rose about 9× in a year (June 2025 to June 2026 month-end closes), Lumentum's 2026, 2028 and 2029 convertible notes were deep in the money. In the June quarter the company exchanged stock for a portion of them and, under the accounting rules, recognised the excess of the conversion value delivered over the carrying value of the debt as a loss: $7.8B, plus $3.1M of transaction costs and $2.9M of unamortised issuance costs. No cash left the building. What did happen is real and permanent: the share count went up (a non-GAAP diluted count of ~101M in the June quarter, including the remaining converts), total debt fell from $3.28B at March 28 to $1.64B at June 27, and stockholders' equity rose from $2.97B to $4.64B (results releases). So the June quarter's −$84.65 of GAAP EPS is an accounting recognition of dilution that shareholders had already suffered economically as the stock rose. Read the GAAP loss as a footnote to the share count, not as a statement about the business.

Q1 FY2027 items. GAAP stock-based compensation in the September quarter included $89.4M of equity-award modification tied to an executive's retirement and $14.2M of payroll taxes on stock awards; both are added back in the non-GAAP figures. During the quarter the company also paid $493.3M in cash and issued 1.5M shares to settle convertible-note conversions (10-Q).

Recurring non-GAAP adjustments are modest by the standards of high-growth tech: stock-based compensation of $191.3M in FY2026 (6.3% of revenue; $50.1M in Q4, 5.0%), amortisation of acquired intangibles of $135.7M (mostly Cloud Light and NeoPhotonics), and restructuring of $11.4M. The gap between GAAP operating margin (17.4%) and non-GAAP (29.8%) for the year is therefore explained almost entirely by SBC and amortisation, not by exotic add-backs — and the GAAP operating margin was already 27.8% in the June quarter and 28.5% in September.

Prior-year comparison quirk: Q4 FY2025 GAAP net income of $213.3M ($2.96/share) on an operating loss of $8.4M reflects a non-operating gain; the FY2025 base for GAAP earnings-growth comparisons is therefore not clean either. Use operating income and non-GAAP EPS for trend.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and a stock that goes up 10× spends most of that time "overbought".

Five-year trajectory — stated honestly. From the November 2021 month-end close of $86.77 to the November 4, 2026 close of $626.75, LITE returned +622%, about 49% compounded annually, with no dividend; over the same dates SPY returned +68.3% and QQQ +75.7% (price only). Almost none of it happened in the first three years. The stock fell from $105.77 (Dec 2021) to a monthly-close low of $39.21 in October 2023 — a 63% drawdown — as telecom customers digested inventory and the 3D-sensing business shrank; an investor who bought at the December 2021 month-end close was underwater for more than three and a half years. From that October 2023 low to the record month-end close of $971.26 in September 2026 the stock rose 24.8×, including a single month — February 2026 — of +79%. Over the last twelve months it is up 233% (from $188.36 on Nov 4, 2025) against +13.6% for SPY and +11.7% for QQQ; year to date from $368.59 it is up 70%. Since the September peak it has fallen 35.5% on month-end closes: −13.4% in October, after an intraday high of $1,143.00 on October 9, and −25.4% in the first three sessions of November as the Marvell deal tied its price to Marvell's. On daily closes it ended November 4 31% below its 50-day average ($911.08) and 22% below its 200-day average ($806.01), with a 14-day RSI of 31.6, near the oversold line. Essentially all of the outperformance came in the two years since October 2024, and the 5-year beta of 1.65 does not describe a stock that moved −17%, +28%, +6%, −13% and −25% in its last five months.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The setup, in one paragraph. Lumentum sells the scarcest input in the AI data-center build that is not a GPU: indium phosphide lasers. Demand for optical links grows with the number of AI chips and with the bandwidth per link, so it has grown faster than the chip market itself — Lumentum's revenue grew +65%, +90%, +109% and +136.5% in the last four quarters and is guided to roughly +140% in the December quarter. Because InP capacity is fixed in the short run, the company has been raising prices on its backlog: the non-GAAP gross margin reached 53.2%, with a 45–46% operating margin guided next. Consensus has FY2027 EPS of $24.77 and FY2028 of $38.54 (non-GAAP, stockanalysis.com); at $626.75 the stock trades at 25.3× and 16.3× those numbers. But the business no longer sets the price on its own: under the Marvell agreement each share becomes 4.5 Marvell shares, worth $719.10 at the November 4 close, and LITE trades 12.8% below that — the market's charge for a deal that is not expected to close until the first half of 2028.

Cyclical supplier or structural winner? The honest answer is: it depends on what year you are asking about. The 2021–2023 chart says cyclical — a 63% drawdown driven by telecom inventory and a shrinking Apple line, in a company with the same fabs and largely the same engineers. The 2024–2026 chart says structural — a 24.8× rise on a genuine architectural shift from copper to light inside the data center, backed by long-term customer agreements that pre-fund capex and a qualification cycle that takes a year. Both are true. The right frame is that Lumentum is a structurally advantaged supplier into a cyclical end market: the InP moat is real and will persist; the 50% gross margin is a shortage price and will not. Morgan Stanley's August range said much the same: its $450 bear case is 15× calendar-2028 earnings power of $30 and its $1,500 bull case 30× $50, so most of the gap is the multiple a supplier at the top of a spending cycle deserves. Marvell's bid is a third answer: a buyer that wants to own the InP fabs through the cycle, and pays in its own shares.

What the numbers do and do not support. Supported: the growth (audited), the margin expansion (audited), the net-cash balance sheet after the convertible-note conversions ($1.98B cash and short-term investments vs $1.40B debt), and a 23% ROIC (stockanalysis.com) that survives the GAAP noise. Not yet supported by disclosure: any dollar figure for OCS, CPO lasers or 3D sensing; any named-customer percentage; any third-party market-share number (the 70–80% pump-laser share is management's). The $7.8B GAAP loss is an accounting artefact of dilution that already happened — but the dilution itself is real: the September quarter's 101.7M GAAP diluted share count is 12% above the 90.7M shares outstanding on October 30.

Bull Case

The deal closes and LITE holders end up with about a third of a combined Marvell–Lumentum, which the companies say has a line of sight to more than $1B a year of cost savings and adds to Marvell's non-GAAP earnings per share from the first year. The 12.8% gap to 4.5 Marvell shares closes as approvals arrive, and any recovery in Marvell lifts LITE with it. If regulators block the deal, Marvell owes Lumentum a $3.2B fee — about $35 a share — and holders keep a business guiding to $1.6B a quarter at a 45% operating margin, at 16× fiscal 2028 consensus earnings.

Bear Case

A LITE share is now a claim on Marvell stock: Marvell fell 36.5% from October 28 to November 4 and LITE fell 31.4% with it. The deal needs two stockholder votes and regulatory approvals that Bank of America expects to take a long time, including in China, and its outside date runs to November 2027, extendable to August 2028. Underneath, this is still a component supplier with a 53% gross margin that management ties to demand exceeding supply; every speed generation re-opens the socket and Chinese InP fabs are entering at the low end. If the deal breaks, the stock goes back to trading on its own at 15× trailing sales and 49× EV/EBITDA (stockanalysis.com) — and in certain cases Lumentum would owe Marvell $2.9B. Morgan Stanley's August bear case is $450, −28%.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deal risk and a long road to closing. The merger needs Lumentum and Marvell stockholder approval, the US antitrust waiting period (HSR) and other regulatory approvals; the companies expect to close in the first half of 2028, and the agreement's outside date is November 1, 2027, extendable to August 1, 2028 (8-K). Bank of America cited an extended regulatory process, including in China, when it downgraded Marvell in a November 2 note (CNBC, Nov 3). The 12.8% discount to the deal's current value is the market's price for that uncertainty and the wait.
02
Marvell's share price. The exchange ratio is fixed at 4.500, so a LITE holder now carries Marvell's risks too: its own customers and products, the cost of absorbing Lumentum, and the share-count dilution that Bank of America said limits the gain to earnings per share. The offer's value fell from about $1,132 a share at the October 28 prices to $719.10 by November 4.
03
Cloud spending and one large customer. The business case — Lumentum's or Marvell's — rests on cloud companies' AI spending and on "our largest customer" raising orders, as management said in August. A pause in AI chip deployment, a delayed GPU generation (a risk Morgan Stanley named) or a change in that customer's optics design would hit revenue, then pricing; and as Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom and Chinese entrants add InP capacity in 2027–2029, the 53% gross margin has room to fall.
  Industry Commentary
Indium Phosphide Is the Bottleneck — for Now
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every 800G or 1.6T transceiver needs InP lasers, and InP cannot be made on a silicon line. Lumentum says it is shipping behind demand on EMLs, is "way behind" on high-power lasers, has pump lasers "effectively sold out", and that it has signed new substrate (raw wafer) supply deals with AXT and a Japanese supplier. The response is capex: $229.8M in the September quarter alone, two Japanese fab expansions, and the Greensboro fab (first revenue early 2028, ramping through 2028 into 2029). Industry-wide, Coherent and Broadcom are expanding too and Chinese fabs are entering at the low-power end. The constraint is real today and is the main reason for the 53% gross margin; it is also being solved by everyone at once, with delivery dates clustered in 2028.
Every Speed Generation Re-Opens the Socket
ONGOING HEADWIND
Optics moves from 800G to 1.6T to 3.2T roughly every two years, and the architecture moves with it: pluggable transceivers, then near-packaged optics (NPO), then co-packaged optics (CPO) with external laser sources. Management's own view is that EML-based transceivers' share of the market falls from 70–80% at 800G to 40–50% at 1.6T, and that silicon photonics "again runs out of steam" at 3.2T; CPO, if it scales, removes the pluggable module that Cloud Light sells while multiplying demand for the ultra-high-power lasers Lumentum makes. Net, the company believes it wins each transition; the market has to underwrite that belief every 24 months. Fabrinet, as the contract manufacturer for much of the industry's module assembly, gains whichever design wins, and trades at 16.5× forward earnings (stockanalysis.com) against Lumentum's 25.3× fiscal 2027 consensus. Marvell's bid is the largest bet yet that owning the laser fab pays across generations; on the deal call Hurlston said "we supply our lasers to almost all of our competitors."
Optical Circuit Switching and the Backbone Build
EMERGING TAILWIND
Two demand sources sit outside the transceiver cycle. OCS — mirror-based switches that reroute fibre without electronics — moves from a Google-internal technology to a merchant market, and Lumentum guided its first $100M+ OCS quarter for September 2026 after doubling shipments sequentially; the November release named pump lasers and OCS the leading contributors to that quarter's growth, without an OCS dollar figure. Separately, hyperscalers are building long-haul fibre backbones between AI sites on a scale management described by saying the network capacity connecting just two AI data-center sites "is double the total global backbone capacity", which is why telecom-style pump lasers are sold out and shipments are set to quadruple. Bull framing: these are second and third legs that do not depend on the 1.6T socket fight. Bear framing: neither OCS nor CPO nor 3D sensing has a disclosed revenue line, so the diversification argument is, for now, a set of ramp dates; the only numbers you can audit are the two product-type lines, and both are hyperscaler-optics-driven and both are on the same capex cycle.
