01 Company Overview
Lockheed Martin is a defense contractor that designs and produces combat aircraft, missiles and missile-defense interceptors, military helicopters, radars, naval combat systems and satellites — with the US Government accounting for the large majority of sales and international governments the balance. FY2025 revenue was $75.05B, up 5.6% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue has since reached $79.12B (+7.9%) as munitions and missile-defense production ramped. The F-35 fighter alone generated 27% of 2025 sales, per the 2025 annual report. The company ranks #86 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization at $139.57B.
The defining fact of the current investment case is backlog. At the end of Q3 2026 Lockheed reported an all-time record $292.0B backlog, up from $230.4B three months earlier and, per CFO Evan Scott on the call, up $113 billion from a year earlier, on a quarterly book-to-bill of 4x. The largest addition was the seven-year contract for PAC-3 MSE interceptors (total value up to $58.62B, awarded July 29), of which the release counts $52.5B in backlog; it follows the $35B multi-year THAAD interceptor award in Q2. Missiles and Fire Control alone now holds $142.5B, or 49% of the total, and the whole backlog equals roughly 3.7 years of trailing revenue. On the day of the results, Lockheed and the Department of War also announced a ten-year framework for F-35 production — 156 jets a year across Lots 21–30, at least 850 of them for the US — which becomes a contract only once Congress grants multiyear authority and the money.

Aeronautics builds the F-35 Lightning II, the F-22 Raptor (sustainment and upgrades), the F-16 Fighting Falcon, the C-130J Hercules and classified programs at Skunk Works. The F-35 alone generated 27% of 2025 company sales and is the single most important program in the portfolio; it is the only fifth-generation fighter in production in the West. Q3 2026 segment sales were $8,214M, up 13% YoY from $7,256M: higher F-35 production volume added about +$710M and C-130 program liquidation +$270M, partly offset by lower F-16 production (−$130M). Segment operating profit was $743M (9.0% margin) versus $682M (9.4%) a year earlier; management cited unfavorable profit adjustments on the F-16 and C-130 programs. For the first nine months Aeronautics profit is $2,122M against $1,304M in 2025, when a $950M pretax reach-forward loss on a classified program turned Q2 2025 into a $(98)M segment loss. FY2025 operating profit was $2.1B, down 17% on those charges.

TR-3 and Block 4: deliveries were halted in July 2023 over Technology Refresh 3 (the processor/memory/display backbone required before the Block 4 weapons-and-sensors modernization can be fielded), resumed in July 2024 on a partial "truncated" software build, and the parked-jet backlog was cleared in May 2025. The company then delivered a record 191 F-35s in 2025, versus 110 in 2024. The October 2026 framework sets production at 156 aircraft a year for ten years (Lots 21–30), at least 850 of them for the US, and Lockheed has agreed to invest at least $7B of its own money in the program; it still needs congressional authorization and appropriations. Meanwhile the Q3 release warns that Lockheed's costs on the Lot 20 advance contract began to exceed the government's current authorization and funding during the quarter, and that results and cash flow will suffer without more of both; the CFO said a Lot 20 signing this year is not in guidance. More than 1,375 F-35s are now fielded worldwide, per the October 22 release. Block 4 capability is being delivered incrementally rather than as a single block drop, and remains the key technical and cost risk on the program.

The NGAD loss and the "Ferrari" response: in March 2025 the US Air Force awarded the Next Generation Air Dominance sixth-generation fighter — designated F-47 — to Boeing. Lockheed declined to protest. Taiclet's answer was to treat the F-35 as a chassis to be turned into a Ferrari: redirecting NGAD-derived stealth, infrared sensing, extended-range strike and crewed–uncrewed teaming technology into a "fifth-generation-plus" F-35/F-22 upgrade aimed, as he put it on the April 2025 call and again in October 2026, at about 80% of sixth-generation capability at half the cost. Whether the Air Force funds that path is unresolved and is the central strategic question for the segment.

MFC is where the rearmament cycle shows up first and most cleanly. The portfolio includes PAC-3 MSE (the hit-to-kill interceptor for Patriot air-defense batteries), THAAD (Terminal High Altitude Area Defense), JASSM and LRASM (long-range air-launched cruise and anti-ship missiles), HIMARS launchers with GMLRS guided rockets, PrSM (Precision Strike Missile), Javelin and Hellfire. It is currently the strongest segment in the company: Q3 2026 sales of $4,501M, up 24% YoY from $3,624M, on PAC-3 and THAAD production ramps (+$480M) and GMLRS and PrSM ramps (+$330M), with operating profit of $631M — a 14.0% margin, the highest of the four segments, versus $510M a year earlier.

The order book is what changed the story. In June 2026 the government awarded a seven-year THAAD interceptor contract of up to $35B, and on July 29 the government awarded a seven-year PAC-3 MSE contract modification of up to $53.86B, taking the multiyear total to $58.62B after a $4.7B first-year award in April; the Q3 release counts $52.5B of it in backlog. A $3B GMLRS production contract and a $1.2B Precision Strike Missile Increment 2 contract (September 21) add to it, and the segment's backlog is now $142.5B. Management has committed to tripling PAC-3 MSE production capacity by the end of 2030 and quadrupling THAAD interceptor output; on the Q3 call it described adding 2 million square feet of facilities across eight sites over three years, adding 3,000 people at MFC sites this year and a $4.7B PAC-3 propulsion supply contract with L3Harris. Capital expenditure guidance for 2026 is $2.1–2.3B, largely aimed at this capacity build. Demand sources are Ukraine and Israel consumption, European and Indo-Pacific allied restocking, and US inventory replenishment — a combination that has turned munitions from a low-visibility spares business into the segment with the best margin and the longest order cover in the company.

RMS contains Sikorsky (Black Hawk, Seahawk, CH-53K King Stallion, Combat Rescue Helicopter), naval combat systems including Aegis, radars such as TPY-4 and SPY-7, undersea and training systems, and C4ISR integration. It was the problem child of 2025: full-year sales were roughly flat at $17.3B and operating profit fell 31% to $1.3B (~7.5% margin) on $665M of pretax reach-forward losses across the Canadian Maritime Helicopter Program ($570M) and the Turkish Utility Helicopter Program ($95M), plus a $66M fixed-asset write-off.

The recovery is visible. Q3 2026 sales rose 3% to $4,511M on higher radar volume, and operating profit was $524M (11.6% margin), the same margin as a year earlier; for the first nine months RMS profit is $1,384M against $855M in 2025, when the helicopter-program losses landed. Naval and radar demand is the durable part of the story: Aegis integration work is tied directly to the layered missile-defense architecture, and in a June 2026 test at Yuma Lockheed's Sanctum counter-drone software, with partner radars and a containerized launcher, brought down a drone target; the company says integration and live-fire testing took under 45 days, its showcase for a faster, cheaper development model. Sikorsky remains the structurally weakest franchise: fixed-price international helicopter contracts are exactly where the 2025 losses came from, although on September 30 Sikorsky and the US Army reached a new production agreement for the Black Hawk.

Space builds military and civil satellites, strategic and missile-defense systems including the Trident II D5 fleet ballistic missile and Next Generation Interceptor work, missile-warning constellations, and holds a 50% stake in the United Launch Alliance joint venture. It is the steadiest segment: FY2025 sales of $13.0B (+4%) with operating profit of $1.3B (+10%, ~10.0% margin), and Q3 2026 sales of $3,485M, up 4%, as strategic and missile-defense work (the Trident fleet ballistic missile and Next Generation Interceptor, +$185M) outweighed lower missile-warning satellite volume (−$115M). Operating profit was flat at $331M (9.5% margin), now the third-best margin of the four segments. Management said a key solid-rocket-motor test keeps the Next Generation Interceptor on track for fielding by 2030.

The call option is Golden Dome, the US homeland missile-defense architecture. The Congressional Budget Office has estimated the program could cost roughly $1.2 trillion over 20 years against an earlier administration figure of ~$175B — a spread wide enough that the program's actual funded size is genuinely unknown. Lockheed was named one of 12 firms selected by the Space Force to develop space-based interceptor (SBI) prototypes, with capability demonstration targeted for 2028. Being one of twelve is a seat at the table, not a franchise; the meaningful revenue decision points are the downselects that follow the prototype phase. Note also that a large share of any Golden Dome spend would flow to MFC (PAC-3, THAAD) and RMS (Aegis) as much as to Space.

IRREPLACEABLE TOOLING BASE
Lockheed's moat is regulatory, industrial and temporal rather than technological alone. Once a government selects a combat aircraft or an interceptor, it is locked into decades of spares, upgrades, training and sustainment — the F-35 fleet of more than 1,375 aircraft generates a multi-decade annuity that no competitor can bid away, and the same is true of Aegis-equipped hulls and Black Hawk fleets. Entry requires cleared workforces, accredited facilities, qualified supply chains and a track record measured in decades; the practical number of firms that can prime a fifth-generation fighter or a hit-to-kill interceptor is one or two. Foreign Military Sales deepen this: allied buyers adopt the US logistics, training and munitions ecosystem, which raises their switching cost far above the aircraft price. The $292.0B backlog at 3.7x trailing revenue, a 4x book-to-bill in Q3 2026 and sole-source positions on PAC-3 and THAAD together mean the revenue line for the rest of the decade is unusually pre-committed. What that moat does not do is protect margins — the customer is effectively the only buyer, and it sets profit rates, audits costs, and increasingly pushes fixed-price development terms onto the contractor.
Key Vulnerabilities
1. Single-customer, single-program concentration. The US Government is the overwhelming majority of revenue, and the F-35 alone was 27% of 2025 sales. A budget decision, a production-rate cut or a technical stand-down on one program — as happened with the TR-3 delivery halt from July 2023 to July 2024 — moves the whole company; in Q3 2026 Lockheed's costs on the F-35 Lot 20 advance contract were already running ahead of government funding. There is no commercial business of scale to offset a defense-budget air pocket.
2. Fixed-price development and reach-forward losses. Lockheed booked $950M of pretax reach-forward losses on a classified Aeronautics program and $665M on the Canadian and Turkish helicopter programs in 2025 — together roughly $1.6B pretax, enough to cut FY2025 EPS meaningfully. Q3 2026 brought smaller unfavorable profit adjustments: $40M each on the F-16 and C-130 and $45M on the Heavy Lift and Seahawk helicopter programs, per the release. Charges like these recur in a portfolio with fixed-price development content.
3. Losing the sixth-generation franchise. Boeing won NGAD / F-47 in March 2025. If the Air Force funds F-47 aggressively and declines to pay for a "fifth-generation-plus" F-35, Lockheed's tactical-aircraft franchise becomes a long, slowly declining sustainment annuity rather than a growth business — and the "Ferrari upgrade" thesis is currently a management proposal, not a funded program of record.
4. Technology disruption from autonomy and attritable systems. Anduril and similar entrants are competing on speed, software and unit cost rather than on platform pedigree, and the collaborative combat aircraft / counter-drone market rewards exactly that. Lockheed's own Sanctum counter-drone test — integration and live fire in under 45 days, by the company's account — is an explicit acknowledgement that the traditional development cadence is a liability. The economics of attritable systems are also structurally lower-margin per unit than exquisite platforms.

Notable One-Off Items

FY2025 earnings were materially distorted. The Q2 2025 charges have now rolled out of the trailing twelve months, which is why trailing EPS ($28.16) sits close to the 2026 guide:

· Classified Aeronautics reach-forward loss — $950M pretax (~$713M after tax), recognized in Q2 2025. This single item turned the Aeronautics segment to a $(98)M operating loss for that quarter and drove FY2025 Aeronautics profit down 17%.

· Rotary and Mission Systems program losses — $665M pretax: $570M on the Canadian Maritime Helicopter Program and $95M on the Turkish Utility Helicopter Program, plus a $66M fixed-asset write-off. RMS operating profit fell 31% as a result.

· Pension settlement charge — $479M in FY2025. Pension accounting remains a recurring source of non-operating noise in reported GAAP results; investors should read segment operating profit and free cash flow rather than headline net income.

· Tax — CAMT relief in 2026. In July management said new Treasury guidance means the company is no longer subject to the corporate alternative minimum tax (CAMT) this year, and expects lower federal cash tax payments. This is a genuine cash-flow tailwind supporting the raised FCF guide (now $7.8–8.0B), but it is a policy-dependent benefit rather than operating improvement.

Aggregate FY2025 pretax charges of roughly $2.16B against reported net earnings of $5.02B mean the "clean" 2025 earnings power was substantially higher than the $21.49 GAAP EPS suggests — which is precisely why FY2026 consensus EPS of $30.65 (stockanalysis) implies +42.6% growth off an artificially low base. Do not extrapolate that growth rate: for 2027 CFO Evan Scott said on the Q3 call, "We see clear line of sight to high single-digit revenue growth next year as a company," and expects a pension contribution above $1 billion next year.

· No buybacks in 2026 so far. The Q3 release shows no share repurchases in the first nine months of 2026, against $2.25B in the same period of 2025; the dividend rose to $3.60 a quarter in October.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A record $292.0B backlog, 3.7 years of trailing revenue, on a 4x book-to-bill in Q3 gives revenue visibility that almost no industrial company can match, and it is converting: Q3 sales +11%, FY2026 guidance implying about 8% growth, and the CFO pointing to high single-digit growth in 2027. The munitions franchise is sole-source, capacity-constrained and expanding (triple PAC-3, quadruple THAAD), and it is the highest-margin part of the portfolio at 14.0%. The ten-year F-35 framework, if Congress funds it, locks in 156 jets a year. The stock trades at 18.9x forward earnings versus 20.05x for the S&P 500 and yields 2.4% after a 24th straight annual dividend increase. Golden Dome is free optionality on top.

Bear Case

Lockheed lost the sixth-generation fighter to Boeing, and the "Ferrari" fifth-generation-plus F-35 is a pitch, not a funded program; even the new ten-year framework is not a contract until Congress grants multiyear authority and money, and Lot 20 costs are already running ahead of funding. Margins are the problem the backlog cannot fix: Aeronautics ran at 9.0% in Q3 and the company at an 8.2% net margin, capped by a single dominant customer and broken in 2025 by fixed-price reach-forward losses ($1.6B pretax). Buybacks have stopped this year, a pension contribution above $1B is due in 2027, and after a 23.8% twelve-month run the average target is only 5.0% above the close, with 10 of 22 analysts at Hold and Goldman Sachs at Sell ($498).

01
Program concentration in the F-35. 27% of 2025 company sales rode on one program whose deliveries were halted for a full year over TR-3 software as recently as 2023–24, and whose Block 4 modernization remains a live technical and cost risk. A rate cut, a further software stand-down or an unfavorable lot negotiation hits the Aeronautics segment, about 40% of sales, at once — and the Lot 20 contract is still unsigned, with costs running ahead of funding.
02
Recurring fixed-price reach-forward losses. $950M classified, $570M Canadian helicopter, $95M Turkish helicopter and a $66M fixed-asset write-off in 2025 — roughly $1.7B pretax across two segments. Q3 2026 still brought $125M of smaller unfavorable adjustments (F-16, C-130, Heavy Lift and Seahawk), so the exposure has not gone away. The new munitions capacity commitments create fresh execution risk of the same type.
03
Budget and political dependency, with a very wide Golden Dome range. Nearly all revenue depends on US and allied appropriations. Golden Dome cost estimates span ~$175B to CBO's ~$1.2T over 20 years, and LMT is one of twelve SBI prototype awardees — a seat at the table, not a franchise. A continuing resolution, a topline cut, or a budget rotation toward low-cost autonomy over exquisite platforms would compress both growth and multiple.
  Industry Commentary
Global Rearmament and the Munitions Restocking Cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
The defining industrial fact of this decade is that Western munitions inventories were sized for deterrence, not for consumption — and Ukraine and Israel have demonstrated consumption rates that stockpiles cannot sustain. The result is visible directly in Lockheed's order book: a $35B multi-year THAAD interceptor contract, a $58.62B seven-year PAC-3 MSE contract, a $3B GMLRS production contract, and a Q3 book-to-bill of 4x that lifted backlog by $113B year over year to $292.0B. Management has responded with capacity commitments — triple PAC-3 MSE output by the end of 2030, quadruple THAAD, 2 million square feet of new facilities across eight sites and 3,000 MFC hires this year — funded by $2.1–2.3B of 2026 capex. The same dynamic is visible at peers: General Dynamics reported a $136.5B backlog at Q2 2026. Critically, this is the highest-margin segment Lockheed has (14.0% in Q3 2026), so the shift toward munitions raises the company's overall margin rather than diluting it. The constraint is no longer demand — it is solid rocket motors, energetics, machine tools and cleared labor; on October 13 Lockheed signed a solid-rocket-motor cooperation agreement with Avio.
Budget Priorities Rotating Toward Missile Defense and Autonomy
ONGOING HEADWIND
Two shifts are happening at once, and they cut in opposite directions for Lockheed. The first — homeland and theater missile defense — is unambiguously favorable: Golden Dome, with CBO estimating ~$1.2 trillion over 20 years against an earlier ~$175B official figure, sits squarely on top of PAC-3, THAAD, Aegis, Next Generation Interceptor and the Space segment. Lockheed is one of 12 SBI prototype awardees with a 2028 demonstration target. The second shift is harder. Budget rhetoric increasingly favors cheap, numerous, software-defined and attritable systems over exquisite platforms — the domain where Anduril and similar entrants compete on development speed rather than institutional pedigree. Lockheed's own answer, the Sanctum counter-drone test, integrated and fired in under 45 days, and Taiclet's repositioning of the company as a defense-technology firm matching low-cost effectors to targets, is a direct admission that the traditional cadence is a competitive liability. Attritable systems also carry structurally lower per-unit margin than fifth-generation aircraft — winning that market is not the same as replacing the profit pool.
Program Concentration in the F-35 — Bull vs Bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The bull framing: the F-35 is the only fifth-generation fighter in production in the Western world, more than 1,375 are already fielded, deliveries hit a record 191 in 2025 after the TR-3 backlog cleared, and the October 2026 framework sets production at 156 aircraft a year for ten years, at least 850 of them for the US. That installed base is a multi-decade sustainment annuity that cannot be competed away, and the fifth-generation-plus upgrade path — which Taiclet says aims at about 80% of sixth-generation capability at half the cost — would extend the franchise's growth phase by a decade at very high incremental margin.

The bear framing: 27% of 2025 company sales depends on a single program that Boeing now leapfrogs with the F-47, that was grounded from delivery for a full year over software, whose Block 4 modernization is still being delivered piecemeal, and whose 2025 profitability was erased by a $950M classified charge in the same segment. Aeronautics ran a 9.0% Q3 margin — the lowest of the four segments — while MFC ran 14.0%, and the Lot 20 contract is still unsigned with costs running ahead of funding. If the Air Force funds F-47 and declines the "Ferrari" upgrade, Lockheed's largest business becomes its slowest-growing and lowest-margin one, and the equity story narrows to munitions and missile defense alone.

How to weigh it: the market is currently paying 18.9x forward earnings against 20.05x for the S&P 500 — about 6% below, roughly in line with the market — which is consistent with a company whose growth is real but whose largest franchise is contested. Targets near $700 assume both the munitions ramp and a funded F-35 path; the low end of the range ($498–$517) assumes neither. Backlog conversion and Aeronautics margin are the two numbers that will settle it.
