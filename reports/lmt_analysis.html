01 Company Overview
Lockheed Martin is the world's largest pure-play defense contractor by revenue, designing and producing combat aircraft, missiles and missile-defense interceptors, military helicopters, radars, naval combat systems and satellites — with the US Government accounting for the large majority of sales and international governments the balance. FY2025 revenue was $75.05B, up 5.6% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue has since accelerated to $77.01B (+7.2%) as munitions and missile-defense production ramped. The company ranks #172 globally by market capitalization at $135.97B, and sits inside the top five holdings of the iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA) at a 4.63% weight.
The defining fact of the current investment case is backlog. At Q2 2026 LMT reported an all-time record $230.4B backlog — up roughly $64B year over year and up from $193.6B at the end of 2025 — on a 3.2-to-1 book-to-bill quarter, driven by a $35B multi-year Terminal High Altitude Area Defense (THAAD) interceptor award from the Missile Defense Agency. That backlog equals roughly 3.0 years of trailing revenue, an unusually long visibility window even by defense-prime standards.

Aeronautics builds the F-35 Lightning II, the F-22 Raptor (sustainment and upgrades), the F-16 Fighting Falcon, the C-130J Hercules and classified programs at Skunk Works. The F-35 alone is roughly a quarter of total company revenue and is the single most important program in the portfolio — and, per management, "the only in-production fifth-generation fighter in the free world." Q2 2026 segment sales were $8,112M, up 9.3% YoY from $7,420M, with higher F-35 production volume alone contributing +$475M. Segment operating profit was $760M (9.4% margin), versus a $(98)M loss in Q2 2025 when a $950M pretax reach-forward loss on a classified program wiped out the quarter. FY2025 operating profit was $2.1B, down 17% on those charges, for a depressed ~6.9% full-year margin.

TR-3 and Block 4: deliveries were halted in July 2023 over Technology Refresh 3 (the processor/memory/display backbone required before the Block 4 weapons-and-sensors modernization can be fielded), resumed in July 2024 on a partial "truncated" software build, and the parked-jet backlog was cleared in May 2025. The company then delivered a record 191 F-35s in 2025 — versus 110 in 2024 and 98 in 2023 — and management now points to a sustained global production rate of about 156 aircraft per year. The fleet has passed roughly 1,300 aircraft and 1 million flight hours. Block 4 capability is being delivered incrementally rather than as a single block drop, and remains the key technical and cost risk on the program.

The NGAD loss and the "Ferrari" response: in March 2025 the US Air Force awarded the Next Generation Air Dominance sixth-generation fighter — designated F-47 — to Boeing. Lockheed declined to protest. Taiclet's framing: "We're basically going to take the chassis and turn it into a Ferrari," redirecting NGAD-derived stealth, infrared sensing, extended-range strike and crewed–uncrewed teaming technology into a "fifth-generation-plus" F-35/F-22 upgrade he claims can deliver "80% of sixth-gen capability at 50% of the cost." Whether the Air Force funds that path is unresolved and is the central strategic question for the segment.

MFC is where the rearmament cycle shows up first and most cleanly. The portfolio includes PAC-3 MSE (the hit-to-kill interceptor for Patriot air-defense batteries), THAAD (Terminal High Altitude Area Defense), JASSM and LRASM (long-range air-launched cruise and anti-ship missiles), HIMARS launchers with GMLRS guided rockets, PrSM (Precision Strike Missile), Javelin and Hellfire. It is currently the strongest segment in the company: Q2 2026 sales of $4,101M, up 19.5% YoY from $3,433M, with operating profit of $594M — a 14.5% margin, the highest of the four segments, versus $479M a year earlier.

The order book is what changed the equity story. Q2 2026 brought a $35B multi-year THAAD interceptor contract from the Missile Defense Agency — the single largest driver of the record $230.4B backlog and of the 3.2:1 book-to-bill — plus a $3B GMLRS production contract and a $4.76B Army PAC-3 award. Management has committed to tripling PAC-3 MSE production capacity by 2030 and quadrupling THAAD interceptor output, and says PrSM is scaling "faster than we ever thought." Ten munitions lines are delivering more units in 2026 than in 2025. Capital expenditure guidance of $2.0–2.4B for 2026 is largely aimed at this capacity build. Demand sources are Ukraine and Israel consumption, European and Indo-Pacific allied restocking, and US inventory replenishment — a combination that has turned munitions from a low-visibility spares business into the segment with the best margin and the longest order cover in the company.

RMS contains Sikorsky (Black Hawk, Seahawk, CH-53K King Stallion, Combat Rescue Helicopter), naval combat systems including Aegis, radars such as TPY-4 and SPY-7, undersea and training systems, and C4ISR integration. The recovery is visible. Naval and radar demand is the durable part of the story: Aegis integration work is tied directly to the layered missile-defense architecture, and the segment produced the Sanctum counter-drone system, taken from concept to successful live-fire test in 45 days — management's showcase for its faster, cheaper, higher-volume development model. Space builds military and civil satellites, strategic and missile-defense systems including the Trident II D5 fleet ballistic missile and Next Generation Interceptor work, missile-warning constellations, and holds a 50% stake in the United Launch Alliance joint venture. It is the steadiest segment: FY2025 sales of $13.0B (+4%) with operating profit of $1.3B (+10%, ~10.0% margin), and Q2 2026 sales of $3,496M, up 5.7%, with operating profit of $371M (10.6% margin) — the segment's margin is now the second-best in the company.

The call option is Golden Dome, the US homeland missile-defense architecture. The Congressional Budget Office has estimated the program could cost roughly $1.2 trillion over 20 years against an earlier administration figure of ~$175B — a spread wide enough that the program's actual funded size is genuinely unknown. Lockheed was named one of 12 firms selected by the Space Force to develop space-based interceptor (SBI) prototypes, with capability demonstration targeted for 2028. Being one of twelve is a seat at the table, not a franchise; the meaningful revenue decision points are the downselects that follow the prototype phase. Note also that a large share of any Golden Dome spend would flow to MFC (PAC-3, THAAD) and RMS (Aegis) as much as to Space.

Lockheed's moat is regulatory, industrial and temporal rather than technological alone. Once a government selects a combat aircraft or an interceptor, it is locked into decades of spares, upgrades, training and sustainment — the F-35 fleet of roughly 1,300 aircraft generates a multi-decade annuity that no competitor can bid away, and the same is true of Aegis-equipped hulls and Black Hawk fleets. Entry requires cleared workforces, accredited facilities, qualified supply chains and a track record measured in decades; the practical number of firms that can prime a fifth-generation fighter or a hit-to-kill interceptor is one or two. Foreign Military Sales deepen this: allied buyers adopt the US logistics, training and munitions ecosystem, which raises their switching cost far above the aircraft price. The $230.4B backlog at 3.0x trailing revenue, a 3.2:1 book-to-bill and sole-source positions on PAC-3 and THAAD together mean the revenue line for the rest of the decade is unusually pre-committed. What that moat does not do is protect margins — the customer is a monopsonist that sets profit rates, audits costs, and increasingly pushes fixed-price development terms onto the contractor.
Key Vulnerabilities
1. Single-customer, single-program concentration. The US Government is the overwhelming majority of revenue, and the F-35 alone is roughly a quarter of it. A budget decision, a production-rate cut or a technical stand-down on one program — as happened with the TR-3 delivery halt from July 2023 to July 2024 — moves the whole company. There is no commercial business of scale to offset a defense-budget air pocket.
2. Fixed-price development and reach-forward losses. Lockheed booked $950M of pretax reach-forward losses on a classified Aeronautics program and $665M on the Canadian and Turkish helicopter programs in 2025 — together roughly $1.6B pretax, enough to cut FY2025 EPS meaningfully. Management now says the two classified programs have been "stable," with the last adjustments in Q4 2024 and Q2 2025. That is reassurance, not a guarantee: these charges are structurally recurring in a portfolio with fixed-price development content.
3. Losing the sixth-generation franchise. Boeing won NGAD / F-47 in March 2025. If the Air Force funds F-47 aggressively and declines to pay for a "fifth-generation-plus" F-35, Lockheed's tactical-aircraft franchise becomes a long, slowly declining sustainment annuity rather than a growth business — and the "Ferrari upgrade" thesis is currently a management proposal, not a funded program of record.
4. Technology disruption from autonomy and attritable systems. Anduril and similar entrants are competing on speed, software and unit cost rather than on platform pedigree, and the collaborative combat aircraft / counter-drone market rewards exactly that. Lockheed's own Sanctum counter-drone effort — concept to live fire in 45 days — is an explicit acknowledgement that the traditional development cadence is a liability. The economics of attritable systems are also structurally lower-margin per unit than exquisite platforms.

Notable One-Off Items

FY2025 earnings were materially distorted and the distortions are the reason trailing multiples look the way they do:

· Classified Aeronautics reach-forward loss — $950M pretax (~$713M after tax), recognized in Q2 2025. This single item turned the Aeronautics segment to a $(98)M operating loss for that quarter and drove FY2025 Aeronautics profit down 17%.

· Rotary and Mission Systems program losses — $665M pretax: $570M on the Canadian Maritime Helicopter Program and $95M on the Turkish Utility Helicopter Program, plus a $66M fixed-asset impairment. RMS operating profit fell 31% as a result.

· Pension settlement charge — $479M in FY2025. Pension accounting remains a recurring source of non-operating noise in reported GAAP results; investors should read segment operating profit and free cash flow rather than headline net income.

· Tax — CAMT relief in 2026. Management stated the company "is no longer subject to CAMT this year" following new Treasury guidance, and expects reduced federal cash tax payments. This is a genuine cash-flow tailwind supporting the raised $7.0–7.2B FCF guide, but it is a policy-dependent benefit rather than operating improvement.

Aggregate FY2025 pretax charges of roughly $2.16B against reported net earnings of $5.02B mean the "clean" 2025 earnings power was substantially higher than the $21.49 GAAP EPS suggests — which is precisely why 2026 consensus EPS of $30.48 implies +41.8% growth off an artificially low base. Do not extrapolate that growth rate; 2027 consensus is +7.3%.

Five-year trajectory. From the September 2021 close of $345.10 to the August 19, 2026 close of $589.15, LMT returned +70.7% on price alone over five years — roughly 11.3% annualized before dividends, and meaningfully more with the ~2.3% yield reinvested. That headline masks how little of the gain was earned in the middle: the stock spent the entire period from October 2022 to June 2024 chopping in a $409–$487 band, going essentially nowhere for twenty months while the S&P 500 compounded. Virtually all of the five-year return has been generated in two bursts — the Ukraine-driven re-rating of late 2022, and the missile-defense/backlog re-rating that began in late 2025.
Recent performance. Over the trailing twelve months LMT returned +33.7%, against +20.9% for SPY (S&P 500, total return) and +26.3% for ITA (iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense). So LMT has outperformed both its index benchmark and its own sector over the last year — a reversal after several years of lagging both. Note that ITA is 21.4% GE Aerospace and 16.7% RTX, so the sector benchmark is heavily weighted toward commercial-aero exposure that LMT does not have; LMT itself is only a 4.63% weight. Volatility remains high in absolute terms — a $437.25 to $692.00 fifty-two-week range is a 58% spread — even though measured beta is just 0.11, because the moves are driven by program and budget news uncorrelated with the market.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A record $230.4B backlog at 3.0x trailing revenue on a 3.2:1 book-to-bill gives revenue visibility that almost no industrial company can match, and it is being converted at accelerating rates — Q2 sales +11%, FY2026 guidance implying ~8% growth and 28% higher segment operating profit. The munitions franchise is sole-source, capacity-constrained and expanding (triple PAC-3, quadruple THAAD), which is the highest-margin part of the portfolio at 14.5%. The 2025 charges are in the base, management says the classified programs are stable, and the stock trades at 19.0x forward earnings versus 28x for the S&P 500 while returning ~4.3% of market cap annually in dividends and buybacks. Golden Dome is free optionality on top.

Bear Case

Lockheed lost the sixth-generation fighter to Boeing, and the "Ferrari" fifth-generation-plus F-35 is a pitch, not a funded program — which means the largest segment, 40% of revenue, may be a slowly maturing annuity. Margins are the problem the backlog cannot fix: Aeronautics ran at 9.4% and the company at an 8.2% net margin, capped by a monopsony customer and repeatedly broken by fixed-price reach-forward losses ($1.6B pretax in 2025 alone). The consensus rating is Hold with 14 of 21 analysts neutral and an average target only 7.4% above spot after a 33.7% twelve-month run — and the PEG of 0.99 is an artifact of a charge-depressed 2025 base, with 2027 growth of just 7.3%.

Program concentration in the F-35. The new munitions capacity commitments create fresh execution risk of the same type.

Budget and political dependency, with a very wide Golden Dome range. Nearly all revenue depends on US and allied appropriations. Golden Dome cost estimates span ~$175B to CBO's ~$1.2T over 20 years, and LMT is one of twelve SBI prototype awardees — a seat at the table, not a franchise. A continuing resolution, a topline cut, or a budget rotation toward low-cost autonomy over exquisite platforms would compress both growth and multiple.
  Industry Commentary
Global Rearmament and the Munitions Restocking Cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
The defining industrial fact of this decade is that Western munitions inventories were sized for deterrence, not for consumption — and Ukraine and Israel have demonstrated consumption rates that stockpiles cannot sustain. The result is visible directly in Lockheed's order book: a $35B multi-year THAAD interceptor contract, a $4.76B Army PAC-3 award, a $3B GMLRS production contract, and a 3.2:1 book-to-bill that lifted backlog by $64B year over year to $230.4B. Management has responded with capacity commitments — triple PAC-3 MSE output by 2030, quadruple THAAD, PrSM "faster than we ever thought," and ten munitions lines delivering more in 2026 than 2025 — funded by $2.0–2.4B of 2026 capex. The same dynamic is visible at peers: RTX won a $22.9B Navy Tomahawk expansion and grew revenue 11.8%; General Dynamics reported a record $136.5B backlog. Critically, this is the highest-margin segment Lockheed has (14.5% in Q2 2026), so the mix shift toward munitions is margin-accretive, not dilutive. The constraint is no longer demand — it is solid rocket motors, energetics, machine tools and cleared labor.
Budget Priorities Rotating Toward Missile Defense and Autonomy
ONGOING HEADWIND
Two shifts are happening at once, and they cut in opposite directions for Lockheed. The first — homeland and theater missile defense — is unambiguously favorable: Golden Dome, with CBO estimating ~$1.2 trillion over 20 years against an earlier ~$175B official figure and roughly 70% of funding expected to target space-based interceptors, sits squarely on top of PAC-3, THAAD, Aegis, Next Generation Interceptor and the Space segment. Lockheed is one of 12 SBI prototype awardees with a 2028 demonstration target. The second shift is harder. Budget rhetoric increasingly favors cheap, numerous, software-defined and attritable systems over exquisite platforms — the domain where Anduril and similar entrants compete on development speed rather than institutional pedigree. Lockheed's own answer, the Sanctum counter-drone system taken from concept to live fire in 45 days, and Taiclet's repositioning of the company as a "defense technology" firm matching low-cost effectors to targets, is a direct admission that the traditional cadence is a competitive liability. Attritable systems also carry structurally lower per-unit margin than fifth-generation aircraft — winning that market is not the same as replacing the profit pool.
Program Concentration in the F-35 — Bull vs Bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The bull framing: the F-35 is the only in-production fifth-generation fighter in the Western world, roughly 1,300 aircraft are already flying with over a million flight hours, deliveries hit a record 191 in 2025 after the TR-3 backlog cleared, and the production rate is guided to a sustained ~156 aircraft per year. That installed base is a multi-decade sustainment annuity that cannot be competed away, and the fifth-generation-plus upgrade path — Taiclet's claim of "80% of sixth-gen capability at 50% of the cost" — would extend the franchise's growth phase by a decade at very high incremental margin.

The bear framing: a quarter of company revenue and 40% of segment revenue depends on a single program that Boeing now leapfrogs with the F-47, that was grounded from delivery for a full year over software, whose Block 4 modernization is still being delivered piecemeal, and whose 2025 profitability was erased by a $950M classified charge in the same segment. Aeronautics ran a 9.4% Q2 margin — the lowest of the four segments — while MFC ran 14.5%. If the Air Force funds F-47 and declines the "Ferrari" upgrade, Lockheed's largest business becomes its slowest-growing and lowest-margin one, and the equity story narrows to munitions and missile defense alone.

How to weigh it: the market is currently paying 19.0x forward earnings — a discount to the S&P 500's 28x trailing — which is roughly consistent with a company whose growth is real but whose largest franchise is contested. The bull targets at $691–$756 require both the munitions ramp and a funded F-35 upgrade path; the $503 bear target requires neither. Backlog conversion and Aeronautics margin are the two numbers that will settle it.
