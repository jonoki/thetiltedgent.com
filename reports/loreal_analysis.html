01 Company Overview

L'Oréal describes itself as the world's leading beauty company, and its shelf is long: L'Oréal Paris, Garnier, Maybelline, Lancôme, Yves Saint Laurent Beauté, Kérastase, La Roche-Posay and CeraVe are some of its 40 global brands, sold in more than 150 countries. FY2025 sales were €44.05B, up 4.0% like-for-like and 1.3% reported, with a record 20.2% operating margin and EPS excluding non-recurring items of €12.71 (+0.4%). The first half of 2026 was faster: €23.78B of sales, +6.8% like-for-like (+6.5% after adjusting for the timing of an IT changeover), an operating margin of 21.3% and EPS excluding non-recurring items of €7.40 (+4.8%).

The second quarter beat forecasts. Q2 sales were €11.6B, up 6.3% like-for-like on the adjusted measure, against the 5.7% analysts expected in a Visible Alpha consensus cited by Reuters; mass-market Consumer Products did better than expected, while Luxe, at 4.7%, fell short. L'Oréal does not give numeric guidance. Its outlook in the release is "another year of growth in sales and profit" despite the current context, and on the results call CEO Nicolas Hieronimus said: "In the full year, we expect the global beauty market to grow somewhere between 4.5% and 5%, and we expect to keep outperforming this market. So far, July is off to a very good start, and our teams are confident for the rest of the year." On margins, CFO Christophe Babule said the company "could expect on a yearly basis and based on the past experience, around 20 to 30 basis points improvement if we keep with the same rhythm of growth." At June 30 exchange rates, L'Oréal estimated currency would take about 0.6% off 2026 sales.

The share price tells a slower story. The October 2, 2026 close of €372.75 is only 4.5% above the September 2021 month-end close of €356.85, while France's CAC 40 index rose 21.1% over the same five years. It sits 17.6% below the May 2024 month-end high of €452.10. The valuation is still a premium one: 31.6 times trailing earnings on IFRS figures, 28.6 times on the company's own headline EPS. The table has two very big stacks. The Bettencourt Meyers family owns 34.79% and Nestlé 20.16%, so about 45% of the shares trade freely. Since 2025 L'Oréal has also been spending heavily: Kering Beauté (Creed, plus the Bottega Veneta and Balenciaga licences, with Gucci from July 2027), a second 10% of Galderma, and Medik8 and Color Wow, paid for partly with new bonds.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

40 global brands, 4 channels A&P spend: 32.6% of sales 4,000 researchers · 725 patents (2025) €1B+ a year on IT & tech 37 plants · 160 fulfilment centres AA (S&P) / Aa1 (Moody's) credit Patient anchor shareholders

The moat is scale in advertising plus breadth of channel. In H1 2026 L'Oréal spent €7.74B, or 32.6% of sales, on advertising and promotion. Because the four Divisions cover the supermarket, the department store, the pharmacy and the salon, a shift in where people buy moves sales between L'Oréal's own pockets more often than it moves them to a competitor. The gross margin of 74.8% pays for that spending and still leaves a 21.3% operating margin. The risk is that this is a premium hand, and the price already assumes it keeps winning.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The deal spree has added leverage. Kering Beauté (acquisition price €4,246.5M, closed March 31, 2026), the second 10% of Galderma (€4.2B, closed February 10, 2026) and smaller deals took net debt to €12.66B at June 30, 2026, including €2.38B of lease liabilities. Bonds followed: €3B in November 2025, €1.75B in January 2026, CHF 500M in June 2026 and €2B priced on September 23, 2026. The ratings are still AA/Aa1, but the €4.0B of goodwill and intangible assets booked on Kering Beauté alone has to earn its keep.
02
China and travel retail. North Asia is 23.2% of sales and grew 4.6% like-for-like in H1 2026, below the group. The company describes Chinese selective (prestige) beauty as a "still challenging market" and travel retail as still affected by conditions in mainland China. The Luxe Division, a third of sales, depends most on that customer, and in Q2 its 4.7% growth lagged forecasts (Reuters).
03
A second-half slowdown is the bear case. Deutsche Bank cut the stock to Sell from Hold on June 23, 2026, with a €340 target (from €360). According to Investing.com's report it cited weaker Chinese credit data, retailer stock built up around the IT switch-over that could pull demand into the first half, competition from fast-growing Chinese beauty exports to the US, and currency hedges rolling off. The company's own adjusted growth figure strips out IT phasing of −0.7 points in H1 2026, which shows the timing effect is real.
04
The price already assumes the winning streak continues. At 31.6 times IFRS trailing earnings and a 1.93% dividend yield, the shares price L'Oréal as a compounder. A premium multiple can shrink fast: the stock fell 27.3% from the May 2024 month-end high of €452.10 to the November 2024 month-end of €328.65.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items separate the IFRS figures from the company's headline numbers. First, non-recurring items of €412.5M net of tax in H1 2026 (other income and expenses of −€300.5M before tax), which is why IFRS EPS (€6.63) trails headline EPS (€7.40). Second, France's exceptional corporate-tax contribution on large companies, €252.7M booked at December 31, 2025 and renewed for 2026. L'Oréal adjusts it out of net profit excluding non-recurring items. Separately, the Gucci licence changeover: Gucci's beauty licence with Coty ended early on July 7, 2026, at a cost to L'Oréal of about $360 million, of which $188 million was paid in July 2026. L'Oréal also holds two large listed stakes off the operating line: 7.30% of Sanofi (€6.66B at June 30, 2026) and 20% of Galderma (valued at €9.49B at the June 30 share price). Together they equal about 8% of L'Oréal's market value (our calculation). Dividends received added €365.8M to H1 2026 pre-tax profit.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of €356.85 to the October 2, 2026 close of €372.75, L'Oréal gained 4.5% on price. The CAC 40 gained 21.1% over the same window. Dividends narrow the gap: the five ordinary dividends paid in the window (€4.80, €6.00, €6.60, €7.00 and €7.20, per Yahoo's dividend record) add up to €31.60 a share, which brings the simple, not-reinvested return to about 13.3%. The 6-period monthly RSI reads 44.9, in neutral ground. Its extremes in the window were 74.7 in April 2023 and 17.2 in November 2024, the month after October's −14.4%. The 10-month moving average is €378.99, a little above the price: after gains of 4.8% and 4.5% in April and May 2026, the stock slipped in August, in September and over the first two days of October. The trend is flat to slightly down, and the price is waiting on the October 22 sales figures.

Relative performance

Against its home index, L'Oréal has lagged: +4.5% versus +21.1% for the CAC 40 over five years on price, but slightly ahead over one year (−0.4% versus −2.0%). Against beauty peers the one-year picture is mixed. Shiseido rose 28.0% in yen, Estée Lauder 3.6% in dollars, while Beiersdorf fell 16.9%. The three-peer average of +4.9% is ahead of L'Oréal by about five points. In plain terms: L'Oréal's sales and margins kept rising in the periods covered here, while its share price has done little for five years.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Growth has accelerated through 2025 and into 2026 (+3.0% like-for-like in H1 2025, +4.2% in Q3, +6.0% in Q4, +6.8% in H1 2026) and was broad-based: +6.5% adjusted like-for-like, every Division and every region growing, a gross margin up to 74.8% and a record first-half operating margin of 21.3% even while advertising rose 70 basis points. The fastest-growing Divisions, Dermatological Beauty (+11.3%) and Professional Products (+12.6%), are also the most profitable, so mix keeps lifting margins. The acquisitions add growth that hasn't shown up in the numbers yet: Creed and the Kering licences from April 2026, Gucci from July 2027, and a 20% Galderma stake worth €9.49B at June 30. Free cash flow of €7.54B a year covers the dividend (about €3.8B at €7.20 a share, our calculation) roughly twice. With two long-term anchor shareholders, the company can be managed for the next decade rather than the next quarter. Q2 growth of 6.3% adjusted beat the 5.7% consensus, Hieronimus told analysts "July is off to a very good start", and since the print BNP Paribas has upgraded to Outperform and HSBC (€450) and Berenberg (€447) have backed Buy ratings. On MarketScreener's 24-analyst panel the average target is €417.96, 12.1% above the close.

Bear Case

Nearly 32 times trailing IFRS earnings for a company whose TTM reported sales grew 3.5% leaves little room for error, and five years of a flat share price show how hard it has been to earn a return from that premium. Deutsche Bank's Sell case says the strong first half borrowed from the second: retailers stocked up around the IT switch-over, China credit is weakening, and currency hedges are rolling off; in September it added that falling cosmetics imports into Jiangsu point to a second-half slowdown in China. Luxe, the Division most tied to China, already lagged forecasts in Q2. Net debt has risen to €12.66B, and four bond issues in eleven months helped pay for €8B+ of deals, which now have to deliver. North Asia, at almost a quarter of sales, is still growing more slowly than the group. And 20.16% of the company sits with a shareholder whose own chairman now sits on L'Oréal's board; Nestlé sold 4% back to L'Oréal at €400 a share in 2021, and any further sale would put a large block of stock on the market.

Top 3 Risks

01
A second-half slowdown. If Q3 sales on October 22 show adjusted like-for-like growth falling well below H1's 6.5% and the 5.5% consensus HSBC cited in September, the Deutsche Bank timing argument is confirmed and the premium multiple is exposed. The October 2025 release, followed by a 6.70% one-day fall, shows how the stock reacts to a soft quarter.
02
Integration and leverage. Kering Beauté cost €4,246.5M, of which €2,840.9M is goodwill; Galderma's second 10% cost €4.2B; the Gucci switch-over costs about $360 million. Net debt is €12.66B. The AA/Aa1 ratings leave room, but a stumble in Creed or the Gucci launch would hit returns on a much larger capital base.
03
China, travel retail and currency. North Asia is 23.2% of sales; travel retail is still affected by conditions in mainland China; currency took 2.8 points off H1 2026 reported growth. About a quarter of sales comes from North America, so a weaker dollar keeps shrinking reported euro growth, as it did in H1 (+8.6% like-for-like, +4.2% reported).
Industry Commentary
Buying the next decade of prestige beauty
STRATEGIC SHIFT

In about twelve months L'Oréal agreed or closed a run of deals: Medik8 (77.4%, closed September 10, 2025), Color Wow (agreed June 2025), the Kering Beauté alliance (agreed October 19, 2025, closed March 31, 2026, €4,246.5M), a second 10% of Galderma (€4.2B, February 10, 2026) and a majority of India's Innovist (pending). In the 2025 results release CEO Nicolas Hieronimus called it the group's "most strategic and transformational M&A offensive to date". The logic is portfolio breadth: luxury fragrance (Creed, Gucci from 2027), medical aesthetics (Galderma), science-led skincare (Medik8) and premium haircare (Color Wow). The test is whether these brands grow faster than the group once inside it. The first sign was positive: the H1 2026 release says Creed is off to a good start.

China, travel retail and the shape of demand
ONGOING HEADWIND

China is the region L'Oréal watches most closely, both at home and through duty-free sales to Chinese travellers. Its North Asia sales grew 4.6% like-for-like in H1 2026, and the company describes the Chinese beauty market as gradually improving, driven by a recovery in selective (prestige) beauty, with travel retail still under pressure. On the July 30 results call Hieronimus put a number on it: "In China, we have seen indeed the market stabilizing at a growth of around 2% over the last quarters." Deutsche Bank is more sceptical: in September it read China's rising cosmetics imports as almost entirely a Hainan duty-free effect rather than a consumer recovery. Elsewhere the mix is shifting toward online (over 50% of North Asia sales, more than 30% for the group in 2025) and toward the emerging regions, where SAPMENA-SSA grew 13.9%. The simple version: no region is more than about 35% of L'Oréal's sales, so one weak market slows the group rather than stalling it.

The Nestlé question
OWNERSHIP

In December 2021 Nestlé sold 22.26 million shares back to L'Oréal at €400 each (€8.9B), saying it would keep 20.1% and "remains fully supportive of the company's value creation strategy". At December 31, 2025 the stake was 20.16%. At the €372.75 close that is a holding worth roughly €40B (our calculation: 20.16% of the €198.55B market value). In 2026 Nestlé's representation on L'Oréal's board changed: Paul Bulcke stepped down at the April 24 AGM and Nestlé's new chairman, Pablo Isla, joined as a director and Vice-Chairman, alongside Nestlé executive Anna Lenz. The bull framing: a patient shareholder that reinforces stability. The bear framing: a 20% block that, if Nestlé ever chose to sell, would be a very large supply of stock.
