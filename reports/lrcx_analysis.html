01 Company Overview

Lam Research designs, builds and services the wafer fabrication equipment (WFE — the machines that pattern and build transistors on silicon wafers) used to etch, deposit and clean the microscopic three-dimensional structures inside every advanced logic and memory chip. It is the world's dominant supplier of plasma etch tools and a top-two supplier of thin-film deposition, with an especially concentrated franchise in memory — where 3D NAND layer stacking and DRAM scaling are effectively etch- and deposition-limited problems.

Fiscal 2026 (ended June 28, 2026) was a record: revenue of $23.23 billion, up 26.0% from $18.44 billion in FY2025, with GAAP diluted EPS of $5.76 and non-GAAP EPS of $5.82, up 41%. The June quarter alone produced $6.72 billion of revenue (+30% YoY), a 52.0% non-GAAP gross margin (a 20-year high) and a record 38.4% operating margin. At roughly $408 billion of market capitalization Lam now ranks among the 30 largest listed companies globally and is the second-largest US-listed semiconductor equipment maker behind Applied Materials — a position it did not hold twelve months ago, having re-rated more than 200% over the past year on the memory capital-expenditure supercycle.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
3D NAND Etch Monopoly ~100K Installed Chambers Process Recipe Lock-In Qualification Switching Cost $2B+ Annual R&D Memory Customer Depth Molybdenum Metallization Lead

Lam's moat is best understood as process ownership, not product ownership. A leading-edge memory or logic device is defined by a recipe — gas chemistries, pressures, temperatures, plasma conditions — co-developed with the customer over 18 to 36 months and then qualified for high-volume production. Once a step is qualified on a Lam chamber, moving it to a competitor's chamber means re-qualifying the device, re-running yield learning, and accepting months of production risk on a node where each week of delay costs a memory maker hundreds of millions of dollars. That is why WFE share shifts glacially: Lam has held roughly 45%+ of etch for over a decade despite well-capitalized attackers in Tokyo Electron and Hitachi High-Tech. The moat is deepest exactly where the physics is hardest — high-aspect-ratio cryogenic etch through 300+ layers of alternating film in 3D NAND, where Lam's served market per wafer roughly doubles from 128 layers to 500+ layers, and where no credible second source exists at volume. Layered on top is the installed base: ~100,000 chambers generating spares, service and upgrade revenue that grew 43% year-over-year in the June quarter and now represents about 36% of total revenue at above-corporate margins, converting a violently cyclical capital-equipment business into something with a meaningful annuity floor. R&D at over $2 billion annually — more than a quarter of the workforce is in R&D — sustains the cadence, and the April 2026 five-year IBM collaboration on High-NA EUV-era and sub-1nm process integration keeps Lam inside the roadmap conversations that determine tool specifications three nodes out.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. China concentration and export-control whiplash. China was 26% of June-quarter revenue and had been 34% in the March quarter — a swing of roughly $500 million of quarterly revenue mix in ninety days. Lam is required to halt certain shipments to Hua Hong, China's second-largest foundry, and further restrictions landed in June 2026. The company's own 10-K states plainly that sales to China "have been impacted, and are likely to be materially and adversely affected by export license requirements." Every incremental rule tightens a revenue base that Lam does not control and cannot forecast.

2. Chinese domestic substitution. On July 27, 2026 the stock fell 7.9% in a single session on a report that a Shanghai state-backed firm had begun mass-producing domestic immersion DUV lithography scanners. Lithography is not Lam's business, but the read-through is that China's toolchain gaps are closing faster than assumed — and etch, where AMEC and Naura already compete at trailing nodes, is the segment most exposed. Localization directly attacks the trailing-edge and Reliant revenue that has been a stabilizer through prior downturns.

3. Memory cyclicality and customer concentration. Micron, Samsung, SK hynix and TSMC are named principal customers; memory alone was 46% of June-quarter systems revenue. Memory capital spending has historically swung 40–60% peak to trough. The FY2026 result was produced by a genuine capex boom — DRAM contract prices rose roughly 90–95% quarter-over-quarter in Q1 2026 — and equipment orders are the derivative of that. If memory pricing normalizes, order cuts arrive with a two-to-three quarter lag and hit Lam harder than it hits diversified peers like Applied Materials.

4. Valuation and positioning risk. At 56.6× trailing and 34.5× forward earnings against a semiconductor equipment industry trailing average near 54× and a NASDAQ-100 near 29×, Lam is priced for the FY2027 consensus of $34.8 billion revenue and $9.46 EPS to be delivered, not attempted. The June-to-July 2026 drawdown of roughly 42% from the $438.50 intraday high to the $252.42 close on July 29 — on news that changed no near-term number — is the clearest evidence of how little margin for error the multiple carries.

Notable One-Off Items

No large discrete charge distorts FY2026 results. The gap between GAAP EPS of $5.76 and non-GAAP EPS of $5.82 is only six cents — roughly a 1% reconciliation, consisting mainly of amortization of acquired intangibles and restructuring-related items. That is unusually clean for a company of this size and stands in contrast to FY2023–FY2024, when export-control-driven inventory and reserve charges materially depressed reported gross margin.

Three items still deserve flagging. First, export-control effects are embedded in the revenue line rather than shown as a charge — the halt on certain Hua Hong shipments and the June 2026 restrictions reduce revenue and reserve inventory without a separately captioned expense, so the true "underlying" China run-rate is not disclosed. Second, the buyback pace decelerated sharply. Lam repurchased $4.55 billion of stock in the first three quarters of FY2026 (including $1.16 billion in Q3) but only $246 million in the June quarter — a roughly 80% sequential reduction, consistent with management declining to buy shares near the $433 June high. Approximately $4.3 billion remained under the $10 billion authorization approved in May 2024. Investors extrapolating first-half buyback support into FY2027 should reset that assumption. Third, the 10-for-1 stock split effective October 2024 means all pre-October-2024 per-share figures in this analysis have been restated on a split-adjusted basis; historical quotes from unadjusted sources will be ten times higher.

Five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 monthly close of $54.16 to $326.11 on August 12, 2026, LRCX returned approximately +502% on a split-adjusted basis — a compound annual rate near 43% before dividends. That headline number conceals three distinct regimes. The first, September 2021 through October 2022, was a brutal de-rating: the memory glut, post-COVID demand normalization and the October 2022 China export controls drove the stock from $68.58 at the end of 2021 to a $35.27 monthly close in September 2022, a 49% drawdown. The second, late 2022 through mid-2025, was a slow grind in which the AI logic buildout lifted WFE but memory capex stayed disciplined; the stock roughly doubled off the low but spent all of 2024 and the first half of 2025 range-bound between roughly $70 and $105, going nowhere for eighteen months. The third regime began in September 2025, when DRAM contract pricing inflected and the market re-underwrote memory equipment demand: from a $99.60 close in August 2025 the stock rose to $433.33 by June 2026, a 335% advance in ten months. Essentially the entire five-year return was earned in the final twelve, which is the core risk to owning it here.

The July 2026 unwind deserves its own accounting. From the $438.50 intraday high the stock fell to a $252.42 close on July 29 — roughly −42% in five weeks, with single-session losses of 9.7%, 10.2% and 6.9% in the first week of July alone. Nothing in the fundamentals broke; the catalysts were a stretched multiple, insider selling, and the July 27 report of Chinese domestic DUV lithography production. The Q4 print that evening then reversed roughly half of it. A stock capable of moving 42% down and 22% up inside a single month on unchanged guidance is trading on positioning, not earnings.

Against direct peers the picture is more nuanced: Applied Materials trades at a similar $445B market cap on $29.0B of trailing revenue growing just 3.3%, versus Lam's $408B on $23.2B growing 26.0% — the market is explicitly paying for Lam's memory leverage. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Lam owns the two process steps — high-aspect-ratio etch and metal deposition — that gate 3D NAND layer scaling and DRAM node transitions, and its served market per NAND wafer roughly doubles from 128 layers to 500-plus layers. With NAND makers only now beginning 256-layer-plus conversions, DRAM still flat in dollars despite a 4.9% supply deficit, WFE guided to the low $150B range for 2026 and plausibly $200B in 2027 on 8–10 new fab startups, the revenue runway is visible rather than hoped for. September-quarter guidance of $8.10B implies over 50% YoY growth, gross margin has already reached a 20-year high of 52% with a mid-50s target, and 36% of revenue now comes from a high-margin installed-base annuity growing 43%. At a 1.29 PEG the stock is not expensive against the growth it is actually delivering.

Bear Case

Essentially the entire five-year return was earned in the last twelve months, and the stock is priced at 56.6× trailing earnings for a business whose end market has historically swung 40–60% peak to trough. The FY2027 consensus of $34.8B revenue and $9.46 EPS assumes a memory capex boom compounds for another two years; if DRAM and NAND pricing normalizes from a 125% and 234% annual increase, orders can be cut faster than backlog reveals. China fell from 34% to 26% of revenue in one quarter, Hua Hong shipments are restricted, and domestic Chinese etch suppliers are attacking the trailing-edge base. Management's own behaviour is a tell: buybacks collapsed from $1.16B in the March quarter to $246M in June. The July −42% drawdown on no fundamental news showed how quickly the multiple can vanish.

Top 3 Risks
01
Memory capex cycle reversal. Memory was 46% of June-quarter systems revenue and the FY2026 result was manufactured by the sharpest DRAM/NAND price spike in fifteen years. Equipment orders are a second derivative of memory pricing: if contract prices flatten, capex plans are cut within one to two quarters and Lam's revenue can fall 30%+ from peak, as it did in FY2023. The forward multiple offers no cushion for that scenario.
02
China — both export controls and substitution. Roughly a quarter of revenue is at the mercy of US licensing decisions Lam does not control, and the June 2026 restrictions plus the Hua Hong halt show the direction of travel. Simultaneously, evidence that China can produce domestic DUV lithography implies faster domestic etch and deposition qualification, structurally shrinking the trailing-edge and Reliant revenue that historically cushioned downturns.
03
Multiple compression on a high-beta name. With a beta of 1.86, a 56.6× trailing P/E and 89% index-driven ownership, LRCX is a leveraged proxy for AI capex sentiment. A broad de-rating of AI infrastructure — or simply a peer's cautious guidance, such as Applied Materials' August 13 report — can remove 20–40% of the price without any change to Lam's own earnings, exactly as occurred in July 2026.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Drives This Stock
The 2026 Memory Supercycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
This is not a normal memory upturn. DRAM contract prices rose 90–95% quarter-over-quarter in Q1 2026 with a further 58–63% expected in Q2, putting the full-year 2026 forecast near +125%; NAND is tracking toward +234%. Korean export data showed DRAM at nearly $89,500 per kilogram, a 497% year-over-year increase. The cause is structural rather than cyclical: HBM production carries roughly a 3:1 trade-off — three bits of conventional DRAM capacity sacrificed per bit of HBM — and the top three manufacturers have reallocated 70–80% of advanced capacity to AI applications, creating a 4.9% global DRAM supply gap, the largest in fifteen years. Because new fabs take four to five years, meaningful relief is not expected before late 2027. For Lam this converts directly into equipment demand: management raised its calendar 2026 WFE estimate from $135B to $140B in April and to the "low $150 billion range" in July, and Tim Archer described 2027 as an "extraordinary setup" with 8–10 fab startups. The asymmetry worth noting is that DRAM systems revenue was flat in dollars in the June quarter even as NAND doubled — meaning the DRAM leg of this cycle has largely not yet been billed.
The NAND Upgrade Cycle — Lam's Highest-Leverage Exposure
EMERGING TAILWIND
NAND capex was effectively frozen from 2022 through 2025 while the industry absorbed oversupply. That freeze is now reversing for a specifically AI-driven reason: expanding context windows, persistent memory and KV-cache offloading push AI inference workloads onto enterprise SSDs, forcing conversions to 256-layer-plus and eventually 500-plus-layer devices. NAND represented 23% of Lam's June-quarter systems revenue with dollar revenue more than doubling sequentially. Lam is more levered to this than any peer: vertical layer stacking is fundamentally an etch-and-deposition problem, lithography is largely irrelevant to it, and Lam has stated its served market per NAND wafer roughly doubles from the 128-layer node to 500-plus layers. Critically, much of the near-term spend flows as upgrades to existing chambers through CSBG — record upgrade revenue drove CSBG to $2.47B, up 43% YoY — which is faster to recognize and higher margin than greenfield systems. Advanced packaging, growing over 70% in calendar 2026 on HBM copper plating, sits alongside as a second AI-native line, now including a first 310mm × 310mm panel-level tool.
Export Controls & Chinese Substitution — The Constraint That Sets the Multiple
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every valuation argument on Lam eventually reduces to one question: what is the terminal China contribution? The empirical record from FY2026 is a collapse from 34% of revenue in the March quarter to 26% in June, restrictions on shipments to Hua Hong, and new US rules in June 2026 — with the company's own 10-K stating that China sales "are likely to be materially and adversely affected." The July 27 report of Shanghai state-backed mass production of domestic immersion DUV scanners took 7.9% off the stock in one session, not because lithography is Lam's market but because it recalibrated how fast the entire Chinese toolchain is closing. Etch is the segment where domestic players (AMEC, Naura) are furthest advanced.

Bull framing: China is now the smallest it has been in years as a revenue share, meaning the derating is largely done — Taiwan at 27% and Korea at 20% have absorbed the mix, AI-driven demand outside China is more than replacing it, and any thaw in US–China chip negotiations is pure upside optionality that no analyst currently models.

Bear framing: the substitution is permanent and compounding. Chinese trailing-edge tool purchases were a profit pool that cushioned prior downturns; if that base is structurally lost, Lam's next cyclical trough is deeper than any in its history, and a 56.6× trailing multiple is being applied to peak-cycle earnings from a shrinking addressable footprint. The 72% spread between the $290 low and $500 high analyst targets is precisely this disagreement, priced.
