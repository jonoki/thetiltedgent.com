01 Company Overview

lululemon designs and sells technical athletic apparel — the $100-plus yoga legging is the archetype — mostly through its own stores and its own website. It reports three regions: the Americas, China Mainland and the Rest of World. Fiscal 2025 (the 53-week year ended February 1, 2026) brought revenue of $11.10B, up 4.86% from $10.59B, with diluted EPS of $13.26. Underneath that total, two companies were pulling in opposite directions: the Americas shrank 1% while China Mainland grew 29% and the Rest of World 16%.

This is a report about a brand that the market no longer trusts to sell leggings to Americans. The stock closed December 2023 at $511.29; it settled on September 21, 2026 at $101.30 — down 80.2% from that month-end, and 75.0% below its September 2021 close. The descent came with a full set of side-plots: CEO Calvin McDonald announced his departure in December 2025, the stock was dropped from the NASDAQ-100 the same month, founder Chip Wilson ran a proxy contest that ended in a May 2026 settlement, and a new CEO, former Nike executive Heidi O'Neill, took the chair on September 8, 2026 — four days after the stock fell 17.38% in a single session on the Q2 print. The full-year outlook has now been cut twice since March. The question for the table is simple to state and hard to price: at roughly 8.3x trailing earnings, is this a franchise with a fixable merchandise problem, or a fashion brand whose moment has passed and whose earnings are still on the way down?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Brand & pricing power (historically) Vertical model: own stores + own e-commerce Technical-fabric product reputation 825-store global footprint International growth runway Cash generation (TTM FCF $1.35B)

The moat was always the brand, and a brand moat behaves differently from a switching-cost moat: there is nothing to stop a customer walking to the next shop. What lululemon does own is the whole chain from design to till — it keeps the retailer's margin and sees its customer's data — which is why fiscal 2025 gross margin was still 56.6% after falling 260 bps. That structure cuts both ways. When demand is strong, owning the stores magnifies profit; when comparable sales fall 9%, the rent, staff and depreciation do not fall with them. The company's own Q2 commentary makes the point: fixed costs rose 230 bps as a share of revenue, and SG&A deleveraged by 400 bps. The vulnerability is not that the franchise can be copied. It is that it no longer needs to be — customers can simply buy something else.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The core market is in outright decline. Americas comparable sales −12% in Q2 FY2026 against −3% for fiscal 2025. BMO, initiating at Underperform on September 8–9, 2026, argued that demand is declining in both the Americas and China and that the premium margin structure is unwinding (as reported by Investing.com).
02
Reported earnings are flattered by a one-off. Q2 diluted EPS of $2.92 includes $0.86 from tariff refunds; underlying, about $2.06 (our calculation). Full-year guidance of $9.48–$9.73 also includes the $0.86 — so the underlying run-rate the market is capitalising is closer to $8.62–$8.87 (our calculation), about 28% below the original $12.10–$12.30 guided in March.
03
International is no longer a clean offset. International revenue grew 22% in fiscal 2025 and 22% in Q1 FY2026, then 4% in Q2 (2% constant dollar), with China comps −8% constant dollar. The stated FY2026 revenue outlook of $10.35–$10.50B implies a 5–7% decline for the year.
04
Governance has been a distraction. A CEO exit, a seven-month interim co-CEO structure, a founder proxy contest (settled May 27, 2026, with two Wilson nominees — Laura Gentile and Marc Maurer — joining the board and a further apparel-expert director due by October 1) and reported activist involvement. Proxy-contest fees appear in the Q2 SG&A bridge. The new CEO starts with a reconstituted board and a founder who publicly questioned her appointment.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the IEEPA tariff refund: $134.5M in Q2 FY2026 plus $4.1M of interest, worth $0.86 of diluted EPS and embedded in full-year guidance; the company says guidance excludes any further refunds. Second, the 53rd week: fiscal 2025 had 53 weeks, and excluding it revenue growth was 7% rather than 5%, so FY2026 comparisons start from an inflated base. Third, buybacks: the Q2 diluted share count was 112.9M against 119.7M a year earlier, a 5.7% reduction (our calculation), so per-share earnings have fallen less than profit. Proxy-contest fees also ran through Q2 SG&A; their size was not disclosed in the commentary.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 close of $404.70 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $101.30, LULU lost 75.0% of its price. There is no dividend to soften that, so price return is total return. The shape is a mountain, not a slope: a choppy 2022 between roughly $270 and $380, a climb through 2023 to a $511.29 December 2023 close — the highest month-end in the series, two months after S&P 500 inclusion — and then almost uninterrupted decline. From that peak the stock has fallen 80.2%, and it is down 51.3% since the December 2025 close of $207.81.

The 6-period monthly RSI reads 21.4 at the September 21 point — the lowest reading anywhere in the series (our calculation from the chart data), below the 22.2 of June 2026 and the 23.2 of July 2024. The 10-month moving average sits near $144, some 43% above the price. The trend is unambiguously down and momentum is at its most stretched anywhere in the series. Oversold is a description, not a signal: the stock has printed sub-30 RSI readings in ten of the last eighteen months without a sustained recovery.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 8.3x trailing and 10.5x guided earnings, roughly 5x EV/EBITDA and a ~12% free-cash-flow yield for a brand that still earns a 23.7% return on invested capital, still grew outside the Americas in Q2, and still carries a 54.9% trailing gross margin. Inventory units are down 7%, so the markdown cycle may be closer to its end than its beginning. A new CEO with long experience in women's athletic apparel, a board refreshed with apparel and marketing talent, and a founder and reported activist both pushing for change give the turnaround real sponsors. With 13.24% of the float short and 29 of 35 analysts at Hold, the setup is one where "less bad" could move the stock — and management has been buying back shares at prices well above today's.

Bear Case

The low multiple is on earnings that are still falling. Strip out the $0.86 tariff refund and FY2026 EPS guidance is about $8.62–$8.87, down roughly 28% from what management guided in March; Q3 is guided to revenue down 10–11%. Americas comps are −12% and accelerating downward, China comps turned negative, and international growth slowed from 22% to 4% in one quarter. A vertically integrated retailer with a growing store base and shrinking same-store sales suffers operating deleverage every quarter the trend continues. BMO's $70 target and $6.35 FY2027 EPS estimate describe the scenario in which the "cheap" P/E is simply what a de-rating brand looks like on the way down.

Top 3 Risks

01
Brand erosion in the Americas. Two-thirds of revenue, and Americas revenue has gone from −1% in fiscal 2025 to −3% in Q1 FY2026 to −8% in Q2, with Q2 comparable sales −12%. If this is a brand-relevance problem rather than a product-cycle problem, no amount of cost control fixes it.
02
Earnings quality and further guidance cuts. Full-year guidance has been cut twice since March and now leans on a one-off refund. A new CEO has every incentive to reset expectations low in her first report.
03
Tariffs and margin structure. The Q2 bridge shows higher tariffs and markdowns costing 150 bps of product margin before the refund, and fixed costs deleveraging 230 bps. Trade policy remains outside the company's control, and the refund that flattered Q2 is not in the run-rate.
Industry Commentary
Premium athleisure has lost its pricing power
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The pattern in lululemon's own disclosures is a premium brand defending price through promotion: markdowns and credit-card affiliate programmes are named in the Q2 margin bridge, alongside "higher pricing". BMO's initiation framed the result as demand declining in both the Americas and China with the premium margin structure unwinding (as reported by Investing.com). The strategic question for O'Neill is whether lululemon can restore full-price demand through product and marketing — the Q2 commentary cites higher brand spend — before the fixed-cost base does more damage.

Tariffs — a cost and, briefly, a refund
ONGOING HEADWIND

U.S. trade policy lands directly in lululemon's gross margin. The company's forward-looking statements list the elimination of the de minimis exemption among its risks, and Q2 shows the two sides of the same policy: higher tariffs pressed product margin, while $134.5M of IEEPA tariff refunds added 560 bps. Guidance assumes no further refunds. Investors should read every margin figure this year twice — once as reported, once without the refund.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $101.30 the market is valuing lululemon at about $11.2B — roughly one times trailing revenue — for a business generating $1.35B of trailing free cash flow and returning it through buybacks. The bull framing: this is a brand in a cyclical fashion trough with a new CEO, a cleaner inventory position, a refreshed board and a crowded short, priced as if the decline is permanent. The bear framing: fashion brands do not mean-revert on schedule, and a business whose Americas comps are −12% while it keeps opening stores is capitalising a falling earnings stream at a multiple that only looks low against yesterday's profit. The adjudicator is Americas comparable sales: one quarter of improvement would matter more than any multiple argument.
