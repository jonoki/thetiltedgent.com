01 Company Overview

For fifty years Southwest sold one idea: no assigned seats, two bags fly free, and one kind of airplane. Founded in 1967 by Herb Kelleher and Rollin King and flying since June 1971 out of Dallas Love Field, it grew into an all-Boeing 737 airline of 803 aircraft serving more than 100 destinations. The ticker says the rest: LUV. Then in June 2024 an activist, Elliott Investment Management, disclosed a roughly $1.9 billion stake and argued that the charm had stopped paying. Within two years the bags stopped flying free (from May 28, 2025), open seating ended (assigned seating began January 27, 2026), the company made its first-ever mass layoff (about 1,750 corporate roles, ~15% of corporate staff, in 2025), and it added premium seats, extra legroom, basic economy fares and red-eye flights.

The reinvention is showing up in the numbers. TTM revenue is $30.07B, up 9.5%, against FY2025's $28.06B, and TTM GAAP EPS is $1.60, roughly double FY2025's $0.79. In Q2 2026 RASM rose 16.2% on capacity up just 0.2%. CFO Tom Doxey told a September 16 conference that the share of passengers buying paid extras has gone "from less than 20% historically to about 60% now." The trouble is fuel. Southwest ended its fuel-hedging program in early 2025, and in March 2026 WTI crude hit $104.69 after, as 24/7 Wall St. reported, the Strait of Hormuz closed during the U.S.–Iran conflict. Q2 fuel cost $3.92 a gallon versus $2.32 a year earlier, about $889M of extra expense in one quarter. The full-year adjusted EPS guide went from "at least $4.00" to $3.25–$4.25. So the question for this stock is whether the new revenue engine can outrun an unhedged fuel bill.

The airline arithmetic: RASM vs. CASM

An airline is a spread business. It earns revenue per seat-mile (RASM) and pays cost per seat-mile (CASM). CASM-X removes fuel, so it shows the part of cost that management actually controls. In Q2 2026 the spread was thin: 17.91¢ earned vs 17.30¢ spent per seat-mile. The 0.61¢ margin × 47.1B seat-miles ≈ $287M, which ties to reported operating income of $285M.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Single-type 737 fleet Dallas Love Field home base ~100M Rapid Rewards members Chase co-brand economics #1 J.D. Power economy satisfaction (5 yrs) Investment-grade balance sheet

The old moat was simplicity. With one aircraft type, pilots, mechanics and spare parts are interchangeable, and a point-to-point network with fast turns kept costs below the hub carriers'. The new strategy deliberately gives up part of that simplicity to earn more per seat: premium rows, fewer seats on the 737-700s, lounges, a premium card. The customer franchise has so far survived the changes. Southwest ranked #1 in J.D. Power customer satisfaction among economy passengers for a fifth straight year, and its CFO says it was first in the Wall Street Journal's 2025 operational rankings. What it gave up is the "different from everyone else" story that made price-sensitive leisure travellers loyal without a loyalty program.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
No fuel hedge. Southwest ended its hedging program in early 2025, so every move in jet fuel goes straight to earnings. In the August 2026 sell-off, per 24/7 Wall St., this is why Southwest fell 14% while the hedged peers it names lost about 11–12%. Management put the year-to-date fuel headwind at $1.33 per share when it cut guidance on July 23.
02
Thin unit margin. A 0.61¢ RASM-over-CASM spread in Q2 is only about 3.4% of revenue. A 1¢ rise in fuel cost per seat-mile would more than wipe it out. The initiatives have widened the revenue side, but not by enough to make the business fuel-proof.
03
The brand trade-off is not yet tested through a downturn. The new fees and seats were introduced into strong demand. How loyal travellers stay once free bags and open seating are gone will only become clear when demand softens. Q2 revenue passengers were already down 3.3% year over year, even though yield more than made up for it.
04
Boeing dependency. An all-737 fleet means Boeing's delivery schedule is Southwest's growth schedule. The 737 MAX 7 is expected to be delivered around year-end 2026 and enter service in early 2027, and 27 already-built aircraft are in storage awaiting specification updates. Any further certification slip delays the retirement of the 137-seat 737-700s.

Notable One-Off Items

Two items distort the reported figures. First, the $285M breakage reversal in Q2 2026. That is revenue booked in 2022–2025 for flight credits Southwest assumed would expire unused, taken back because customers are redeeming more of them. It is why Q2 GAAP EPS was $0.47 while adjusted EPS was $0.94 (+120% YoY). Second, buybacks: $1.25B repurchased in the first half of 2026 under a $2.0B authorization, with $450M remaining, which shrank the share count 13.0% year over year. Per-share growth is therefore much faster than profit growth. The $209M of remaining premium costs on the old hedge program, which run through 2027, are a small additional drag.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $51.43 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $41.77, LUV lost 18.8% of its price over five years. No dividend was paid in FY2021. It resumed in FY2022 and has been $0.72 a year since 2023, which recovers only part of that loss. The shape matters more than the endpoints. The stock spent 2022–2024 grinding lower to a $22.23 close in October 2023, less than half the starting level. Elliott arrived in mid-2024 and the business model was rebuilt. The stock then rose to $51.42 in June 2026, a close almost identical to where the window began, and fell back 18.8% in two months as fuel costs ate the guidance.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows the change of regime. It hit 15.4 in June 2022 and 23.4 at the October 2023 low, then climbed to 83.6 in February 2026, the most overbought reading in the window, the month after assigned seating launched. It now reads 51.2, neutral. The 10-month moving average is about $43.32, just above the price, so the medium-term trend is flat to slightly down rather than broken.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The reinvention is working. RASM is up 16–20% on flat capacity, paid-ancillary take-up has gone from under 20% to about 60%, and CASM-X is growing at a fifth of the rate of unit revenue. Management says the initiatives are worth more than $2B of EBIT in 2026, with seating alone at about $1.5B in 2027. Consensus EPS is $3.22 for FY2026 and $4.73 for FY2027. At $41.77 the stock trades at 8.8x FY2027 consensus, the share count is falling 13% a year, and the balance sheet remains investment grade. If oil simply stops rising, the earnings power in Q2's "well ahead of consensus" quarter should flow through.

Bear Case

Southwest has swapped a cost advantage for a pricing strategy and removed its fuel hedge at the same moment. Q2 unit margin was 0.61¢ per seat-mile. The FY2026 guide has already been cut from "at least $4.00" to a range whose bottom is $3.25. Free cash flow has been negative every year from FY2022 to FY2025 while the company spends $1.25B in a half-year on buybacks. Passenger counts are falling. Four analysts rate the stock a sell, and targets run as low as $35. At 26x trailing GAAP earnings, the stock is priced on a forecast that depends on the oil price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Unhedged jet fuel. Q2 fuel was $3.92 a gallon versus $2.32, costing $889M more year over year. There is no hedge book to absorb another spike like March 2026's.
02
Customer response to the new model. Revenue passengers fell 3.3% in Q2 even as yield rose 15.4%. If the lost volume grows faster than price can make up for it, the RASM story ends.
03
Capital intensity and Boeing. TTM capex of $2.93B exceeds operating cash flow. The fleet plan (64 737-8s in 2026, the MAX 7 from early 2027) depends on Boeing delivering on time.
Industry Commentary
Fuel shock without a hedge
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Per 24/7 Wall St., the closure of the Strait of Hormuz during the U.S.–Iran conflict pushed WTI up 56.3% in a month to $104.69 by March 30, 2026. Southwest had terminated its hedging program in early 2025 and still carries $209M of legacy hedge premium costs through 2027, so it absorbed the shock at spot prices. Management cut 2026 capacity growth to about 1–1.5% and widened the EPS range. It still guided Q4 capacity up 4–4.5% year over year, a bet that the revenue initiatives can cover fuel.

Activism as a business plan
EMERGING TAILWIND

Most activist campaigns end in a board reshuffle. Elliott's changed Southwest's product: bags, seats, fare classes, lounges, cards, and a first-ever layoff. By early 2026 the activist had cut its stake from a 16% peak to about 9% and two of its directors had left, which suggests it thinks the job is largely done. The bull framing: the airline now charges for extras the way its rivals do while keeping a low-cost structure, a combination that did not exist before. The bear framing: the easy wins (turning free things into paid ones) are one-offs that are now being lapped. Q3 RASM guidance already includes the headwind from lapping bag fees, and from here growth has to come from harder work.

Unit revenue vs. inflation
ONGOING DEBATE

The CFO said in September that RASM is "still below 2019 inflation-adjusted levels." Read one way, that means further room to run: pricing has not yet caught up with the cost base. Read the other way, the whole 2025–2026 revenue surge has only just recovered lost ground. Q3's 24% yield growth and 30% corporate revenue growth support the first reading. The October 21 print is the next data point.
