01 Company Overview

Las Vegas Sands is an American-listed company that operates no casinos in America. It sold The Venetian, The Palazzo and the Sands Expo & Convention Center in 2022, and what remains is an Asian gaming business with a Nevada corporate address: five integrated resorts in Macao (held through majority-owned, Hong Kong-listed Sands China) and one building in Singapore — Marina Bay Sands. That is the entire company. FY2025 revenue was $13.02B, up 15.2% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue is $13.72B, up 18.1%. At a $29.2B market capitalisation it is the largest US-listed casino operator.

A correction to a widely repeated claim. This report was commissioned on the premise that LVS is the last casino operator standing in the S&P 500. The sources I could reach do not support that. CDC Gaming's report on the September 22, 2025 index change states plainly that "MGM Resorts International, Wynn Resorts, and Las Vegas Sands Corp. qualify to remain with the S&P 500," while Caesars Entertainment was moved down to the S&P MidCap 400 and Penn Entertainment "got booted in 2022." What is true, and is the more interesting fact anyway: the casino cohort in the index has been shrinking — two operators removed since 2022 — and LVS is the only US-listed casino operator with essentially no US casino revenue. An investor buying LVS is buying Chinese and Singaporean consumer discretionary spending in a New York wrapper. Where this brief conflicted with the source, the source wins.

A vocabulary note before the numbers, because casino accounting has its own dialect. GGR (gross gaming revenue — the total amount players lose to the house before any operating costs) is the top-line measure of a gaming market. Property EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, measured at the individual resort before corporate overhead) is the metric operators and analysts actually use. Hold rate is the percentage of money wagered that the house keeps — it is statistically stable over millions of hands but swings violently over a single quarter. VIP play is high-stakes credit-backed baccarat; mass is walk-in cash play; premium mass is the wealthy end of walk-in play. A junket was the middleman that recruited VIP gamblers and extended them credit. A concession is the government licence that permits a company to run casinos at all.

The single most valuable casino asset in the world by property EBITDA, and it earns that title on structure rather than scale. Singapore licenses two integrated resorts, full stop — MBS and Genting's Resorts World Sentosa. This is a legally enforced duopoly, and it is a categorically different competitive position from Macao, where LVS is one of six licensed operators fighting for the same visitor. A duopolist does not have to discount to fill rooms. That is precisely what a 49.9% property EBITDA margin looks like on the income statement, against 24.0% for the Macao portfolio, and it is why the market pays for Singapore earnings and merely tolerates Macao earnings. Q2 2026 occupancy was 95.6%. Revenue was essentially flat year over year and EBITDA fell from $768M to $689M — but that decline is misleading in the bull's favour: MBS benefited from $37M of favourable hold in the quarter, so underlying performance was softer than the headline, while the prior-year comparison was itself elevated. Management describes MBS as continuing "to deliver industry-leading financial performance," and on these margins that is not marketing.

The expansion (IR2). LVS is building a second tower: an $8.0 billion project comprising 570 luxury suites, a 15,000-seat arena, 110,000 net square feet of premium facilities and rooftop attractions. Construction commenced May 2025, estimated completion June 2030, anticipated opening January 2031, subject to government approval. It consumed $215M of the company's $332M total capex in Q2 2026 alone and is backed by a dedicated $4.68B delayed draw term loan. This is the company's single largest capital commitment and the clearest statement of where it believes its returns are. It is also a five-year cash drain before a dollar of revenue arrives.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SINGAPORE DUOPOLY LICENCE MACAO CONCESSION (1 OF 6) IRREPLACEABLE COTAI LAND 25% MACAO MASS SHARE MICE / CONVENTION SCALE $8B IR2 CAPITAL DEPTH FAMILY-CONTROLLED LONG HORIZON

LVS's moat is not brand and it is not technology — it is permission. Two governments have decided that a very small number of companies may operate casinos, and LVS holds a seat at both tables. In Singapore there are two licences and LVS holds one; that is the strongest competitive position in the entire global gaming industry, and its 49.9% property EBITDA margin is the receipt. In Macao there are six concessions and LVS holds one, which is a weaker but still formidable barrier — no amount of capital lets a seventh operator in. Underneath the licences sits genuinely scarce physical inventory: the Cotai Strip parcels cannot be replicated, the Venetian's convention and exhibition footprint is the largest in the market, and MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences and exhibitions) capacity is what converts a casino into a weekday business. Sands China holds 25.0% of Macao mass gaming revenue (up 100 basis points year over year) and 29.3% of the entire Macao market's EBITDA — it takes a disproportionate share of the market's profit relative to its share of its revenue, which is the definition of a quality position. Family control, at 59.7%, adds a long time horizon and a willingness to spend $8B on a building that opens in 2031; it also removes any prospect of a takeover, an activist, or a board that can be pressured.

Concession expiry is existential and unhedgeable. The moat has an expiry date printed on it. Sands China's current Macao gaming concession is a ten-year licence signed in December 2022 and running to 2032. When it lapses, the Macao SAR government decides — at its sole discretion, on criteria it sets — whether LVS continues to operate casinos in Macao at all. There is no insurance product, no derivative, no diversification within Macao that protects against this. Roughly 57% of revenue and 38% of EBITDA rest on a decision by a government that has already demonstrated, in the 2021–2022 junket crackdown, that it will restructure this industry unilaterally and at speed. This single fact is the largest reason LVS trades at 8.9× EV/EBITDA rather than the mid-teens multiple its margins would otherwise command.

Mandated investment is a tax that does not look like one. The concession was not free. On winning it, Sands China committed to invest MOP 30 billion (~US$3.75 billion) through 2032, of which MOP 27.8 billion (~US$3.5 billion) is non-gaming — MICE facilities, a ~50,000 sq m garden-themed destination, Cotai Arena reinvestment, restaurants, a yacht experience, six training academies, heritage programmes. As of the Q2 2026 materials the figure is stated as at least $4.5 billion through 2032, with 93% allocated to non-gaming. Read that carefully: the government has contractually directed billions of shareholder capital into the lowest-returning parts of the business, and it grades operators on their non-gaming contribution. This is a permanent drag on returns on invested capital that no competitor escapes either — but it means Macao capital is not deployed where it earns the most, it is deployed where it renews the licence.

Concentration risk is total. Two jurisdictions, six buildings, one currency bloc's consumer. There is no third leg. A Singapore regulatory change, a Macao visa-policy tightening, a Chinese consumer recession, or a typhoon that closes Cotai for a week all hit with no offset. Contrast this with MGM or Caesars, whose regional US portfolios are diversified across dozens of jurisdictions. LVS deliberately sold its diversification in 2022 to concentrate on Asia — a decision that has been rewarded with higher margins and punished with a lower multiple.

Balance-sheet fragility relative to the earnings stream. Total debt of $15.28B against $3.38B of cash and book equity under $1B. The stated debt-to-equity ratio of 17.0× is arithmetically extreme because years of buybacks have all but erased book equity — 124 million shares, 16.3% of the company, repurchased since Q4 2023. The current ratio is exactly 1.00 and the Altman Z-Score is 2.75, in the grey zone. This is a manageable position for a business generating $4.64B of trailing EBITDA — but it is not a fortress, and it is being levered further to fund an $8B project.

Notable One-Off Items — and why EBITDA is the working metric here. Las Vegas Sands owns roughly $30B of buildings. Depreciation and amortisation on that base is enormous and non-cash, and it swamps reported net income: the company converted $3.19B of operating income into $1.73B of net income on the trailing twelve months, and $4.64B of EBITDA into that same $1.73B. Reported EPS therefore tells you more about the vintage of the concrete than about the trading performance of the casinos. Analysts, management and the debt covenants all work in adjusted property EBITDA — profit measured at each resort before corporate overhead, interest, tax and depreciation — because that is the number that approximates the cash the buildings actually throw off. Every operating figure in this report is stated on that basis.

The material non-recurring distortion in the most recent quarter is hold-rate variance, and it is large enough to invalidate a naïve reading of Q2 2026. Unfavourable hold on rolling play cost Macao $87 million of EBITDA; favourable hold at Marina Bay Sands added $37 million. That is a $124 million combined swing against theoretical expectation on a quarter that reported $1.12B. Hold-adjusted Macao EBITDA would have been $517M at a 26.7% margin rather than the reported $430M at 24.0% — a 20% difference caused by nothing but the sequence of cards. This is not an accounting adjustment; it is the arithmetic of a business whose revenue is a probability distribution. Over a year hold normalises. Over a quarter it can make a good business look broken, and it did here. Also worth flagging: the buyback authorisation was increased to $6.0B (through July 21, 2029) and $787M was deployed in the quarter at a $52.37 weighted average — above the current price.

In plain English: RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been. Above 70 the stock has risen so persistently that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 it has fallen so persistently that sellers may be. It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a year.

Five years, and essentially nowhere. On a dividend-adjusted basis LVS went from $34.56 in September 2021 to $45.04 at the August 28, 2026 close — a total return of about +30.3%, roughly a 5.4% compound annual rate. That is a poor five years in absolute terms and it conceals two enormous round trips: a rally from $31.72 in June 2022 to $60.30 in April 2023 as China reopened (+90%), a slide back to $35.72 by April 2025 (−41%), a surge to a cycle-closing high of $67.41 in November 2025 (+89%), and then a nine-month, −33% decline to today. Over the trailing twelve months the stock is −23.4%. The $45.04 close sits just 3.1% above the 52-week low of $43.70 and 36.1% below the 52-week high of $70.46. (That $43.70 low was itself printed intraday on August 31, the session after the reference close.)

Why the swings are this violent — operating leverage, quantified. An integrated resort is almost entirely fixed cost. The building, the 41,500 employees, the utilities, the concession obligations and the debt service are all there whether the casino floor is full or empty. Every incremental dollar of gaming revenue drops through to EBITDA at a very high rate; every dollar lost comes out at the same rate. Q2 2026 is the textbook case, delivered by the company itself: revenue fell 0.9% and EBITDA fell 15.8%. That is roughly 17× amplification in a single quarter. The Venetian Macao alone turned an 11% revenue decline into a 30% EBITDA decline; Plaza/Four Seasons turned a 29% revenue decline into a 70% EBITDA decline. The mechanism is symmetric — this is the same leverage that produced the +89% rally into November 2025 — but investors reliably underestimate it in both directions, which is precisely why the stock travels this far on modest changes in Macao visitation. Layer hold-rate variance on top, worth $124M of EBITDA swing in Q2 alone, and you have a security whose quarterly results are genuinely noisy around a much steadier underlying business.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the world's best casino asset — Marina Bay Sands, a 49.9%-margin property inside a two-licence government-protected duopoly, currently being expanded by $8.0B — for 8.9× EV/EBITDA and 13.7× forward earnings, plus a free option on Macao normalising. The Q2 miss was hold-rate variance, not demand: volumes grew in every Macao segment, mass gaming revenue rose 8%, and hold-adjusted Macao EBITDA was $517M against management's $700M/quarter target. Meanwhile the company is retiring stock aggressively — 16.3% of shares since Q4 2023 — and the consensus target of $59.07 implies 31% upside with no analyst target below the current price.

Bear Case

Fifty-seven percent of revenue depends on a licence that expires in 2032, granted by a government that has already dismantled this industry's business model once and that now directs billions of shareholder capital into non-gaming projects it grades you on. Growth has stopped — Q2 revenue was −0.9% — and the operating leverage that produced the +89% rally works identically in reverse, converting a 0.9% revenue decline into a 15.8% EBITDA decline. Book equity is under $1B against $15.28B of debt, the current ratio is exactly 1.00, and the company is levering further into an $8B project that opens in 2031. And a family controlling 59.7% — a stake that grows every time the buyback runs — means minority holders have influence over none of it.

01
Macao concession non-renewal or punitive renewal terms (2032). The most consequential and least analysable risk in the business. The Macao SAR government decides unilaterally whether Sands China continues to operate, and on what investment conditions. There is no hedge, no insurance and no diversification within Macao that mitigates it — and the 2021–22 junket crackdown established that Beijing will restructure the industry at speed when it chooses to.
02
Chinese consumer weakness transmitted through 17× operating leverage. Macao GGR tracks mainland Chinese discretionary spending and travel policy. Because LVS's cost base is almost entirely fixed, a modest visitation decline produces a severe EBITDA decline — as Q2 2026 demonstrated, and as the −37.6% drawdown from April 2023 to August 2024 demonstrated before it. Add visa policy, currency and any renewed anti-corruption campaign, and the earnings distribution has a genuinely fat left tail.
03
Balance-sheet and capital-allocation strain through the IR2 build. $15.28B of debt, $3.38B of cash, a 1.00 current ratio and an Altman Z-Score of 2.75 in the grey zone, while committing $8.0B to Singapore and at least $4.5B to Macao through 2032 — with the Macao spend contractually 93% non-gaming, i.e. directed at the lowest-returning assets in the portfolio. If Macao EBITDA does not recover toward the $700M/quarter target, the company is funding a 2031 opening and a $6.0B buyback out of a shrinking cash flow base.
  Industry Commentary
The VIP Business Is Gone, and That Is Mostly Good News
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
For two decades Macao ran on junkets — licensed middlemen who recruited wealthy mainland gamblers, extended them credit, and collected the debts back in China where gambling debts are unenforceable. It produced spectacular headline GGR at thin margins: junkets took large commissions, the operator carried counterparty risk on credit it could not legally collect, and the whole apparatus sat in an uncomfortable relationship with capital controls. Beijing dismantled it in 2021–22. The junket industry effectively ceased to exist, and Macao's total market shrank dramatically.

Here is the part that gets missed: a smaller Macao is a better Macao for LVS. Mass and premium-mass play is cash, settled on the spot, with no commission to a middleman and no credit risk — it carries far higher margins per dollar of revenue than VIP ever did. Non-gaming spend at hotels, retail and restaurants comes attached to it. The result is that a market with much lower GGR than 2019 supports properties earning more than 2019: management states The Londoner and Four Seasons are "operating above 2019 normalized levels on a hold-adjusted basis." Q2 2026 confirms where the business is now — Macao mass gaming revenue of roughly $1.7B, up 8%, with rolling table volumes up 73%, non-rolling up 15% and slots/ETG up 30%. The remaining VIP-linked rolling business is now small enough that it functions mainly as a source of quarterly earnings noise rather than a profit centre: it caused the $87M unfavourable hold hit in Q2 while contributing a fraction of the profit. The constraint is real — the ceiling on Macao is lower than it was — but the floor is higher and the earnings are cleaner.
Six Operators in Macao, Two in Singapore — and the Gap Between Them Is the Whole Thesis
ONGOING HEADWIND
Set the two markets side by side. In Macao, six licensed operators — LVS, Galaxy, MGM China, Wynn Macau, Melco and SJM — compete for the same finite pool of mainland visitors on a concession that expires in 2032, while each is contractually obliged to spend billions on non-gaming amenities the government grades them on. The predictable equilibrium: promotional intensity, reinvestment competition, and a portfolio EBITDA margin of 24.0%. In Singapore there are two licences. LVS holds one. The margin is 49.9%. Same company, same management, same operating playbook — twice the margin, produced almost entirely by the number of competitors the government permits.

That gap is why capital is flowing the way it is. Of $332M of Q2 2026 capex, $215M went to Singapore and $86M to Macao, and the $8.0B IR2 project is the largest commitment in the company's history. LVS is voting with its balance sheet: it is expanding where it is protected and maintaining where it is not. The headwind is that Macao is still 57% of revenue and cannot be exited, so the blended business will keep carrying a six-operator market's economics and a sovereign licence risk that Singapore does not impose.
What Would Have to Be True — Framing the Bull and Bear Cases
EMERGING TAILWIND
For the bull: hold normalises and Macao EBITDA moves from $430M reported toward $517M hold-adjusted and then toward management's $700M/quarter goal; operating-expense growth moderates in H2 2026 as renovations complete, exactly as guided; Marina Bay Sands holds its ~50% margin through the construction period; and the 2032 concession renewal proceeds without incident because Sands China has visibly delivered its 93%-non-gaming investment programme. On that path, 17× operating leverage works upward from a $13.7B revenue base, and 8.9× EV/EBITDA on a duopoly-anchored asset base is plainly too cheap — the $59.07 consensus target is not heroic.

For the bear: Chinese discretionary spending stays soft, Macao's six operators keep competing away the margin recovery, and the same leverage grinds EBITDA down while $8B of Singapore capex and a $6.0B buyback drain a balance sheet already at a 1.00 current ratio and sub-$1B book equity. Then 2032 arrives with the government holding every card. The honest framing: LVS is a high-quality operator with two genuinely excellent assets, priced at a discount that is not a mistake — it is compensation for a sovereign risk that cannot be modelled, only sized. An investor's real question is not whether the casinos are good. They are. It is how much of the portfolio should sit on a licence that a government can decline to renew.
