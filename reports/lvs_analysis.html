01 Company Overview

Las Vegas Sands is an American-listed company that operates no casinos in America. It sold The Venetian, The Palazzo and the Sands Expo & Convention Center in 2022, and what remains is an Asian gaming business with a Nevada corporate address: five integrated resorts in Macao (held through Hong Kong-listed Sands China, 75.6% owned at September 30, 2026) and one building in Singapore — Marina Bay Sands. That is the entire company. FY2025 revenue was $13.02B, up 15.2% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue is $13.60B, up 10.9% — but the last two quarters both came in below the year before.

Where it sits in the index. When Caesars Entertainment was moved out of the S&P 500 in September 2025, CDC Gaming reported that MGM Resorts, Wynn Resorts and Las Vegas Sands all qualified to stay. Of those three, LVS is the only one with essentially no US casino revenue: MGM and Wynn both run casinos in Las Vegas. An investor buying LVS is buying Chinese and Singaporean consumer spending in a New York wrapper.

A vocabulary note before the numbers, because casino accounting has its own dialect. GGR (gross gaming revenue — the total amount players lose to the house before any operating costs) is the top-line measure of a gaming market. Property EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, measured at the individual resort before corporate overhead) is the metric operators and analysts actually use. Hold rate is the percentage of money wagered that the house keeps — it is statistically stable over millions of hands but swings violently over a single quarter. VIP play is high-stakes credit-backed baccarat, measured as rolling chip volume — the only play whose luck LVS adjusts for in its "hold-adjusted" figures; mass is walk-in cash play, measured as drop (cash exchanged for chips at the table); premium mass is the wealthy end of walk-in play. A junket was the middleman that recruited VIP gamblers and extended them credit. A concession is the government licence that permits a company to run casinos at all.

The company's most profitable property — $2.87B of property EBITDA over the last four quarters, more than all five Macao resorts together — and it earns that on structure rather than scale. Singapore licenses two integrated resorts, full stop — MBS and Genting's Resorts World Sentosa. This is a legally enforced duopoly, and it is a categorically different competitive position from Macao, where LVS is one of six licensed operators fighting for the same visitor. A duopolist does not have to discount to fill rooms, which is why the margin was still 45.6% in a bad quarter. Q3 2026 was that bad quarter: revenue fell from $1,436M to $1,294M and EBITDA from $743M to $590M. The luck the company adjusts for was not the cause — rolling-chip hold cost only $4M. Dumont's explanation on the call: "We just know that our highest value patrons visited less. And to be fair, we did experience some more hold." Mass-table win fell to 18.7% from 24.5% a year earlier, while mass drop rose 12.6%. The rooms did fine: occupancy 95.8%, average room rate $1,038, up $56. Dumont called the quarter "aberrational" and said: "This is a $2.7 billion, $2.8 billion run rate property in my mind." Over the last four quarters it earned $2.87B.

The expansion (IR2). LVS is building a second tower: an $8.0 billion project — about $2 billion of it land premiums, the up-front price of the land lease, Dumont said on the call — with 570 luxury suites and a 15,000-seat arena. The groundbreaking ceremony was held in July 2025 and the company expects to open in early 2031, subject to government approvals. It took $121M of the company's $281M capex in Q3, and $4.57B remains available under a delayed draw term loan (a loan drawn as construction bills come in) set up for the project. This is the company's largest current capital commitment, more than twice its Macao concession pledge, and the clearest statement of where it believes its returns are. It is also four more years of cash out before a dollar of revenue arrives.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SINGAPORE DUOPOLY LICENCE MACAO CONCESSION (1 OF 6) IRREPLACEABLE COTAI LAND 26% MACAO MASS SHARE MICE / CONVENTION SCALE $8B IR2 CAPITAL DEPTH FAMILY-CONTROLLED LONG HORIZON

LVS's moat is not brand and it is not technology — it is permission. Two governments have decided that a very small number of companies may operate casinos, and LVS holds a seat at both tables. In Singapore there are two licences and LVS holds one; that is the strongest competitive position in the global gaming industry, and a 45.6% property EBITDA margin in its weakest quarter of the year is the receipt. In Macao there are six concessions and LVS holds one, which is a weaker but still formidable barrier — no amount of capital lets a seventh operator in. Underneath the licences sits genuinely scarce physical inventory: the Cotai Strip parcels cannot be replicated, the Venetian's Cotai Expo gives it a large convention and exhibition business, and MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences and exhibitions) capacity is what converts a casino into a weekday business. Sands China held 26.1% of Macao mass gaming revenue in Q3 2026, its highest share since the fourth quarter of 2023, and Chum said it gained share in every segment while controlling reinvestment, the promotions and rebates paid to win customers. Family control, at about 62%, adds a long time horizon and a willingness to spend $8B on a building that opens in 2031; it also removes any prospect of a takeover, an activist, or a board that can be pressured.

Concession expiry is existential and unhedgeable. The moat has an expiry date printed on it. Sands China's current Macao gaming concession is a ten-year licence signed in December 2022 and running to 2032. When it lapses, the Macao SAR government decides — at its sole discretion, on criteria it sets — whether LVS continues to operate casinos in Macao at all. There is no insurance product, no derivative, no diversification within Macao that protects against this. About 60% of revenue and 47% of property EBITDA in Q3 2026 rest on a decision by a government that has already demonstrated, in the 2021–2022 junket crackdown, that it will restructure this industry unilaterally and at speed. This is the main reason LVS trades at about 6.9× trailing EV/EBITDA (our calculation, see the fine print) rather than the mid-teens multiple its margins would otherwise command.

Mandated investment is a tax that does not look like one. The concession was not free. On winning it, Sands China committed to invest MOP 30 billion (~US$3.75 billion) through 2032, of which MOP 27.8 billion (~US$3.5 billion) is non-gaming — MICE facilities, a ~50,000 sq m garden-themed destination, Cotai Arena reinvestment, restaurants, a yacht experience, six training academies, heritage programmes. That is about 93% non-gaming, and on the Q3 call Chum said the company wants to bring the botanic garden project forward. Read that carefully: the government has contractually directed billions of shareholder capital into the lowest-returning parts of the business, and it grades operators on their non-gaming contribution. This is a permanent drag on returns on invested capital that no competitor escapes either — but it means Macao capital is not deployed where it earns the most, it is deployed where it renews the licence.

Concentration risk is total. Two jurisdictions, six buildings, one currency bloc's consumer. There is no third leg. A Singapore regulatory change, a Macao visa-policy tightening, a Chinese consumer recession, or a typhoon that closes Cotai for a week all hit with no offset. Contrast this with MGM or Caesars, whose regional US portfolios are diversified across dozens of jurisdictions. LVS deliberately sold its diversification in 2022 to concentrate on Asia — a decision that has been rewarded with higher margins and punished with a lower multiple.

Balance-sheet fragility relative to the earnings stream. Total debt of $15.30B (excluding finance leases) against $2.86B of unrestricted cash at September 30, 2026 — and shareholders' equity is now below zero: the 10-Q shows a total deficit of $115M ($344M attributable to LVS). That is not losses; it is buybacks. The company has bought back about 145 million shares, 19.0% of the company, since Q4 2023 for $6.98B. Debt-to-equity and return on equity no longer mean anything. The current ratio is 0.83. Management's guardrail is a gross leverage target (debt divided by EBITDA) of "approximately 2 times to 3 times," before development debt, and Dumont said the company values "maintaining our investment-grade rating." That is a manageable position for a business producing $5.07B of trailing property EBITDA — but it is not a fortress, and it is being levered further to fund an $8B project.

Notable One-Off Items — and why EBITDA is the working metric here. Las Vegas Sands owns a very large base of buildings. Depreciation and amortisation on that base is enormous and non-cash, and it swamps reported net income: over the trailing twelve months the company converted $2.85B of operating income into $1.61B of net income attributable to its shareholders, and $5.07B of consolidated adjusted property EBITDA into that same $1.61B (company releases, Q4 2025 plus the first nine months of 2026). Reported EPS therefore tells you more about the vintage of the concrete than about the trading performance of the casinos. Analysts, management and the debt covenants all work in adjusted property EBITDA — profit measured at each resort before corporate overhead, interest, tax and depreciation — because that is the number that approximates the cash the buildings actually throw off. Every operating figure in this report is stated on that basis.

Q3 2026 is the quarter where the usual explanation runs out. The company's hold adjustment, which covers only rolling chip (VIP) play, moved EBITDA by just $7M — and the other way: Macao held luckily (+$11M) and Marina Bay Sands slightly unluckily (−$4M), so hold-adjusted EBITDA would have been $1.11B, lower than reported. What hurt sits outside that adjustment. Non-rolling (mass-table) win fell to 18.7% from 24.5% at Marina Bay Sands and to 18.6% from 23.4% at The Londoner; Chum said the Londoner shortfall "was primarily due to playing unlucky at the super high-end segment of the premium mass." Dumont said Marina Bay Sands "had less visitation from our highest value patrons," and that in Macao "The drag on our EBITDA was higher expenses" — extra table hours, sales and service staff the company chose to add. Chum said "sequentially we are essentially flat in terms of fixed expenses" now, and the company does not expect significant further hiring. Two other items: the effective tax rate rose to 22.5% from 15.6%, and the company bought $950M of its own stock in the quarter at a $45.31 average — 27% above the $35.68 close.

In plain English: RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been. Above 70 the stock has risen so persistently that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 it has fallen so persistently that sellers may be. It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a year.

Five years, and below where it started. On an actual (non-dividend-adjusted) month-end close basis LVS went from $38.81 at the end of October 2021 to $35.68 at the October 23, 2026 close — a price return of −8.1%, about −1.7% a year. Over the same window SPY rose 65.5% and QQQ 82.4%. That conceals two enormous round trips: a rally from $33.59 in June 2022 to $63.85 in April 2023 as China reopened (+90%), a slide back to $36.67 by April 2025 (−42.6%), a surge to a cycle-closing high of $68.16 in November 2025 (+85.9%), and then an eleven-month, −47.7% decline to the lowest point on the chart since June 2022. Over the trailing twelve months the stock is −37.3%. The $35.68 close sits 1.9% above the 52-week low of $35.03 (an intraday print on October 22, the session after the release) and 49.4% below the 52-week high of $70.45 (December 1, 2025). On daily closes it is 12.7% below its 50-day average ($40.87) and 28.0% below its 200-day average ($49.57), with a 14-day RSI of 30.5, just above the line conventionally read as oversold.

Why the swings are this violent — operating leverage, quantified. An integrated resort is almost entirely fixed cost. The building, the 41,500 employees, the utilities, the concession obligations and the debt service are all there whether the casino floor is full or empty. Every incremental dollar of gaming revenue drops through to EBITDA at a very high rate; every dollar lost comes out at the same rate. The last two quarters are the textbook case, delivered by the company itself: in Q2 2026 revenue fell 0.9% and EBITDA fell 15.8%; in Q3 revenue fell 3.5% and EBITDA fell 16.7%. Marina Bay Sands turned a 9.9% revenue drop into a 20.6% EBITDA drop; The Londoner turned flat revenue into an 18.7% EBITDA decline. The mechanism is symmetric — this is the same leverage that produced the +85.9% rally into November 2025 — but investors reliably underestimate it in both directions, which is why the stock travels this far on modest changes in Macao visitation. Layer luck on top — $124M of EBITDA swing from rolling-chip hold in Q2, and mass-table hold the company does not adjust for in Q3 — and you have a security whose quarterly results are genuinely noisy around a much steadier underlying business.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the world's best casino asset — Marina Bay Sands, which earned $2.87B of EBITDA over the last four quarters inside a two-licence government-protected duopoly and is being expanded by $8.0B — for about 6.9× trailing EV/EBITDA and 11.9× forward earnings, plus a free option on Macao. Management calls Q3 aberrational: MBS room rates rose, its mass-table drop grew 12.6%, mass-table hold ran well below a year earlier, and Dumont still sees "a $2.7 billion, $2.8 billion run rate property." Macao EBITDA recovered from $430M to $529M while Sands China took its highest mass-market share since Q4 2023. Meanwhile the company is retiring stock fast — 19.0% of shares since Q4 2023, $950M in Q3 alone — the dividend goes up 20% in 2027, and the S&P Global consensus target of $57.04 implies 60% upside with no target in either published tally below the current price.

Bear Case

Two straight misses, and this one cannot be pinned on the rolling-chip luck the company adjusts for: its highest-value customers visited less in both markets, and Macao costs are up because the company chose to add staff and table hours. About 60% of revenue depends on a Macao licence that expires in 2032, granted by a government that has already dismantled this industry's business model once and that now directs billions of shareholder capital into non-gaming projects it grades you on. Revenue fell for a second straight quarter (−3.5% in Q3), and operating leverage turned that into a 16.7% EBITDA decline. Shareholders' equity is now negative, debt is $15.30B, the current ratio is 0.83, and the company is spending more on buybacks than it generates in free cash flow while funding an $8B project that opens in 2031. And a family controlling about 62% — a stake that grows every time the buyback runs — means minority holders have influence over none of it.

01
Macao concession non-renewal or punitive renewal terms (2032). The most consequential and least analysable risk in the business. The Macao SAR government decides unilaterally whether Sands China continues to operate, and on what investment conditions. There is no hedge, no insurance and no diversification within Macao that mitigates it — and the 2021–22 junket crackdown established that Beijing will restructure the industry at speed when it chooses to.
02
Chinese consumer weakness transmitted through operating leverage. Macao GGR tracks mainland Chinese discretionary spending and travel policy. Because LVS's cost base is almost entirely fixed, a modest revenue decline produces a severe EBITDA decline — as Q2 and Q3 2026 showed (EBITDA down 15.8% and 16.7% on revenue down 0.9% and 3.5%), and as the −38.9% slide from April 2023 to August 2024 showed before it. Q3 added a new version: management said its highest-value patrons in both markets simply came less often, and could not say why. Add visa policy, currency and any renewed anti-corruption campaign, and the earnings distribution has a genuinely fat left tail.
03
Balance-sheet and capital-allocation strain through the IR2 build. $15.30B of debt, $2.86B of unrestricted cash, negative shareholders' equity and a 0.83 current ratio at September 30, 2026, while committing $8.0B to Singapore and MOP 30 billion (about US$3.75B) to Macao through 2032 — about 93% of the Macao pledge non-gaming, i.e. directed at the lowest-returning assets in the portfolio. If Macao EBITDA does not recover toward the $700M/quarter goal and Singapore stays soft, the company is funding a 2031 opening and a buyback that ran at $950M a quarter out of an EBITDA base that fell 17% this quarter, with the investment-grade rating management says it values as the limit.
  Industry Commentary
The VIP Business Is Gone, and That Is Mostly Good News
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
For two decades Macao ran on junkets — licensed middlemen who recruited wealthy mainland gamblers, extended them credit, and collected the debts back in China where gambling debts are unenforceable. It produced spectacular headline GGR at thin margins: junkets took large commissions, the operator carried counterparty risk on credit it could not legally collect, and the whole apparatus sat in an uncomfortable relationship with capital controls. Beijing dismantled it in 2021–22. The junket industry effectively ceased to exist, and Macao's total market shrank dramatically.

Here is the part that gets missed: a smaller Macao is a better Macao for LVS. Mass and premium-mass play is cash, settled on the spot, with no commission to a middleman and no credit risk — it carries far higher margins per dollar of revenue than VIP ever did. Non-gaming spend at hotels, retail and restaurants comes attached to it. Q3 2026 shows where the business is now: Sands China's gaming volumes grew in every segment (rolling chip volume +31%, non-rolling drop +11%, slot and electronic table game handle +26%), its share of Macao mass gaming revenue reached 26.1%, and Chum said retail tenant sales grew 10% against a weaker consumer backdrop in China. The remaining VIP-linked rolling business is now small enough that it mostly adds quarterly noise: unlucky rolling hold cost Macao $87M of EBITDA in Q2, and lucky hold added $11M in Q3. The newer source of noise is the very top of premium mass, which bets like VIP play but sits outside the company's hold adjustment: unlucky play there pulled The Londoner's margin down to 26.0% in Q3. The constraint is real — the ceiling on Macao is lower than it was — but the floor is higher and the earnings are cleaner.
Six Operators in Macao, Two in Singapore — and the Gap Between Them Is the Whole Thesis
ONGOING HEADWIND
Set the two markets side by side. In Macao, six licensed operators — LVS, Galaxy, MGM China, Wynn Macau, Melco and SJM — compete for the same finite pool of mainland visitors on a concession that expires in 2032, while each is contractually obliged to spend billions on non-gaming amenities the government grades them on. The predictable result: heavy promotions, free rooms and rebates to win customers, and a portfolio EBITDA margin of 27.3% in Q3 2026. On the call Chum said "The market remains very competitive," adding that "the profit margin of the industry is simply not where it was." In Singapore there are two licences. LVS holds one. The margin was 45.6% in a bad quarter and 49.9% in the one before. Same company, same management, same operating playbook — far higher margin, produced almost entirely by the number of competitors the government permits.

That gap is why the biggest money is going to Singapore: the $8.0B IR2 project is more than twice the Macao concession pledge. In Q3 the quarter's capex tilted the other way — $154M in Macao and $121M at Marina Bay Sands of $281M — as the Venetian renovation got going, and Chum named the Venetian renovation, a Conrad renovation rebranded as a Londoner-themed hotel and the concession's botanic garden project as the key Macao projects. The headwind is that Macao is still about 60% of revenue and cannot be exited, so the blended business will keep carrying a six-operator market's economics and a sovereign licence risk that Singapore does not impose.
What Would Have to Be True — Framing the Bull and Bear Cases
EMERGING TAILWIND
For the bull: Marina Bay Sands' best customers come back and it returns to the $2.7–2.8 billion annual EBITDA run rate Dumont described on the call (it earned $2.87B over the last four quarters, so this is not a stretch); Macao keeps gaining share and moves from $529M toward management's goal of "reaching $700 million in quarterly EBITDA and more over time"; Macao fixed costs stay flat, as Grant Chum, who runs Sands China, said they now are; and the 2032 concession renewal proceeds without incident because Sands China has visibly delivered its 93%-non-gaming investment programme. On that path, operating leverage works upward from a $13.6B revenue base, and about 6.9× EV/EBITDA on a duopoly-anchored asset base is plainly too cheap — the $57.04 consensus target is not heroic.

For the bear: the high-end softness management could not explain proves to be a trend rather than a quarter — whether from the scrutiny of Chinese offshore trusts and outbound investment that an analyst raised on the call, a weaker Chinese consumer or something else — Macao's six operators keep competing away the margin recovery, and the same leverage grinds EBITDA down while $8B of Singapore capex and a buyback running ahead of free cash flow drain a balance sheet that already shows negative shareholders' equity. Then 2032 arrives with the government holding every card. The honest framing: LVS is a high-quality operator with two genuinely excellent assets, priced at a discount that is not a mistake — it is compensation for a sovereign risk that cannot be modelled, only sized. An investor's real question is not whether the casinos are good. They are. It is how much of the portfolio should sit on a licence that a government can decline to renew.
