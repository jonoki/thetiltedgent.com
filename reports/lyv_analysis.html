01 Company Overview

Live Nation Entertainment is the world's largest live-entertainment company: it promotes concerts, owns and operates venues and festivals, sells the tickets through Ticketmaster, and monetises the resulting audience through sponsorship and advertising. FY2025 revenue was $25.20B, up 8.8% from $23.16B, with 159 million fans across roughly 55,000 shows and 346 million fee-bearing tickets sold. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through September 2026 is $27.88B, up 13.5%, and the third quarter alone brought in a record $10.1B. At $41.2B of market value it ranks #268 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation.

The central debate on this page is not whether the live business is growing — it plainly is, at record scale. It is where the profit actually sits, and whether the courts are about to take it apart. Concerts produced 82.6% of FY2025 revenue but only ~26% of segment adjusted operating income; Ticketing produced 12.3% of revenue and ~42% of segment AOI. That asymmetry is the business model — and it is also, almost verbatim, the government's theory of harm. In March 2026 the U.S. Department of Justice settled its portion of the antitrust case; in April 2026 a jury found for the plaintiff states on every claim, and the company’s post-trial motions were still pending in November. Section 03 and the Industry Commentary work through what is and is not settled.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
VERTICAL INTEGRATION VENUE OWNERSHIP ARTIST RELATIONSHIPS TICKETMASTER SCALE GLOBAL ROUTING FIRST-PARTY FAN DATA FESTIVAL PORTFOLIO

The core advantage is that Live Nation is the only participant that touches every link in the chain at global scale: it books the tour, owns or controls many of the rooms it plays, sells the ticket, collects the fan's email address, and sells a sponsorship against the whole thing. An artist routing a world tour can get venues, promotion, ticketing and marketing from one counterparty in dozens of countries. That is a real operating advantage — and it is legally the most exposed thing the company owns, because the government's case was built on exactly the same integration. Note also what the moat does not do: it does not protect Concerts margin. At 3.3% AOI margin, the promotion business has essentially no pricing power against talent. The economic moat lives in Ticketing and Sponsorship, and only Ticketing is under direct legal attack.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

01
An adverse jury verdict already on the books. On April 15, 2026, after a trial that began in March, a jury found for the plaintiff states on all claims — unlawful monopolisation of primary ticketing, unlawful monopolisation of large amphitheatre markets, illegal tying of amphitheatres to promotion services, and the corresponding state-law claims. In July 2026 the court heard the company’s motions for judgment as a matter of law (asking the judge to set the verdict aside) and for a new trial; at the Q3 10-Q (Nov 5, 2026) they were still pending, and the company plans to appeal to the Second Circuit once they are decided. A remedies phase follows, in which the court will weigh what the plaintiffs sought — the original complaint asked for divestiture of Ticketmaster. Separately, in September 2026 a federal court in California declined to dismiss the FTC’s suit alleging that Ticketmaster advertised prices lower than those shown at checkout. No outcome is predicted here.
02
Concerts margin is thinner than a year ago, even after a strong Q3. First half of 2026: Concerts AOI −14% while fans grew. Q3 brought it back up 17%, but over nine months Concerts AOI is up only 4% on 14% more revenue, a 5.1% margin against 5.6%. Bigger tours mean bigger artist guarantees, and record attendance does not by itself mean record profit per show.
03
Leverage and a thin balance sheet. Debt-to-equity of 5.10, total debt of $11.91B (stockanalysis.com; $9.75B of borrowings on the Sep 30, 2026 balance sheet), a current ratio of 0.95 and an Altman Z-Score (a bankruptcy-risk score; higher is safer) of 1.97. Much of the cash on the balance sheet is fan money held against future events, not free capital. A legal remedy requiring cash payment or divestiture lands on a structure with little slack.
04
Consumer discretionary exposure at record prices. The entire thesis rests on fans continuing to pay record prices for live events. Ticket spend is one of the most deferrable line items in a household budget, and the company has already pushed prices: Q2 2026 Ticketing revenue grew 15% on 8% more tickets. In Q3 the gap closed (revenue +9% on 10% more tickets).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Demand is at a record and largely sold in advance: 61 million fans in Q3 (+19%), about 170 million tickets sold for 2026 shows through late October (+13%), already more than all of 2025, and primary ticket value for 2027 shows up double digits at the end of Q3. All three segments grew AOI by double digits in Q3, and Concerts swung from −14% in the first half to +17% in the quarter. Ticketing and Sponsorship earned roughly 36% and 69% AOI margins in Q3. Management expects another record year and raised the Ticketmaster guide. With the DOJ settled without a break-up and the 2027 secured notes refinanced to 2032, the bull case reads the 2.2% free-cash-flow yield as a litigation discount on a growing business.

Bear Case

A jury has already found the company an illegal monopolist on every claim; the remedies phase, where the plaintiffs’ original complaint asked for divestiture of Ticketmaster, has not begun, and Ticketmaster produced about 35% of segment AOI over the first nine months of 2026 (42% in FY2025). A second front, the FTC’s pricing suit, survived a motion to dismiss in September. The Concerts margin is still below last year’s over nine months (5.1% against 5.6%), trailing GAAP earnings are negative, debt-to-equity is 5.10, the current ratio 0.95, and consensus 2026 EPS is still below zero. The stock sits 3.5% below its five-year month-end high with a 15.4% short float — investors are paying for a mild remedy no court has ordered.

Industry Commentary
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The verdict is in; the remedy is not
Two separate things happened in 2026 and they are often conflated. The DOJ settled on March 9, 2026 — Proposed Final Judgment filed June 12 and published for comment in July, no Ticketmaster divestiture, but a $280M fund for the settling states, a four-year limit on new fully exclusive ticketing contracts, the right for promoters and artists to sell up to half the tickets at Live Nation’s amphitheatres through other ticketers, a 15% service-fee cap there and loosened control of 13 amphitheatres; the court must still approve it. Separately, the plaintiff states went to verdict and won on April 15, 2026 on all claims, with a $1.72-per-ticket overcharge finding. The company’s motions to set the verdict aside or order a new trial were argued in July and were still pending at the Q3 10-Q (Nov 5, 2026); a remedies phase comes after, and the company plans to appeal. Anyone telling you how it ends is guessing.
ONGOING HEADWIND
Artists are winning the split
A 3.3% Concerts AOI margin on $20.9B of FY2025 revenue is what it looks like when the supplier has the pricing power. The first half of 2026 sharpened it (Concerts AOI −14% while fans grew); a strong Q3 (+17%) still left nine-month Concerts AOI up only 4% on 14% more revenue. Bigger tours in bigger buildings mean larger guarantees and a thinner promoter spread, and the stadium-heavy content cycle that drives the attendance record is precisely what compresses the margin. The structural response is to push fans toward on-site ancillary spend and toward the two high-margin segments — which is what the nine-month numbers show working (Sponsorship AOI +14%, Ticketing +9%), and exactly what the litigation constrains.
EMERGING TAILWIND
Deferred revenue is the tell
Event-related deferred revenue — cash in hand for shows not yet played, the closest thing this business has to an order book — was a record $3.8B for a third quarter, up 8% at Sep 30, after $6.4B, up 25% at June 30, before the summer shows were played. Ticket sales for 2026 shows reached about 170 million through late October, up 13%, and primary ticket value sold for 2027 shows was up double digits at the end of Q3. Bull framing: next year’s book is already growing faster than this year’s revenue base, so much of 2027’s volume is sold before it starts. Bear framing: growth in the deferred balance slowed from 25% to 8% between June and September, a growing deferred balance on a 0.95 current ratio is also a growing obligation, and it says nothing about the margin at which those shows convert — Concerts margin over nine months is still below last year’s.
