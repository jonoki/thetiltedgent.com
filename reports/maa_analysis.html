01 Company Overview

MAA is a landlord. It owns and runs apartment communities, mostly across the Southeast, Southwest and Mid-Atlantic, and at September 30, 2026 owned or had an ownership interest in 105,136 apartment units, including communities under development, across 16 states. It is a REIT, so the number that matters is not GAAP earnings. It is FFO, and more precisely what's left after the roofs and carpets are paid for: AFFO. Over the trailing twelve months MAA produced $8.54 of Core FFO and $7.44 of Core AFFO per share, and it pays out $6.12 of dividends. FY2025 revenue was $2.21B, up 0.83%. The top line has been close to flat for two years.

In one sentence: the Sun Belt got too many new apartments at the same time. The same migration story that made MAA a market darling in 2021 brought a wave of new construction into its markets, and new supply takes new-lease pricing first. At the December 2021 month-end close the stock was $229.44. On October 30, 2026 it closed at $117.21, down 48.9% from that peak and 2.9% above its 52-week low of $113.95, set intraday on October 8. Management's line all year has been that the supply wave is fading and pricing is turning. The Q3 2026 numbers say it is turning more slowly than promised: blended lease pricing slowed to +0.4% from +0.7% in Q2, new leases were signed 6.0% below the previous rent (5.3% below in Q2), and renewals edged up to +5.3% from +5.2%. Same-store NOI fell 1.8%, and full-year guidance was cut for the second quarter running, this time including Core FFO (midpoint $8.49, from $8.53). Management says the recovery is arriving about a quarter later than it expected, and points to October, when new-lease pricing beat the year-earlier level for the first time this year. At 13.7x trailing Core FFO and a 5.22% yield, the market is pricing a slow recovery at best. The central debate: is this the bottom of a supply cycle, or is it a landlord with rising debt costs whose rents have stopped growing?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

105,136-unit Sun Belt scale Low resident turnover (39.6%) Renewal pricing power (+5.3%) Mostly fixed-rate debt (79.3%) Self-funded development pipeline 131 straight quarterly dividends

Apartments are a commodity. What an apartment REIT can have is density and cost of capital. Owning thousands of units in the same metros spreads management, marketing and maintenance costs, and a large listed balance sheet lets MAA keep building when smaller developers can't get financing. That second edge matters most at this point in the cycle. A landlord that can still break ground while others can't gets its new buildings delivered into the tighter market that follows. The vulnerability is the reverse of the moat. MAA has no control over what its neighbours build, and every new building within a few miles competes directly with its new-lease pricing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
New-lease pricing is still negative. New leases were signed 6.0% below the previous lease on the same unit in Q3 2026, worse than the 5.3% gap in Q2. Renewals at +5.3% are holding the blend up. That works while turnover stays low, but every unit that turns over reprices into a market still offering concessions.
02
Guidance keeps moving the wrong way. In July, same-store revenue growth guidance was cut to a 0.10% midpoint from 0.55%, and the Core FFO midpoint held at $8.53 only because expense growth guidance fell to 1.75% from 2.65%. In October revenue guidance went to a −0.25% midpoint, NOI growth to −1.25% from −0.90%, and Core FFO to $8.49, even with expense growth trimmed again to 1.40%. Holding the number through cost control worked once. It didn't hold.
03
Rent-setting software litigation. On January 26, 2026 MAA agreed to pay $53M to settle the consolidated class action alleging that RealPage and roughly 50 landlords used revenue-management software to inflate rents. The settlement is subject to court approval, and MAA raised its loss reserve to $62.5M in its 2025 financials. On September 14, 2026 it agreed to pay $1.2M to settle the District of Columbia Attorney General's parallel case, which concerned one DC property. Both agreements include commitments on the use of nonpublic data and revenue-management tools. The cash is small. The lasting cost is that a pricing tool the industry leaned on is now legally constrained.
04
Debt is repricing upward. Interest expense rose 17.9% YoY in Q3 while total NOI slipped 0.1%. The average effective rate is 4.1%, up from 3.9% in June, and each maturity that refinances at today's rates adds cost. That's a slow, predictable headwind on FFO per share even if rents recover.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the RealPage litigation. MAA recognised $61.9M of accrued legal settlements and defence costs in 2025. The company excludes it from Core FFO, but it is why Q4 2025 NAREIT FFO was $1.79 against Core FFO of $2.23. Second, property-sale gains: $55.4M in the first nine months of 2026, all of it before Q3, which inflate GAAP EPS and the P/E comparison. Third, a net $16.2M gain on investments (net of tax) in 2026 to date, excluded from Core FFO but included in GAAP earnings. Buybacks are small. MAA repurchased 0.4M shares at an average of $126.72 ($50.0M) in Q3 2026, 8.1% above the October 30 close, and on the call CEO Brad Hill said that, with no property sales planned for the rest of the year, investors are "probably not going to see us active in the share repurchase area right now".

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $204.21 to the October 30, 2026 settled close of $117.21, MAA lost 42.6% of its price, and it is 48.9% below the December 2021 peak of $229.44. For a REIT the dividend is a large part of the return, and MAA paid $28.34 a share over the period (20 quarterly dividends, January 2022 to October 2026, stockanalysis dividend history). That brings the five-year return with dividends (not reinvested) to −28.7%: ($117.21 + $28.34) ÷ $204.21 − 1. The dividend cushioned the fall. It didn't offset it. The shape: a 2022 sell-off, a low in October 2023, a rally to $168 by February 2025, then a 20-month slide that has now taken the price below the October 2023 low.

Today's price is the notable part. The October 30 close is 0.8% below the October 2023 month-end low of $118.15, and the September 2026 month-end close of $117.74 is now the lowest of the five-year window. The 6-period monthly RSI reads 29.1, just under the oversold line. It has been lower in four stretches of the window: September 2022 (26.9), autumn 2023 (bottoming at 16.5 in October), October 2025 (25.0) and March 2026 (25.7). The 14-day RSI on daily closes is 45.0, so the last few weeks have been calmer than the monthly picture. The 10-month moving average, about $128.36, sits 9.5% above the price, and the stock closed below its 50-day ($121.38) and 200-day ($129.10) averages. The trend is down, monthly momentum is stretched, and the price is back at the level where buyers stepped in in 2023.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 13.7x trailing Core FFO, 15.8x Core AFFO and getting a 5.22% dividend that is covered by AFFO, from a landlord now trading below its 2023 low. The leading indicators are slow but not dead. Blended lease pricing went from −1.7% in Q4 2025 to +0.4% in Q3 2026, renewals hold above 5%, turnover is at a record low and few tenants are leaving to buy homes. Management says October new-lease pricing beat the prior October for the first time this year, and that the recovery is late, not cancelled. The development pipeline adds NOI regardless of the rent cycle. Debt is 79.3% fixed at 4.1% with 5.6 years of maturity, and even the most bearish target on the Street is above the price.

Bear Case

Core FFO per share has fallen from $8.88 (2024) to $8.74 (2025) to a guided $8.49 (2026), a third straight annual decline, and the 2026 guide was cut in October. Same-store NOI is still shrinking (−1.8% in Q3), and the revenue guide has been cut in July and again in October. New leases still sign 6% below the prior tenant. Interest expense rose 18% YoY in Q3 while NOI was flat, so each refinancing takes a piece of FFO. The dividend absorbs 85.5% of quarterly AFFO. The RealPage settlements limit the pricing tool the industry leaned on, and analysts cut targets right through October, following the price down. A low price per dollar of FFO is not a bargain while the FFO keeps shrinking.

Top 3 Risks

01
The supply recovery takes longer than guided. New-lease pricing at −6.0% is the leading indicator, and it got worse in Q3. Management already pushed its expected improvement back about a quarter. If October's better reading doesn't carry through the winter, same-store revenue (guided at −0.25% for the year) slides further and the Core FFO guide, already cut once, has little left to hold it.
02
Interest costs compounding against flat NOI. Q3 interest expense was $54.6M, +17.9% YoY, with net debt/EBITDAre up to 4.7x from 4.3x in nine months while the company funds $364M of remaining development. Every refinancing at today's rates (the November 2025 notes priced at a 4.650% coupon) above a 4.1% average rate is a lasting FFO headwind, and management named higher interest rates as one reason for October's guidance cut.
03
Regulatory and legal constraints on pricing. The $53M class settlement awaits court approval, and the DC settlement adds business-practice commitments. Further state actions or new rules on algorithmic rent-setting would limit how quickly MAA can push rents when the market turns. That's the recovery the bull case depends on.
Industry Commentary
The Sun Belt supply wave
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

MAA's markets were where the migration story was strongest, so they were where developers built most. In February 2026 management described new deliveries as "still elevated by historical standards" while decelerating. By July it spoke of "declining pressure from new deliveries" and a recovery that "should become more broad-based." On the October call CEO Brad Hill was blunter: "This is a supply issue and it is taking time to certainly work through that supply." The data backs the slower story. New-lease pricing improved from −8.1% in Q4 2025 to −5.3% in Q2 2026, then slipped back to −6.0% in Q3, and full-year same-store revenue guidance is now −0.25%. Hill said he expects "the balance will continue to shift more broadly from supply-driven competition toward demand-led improvement". That is management's forecast, not a result.

Revenue-management software on trial
ONGOING HEADWIND

The consolidated class action alleged that RealPage and roughly 50 large landlords shared nonpublic data through rent-setting software to inflate rents. MAA settled for $53M in January 2026 while stating that its prospective commitments align with current practice and should not materially change operations. On September 14, 2026 the DC Attorney General announced $9.3M of combined settlements with two landlords, of which MAA pays $1.2M. The dollars are small against $2.2B of revenue. The industry-level question is whether the pricing tools landlords used in the 2021–2022 upcycle will be available in the same form in the next one.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $117.21 the market values MAA's equity at about $13.9B, and with $5.81B of net debt the whole enterprise at roughly $19.7B (noncontrolling interests aside). That is about 13.7x TTM Core FFO. The bull framing: the price is below the bottom of the 2023 sell-off, but the portfolio now has three more years of development behind it, renewal pricing is holding, and a 7.3% Core FFO yield and an AFFO-covered 5.22% dividend is paying you to wait for the cycle. The bear framing: this isn't the 2023 company. Core FFO per share is lower, interest cost is higher, and the rent-setting tools are constrained. A steady price-to-FFO ratio on falling FFO is the right price. The way to settle it is not the ratio. It's two numbers the company reports every quarter: new-lease pricing and same-store NOI growth. Both have to turn positive together for the bull case to be about more than the dividend.
