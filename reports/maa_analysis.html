01 Company Overview

MAA is a landlord. It owns and runs apartment communities, mostly across the Southeast, Southwest and Mid-Atlantic, and as of June 30, 2026 held an ownership interest in 104,698 apartment units across 16 states and the District of Columbia. It is a REIT, so the number that matters is not GAAP earnings. It is FFO, and more precisely what's left after the roofs and carpets are paid for: AFFO. Over the trailing twelve months MAA produced $8.60 of Core FFO and $7.46 of Core AFFO per share, and it pays out $6.12 of dividends. FY2025 revenue was $2.21B, up 0.83%. The top line has been close to flat for two years.

In one sentence: the Sun Belt got too many new apartments at the same time. The same migration story that made MAA a market darling in 2021 brought a wave of new construction into its markets, and new supply takes new-lease pricing first. At the December 2021 month-end close the stock was $229.44. On September 21, 2026 it closed at $118.86, down 48% from that peak and within a dollar of its 52-week low of $117.98, set intraday that same day. Management's line all year has been that the supply wave is fading and pricing is turning. The Q2 2026 numbers partly back that up: blended lease pricing turned positive (+0.7%), new-lease pricing improved 170 bps from Q1, and renewals held at +5.2%. But same-store NOI still fell 1.0%, full-year revenue guidance was cut, and the stock fell 14.5% from the June 30 close. At 13.8x trailing Core FFO and a 5.15% yield, the market is pricing a slow recovery at best. The central debate: is this the bottom of a supply cycle, or is it a landlord with rising debt costs whose rents have stopped growing?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

104,698-unit Sun Belt scale Low resident turnover (39.6%) Renewal pricing power (+5.2%) Mostly fixed-rate debt (86.6%) Self-funded development pipeline 130 straight quarterly dividends

Apartments are a commodity. What an apartment REIT can have is density and cost of capital. Owning thousands of units in the same metros spreads management, marketing and maintenance costs, and a large listed balance sheet lets MAA keep building when smaller developers can't get financing. That second edge matters most at this point in the cycle. A landlord that can still break ground while others can't gets its new buildings delivered into the tighter market that follows. The vulnerability is the reverse of the moat. MAA has no control over what its neighbours build, and every new building within a few miles competes directly with its new-lease pricing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
New-lease pricing is still negative. New leases were signed 5.3% below the previous lease on the same unit in Q2 2026, and 6.0% below for the first half. Renewals at +5.2% are holding the blend up. That works while turnover stays low, but every unit that turns over reprices into a market still offering concessions.
02
Guidance moved the wrong way on revenue. In July, same-store revenue growth guidance was cut to a 0.10% midpoint from 0.55%, and NOI growth to −0.90% from −0.70%. The Core FFO midpoint held at $8.53 only because expense growth guidance fell to 1.75% from 2.65%. Holding the number through cost control works once. It doesn't compound.
03
Rent-setting software litigation. On January 26, 2026 MAA agreed to pay $53M to settle the consolidated class action alleging that RealPage and roughly 50 landlords used revenue-management software to inflate rents. The settlement is subject to court approval, and MAA raised its loss reserve to $62.5M in its 2025 financials. On September 14, 2026 it agreed to pay $1.2M to settle the District of Columbia Attorney General's parallel case, which concerned one DC property. Both agreements include commitments on the use of nonpublic data and revenue-management tools. The cash is small. The lasting cost is that a pricing tool the industry leaned on is now legally constrained.
04
Debt is repricing upward. Interest expense rose 17.8% YoY in Q2 while total NOI rose 0.3%. The average effective rate is still only 3.9%, so each maturity that refinances at today's rates adds cost. That's a slow, predictable headwind on FFO per share even if rents recover.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 13.8x trailing Core FFO, 15.9x Core AFFO and getting a 5.15% dividend that is covered by AFFO, from a landlord at the price it traded at the bottom of the last scare. The leading indicators are turning. Blended lease pricing went from −1.7% in Q4 2025 to +0.7% in Q2 2026, new-lease pricing improved 170bps in a quarter, renewals hold above 5%, turnover is historically low and few tenants are leaving to buy homes. Management says demand now outweighs the fading pressure from new deliveries. The development pipeline adds NOI regardless of the rent cycle. Debt is 86.6% fixed at 3.9% with six years of maturity, and even the most bearish target on the Street is above the price.

Bear Case

Core FFO per share has fallen from $8.88 (2024) to $8.74 (2025) to a guided $8.53 (2026), a third straight annual decline. Same-store NOI is still shrinking, and July's guidance cut revenue, not just expenses. New leases still sign 5% below the prior tenant. Interest expense rose 18% YoY in Q2 while NOI was flat, so each refinancing takes a piece of FFO. The dividend now absorbs 86% of quarterly AFFO. The RealPage settlements limit the pricing tool the industry leaned on, and analysts are cutting targets into the price, not ahead of it. A cheap multiple on shrinking FFO is not a bargain until the FFO stops shrinking.

Top 3 Risks

01
The supply recovery takes longer than guided. New-lease pricing at −5.3% is the leading indicator. If it stops improving through the Q3 leasing season, same-store revenue goes negative for the year and the Core FFO midpoint, held in July by expense cuts, has nothing left to hold it.
02
Interest costs compounding against flat NOI. Q2 interest expense was $53.1M, +17.8% YoY, with net debt/EBITDAre up to 4.5x from 4.3x in six months while the company funds $237M of remaining development. Every refinancing at today's rates (the November 2025 notes priced at a 4.650% coupon) over a 3.9% average book rate is a lasting FFO headwind.
03
Regulatory and legal constraints on pricing. The $53M class settlement awaits court approval, and the DC settlement adds business-practice commitments. Further state actions or new rules on algorithmic rent-setting would limit how quickly MAA can push rents when the market turns. That's the recovery the bull case depends on.
Industry Commentary
The Sun Belt supply wave
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

MAA's markets were where the migration story was strongest, so they were where developers built most. In February 2026 management described new deliveries as "still elevated by historical standards" while decelerating. By July it spoke of "declining pressure from new deliveries" and a recovery that "should become more broad-based." The data sits between those two statements. New-lease pricing has improved 2.8 points since Q4 2025 (−8.1% to −5.3%), but full-year same-store revenue guidance was cut to +0.10%. A transcript summary (not a verbatim transcript) of the September 2026 BofA Securities Global Real Estate Conference, as posted by stockanalysis, says management pointed to mid-tier markets outperforming, improvement in high-supply Austin, and higher NOI growth expected in 2027. That is management's forecast, not a result.

Revenue-management software on trial
ONGOING HEADWIND

The consolidated class action alleged that RealPage and roughly 50 large landlords shared nonpublic data through rent-setting software to inflate rents. MAA settled for $53M in January 2026 while stating that its prospective commitments align with current practice and should not materially change operations. On September 14, 2026 the DC Attorney General announced $9.3M of combined settlements with two landlords, of which MAA pays $1.2M. The dollars are small against $2.2B of revenue. The industry-level question is whether the pricing tools landlords used in the 2021–2022 upcycle will be available in the same form in the next one.

The valuation question — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $118.86 the market values MAA's equity at about $14.1B, and with $5.64B of net debt the whole enterprise at roughly $19.8B (preferred and noncontrolling interests aside). That is about 13.8x TTM Core FFO. The bull framing: the price is back where it was at the bottom of the 2023 sell-off, but the portfolio now has two more years of development behind it, lease pricing is inflecting, and a 7.2% Core FFO yield and an AFFO-covered 5.15% dividend is paying you to wait for the cycle. The bear framing: this isn't the 2023 company. Core FFO per share is lower, interest cost is higher, and the rent-setting tools are constrained. A flat multiple on falling FFO is the right price. The way to adjudicate is not the multiple. It's two numbers the company reports every quarter: new-lease pricing and same-store NOI growth. Both have to turn positive together for the bull case to be more than a yield trade.
