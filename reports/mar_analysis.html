01 Company Overview

Start here, because it reframes every number on this page: Marriott barely owns any hotels. Of the 1,813,698 rooms in its system at June 30, 2026, exactly 13,333 — seven-tenths of one percent — were owned or leased. Another 1,223,350 rooms (67.5%) were franchised or licensed (an independent owner runs the hotel and pays Marriott a royalty on its room revenue for the brand and reservation system), 560,449 (30.9%) were managed (Marriott runs the hotel day-to-day for a third-party owner under a long-term contract, for a base fee plus a profit-share), and 16,566 were branded residences. Marriott is a fee-collecting franchisor and operator sitting on top of roughly a quarter-trillion dollars of other people's real estate. Read it as a property company and you will misprice it.

That structure is why the reported financials look the way they do. Full-year 2025 total GAAP revenues were $26,186M, but $19,204M of that was cost reimbursement revenue — payroll and marketing costs at managed and franchised hotels that owners reimburse dollar-for-dollar, booked as both revenue and expense and earning Marriott nothing. Strip it out and the real business is $6,982M of net revenue (+5.5% year over year), of which $5,438M was gross fee revenue. That fee stream carried a 79.2% gross margin and a 35.0% net profit margin on the trailing twelve months, and 16.29% ROIC. It also explains the balance sheet: with almost no property to carry, decades of buybacks have driven stockholders' equity to negative $4.53B, or −$17.28 of book value per share. Marriott is the world's largest hotel company by rooms and the 279th most valuable listed company globally.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
BONVOY — 295M+ MEMBERS CO-BRAND CARD ECONOMICS 30 BRANDS, ALL PRICE TIERS LONG-DATED CONTRACTS 629K-ROOM PIPELINE DIRECT-BOOKING SCALE ASSET-LIGHT CAPITAL BASE

The moat is a two-sided network, and Marriott Bonvoy — over 295 million members at June 30, 2026, up from nearly 271 million at the end of 2025 — is the hinge. To a guest it is a reason to book direct instead of through an online travel agency that charges 15–20% commission. To a hotel owner it is the reason to sign a Marriott flag instead of running independent: it delivers demand at lower cost than the alternatives, which is the entire value proposition a franchisor sells. Each new hotel makes the network more useful to members, and each new member makes the flag more valuable to owners.

The part most readers never think about is that Bonvoy is itself a large, high-margin business. Marriott sells loyalty points in bulk to banks, which award them to co-brand credit card holders. Marriott does not disclose the dollar amount separately — it sits inside the franchise fee line, which the Q2 2026 release explicitly attributes in part to "higher co-branded credit card fees" — but the effect is visible: franchise fees rose 19% year over year to $1,023M in Q2 while base management fees rose 1%. This revenue is contractual, essentially costless at the margin, and tied to consumer card spending rather than to hotel occupancy, so it holds up when travel does not. In Q2 2026 Marriott executed new long-term co-brand agreements with JPMorgan Chase and American Express, locking that stream in for years. The second pillar is contractual visibility: a development pipeline of 4,186 properties and approximately 629,000 rooms — a record, and about 35% of the existing system — of which 1,757 properties and over 279,000 rooms are already under construction. For a franchisor, that pipeline is the compounding engine, and it is legally committed years before it shows up in revenue.

Owner economics, not consumer demand, are the real constraint. Marriott's unit growth depends on third parties financing hotels. High construction costs and elevated financing rates slow new-build starts regardless of how strong Marriott's brands are, which is why conversions of existing independent hotels have carried a growing share of net rooms growth. Marriott controls the flag, not the capital.

The negative equity is structural, not distress — but it is not free. Stockholders' equity is −$4.53B against $17.77B of total debt and $462M of cash, with a current ratio of 0.53. This works because fee cash flow is contractual and free cash flow was $2.78B on the trailing twelve months. It also means Marriott has no equity cushion, must refinance continuously, and would face a genuinely tighter box if fee revenue and credit markets deteriorated at the same time.

Distribution and disintermediation. Online travel agencies, and increasingly AI-mediated booking layers, compete for the customer relationship Bonvoy exists to protect. If discovery shifts to an assistant that treats hotel rooms as interchangeable inventory, brand-driven direct booking — the thing owners pay for — loses value.

Geographic and mix concentration. International RevPAR fell 0.5% in Q2 2026 while U.S. & Canada rose 5.0%, with EMEA down more than 5%. Marriott is diversified by brand and price point but is genuinely exposed to Greater China's uneven recovery, Middle East disruption, and the pace at which group and business-transient demand normalises versus leisure.

2. GAAP versus adjusted. FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS was $9.51 against $10.02 adjusted; Q2 2026 GAAP diluted EPS was $2.90 against $3.19 adjusted, on GAAP net income of $766M versus adjusted net income of $844M. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Marriott is a royalty on global travel that requires almost no capital: 0.7% of its 1.81M rooms are owned, ROIC is 16.3%, and free cash flow of $2.78B funds a buyback retiring 4–5% of the company annually. Growth is contractually visible — a record 629,000-room pipeline with 279,000 rooms already under construction supports 4.5–5% net rooms growth — and the fastest-growing, highest-margin line is co-brand credit card fees, newly locked in with JPMorgan Chase and American Express and driven by card spending rather than hotel occupancy. At 36.4× trailing earnings versus Hilton's 47.3×, the larger and more diversified franchisor trades at a discount to its nearest comparable.

Bear Case

Growth has slowed to a mid-single-digit RevPAR-plus-units algorithm — FY2026 guidance is just 3.0–3.5% RevPAR — and the stock is still priced at 36× trailing and 30× guided FY26 earnings against a 32.9× Nasdaq-100. International RevPAR is already negative (−0.5% in Q2) and EMEA is down more than 5%. Unit growth depends on third-party owners financing hotels, which high construction and financing costs constrain regardless of brand strength. Meanwhile equity is −$4.53B against $17.77B of debt and a 0.53 current ratio, and capital return guidance of $4.5B exceeds free cash flow — leaving no cushion if fee revenue and credit conditions tighten together. Twelve of 27 analysts rate it Hold.

01
Rate-led growth is running out of room. Worldwide RevPAR grew 3.4% in Q2 2026 almost entirely on ADR (+3.5%) while occupancy was flat to down (U.S. & Canada occupancy fell 0.2pts, international 0.7pts). Rate-led growth is the good kind — an extra dollar of room rate carries virtually no incremental cost and drops almost entirely to fees and hotel profit — but it is only sustainable while consumers accept price increases. If ADR growth stalls with occupancy already flat, RevPAR goes to zero and the fee line stops growing organically.
02
Owner capital, not consumer demand, gates unit growth. Marriott supplies the flag; someone else supplies the building. Elevated construction costs and financing rates slow new-build starts, and while conversions of existing independent hotels partly offset that, a sustained squeeze on hotel-development economics would erode the pipeline that underpins 4.5–5% net rooms growth — the single most important input to the long-term compounding case.
03
No balance-sheet cushion. Negative $4.53B of stockholders' equity, $17.77B of total debt against $462M of cash, and a current ratio of 0.53 are all sustainable while fee cash flow is contractual and credit markets are open. They are sustainable in exactly that order. A simultaneous travel downturn and credit tightening would force a choice between the buyback and the balance sheet, and the buyback is doing a meaningful share of the EPS growth work.
  Industry Commentary
RevPAR: The Metric, and Why Its Composition Matters More Than Its Level
ONGOING HEADWIND
RevPAR (revenue per available room) is the lodging industry's single comparable top-line metric, and it is simply occupancy × ADR (average daily rate, the average price actually paid per occupied room per night). Marriott's Q2 2026 worldwide RevPAR was $138.74, from 71.6% occupancy and a $193.66 ADR. The critical point is that the two components are not economically equivalent. Filling one more room costs real money — housekeeping, utilities, amenities, credit card fees. Charging one more dollar for a room already occupied costs nothing. Rate-led RevPAR growth therefore flows to profit almost dollar for dollar; occupancy-led growth does not. That is why Q2's mix was better than the headline: worldwide RevPAR rose 3.4% but ADR rose 3.5%, meaning occupancy actually contributed nothing. In U.S. & Canada, RevPAR rose 5.0% on a 4.7% ADR gain with occupancy down 0.2 points to 74.0% — high-quality growth. The headwind is that this configuration has a ceiling: with occupancy already at 74% in the core market and no longer contributing, the entire growth rate now depends on continued consumer willingness to absorb price. FY2026 guidance of 3.0–3.5% RevPAR growth reflects that.
The Geographic and Segment Split: Three Different Recoveries Running at Once
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Marriott is not experiencing one demand environment; it is experiencing several. In Q2 2026, U.S. & Canada RevPAR rose 5.0% while international RevPAR fell 0.5%, occupancy 67.1% and ADR up only 0.6%. Inside international, the dispersion is wider still: EMEA fell more than 5%, with Middle East disruption offsetting European gains, while Asia Pacific rose more than 5% and Greater China rose about 3%, led by the luxury portfolio. Greater China matters disproportionately because its recovery has been genuinely different in shape from the West's — driven by domestic rather than inbound travel and by luxury rather than the mid-scale segments that drove the U.S. rebound. Demand segments diverge too: leisure normalised first and is now the mature part of the mix, business transient (individual corporate travel) recovered second and remains structurally below pre-2020 patterns as remote work persists, and group (conferences, conventions, weddings) recovered last but books furthest ahead and is the most visible forward indicator. This split is the constraint because Marriott's managed rooms — 30.9% of the system and the source of both base and incentive fees — skew international, so the weakest region carries the most profit-sensitive fee line.
Asset-Light Deserves a Different Multiple — and the Bull/Bear Case Turns on How Much
EMERGING TAILWIND
Comparing Marriott to a hotel REIT is a category error, and it is the most common analytical mistake made about this company. A REIT owns buildings: its earnings are levered to occupancy, it carries depreciation and maintenance capex, its returns are capped by the cap rate on real estate, and it is valued on multiples of funds from operations or on net asset value. Marriott owns 13,333 rooms out of 1.81 million and collects a royalty on the rest. Its earnings are a percentage of someone else's revenue, its capex is trivial, its ROIC is 16.3% because invested capital is tiny by construction, and its equity is negative not from distress but because buybacks have exceeded retained earnings for years. That profile — contractual revenue, 79% gross margin, negligible capital intensity, 4.5–5% visible unit growth — legitimately earns a multiple closer to a payments network or a franchise business than to a property owner. The bull reading: at 36.4× trailing, MAR is cheaper than Hilton at 47.3× despite a larger system and a record pipeline, and the co-brand card stream is a hidden, high-margin, spend-linked annuity that most models treat as ordinary franchise revenue. The bear reading: asset-light justifies a premium to a REIT, not an unlimited one — at 30× guided FY2026 earnings against 3.0–3.5% RevPAR growth and negative international comparisons, the multiple already assumes the algorithm holds, and 12 of 27 analysts and a $348 Barclays target say the margin of safety is thin.
