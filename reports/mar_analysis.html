01 Company Overview

Start here, because it reframes every number on this page: Marriott barely owns any hotels. Of the 1,833,801 rooms in its system at September 30, 2026, just 13,340 — seven-tenths of one percent — were owned or leased. Another 1,241,202 rooms (67.7%) were franchised or licensed (an independent owner runs the hotel and pays Marriott a royalty on its room revenue for the brand and reservation system), 562,461 (30.7%) were managed (Marriott runs the hotel day-to-day for a third-party owner under a long-term contract, for a base fee plus a profit-share), and 16,798 were branded residences (Q3 2026 10-Q). Marriott is a fee-collecting franchisor and operator sitting on top of other people's real estate. Read it as a property company and you will misprice it.

That structure is why the reported financials look the way they do. Over the twelve months to September 30, 2026, total GAAP revenues were $27.42B, but $19.84B of that was cost reimbursement revenue — payroll and marketing costs at managed and franchised hotels that owners reimburse dollar-for-dollar, booked as both revenue and expense and earning Marriott nothing. Strip it out and the real business is $7.58B of revenue, most of it fees: third-quarter gross fee revenues alone were $1,522M, up 14% on a year earlier. On that net base the trailing $2.65B of net income is a 35.0% net profit margin (our calculation from the FY2025 earnings release and the Q3 2026 10-Q), and ROIC is 16.6% (stockanalysis.com). It also explains the balance sheet: with almost no property to carry, years of buybacks have driven stockholders' equity to negative $5.08B at September 30, or −$19.74 of book value per share. Marriott ranks #112 in the S&P 500 by market value.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
BONVOY — ~309M MEMBERS CO-BRAND CARD ECONOMICS 30 BRANDS, ALL PRICE TIERS LONG-DATED CONTRACTS 634K-ROOM PIPELINE DIRECT-BOOKING SCALE ASSET-LIGHT CAPITAL BASE

The moat is a two-sided network, and Marriott Bonvoy — about 309 million members at September 30, 2026, up from nearly 271 million at the end of 2025 — is the hinge. To a guest it is a reason to book direct instead of through an online travel agency that charges the hotel a commission. To a hotel owner it is the reason to sign a Marriott flag instead of running independent: it delivers demand at lower cost than the alternatives, which is the entire value proposition a franchisor sells. Each new hotel makes the network more useful to members, and each new member makes the flag more valuable to owners.

The part most readers never think about is that Bonvoy is itself a large, high-margin business. Marriott sells loyalty points in bulk to banks, which award them to co-brand credit card holders. Marriott does not disclose the dollar amount separately — it sits inside the franchise fee line, and the Q3 2026 release again lists higher co-branded credit card fees first among the drivers of fee growth, ahead of RevPAR and rooms growth — and the effect is visible: franchise fees rose 15% year over year to $1,007M in Q3 while base management fees rose 6%. This revenue is contractual, essentially costless at the margin, and tied to consumer card spending rather than to hotel occupancy, so it holds up when travel does not. In Q2 2026 Marriott executed new long-term co-brand agreements with JPMorgan Chase and American Express, locking that stream in for years. The second pillar is contractual visibility: a development pipeline of 4,209 properties and over 634,000 rooms at September 30 — a record, and about 35% of the existing system — of which 1,750 properties and over 278,000 rooms are already under construction; about 37,000 of the rooms are approved but not yet under signed contracts. For a franchisor, that pipeline is where future fee growth comes from, and most of it is signed years before it shows up in revenue.

Owner economics, not consumer demand, are the real constraint. Marriott's unit growth depends on third parties financing hotels. High construction costs and elevated financing rates slow new-build starts regardless of how strong Marriott's brands are, which is why conversions of existing hotels matter: they were over a third of signings and over 40% of openings in the first nine months of 2026 (Q3 release). Marriott controls the flag, not the capital.

The negative equity comes from buybacks, not distress — but it is not free. Stockholders' equity was −$5.08B at September 30 against $16.84B of total debt and $453M of cash, with a current ratio of 0.53. This works because fee cash flow is contractual and free cash flow was $3.48B over the trailing twelve months. It also means Marriott has no equity cushion, must refinance continuously, and would face a genuinely tighter box if fee revenue and credit markets deteriorated at the same time.

Distribution and disintermediation. Online travel agencies, and increasingly AI-mediated booking layers, compete for the customer relationship Bonvoy exists to protect. If discovery shifts to an assistant that treats hotel rooms as interchangeable inventory, brand-driven direct booking — the thing owners pay for — loses value.

Geographic and mix concentration. In Q3 2026 international RevPAR rose 4.4% and U.S. & Canada 7.4%, both better than Q2 (international −0.5%, U.S. & Canada +5.0%), but CEO Anthony Capuano said the conflict in the Middle East continued to affect results there. Marriott is diversified by brand and price point but is exposed to Greater China's uneven recovery, the Middle East, and the pace at which group and business-transient demand normalises versus leisure.

Notable One-Off Items. Three distortions matter here, and none of them is a write-down.

1. The revenue definition is the biggest trap on the page. GAAP revenue ($27.42B over the last twelve months) includes $19.84B of cost reimbursements that earn Marriott nothing, and consensus FY2026 revenue of $27.78B (stockanalysis) is on that basis: +6.1% on FY2025's $26.19B. The business underneath grew faster: revenue net of reimbursements rose 11.3% in Q3 2026 and gross fees 14%. Any margin or growth figure on this page depends on which revenue it uses, so each one says which.

2. GAAP versus adjusted. FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS was $9.51 against $10.02 adjusted, and Q2 2026 was $2.90 against $3.19. Q3 2026 ran the other way: GAAP $3.04 against $3.02 adjusted, on reported net income of $794M versus adjusted $790M. The trailing P/E above uses GAAP; company guidance and consensus are adjusted.

3. FY2023's earnings were not repeatable. Net income of $3,083M and EPS of $10.18 in FY2023 exceeded both FY2024 ($2,375M, $8.33) and FY2025 ($2,601M, $9.51) despite revenue growing in every one of those years. Any multi-year EPS growth rate anchored on 2023 will understate the underlying trend.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. Neither is a signal on its own.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 month-end close of $147.56 to the November 3, 2026 close of $416.38, MAR returned +182.2% on price alone — roughly 23.4% compounded annually before the modest dividend. It is a story about a fee stream compounding through three mechanisms at once: RevPAR growth, net rooms growth of 4–5% a year (4.6% in the year to Q3 2026), and a share count shrinking under a buyback that took 9.6M shares, about 3.7% of the company, in 2026 through October 28. Over the year to November 3 the stock rose 57.8%, from $263.89 to $416.38, and October's $399.30 was the highest month-end close on the chart.

The path was anything but smooth. The 2022 rate shock cut the stock roughly 21% in a single month (May to June 2022), and the March 2025 tariff-and-consumer-demand scare took it from $280.45 to $238.20 in one month, a 15% drop. In both, the price the market would pay for each dollar of earnings fell while the earnings themselves held up — the tell for an asset-light franchisor: the cash flow is contractual, so what moves is the multiple the market is willing to pay for it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Marriott is a royalty on global travel that requires almost no capital: 0.7% of its 1.83M rooms are owned or leased, ROIC is 16.6%, and $3.48B of trailing free cash flow funds a buyback that has retired about 3.7% of the shares so far in 2026. Growth is contractually visible — a record 634,000-room pipeline with 278,000 rooms already under construction — and Q3 showed both levers working: RevPAR up 6.5% on higher rates and occupancy, gross fees up 14%, and the full-year RevPAR guide raised to 4.5–5%. Co-brand credit card fees, which the Q3 release lists first among the drivers of fee growth, are newly locked in with JPMorgan Chase and American Express and tied to card spending rather than hotel occupancy. At 41.5× trailing earnings versus Hilton's 49.2×, the larger franchisor still trades at a discount to its nearest comparable.

Bear Case

The good quarter is already in the price: after a 57.8% one-year gain the stock closed above the $407.58 average analyst target, at 41.5× trailing GAAP earnings and 34.4× the midpoint of adjusted FY2026 guidance. Q4 RevPAR is guided to 4.5–5% after Q3's 6.5%, net rooms growth only to the low end of 4.5–5%, and the Middle East is still weighing on EMEA. Unit growth depends on third-party owners financing hotels, which high construction and financing costs constrain regardless of brand strength. Meanwhile equity is −$5.08B against $16.84B of debt and a 0.53 current ratio, and capital return guidance of at least $4.8B exceeds free cash flow — leaving no cushion if fee revenue and credit conditions tighten together. Fourteen of 29 analysts rate it Hold and two Strong Sell.

01
A strong quarter raises the bar. Q3 worldwide RevPAR grew 6.5% on both rate (systemwide ADR +4.8%) and occupancy (+1.1 points to 72.5%). An extra dollar of room rate carries almost no extra cost and drops almost entirely to fees and hotel profit, so rate is the growth that matters most — but it lasts only while travellers accept higher prices. Q4 guidance of 4.5–5% already assumes slower growth than Q3, and with U.S. & Canada occupancy near 74%, most further growth has to come from price. If rate gains stall, the fee line slows with them.
02
Owner capital, not consumer demand, gates unit growth. Marriott supplies the flag; someone else supplies the building. Elevated construction costs and financing rates slow new-build starts, and while conversions of existing hotels partly offset that, a sustained squeeze on hotel-development economics would erode the pipeline behind net rooms growth, guided to the low end of 4.5–5% for 2026 — the single most important input to the long-term growth case.
03
No balance-sheet cushion. Negative $5.08B of stockholders' equity, $16.84B of total debt against $453M of cash, and a current ratio of 0.53 are all sustainable while fee cash flow is contractual and credit markets are open. Debt rose by $0.6B in the first nine months of 2026 as the buyback outran free cash flow. A simultaneous travel downturn and credit tightening would force a choice between the buyback and the balance sheet, and the buyback is doing a meaningful share of the EPS growth work.
  Industry Commentary
RevPAR: The Metric, and Why Its Composition Matters More Than Its Level
WATCH ITEM
RevPAR (revenue per available room) is the lodging industry's single comparable top-line metric, and it is simply occupancy × ADR (average daily rate, the average price actually paid per occupied room per night). Marriott's Q3 2026 worldwide systemwide RevPAR was $137.91, from 72.5% occupancy and a $190.33 ADR (10-Q). The two components are not economically equivalent. Filling one more room costs real money — housekeeping, utilities, amenities, credit card fees. Charging one more dollar for a room already occupied costs almost nothing. Rate-led RevPAR growth therefore flows to profit almost dollar for dollar; occupancy-led growth does not. Q3 used both: worldwide ADR rose 4.8% and occupancy 1.1 points, and in U.S. & Canada RevPAR rose 7.4% to $146.29 on a 4.9% ADR gain with occupancy up 1.7 points to 73.9%. The watch item is the mix from here: with core-market occupancy near 74%, most further growth has to come from price, and the Q4 guide of 4.5–5% is slower than Q3.
The Geographic Split: Several Demand Environments at Once
KEY SENSITIVITY
Marriott is not experiencing one demand environment; it is experiencing several. In Q3 2026, U.S. & Canada RevPAR rose 7.4% and international RevPAR rose 4.4%, with international occupancy flat at 69.7% and ADR up 4.4%. Inside international, APEC rose over 9% on inbound travel and EMEA over 4% on Europe, while the conflict in the Middle East continued to weigh on that region. Greater China segment revenue rose 13% to $161M. Demand segments differ too: leisure, business travel and group bookings (conferences, conventions, weddings) move on different cycles, and group books furthest ahead, which makes it the most visible forward indicator. The split matters because Marriott's managed rooms — 30.7% of the system and the source of both base and incentive fees — skew international, so the regions most exposed to conflict carry the most profit-sensitive fee line. In Q3, incentive fees grew in every region.
Asset-Light Deserves a Higher Price per Dollar of Earnings — and the Bull/Bear Case Turns on How Much
EMERGING TAILWIND
Comparing Marriott to a hotel REIT is a category error, and a common one. A REIT (real estate investment trust) owns buildings: its earnings are tied to occupancy, it carries depreciation and maintenance capex, its returns are capped by the yield on real estate, and it is valued on multiples of funds from operations or on net asset value. Marriott owns or leases 13,340 rooms out of 1.83 million and collects a royalty on the rest. Its earnings are a percentage of someone else's revenue, its capex is small, its ROIC is 16.6% because invested capital is tiny by construction, and its equity is negative not from distress but because buybacks have exceeded retained earnings for years. That profile — contractual revenue, high fee margins, little capital, a record pipeline — legitimately earns a price per dollar of earnings closer to a payments network or a franchise business than to a property owner. The bull reading: at 41.5× trailing, MAR is cheaper than Hilton at 49.2× despite a larger system and a record pipeline, and the co-brand card stream is high-margin income that rises with card spending, year after year, and sits inside ordinary franchise revenue. The bear reading: asset-light justifies a premium to a REIT, not an unlimited one — at 34.4× the midpoint of FY2026 guidance, with the stock above the average analyst target and 14 of 29 analysts at Hold, the price already assumes the steady growth continues.
