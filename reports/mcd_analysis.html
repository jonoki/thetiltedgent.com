01 Company Overview

McDonald's Corporation operates the world's largest restaurant system by systemwide sales — more than 45,000 locations across over 100 countries, of which approximately 95% are owned and operated by independent franchisees. That structure is the single most important fact about the company: McDonald's corporate revenue is overwhelmingly rent and royalty income collected off a $139 billion global systemwide sales base, not the sale of hamburgers. Systemwide sales — the total spent by customers across all McDonald's restaurants, franchised and company-operated — exceeded $139 billion in fiscal 2025, up 7% (5% in constant currencies), while the corporation's own reported revenue was $26.89 billion, up 3.7% YoY (up 4% as reported by the company, 2% in constant currencies).

On TTM figures through June 2026, revenue is $27.70 billion (+6.3%) with net income of $8.79 billion — a 31.7% net profit margin and a 45.7% operating margin that would be unthinkable for an operator of restaurants and is entirely explained by the franchise model. McDonald's ranks as roughly the 110th most valuable public company in the world by market capitalization at $188.93 billion, and is a 51-year Dividend Aristocrat. The stock is the problem child of that resume right now: down 13.58% over the trailing 52 weeks and trading within roughly 2% of its 52-week low of $260.96, as U.S. guest counts turn negative.

This is the structural key to the whole company. Because roughly 95% of the 45,000+ restaurants are franchisee-owned, McDonald's does not book the food sales, does not pay the crew, and does not carry the food and paper cost for the vast majority of its system. It collects a rent on the real estate it owns or master-leases and a royalty on the franchisee's sales. Franchised revenues of $16.5 billion in FY2025 (up from $15.7B in FY2024) carry margins in the region of 80%+, while company-operated sales of $9.7 billion (down from $9.8B as refranchising continues) carry restaurant-level margins in the mid-teens. Blend them and you get a 57.4% gross margin, a 45.7% operating margin, and a 31.7% net margin — figures that look like a software company's, on a business that sells cheeseburgers. In Q2 2026 the split held: franchised revenue $4,393M (+4%) versus company-operated sales $2,525M (+3%) and other revenue $182M (+6%). The corollary is that systemwide sales, not corporate revenue, is the number that matters for judging brand health, and that a one-point move in comps flows to McDonald's operating income with unusual leverage.
Real economics: $139B systemwide sales → $26.9B corporate revenue → $12.4B operating income (FY2025)
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Owned Real Estate ~95% Franchised $139B Systemwide Scale 220M Loyalty Members Supply-Chain Cost Advantage Global Brand Recognition 51-Yr Dividend Growth Value-Price Leadership

McDonald's moat is unusual because it rests on two independent foundations. The first is real estate: McDonald's owns or master-leases the land and buildings under a large share of its restaurants and sublets them to franchisees, which converts brand strength into contractual, inflation-linked rent that is far more durable than restaurant profit. The second is absolute scale — a $139 billion systemwide sales base spread over 45,000+ units in 100+ countries buys purchasing power in beef, potatoes, chicken and packaging that no competitor can match, and amortizes advertising and technology spend over a sales base an order of magnitude larger than most rivals. Layer on the loyalty program — nearly 220 million 90-day active users across 70 markets, generating over $40 billion of trailing-twelve-month loyalty sales, up more than 20% — and McDonald's now has a first-party data asset that lets it target offers rather than blanket-discount. The result is a business that can afford to lead on price: it can run a $5 meal deal and still clear a 45.7% operating margin, because the discount is borne largely at the franchisee's restaurant-level P&L while McDonald's collects a royalty on the incremental traffic. That is a weapon Wendy's — with $2.20 billion of revenue, U.S. same-restaurant sales down 7.0%, guidance withdrawn and its dividend cut — simply does not have.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Traffic, not check, is the true health metric — and traffic is negative. Q2 2026 U.S. comps of +0.8% were built entirely on positive check growth and favorable product mix, explicitly offset by negative guest counts. A value-led chain that grows only by raising prices is borrowing from future traffic. Comps decelerated from +2.5% a year earlier and from +6.8% in Q4 2025; the deceleration is steep and management's response — replacing the head of the U.S. business — signals internal dissatisfaction rather than confidence.

2. Low-income consumer dependence. McDonald's over-indexes to lower-income households, the cohort most exposed to real-wage compression, restarted student-loan payments and higher shelter costs. Management won that cohort back in late 2025 with Extra Value Meals, McValue and $5/$8 nationally priced bundles, but the win required real price concessions. Any further trade-down to grocery or convenience-store food is a direct traffic loss, and any move to reclaim price is a direct traffic risk. This is the tightest strategic vise the company faces.

3. Share loss in the core burger category. Burger King has overtaken Wendy's to become the second-largest U.S. burger chain by systemwide sales, on the back of a revamped Whopper platform, while Restaurant Brands International grew revenue 6.5% and net income 48.2%. Chicken — where Chick-fil-A is the category leader — has been taking daypart share from beef for a decade. McDonald's is being attacked simultaneously on the value flank and the quality flank.

4. Balance-sheet optionality is constrained. $54.6 billion of total debt against $822 million of cash and negative stockholders' equity of −$1.02 billion means the company funds its dividend and buyback from operating cash flow, not from a war chest. Net debt/EBITDA of 3.28x and a current ratio of 1.08 are manageable for a business with 28% FCF margin and an Altman Z-Score of 4.98, but they leave little room for a large acquisition or a prolonged comps recession without pressuring the 51-year dividend streak.

Notable One-Off Items

Restructuring — "Accelerating the Organization." This is the only material recurring distortion in the reported numbers. McDonald's booked net pre-tax charges of $52 million ($0.06 per share) in Q2 2026 and $99 million ($0.11 per share) in the first half of 2026, following $229 million ($0.25 per share) for full-year 2025. Adjusting for these, Q2 2026 diluted EPS was $3.38 versus $3.32 GAAP, and FY2025 adjusted EPS was $12.20 versus $11.95 GAAP. Note the direction of the adjustment: GAAP growth of +5% for FY2025 becomes +4% adjusted, because the prior-year base was also charged.

Currency translation. Not a one-off in the accounting sense but a material distortion to the growth optics. Q2 2026 revenue grew 4% as reported but only 2% in constant currencies; operating income grew 3% reported versus 2% constant currency; FY2025 systemwide sales grew 7% reported versus 5% constant currency. Roughly two points of headline growth in both periods is dollar weakness. If the dollar stabilizes, reported growth converges down toward the constant-currency rate.

Refranchising. Company-operated sales declined from $9.8B (FY2024) to $9.7B (FY2025) while franchised revenues rose from $15.7B to $16.5B — the continuing shift of restaurants from corporate to franchisee ownership. This mechanically raises the consolidated margin and lowers consolidated revenue growth, and can generate episodic gains on sale. Investors should not read a rising operating margin as pure operating improvement.

Impairments and legal. No material impairment charges or litigation settlements were disclosed in the Q2 2026 or FY2025 earnings releases reviewed for this snapshot. Full detail on any smaller items would appear in the corresponding 10-Q and 10-K filings, which were not parsed here.

Five-year trajectory. MCD closed September 2021 at $216.12 and closed August 18, 2026 at $266.99 — a price return of roughly +23.5% over five years. Adding back roughly $32–33 per share of dividends collected over the period (annual dividends per share rose from $5.25 in 2021 to $7.44 on a TTM basis), the total return is in the region of +38% (dividend total estimated from annual DPS; not independently verified). That is a compound annual return of roughly 6–7% — the profile of a defensive compounder, not a growth stock, and materially behind the broad U.S. equity market over the same window. The path was not linear: the stock spent 2022 range-bound between roughly $211 and $251 while the market fell, ground higher through 2023 to $279, gave it all back to $243 by mid-2024 as the value war and traffic pressure bit, then rallied hard through 2025 and into a February 2026 peak of $336.88 before shedding 20.7% in six months to the current level.

Where it sits now. At $266.99 the stock is 2.3% above its 52-week low of $260.96 and 21.9% below its 52-week high of $341.75, with a 52-week change of −13.58%. All three moving-average proxies sit above the price, and the 10-month (200-day proxy) MA is now rolling over — a technically damaged tape. The 6-period monthly RSI has spent most of 2026 falling from overbought territory toward the oversold threshold, consistent with a persistent, orderly derating rather than a panic. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a 45.7% operating margin, 17.8% ROIC, 51-year dividend-growth franchise at 20.1x forward earnings and a 2.79% yield — near the bottom of its own valuation range and roughly 22% below its February 2026 high. The model is structurally protected: with ~95% of 45,000+ restaurants franchised, McDonald's collects rent and royalties on a $139 billion systemwide sales base and does not bear the food, labour or discount cost of the value war. The loyalty engine is compounding fast — 220 million active users and $40 billion of TTM loyalty sales, growing 20%+ — giving the company a targeting tool to buy traffic surgically rather than by cutting menu boards. If U.S. traffic stabilizes under new leadership, $7.76 billion of free cash flow, an 18.4% consensus upside and a 0.42 beta make this a favourably asymmetric defensive holding.

Bear Case

The core U.S. business is shrinking in the only unit that matters. Q2 2026 comps of +0.8% were entirely price and mix with negative guest counts, decelerating from +6.8% two quarters earlier — a chain that grows only by charging its existing customers more is eroding the value proposition that is its whole reason to exist. Meanwhile Burger King has taken the #2 U.S. burger position, Chick-fil-A continues to take chicken share, France and China are outright negative, and roughly two of the four points of reported revenue growth are dollar weakness that can reverse. A 2.77 PEG says you are paying a premium-durability multiple for mid-single-digit growth, and negative shareholders' equity of −$1.02 billion against $54.6 billion of debt leaves no balance-sheet cushion if the comp goes negative.

Top 3 Risks
01
Sustained negative U.S. guest counts. The single most important number in the company. Q2 2026 already showed negative traffic with comps held positive only by check and mix; a second consecutive quarter of the same pattern would confirm structural share loss rather than a timing effect, and would likely force a further round of price investment that compresses franchisee restaurant-level margins and slows net unit growth.
02
Low-income consumer trade-down. McDonald's over-indexes to the cohort most exposed to real-wage pressure. The company won that cohort back in late 2025 only by conceding price through Extra Value Meals, McValue and $5/$8 national bundles. If the squeeze intensifies, the choice is between losing traffic and cutting price again — and neither outcome is good for the 45.7% operating margin.
03
International rollover removing the offset. International comps carried the system through 2025 (IOM +3.2%, IDL +4.6%). Both decelerated hard in Q2 2026 (to +1.5% and +1.9%), with France and China negative. If the two largest international drags persist while the U.S. is flat, consolidated comps go to roughly zero and the earnings model loses its growth engine entirely — while a stronger dollar would simultaneously strip roughly two points from reported revenue growth.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Matters Right Now
The Low-Income Consumer and the Trade-Down Spiral
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the live issue and it is not abstract. McDonald's built the 2025 recovery explicitly on winning back price-sensitive households: Extra Value Meals relaunched in September 2024 "drove immediate incrementality," McValue launched January 2025, and $5 and $8 nationally priced meals ran in November and January. It worked — U.S. comps hit +6.8% in Q4 2025 and management reported gaining share with low-income consumers in December alongside a "meaningful increase in our value and affordability scores." CEO Chris Kempczinski's framing was blunt: "McDonald's is not going to get beat on value and affordability. It's in our DNA." Six months later, Q2 2026 U.S. comps were +0.8% with negative guest counts. The pattern is the trade-down spiral in action: value wins traffic, traffic gains fade as competitors match, and the only lever left is more price concession. Because McDonald's collects a royalty on franchisee sales rather than a margin on food, the corporation absorbs less of that pain than its franchisees do — but franchisee restaurant-level economics ultimately determine whether the system keeps building units. Watch the guest-count line, not the comp line.
GLP-1 Weight-Loss Drugs and the Shape of Food Demand
ONGOING HEADWIND
GLP-1 receptor agonists (the drug class behind semaglutide and tirzepatide) reduce appetite, and their adoption is a genuine long-duration variable for anyone selling calories. The survey evidence is more nuanced than the headlines: 34% of consumers report dining out less because of GLP-1 use and 70% of users report eating smaller portions at restaurants, but 48% of Gen Z and Millennial users report dining out more, and nearly 48% say they would increase restaurant visits if smaller portions were offered. The quantified industry view is comparatively benign: William Blair Equity Research estimated that even a doubling of GLP-1 usage over five years would reduce overall industry sales by less than 1% and traffic by roughly 5%. For McDonald's specifically the exposure is asymmetric in an unhelpful direction — its economics depend on volume of transactions and on combo-meal attachment (fries and a drink), which is precisely the part of the check a GLP-1 user trims first. The offset is that a smaller-portion, higher-protein menu architecture is a product problem McDonald's has the R&D scale and 220-million-member loyalty data to solve. Treat this as a slow, structural drag on average check and daypart mix rather than as a cliff.
Franchise-Model Resilience — Bull vs Bear Framing
EMERGING TAILWIND
Everything above lands differently because of how McDonald's is structured. The bull framing: with ~95% of the system franchised, McDonald's revenue is contractual rent and royalty income off $139 billion of systemwide sales, not the residual profit on a hamburger. That is why the operating margin is 45.72%, ROIC is 17.80%, FCF margin is 28.0%, and the dividend has risen for 51 consecutive years through every recession in that span. A traffic recession compresses franchisee income first and McDonald's royalty second — a structural shock absorber no fully-owned operator enjoys. On 20.1x forward earnings with a 2.79% yield and a 0.42 beta, the market is charging a modest premium for that resilience. The bear framing: the same structure means McDonald's cannot fix a traffic problem by simply cutting its own prices — it must persuade ~45,000 independent operators to accept thinner restaurant-level margins, which is slow and politically fraught. And the model's cash generation has been fully deployed: negative stockholders' equity of −$1.02 billion and $54.6 billion of debt are the accumulated evidence that the buyback has already been run to its limit. Resilience is not the same as growth, and at a 2.77 PEG the market is still pricing in more of the latter than the last two quarters have delivered.
