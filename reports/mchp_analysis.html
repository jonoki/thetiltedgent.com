01 Company Overview

Microchip Technology designs and manufactures microcontrollers, analog and mixed-signal chips, FPGAs, memory and connectivity silicon — the unglamorous embedded control content inside factory equipment, cars, satellites and, increasingly, data-centre racks. It reports two segments (Semiconductor Products and Technology Licensing) and sells into seven disclosed end markets. Fiscal 2026 (ended March 31, 2026) revenue was $4,713M, up 7.1% — the first growth year after a collapse that took revenue from $8,439M in FY2023 to $4,402M in FY2025, a fall of 47.8%. Over the twelve months to September 2026 revenue was $5,603M, up 33.0% on the year before, and the company's market value at the November 9, 2026 close was $33.47B (544.22M shares × $61.50).

The question has changed. A year ago it was whether Microchip could survive the deepest inventory correction in its history without cutting the dividend. The numbers have answered that: the September 2026 quarter grew 42.2% year over year, GAAP gross margin recovered from a trough of 51.6% (March 2025 quarter) to 65.9%, free cash flow was $396.1M on $1,621.5M of sales — 24.4% of revenue, and that cash covered the quarter's $247.6M of dividends 1.6 times. The live question is different: demand is now running ahead of what Microchip can make, the December quarter is guided flat to down, and the stock has fallen 32.6% from its June 2026 monthly close and 26.3% over the chart's five years. At $61.50 the market is paying 46× trailing GAAP earnings and 17.0× the FY2027 consensus adjusted EPS of $3.62. Which of those is the real multiple depends on where you think normal earnings sit — and this report argues the honest answer is a range, not a number.

Two points against a common framing. First, Microchip is not loss-making at the GAAP level: TTM net income attributable to common shareholders is $726.9M and the trailing P/E is 46.24× on $1.33 of GAAP EPS. The FY2025 GAAP loss of $2.7M is six quarters behind. Second, data centre is no longer a small piece: at 16.7% of September-quarter sales and +55.7% year over year, it is the second-largest end market and the fastest-growing.

The core advantage is inertia, and it is genuine. An embedded microcontroller is not a commodity part swapped on price at the next purchase order — it is the thing the customer's firmware was written for, the thing the schematic was laid out around, and the thing that has been through safety or radiation qualification. Once designed in, it typically stays for the product's life, which in industrial and automotive is measured in years and in aerospace in decades. That is what produces Microchip's gross margin profile — 65.9% GAAP in the September 2026 quarter (66.5% non-GAAP), guided to 62.9–63.9% GAAP for December, with Sanghi calling 65% non-GAAP the long-term level, — on parts that are, in silicon terms, unexotic. The Microsemi and Atmel acquisitions widened this from microcontrollers into FPGAs, timing, power and security, so a customer can source most of a board from one vendor with one set of tools. The cost of that breadth is on the balance sheet: $5.55B of gross debt against $457.0M of cash.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. A leveraged balance sheet built for a bigger company. The debt from the Microsemi acquisition (2018; about $8.35B for the shares, $10.15B including Microsemi's debt) was carried comfortably at FY2023's $8.44B of revenue, much less so at FY2025's $4.40B. Net debt is about $5.1B at 2.36× adjusted EBITDA (operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) on the company's own measure, down from 2.85× in June — manageable now, but at the FY2025 trough this debt is a large part of why the share price fell to $46.08. A second downcycle before the debt is materially reduced would be far more dangerous than the one just survived, and on the November 5 call the CFO said the dividend stays where it is for now while the board keeps taking leverage down.

2. Owned fabs cut both ways. Internal manufacturing gave Microchip supply security in 2021 and cost discipline in the recovery, but it also means fixed costs that do not fall when revenue does. The visible price was under-utilisation charges — still $26.6M in the September 2026 quarter even with revenue up 42%. The response has been capacity reduction: the company is closing Fab 2 in Tempe, Arizona and GAAP guidance explicitly excludes gains or charges from the ongoing evaluation of restructuring activities including the sale of that facility. That is a permanent reduction in the company's own ceiling, taken deliberately — and with lead times now stretching to 32 weeks, one that limits how fast it can meet demand.

3. Distribution-channel opacity. Much of Microchip's revenue goes through distributors, so its view of end demand runs through them. Distributor inventory is 25 days, flat on the quarter — but that same channel showed 29 days in June 2025 and had been far higher before that, and the 2022–24 correction happened precisely because what distributors bought bore no relation to what they sold on. Thin channel inventory is bullish for near-term orders and simultaneously removes the buffer that would absorb a demand shock.

4. Concentration in an AI-related line the company does not control. Data centre is now 16.7% of sales growing 55.7%, and it carries a large share of the recovery. But Microchip supplies parts to data-centre build-outs by the largest cloud and AI companies that it has no influence over, into sockets that Broadcom, Marvell and Astera Labs contest directly — and those same customers are now taking the outside packaging capacity Microchip needs. If AI data-centre spending slows, the fastest-growing sixth of the business slows first and hardest.

RSI compares the size of recent monthly gains to recent monthly losses on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen far and fast relative to its own recent history (often called "overbought"); below 30 means the opposite ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a year, and an expensive one can stay overbought.

Five years, a loss. The chart opens at $83.43 (the November 2021 month-end close) and ends at $61.50 on November 9, 2026 — a price return of −26.3%, before dividends. Over the same dates the S&P 500 fund SPY rose 65.2% and the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ 72.5%. Inside that line is a full boom-and-bust: a grind up to a monthly-close peak of $97.23 in May 2024, a 52.6% collapse to $46.08 in April 2025, a 105% rally to $94.65 in May 2026, and a 35.0% fall since. An investor who bought at the start has been paid dividends to sit through a 1.85-beta ride to a loss.

The critical thing the chart shows that the income statement does not: the share price peaked in May 2024, more than a year after revenue peaked in FY2023, and bottomed in April 2025, the month after the weakest revenue quarter (March 2025, $970.5M). The market was late to sell and on time to buy. The April 2026 monthly gain of +43.8% ($64.61 → $92.91) — the largest single month in the window — came a month before the full-year results. Relative performance. MCHP's price return over the five years to November 9, 2026 (from the Nov 9, 2021 close of $82.92) was −25.8%, against +61.0% for SPY and +71.9% for QQQ over the same dates; over one year (from the Nov 7, 2025 close) it was +9.3%, against +12.2% and +11.4%. With a beta of 1.85 the stock would be expected to amplify index gains; instead it lost money. Within semiconductors the picture is also unflattering: over the year to November 9, stockanalysis.com shows market value up 81.8% at Texas Instruments, 85.4% at STMicroelectronics, 38.3% at Infineon and 22.0% at NXP. For five years the stock has moved with the chip cycle rather than growing through it — the bull case is that the next five start from the bottom of an inventory cycle rather than the top.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The inventory correction, in the only numbers that measure it. Microchip's own inventory went from 214 days of supply at June 30, 2025 to 175 days at June 30, 2026 — then rose nine days to 184 days at September 30, 2026, a balance of $1,117.3M, because the company built finished goods in the quarter; the CFO said it does not want to do that again this quarter. In the distribution channel the work is done: distributor inventory is 25 days, flat on the quarter, against 29 days in June 2025. Book-to-bill "remained well above one" and the December quarter started with a higher backlog than September did. The problem has flipped from too much stock to too little capacity: Sanghi said "Our new lead times are anywhere from six weeks to 32 weeks", that high-margin orders for advanced products could not be filled, and that new capacity takes about six months to bring up, with more arriving over the next few quarters. Capex is still guided to about $100 million for FY2027 ($37.0M in the first half), and Fab 2 in Tempe is being closed. That is a company that sized itself for a smaller revenue ceiling and is now being asked for more.

So where do normal earnings sit? Four anchors. (i) Trailing GAAP: $1.33 of EPS, a 46× multiple. Real and audited, but it still includes the early-recovery quarters. (ii) The December guide: GAAP EPS of $0.47–0.50 annualises to $1.88–2.00, or 31–33×; non-GAAP $0.87–0.95 annualises to $3.48–3.80, or 16–18×. (iii) The September quarter: GAAP EPS of $0.68 annualises to $2.72, or 23× — flattered by the $53M of one-off revenue. (iv) Consensus: FY2027 (ending March 2027) revenue of $6.38B and adjusted EPS of $3.62, or 17.0×; FY2028 EPS of $4.49, or 13.7× (stockanalysis, Nov 9). For context, FY2023 delivered $8,439M of revenue and $4.02 of GAAP EPS. A defensible range is roughly $2.50–$3.75 of sustainable earnings power — GAAP at the bottom of that band, adjusted at the top — putting the stock somewhere between 16× and 25×. That is cheap if long lead times mean real demand is outrunning supply, and not cheap if the September quarter proves to be the top of a restocking wave. The 17% fall between editions reads as the market leaning toward the second view.

Is the dividend safe? Yes on cash, not yet on earnings. The common dividend is $0.455 per quarter, $1.82 a year. Against 544.22M shares that is about $990M of annual cash. Against trailing GAAP earnings the payout is 137% ($1.82 against $1.33 of EPS) — accounting profit still does not cover it. Against trailing free cash flow of $1,455M it is 68%, or 1.47 times covered, and in the September quarter $396.1M of free cash flow paid $247.6M of dividends, 1.6 times over. On top of the common dividend sits the 7.50% Series A Mandatory Convertible Preferred, which ranks ahead of it ($27.8M a quarter). On the November 5 call the CFO said the company will keep the dividend where it is for now while the board keeps taking leverage down. Spare cash is going toward about $5.1B of net debt, which fell $31.7M in the quarter even after paying for Hailo. That is the right order of priorities, and it means the 2.96% yield is a coupon on a debt-reduction story, not a growing income stream.

The Microsemi debt, in context. Gross debt of $5.55B at September 30, 2026 — $3.0B of senior notes, $2.19B of convertible notes and $364.0M of commercial paper (10-Q) — against $457.0M of cash is mostly the residue of the 2018 Microsemi acquisition, and it is why the FY2025 trough was frightening rather than merely unpleasant. It also bought the aerospace & defence franchise now growing 41.0% and much of the data-centre connectivity line now growing 55.7%. Net debt to adjusted EBITDA of 2.36× (from 2.85× in June; trailing adjusted EBITDA $2.162B, per the CFO) improves as earnings recover. The 2025 capital raise the market feared — a dilutive issue at the bottom — is on the balance sheet as the 7.50% Series A Mandatory Convertible Preferred: 1,485,000 shares with a $1,000 liquidation preference each, paying $18.750 per share a quarter ($0.9375 per depositary share) ahead of the common. Note also that the non-GAAP diluted share count is 574.4M against 550.6M GAAP — a 4.3% gap that reflects the preferred's eventual conversion into common shares.

Bull Case

Demand is ahead of supply. Sales grew 42.2% in the September quarter and beat the top of guidance; book-to-bill stayed well above one and backlog rose into December. The December guide's midpoint is 1% below a September quarter that included $53M of one-offs — about 2% above it without them — in a quarter Sanghi says normally falls 3–5%. Non-GAAP gross margin of 66.5% is above the 65% management calls its long-term level, and free cash flow was $1.46B over twelve months with capex of only about $100M a year. Data centre is above $250M a quarter and on course for about $1B in calendar 2026, PCIe Gen6 design wins doubled to 24, net leverage fell to 2.36×, and consensus sees $3.62 of FY2027 adjusted EPS and $4.49 for FY2028 — 17× and 14× today's price.

Bear Case

Five years of ownership has produced a 26.3% price loss while SPY gained 65.2% over the same dates. The September beat leaned on about $53M of one-off revenue, the December guide is flat to down with non-GAAP gross margin guided to 64–65% from 66.5%, and inventory rose nine days to 184. The company shut Fab 2 to cut costs and is now short of outside packaging capacity that AI customers have taken, so it may not be able to serve the upturn it waited three years for. The dividend is 137% of GAAP earnings, $5.55B of debt sits against $457.0M of cash, and the preferred ranks ahead of the common. All eight brokers on the stockanalysis.com table cut targets after the print, 5.85% of the float is sold short, and a 1.85 beta amplifies any disappointment.

Top 3 Risks

01
A second downcycle before the balance sheet is repaired. Net debt of about $5.1B at 2.36× adjusted EBITDA is serviceable while revenue grows 42%, but FY2025 showed what happens when it doesn't: a GAAP operating loss, a dividend covered by neither earnings nor comfortably by cash, and a 52.6% fall in the share price. With distributor inventory at a lean 25 days there is no buffer to absorb a demand shock — lower orders would show up in revenue at once.
02
Long lead times breed double orders. Sanghi says lead times now run up to 32 weeks and high-margin orders for advanced products went unfilled. That is how the last cycle began: in 2021–22 long lead times led customers and distributors to order more than they needed, and the unwinding took revenue down 47.8%. If part of today's backlog is duplicate orders, the next correction starts from a higher base — and at 46× trailing GAAP earnings the stock has little room for it.
03
Data-centre concentration cutting the other way. Data centre, the part of the business most tied to AI build-outs, is 16.7% of sales and grew 55.7%. The November 5 chip sell-off, driven by doubts about the return on AI data-centre spending (CNBC), showed how fast that mood can turn: MCHP fell 13.9% that day before reporting a number. If Broadcom, Marvell and Astera Labs take the PCIe Gen6 sockets Microchip is winning now, the recovery loses its steepest contributor.
  Industry Commentary
The Correction Is Over; Supply Is the New Limit
SHIFTING
Between FY2023 and FY2025 Microchip's revenue fell 47.8% — from $8,439M to $4,402M — while the world did not stop building factories, cars or satellites. The gap was inventory: customers and distributors who had double-ordered through the 2021–22 shortage stopped ordering and ran down what they held. That phase is over: distributor inventory has sat at 25 days for two quarters and sales grew 42.2% in the September quarter. Peers are growing more slowly — trailing-twelve-month revenue +20.3% at Texas Instruments, +15.9% at STMicroelectronics, +14.6% at NXP and +6.5% at Infineon, against Microchip's +33.0%. The new pattern is the old one starting again: lead times of up to 32 weeks and outside capacity taken by AI customers are the conditions in which customers start ordering ahead of need.
Fewer Fabs Cut Costs — and Now Cap Growth
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The in-house manufacturing model that protected Microchip in 2021 hurt it in 2024–25: fixed fab costs do not fall with revenue. Its response was to shrink — Fab 2 in Tempe is being closed, with a sale under evaluation, and capex is guided to about $100M for FY2027. Under-utilisation charges were still $26.6M in the September quarter, with similar levels expected in December. Yet the company is now short of capacity in the steps it outsources: Sanghi said AI companies "came in and took over the whole factory, and they pushed everybody out" at major outside packaging-and-test suppliers. It is adding capacity inside, which he said takes about six months, with more arriving over the next few quarters. The cuts raised returns on what remains; they also mean this upturn is rationed.
Embedded Silicon Found an AI Adjacency — And a New Concentration Risk
EMERGING TAILWIND
The AI build-out needs far more than accelerators: it needs PCIe switches and retimers to connect them, timing and clocking to synchronise them, storage controllers and power management. Microchip's data-centre unit sold over $250M in the September quarter and is, in Sanghi's words, "well on its way to be $1 billion in calendar year 2026", against $591M in calendar 2025; PCIe Gen6 design wins went from 12 at the end of June to 19 at the end of September and 24 by the call. Hailo, closed September 17, adds edge-AI vision processors. Bull framing: data centre (16.7% of sales, +55.7%) and aerospace & defence (16.1%, +41.0%) are a third of the business and growing faster than the cyclical base, which could justify a higher valuation across the whole chip cycle than the stock has carried. Bear framing: the same AI customers are crowding Microchip out of outside packaging capacity, and on November 5 the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2% on doubts about the return on AI data-centre spending (CNBC) — MCHP lost 13.9% that day before reporting. If AI-related revenue is what holds the valuation up, it is also what can take it down.
