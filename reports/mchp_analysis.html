01 Company Overview

Microchip Technology designs and manufactures microcontrollers, analog and mixed-signal chips, FPGAs, memory and connectivity silicon — the unglamorous embedded control content inside factory equipment, cars, satellites and, increasingly, data-centre racks. It reports two segments (Semiconductor Products and Technology Licensing) and sells into seven disclosed end markets. Fiscal 2026 (ended March 31, 2026) revenue was $4,713M, up 7.1% — the first growth year after a collapse that took revenue from $8,439M in FY2023 to $4,402M in FY2025, a fall of 47.8%. On a trailing-twelve-month basis revenue is now $5,122M, up 20.9%, and the company ranks roughly #653 in the world by market capitalisation at $40.27B.

The central debate has moved. A year ago the question was whether Microchip could survive the deepest inventory correction in its history without cutting the dividend. That question has now largely been answered by the numbers: the June 2026 quarter grew 38.0% year over year, gross margin recovered from a trough of 51.6% (March 2025 quarter) to 63.2%, free cash flow was $497.6M on $1,485M of sales — 33.5% of revenue, and the dividend was covered 2.0× out of a single quarter's cash. The live question is different and harder: the stock is flat over five years and has given back 22% since its June 2026 monthly close, even as the operating recovery accelerated. At $74.17 the market is paying 109× trailing GAAP earnings, 18.8× forward earnings, and roughly 20× the FY2027 consensus adjusted EPS of $3.65. Which of those is the real multiple depends entirely on where you think normalised earnings sit — and this report argues the honest answer is a range, not a number.

Two corrections to a common framing. First, Microchip is not currently loss-making at the GAAP level: TTM net income is $367.2M and the trailing P/E is a computable (if unflattering) 109.32×. The FY2025 GAAP loss of $2.7M is now four quarters behind. Second, data centre is no longer a small piece: at 17.1% of June-quarter revenue and +97.8% year over year, it is the second-largest end market and the fastest-growing by a wide margin.

The core advantage is inertia, and it is genuine. An embedded microcontroller is not a commodity part swapped on price at the next purchase order — it is the thing the customer's firmware was written for, the thing the schematic was laid out around, and the thing that has been through safety or radiation qualification. Once designed in, it typically stays for the product's life, which in industrial and automotive is measured in years and in aerospace in decades. That is what produces Microchip's gross margin profile — 63.2% GAAP in the June 2026 quarter, guided to 65.4–66.3% for September — on parts that are, in silicon terms, unexotic. The Microsemi and Atmel acquisitions widened this from microcontrollers into FPGAs, timing, power and security, so a customer can source most of a board from one vendor with one set of tools. The cost of that breadth is on the balance sheet: $5.40B of total debt against $272.3M of cash.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. A leveraged balance sheet built for a bigger company. The debt that funded the $8.35B Microsemi acquisition was sized against FY2023's $8.44B of revenue, not FY2025's $4.40B. Net debt is $5.2B at 2.85× EBITDA on the company's own measure — manageable now, but at the FY2025 trough this ratio was the reason the equity de-rated to $46.08. A second downcycle before the debt is materially reduced would be a much more dangerous event than the one just survived, and management has said explicitly there is no plan to buy back stock and no plan to increase the dividend — capital is going to deleveraging.

2. Owned fabs cut both ways. Internal manufacturing gave Microchip supply security in 2021 and cost discipline in the recovery, but it also means fixed costs that do not fall when revenue does. The visible price was under-utilisation charges — still $38.5M in the June 2026 quarter even with revenue up 38%. The response has been capacity reduction: the company is closing Fab 2 in Tempe, Arizona and GAAP guidance explicitly excludes gains or charges from the ongoing evaluation of restructuring activities including the sale of that facility. That is a permanent reduction in the company's own ceiling, taken deliberately.

3. Distribution-channel opacity. Roughly half the industry sells through distributors, and Microchip's own visibility into end demand runs through them. Channel inventory is now 25 days, down one sequentially and at the lower end of the historical range — but that same channel showed 29 days a year ago and had been far higher before that, and the 2022–24 correction happened precisely because reported sell-in bore no relation to sell-through. Thin channel inventory is bullish for near-term orders and simultaneously removes the buffer that would absorb a demand shock.

4. Concentration in an AI-adjacent line the company does not control. Data centre is now 17.1% of revenue growing 98%, and it is doing a disproportionate share of the margin recovery. But Microchip sells picks and shovels to hyperscaler build-outs it has no influence over, into sockets that Broadcom, Marvell and Astera Labs are contesting directly. If AI capex growth normalises in calendar 2027, the fastest-growing sixth of the business decelerates first and hardest.

RSI compares the size of recent monthly gains to recent monthly losses on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen far and fast relative to its own recent history (often called "overbought"); below 30 means the opposite ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a year, and an expensive one can stay overbought.

Five years, zero return. The chart opens at $74.09 (October 2021, the first month after the 2-for-1 stock split) and closes at $74.17 on September 4, 2026 — a total price return of +0.1% over five years, before the roughly $8 per share of dividends paid over the period. Inside that flat line is a full boom-and-bust: a grind up to a monthly-close peak of $97.23 in May 2024, a 52.6% collapse to $46.08 in April 2025, a violent 102% rally to $92.91 by April 2026, and a 20% give-back since. An investor who bought the split-adjusted open and held has been paid a dividend to endure a 1.74-beta ride to nowhere.

The critical thing the chart shows that the income statement does not: the equity peaked in May 2024, more than a year after revenue peaked in FY2023, and troughed in April 2025, roughly one quarter after revenue troughed. The market was late to sell and roughly on time to buy. The April 2026 monthly gain of +43.8% ($64.61 → $92.91) — the largest single month in the five-year window — preceded the May 2026 fiscal-year-end results and was not attributable to a company-specific announcement in the sources fetched; it is best read as a sector-wide re-rating that Microchip participated in. The subsequent slide from $91.20 (June) to $74.29 (July) and $73.45 (August), through a quarter in which revenue grew 38%, is the market marking down the multiple rather than the numbers.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The inventory correction, in the only numbers that measure it. Microchip's own inventory went from 214 days of supply at June 30, 2025, to 185 days at March 31, 2026, to 175 days at June 30, 2026 — a balance of $1,047.3M against a stated target of 130–150 days. So roughly two-thirds of the excess has been drained and a quarter of it remains. In the distribution channel the work is essentially done: distributor inventory is 25 days, down one sequentially, and management describes it as at the lower end of the historical range — against 29 days a year earlier. Book-to-bill is "well above 1" with backlog higher entering the September quarter. Capacity has been permanently reduced rather than idled: Fab 2 in Tempe is being closed and potentially sold, factory expansion was paused, and FY2027 capex is guided to approximately $100 million — against $13.9M actually spent in the June quarter. That is a company that has decided its FY2023 revenue ceiling is not coming back soon, and has sized itself accordingly.

So where do normalised earnings sit? Four honest anchors, in ascending order. (i) Trailing GAAP: $0.68 of EPS, a 109× multiple. Real, audited, and useless — it averages the trough with the recovery. (ii) The current run rate: annualise the June quarter's GAAP EPS of $0.37 and you get $1.48, or 50×. Still low, because margins are still climbing. (iii) The guided exit rate: September-quarter GAAP EPS is guided to $0.53–0.54, which annualises to roughly $2.14, or about 35×; non-GAAP $0.91–0.95 annualises to about $3.72, or 20×. (iv) Consensus FY2027 (ending March 2027): revenue of $6.40B (+35.7%) and adjusted EPS of $3.65, or 20.3× — closely consistent with the reported forward P/E of 18.81. For context, FY2023 delivered $8,439M of revenue and $4.02 of GAAP EPS on essentially the same asset base, so a genuine cycle peak is plausibly above $4.00. The defensible normalised range is therefore roughly $2.50–$3.75 of sustainable earnings power — GAAP at the bottom of that band, adjusted at the top — putting the stock somewhere between 20× and 30×. That is not obviously cheap for a business that has grown revenue at a compound rate near zero over five years, and not obviously expensive for one exiting a trough with 38% growth and 33.5% free-cash-flow margins. The market's refusal to pay up in July and August 2026 is a judgement about which end of that band is real.

Is the dividend safe? Quantified, and the answer is yes — but only just, on trailing numbers. The common dividend is $0.455 per quarter, $1.82 annualised. Against 543.01M shares that is roughly $988M of annual cash cost. Set that against the three relevant denominators. Against trailing GAAP earnings the payout ratio is 268.25% — the dividend is not covered by accounting profit and has not been for two years. Against trailing free cash flow of $1,111M the payout is 88.9%, or 1.12× covered — technically safe, uncomfortably tight. But against the current run rate the picture is much better: the June quarter generated $497.6M of free cash flow and paid $246.9M of dividends, a 2.0× cover, and with FY2027 capex guided to only ~$100M for the full year, free cash flow should track operating cash flow closely. On top of the common dividend sits the 7.50% Series A Mandatory Convertible Preferred, a senior claim whose total annual cost was not disclosed in the sources fetched. Management's stated policy settles the question of intent: "no plan to buy back stock and no plan to increase the dividend." The dividend is being held flat and every incremental dollar is going to the $5.2B of net debt — roughly $170M of which was repaid in the June quarter alone. That is the right priority order, and it means the yield is a 2.45% coupon on a deleveraging story, not a growing income stream.

The Microsemi debt, in context. The $5.40B of total debt against $272.3M of cash is the residue of the 2018 Microsemi acquisition, and it is the reason the FY2025 trough was frightening rather than merely unpleasant. It is also, in fairness, what bought the aerospace & defence franchise now growing 45.6% and much of the data-centre connectivity portfolio now growing 97.8%. Net debt/EBITDA of 2.85× is serviceable at current cash generation and improves mechanically as EBITDA recovers; at the FY2025 trough the same ratio would have been materially worse. The 2025 capital raise the market feared — a dilutive equity issue at the bottom — is visible on the balance sheet as the 7.50% Series A Mandatory Convertible Preferred. Its issue size and date were not verified from the sources fetched and are therefore not stated; what is verified is that it exists, that it pays $18.750 per share quarterly ($0.9375 per depositary share), and that it sits ahead of the common in the cash-flow queue. Note also that non-GAAP diluted share count is 574.3M against 550.5M GAAP — a 4.3% wedge that reflects the preferred's eventual conversion.

Bull Case

The correction is measurably over and the operating leverage is enormous. Revenue grew 38.0% in the June quarter with gross margin up from 51.6% at the trough to 63.2%, and September guidance of 65.4–66.3% GAAP margin on $1.589–1.618B implies GAAP EPS of $0.53–0.54 — annualising to $2.14 against $0.68 trailing. Inventory has fallen from 214 to 175 days with the channel already at 25 days, book-to-bill is well above 1, capacity has been permanently cut (Fab 2 Tempe) and capex is only ~$100M for FY2027, so nearly all incremental revenue converts to cash. Data centre goes from $591M in CY2025 to ~$1B in CY2026 with PCIe Gen6 design wins doubling quarterly, aerospace & defence grows 45.6%, and consensus sees $6.40B of FY2027 revenue and $3.65 of adjusted EPS — 20× today's price for a business exiting a trough with 33.5% free-cash-flow margins.

Bear Case

Five years of ownership has produced a 0.1% price return, and the stock has fallen 18.7% since June 2026 despite 38% revenue growth — the market is compressing the multiple faster than earnings are recovering, and four brokers cut targets the day after the beat. Trailing ROIC of 5.87% and ROE of 7.19% are below any plausible cost of capital; the dividend consumes 88.9% of trailing free cash flow and 268% of GAAP earnings; and $5.40B of debt against $272.3M of cash leaves no room for a second downcycle. Revenue is still 39% below the FY2023 peak and the company has permanently closed capacity, implying management does not expect to get back there. The fastest-growing sixth of revenue is levered to hyperscaler capex it does not control, 6.07% of the float is short, and a 1.74 beta means any disappointment is amplified.

Top 3 Risks

01
A second downcycle before the balance sheet is repaired. Net debt of $5.2B at 2.85× EBITDA is serviceable while revenue grows 38%, but the FY2025 experience showed what happens when it doesn't: a GAAP operating loss, a dividend covered by neither earnings nor comfortably by cash, and a 52.6% drawdown. With distributor inventory already at a lean 25 days there is no buffer left to absorb a demand shock — orders would fall through to revenue immediately.
02
The normalisation stalls between $2.14 and $3.65 of EPS. The entire valuation case rests on getting from $0.68 trailing to something near consensus $3.65. If gross margin plateaus below the guided 65–66%, or if the remaining 25–45 days of excess inventory take longer than expected to drain, the stock is left at 30–35× a mediocre earnings base with 5.87% ROIC — a multiple no cyclical semiconductor sustains.
03
Data-centre concentration cutting the other way. Data centre is 17.1% of revenue growing 97.8%, communications another 8.2% growing 53.3% — a quarter of the business is now levered to AI-adjacent build-outs. Management has committed publicly to ~$1B of calendar-2026 data-centre revenue. If hyperscaler capex growth normalises, or if Broadcom, Marvell and Astera Labs take the PCIe Gen6 sockets Microchip is currently winning, the recovery loses its steepest contributor and reverts to the mid-teens growth of the industrial base.
  Industry Commentary
The Analog/MCU Correction Was a Channel Event, Not a Demand Event
ONGOING HEADWIND
Between FY2023 and FY2025 Microchip's revenue fell 47.8% — from $8,439M to $4,402M — while the world did not stop building factories, cars or satellites. The gap was inventory: customers and distributors who had double-ordered through the 2021–22 shortage stopped ordering entirely and ran down what they held. The evidence that this is now largely worked through is in the days figures, not the revenue line: Microchip's own inventory has fallen from 214 days (June 2025) to 175 (June 2026) against a 130–150 target, and distributor inventory sits at 25 days, at the low end of its historical range. Peers show the same pattern with different timing — TXN TTM revenue +16.7%, STM +10.5%, NXPI +8.8%, Infineon +6.5%, all positive, none at Microchip's +20.9%, because Microchip fell furthest and therefore rebounds hardest off a lower base. The residual headwind is the 25–45 days of company-held excess still to drain, which suppresses reported revenue below true end-demand for at least another two quarters.
Owning Fabs Has Become a Liability the Industry Is Shedding
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The integrated-device-manufacturer model that protected Microchip in 2021 punished it in 2024–25: fixed fab costs do not fall with revenue, and under-utilisation charges were still $38.5M in the June 2026 quarter even with sales up 38%. The structural response is visible across the sector and explicit at Microchip — Fab 2 in Tempe is being closed, with a potential sale under evaluation, factory expansion was paused, and FY2027 capex is guided to roughly $100M, a fraction of the multi-hundred-million programmes of the boom. That is a permanent reduction in internal capacity, which raises returns on the assets that remain but also caps how fast the company can serve a genuine upturn without leaning on foundry partners. It is management's own statement that they do not expect to need FY2023-scale capacity again soon.
Embedded Silicon Found an AI Adjacency — And a New Concentration Risk
EMERGING TAILWIND
The AI build-out needs far more than accelerators: it needs PCIe switches and retimers to connect them, timing and clocking to synchronise them, storage controllers and power management. Microchip's data-centre revenue goes from $591M (CY2025) to approximately $1.0B (CY2026), PCIe Gen6 programs stand at 14 having doubled sequentially, and the Hailo acquisition adds edge-AI vision processors that Sanghi says advance the roadmap "by about five years." Bull framing: a legacy embedded-control company has acquired a genuine secular growth engine at 17.1% of revenue growing 98%, plus a 16.7% aerospace franchise growing 46% — two-thirds of the growth now comes from structurally advantaged rather than cyclical demand, which justifies a higher through-cycle multiple than the stock has ever carried. Bear framing: that same quarter of the business is levered to a capex cycle Microchip neither controls nor forecasts, competing for sockets against Broadcom, Marvell and Astera Labs, and the market's response to the 98%-growth quarter was to cut price targets and sell the stock 18.7% from its June high. If AI-adjacent revenue is what is holding the multiple up, it is also what will take it down.
