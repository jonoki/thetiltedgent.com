01 Company Overview

McKesson is the largest pharmaceutical distributor in North America and, at $410.98B of trailing-twelve-month revenue, one of the largest companies in the world by sales — comfortably inside the global top ten on that measure while ranking only #220 globally by market capitalisation at $105.86B. Fiscal 2026 (year ended March 31, 2026) revenue was $403.43B, up 12.4% from $359.05B in FY2025. It buys drugs from manufacturers, warehouses them, and delivers them to roughly every pharmacy, hospital, oncology practice and physician office in the United States and Canada — plus a technology business that sits between manufacturers, payers and patients, and a medical-surgical products business.

The central thing to understand about this business, before anything else, is that its margin percentage is meaningless as a quality signal. McKesson earned $14.53B of gross profit on $403.43B of revenue in FY2026 — 3.6 cents on the dollar — and $6.29B of operating income, or 1.56 cents on the dollar. A retailer or a software company with those numbers would be a disaster. A distributor with those numbers is doing exactly what it is supposed to do: earning a thin, contractually-negotiated spread on a vast and largely non-discretionary volume of goods it does not own for very long. The company is not paid to have a fat margin; it is paid to move pills. Nobody who understands the model looks at 1.56%. They look at two things instead: the dollar growth of operating profit (which grew 27.3% in FY2026, more than twice the 12.4% revenue growth), and return on invested capital (31.75%), because the whole trick of distribution is doing it with almost no capital tied up. This report is organised around that lens — and around where inside the business the profit growth is actually coming from, which is not where the revenue is.

The core wholesale business: buying branded and generic drugs from manufacturers and delivering them daily to retail pharmacy chains, independents, hospitals and health systems across the US and Canada. Economics work in two distinct layers. On branded drugs the distributor earns a fee that is largely a negotiated percentage of the manufacturer's list price — high revenue, very low margin, and exposed to manufacturer price actions and to the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare price-setting. On generics, McKesson buys through a global sourcing joint venture and captures a genuine buy-side spread — far higher margin on far lower revenue. That mix is why the segment can grow revenue 11% and operating profit 10% in the same year without either number telling you much on its own. In fiscal Q1 2027 (quarter ended June 30, 2026) the segment did $86.8B of revenue, up 5%, and $894M of operating profit, up 19% — profit growing almost four times faster than revenue is the signature of mix improving underneath a flat-ish spread. Management's own long-term target for this segment is 5–8% annual operating profit growth; it is running well ahead of that.

The GLP-1 distortion: GLP-1 distribution revenue was $15B in fiscal Q1 2027 alone, up 24% YoY — see the discussion below the segment cards. That is 14.2% of total company revenue from one drug class.

This is the reason to own the stock, and McKesson broke it out as its own segment precisely so investors would stop burying it inside the wholesale line. The platform has three parts. First, specialty distribution — the high-cost, cold-chain, limited-distribution oncology and multispecialty drugs that carry structurally better economics than a bottle of generic lisinopril. Second, the US Oncology Network, an affiliated network of community oncology practices — around 3,400 providers — for which McKesson provides practice management, group purchasing, clinical pathways and research infrastructure, and from which it earns service revenue as well as distribution volume. Third, Core Ventures, the business-and-administrative-services platform McKesson built with Florida Cancer Specialists and has since expanded by acquisition, which extends the same model to practices that do not want to join the network outright. The compounding logic: every practice added is simultaneously a distribution customer, a services customer, and a source of real-world clinical data that manufacturers pay for. Segment operating margin is 2.89% — nearly triple North American Pharmaceutical's 1.04%, on drugs whose per-unit prices are enormous. In fiscal Q1 2027 the segment did $14.2B of revenue, up 33%, and $405M of operating profit, up 41%. Strip out the Core Ventures acquisition and organic growth was still roughly 24% revenue and 15% operating profit — so the growth is real, and roughly two-thirds of the profit growth in the quarter came from M&A. Management's long-term target here is 13–16% annual operating profit growth; recent delivery has been three to four times that.

Why it matters: this segment is 12% of revenue but 20% of operating profit, and it grew profit 53% in FY2026 against the company's 27%. It is the swing factor in the entire thesis.

The software-and-services layer, and the highest-margin thing McKesson owns at 18.97% operating margin. It runs prior-authorisation automation, patient affordability and copay-assistance programmes, adherence tools, and the third-party network plumbing that connects pharmacies, manufacturers and payers. Its economics are the opposite of the distribution business: negligible revenue per transaction, but no inventory, no trucks, and no working capital. The clearest recent example of what this segment does is the CMS Medicare GLP-1 Bridge programme, which McKesson launched support for in July 2026 and on which it reports 95% of prior-authorisation requests receiving determinations within 30 minutes. In fiscal Q1 2027 the segment did $1.6B of revenue (+9%) and $303M of operating profit (+13%). Long-term target: 10–13% operating profit growth. If you want the single line that explains why a 1.5%-margin company earns a 31.75% return on invested capital, it is this one.

Medical supplies, equipment and lab products distributed to physician offices, surgery centres, extended-care facilities and home health — a genuinely different business from drug wholesaling, with an 8.70% operating margin and, at 2.9% of revenue, 14% of operating profit. It is also the only segment going backwards: FY2026 revenue and operating profit both grew just 1%, and fiscal Q1 2027 operating profit fell 20% year over year on 4% revenue growth. The business is being repositioned rather than fixed quietly: McKesson has signalled a separation of the unit, which is to operate under the name Wellverse from January 2027, with a private-equity minority partner taking a stake of roughly 13%. Investors should treat FY2027 segment comparisons here as non-comparable and read the separation terms carefully when they are disclosed.

Watch item: the Wellverse separation and the ~13% minority stake were reported in secondary coverage of the fiscal Q1 2027 call; the definitive structure and financial terms were not verified from a primary filing in the sources fetched.

The GLP-1 effect, quantified. This deserves its own paragraph because it is the single biggest reason McKesson's income statement is currently misread. In fiscal Q1 2027, GLP-1 distribution revenue was $15B, up 24% year over year — an increase of roughly $3B. Total company revenue in the quarter was $105.38B, up $7.55B. So one drug class accounted for roughly 40% of the entire company's revenue growth, and 14.2% of its total revenue. Now the profit side. North American Pharmaceutical's operating margin is 1.03% in that quarter ($894M on $86.8B). If that incremental $3B of GLP-1 revenue had carried the segment's average margin, it would have produced about $31M of operating profit — against a segment operating profit increase of $143M. And branded, high-list-price, fee-for-service drugs of this type carry economics below the segment average, not at it, because the distributor's compensation is a small negotiated fee rather than a buy-side spread. The honest reading: GLP-1s inflate the top line enormously and contribute very little to profit. Anyone modelling McKesson off revenue growth is modelling the wrong variable — and anyone alarmed by "margin compression" when GLP-1 volumes surge is watching a mix artefact, not a deterioration. Note: McKesson does not disclose GLP-1 profit contribution on the sources fetched; the $31M figure above is an arithmetic upper-bound illustration, not a company-reported number.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
TRIOPOLY STRUCTURE SCALE COST ADVANTAGE GENERIC SOURCING JV NEGATIVE WORKING CAPITAL DEA-REGULATED BARRIER ONCOLOGY NETWORK LOCK-IN RxTS SWITCHING COSTS

The moat is structural, not clever. Three companies — McKesson, Cencora and Cardinal Health — distribute roughly the entire US pharmaceutical supply. Between them they carry about $998B of trailing revenue ($410.98B + $332.77B + $254.25B). A new entrant would need national warehousing, next-morning delivery to every pharmacy in the country, DEA controlled-substance licensing in every state, and enough purchasing volume to negotiate a spread — and would then be competing for a business that earns one and a half cents on the dollar. That is an unattractive proposition even for a very well-capitalised attacker, which is why the structure has been stable for decades.

The financial expression of the moat is the balance sheet, and it is worth pausing on because it looks alarming until you understand it. McKesson has negative stockholders' equity of −$1.30B and a current ratio of 0.89. It gets paid by pharmacies faster than it pays manufacturers, so it funds its inventory with its suppliers' money — negative working capital. Combine that with years of buying back stock at prices above book value and you get a company whose accounting equity is below zero while it generates $6.42B of free cash flow a year. This is why the ROIC of 31.75% is the number that matters and the ROE is not just unhelpful but mathematically meaningless — a positive profit divided by negative equity produces a negative ratio that means nothing. Any screen or comparison that shows McKesson with a negative or absurd ROE should be discarded on sight.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer concentration. A distributor with a 1.5% operating margin has essentially no cushion against the loss or repricing of a large national pharmacy chain contract. Contract renewals with the biggest customers are the highest-stakes recurring events in the business, and the concentration cuts both ways — a chain that consolidates or shrinks takes volume with it.
▸
Opioid litigation and the compliance overhang. McKesson remains a defendant in and a payer under opioid-related settlements arising from its historical controlled-substance distribution. It recorded a $108M claims-and-litigation charge in FY2026 and a further $114M nine-month charge relating to opioid claims of a nationwide group of third-party payors. The annual cash payment schedule and the remaining accrued liability under the national settlement could not be verified from the sources fetched and are deliberately not quantified here — but the structural point holds: this is a multi-year, contractually fixed cash outflow that reduces free cash flow available for buybacks without ever appearing in adjusted operating profit, and it is a real drag on the capital-return story rather than a closed chapter.
▸
Drug pricing policy. Branded distribution fees are largely a percentage of manufacturer list price. The Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare negotiation programme lowers list prices on selected high-volume drugs; management explicitly attributed part of a recent revenue shortfall to "manufacturer price declines" and the IRA's first pricing wave. Lower list prices mechanically shrink the fee base on exactly the drugs with the most volume.
▸
The cybersecurity incident of August 25, 2026. McKesson disclosed a cybersecurity incident affecting its information systems on August 25, 2026, with the investigation described as being in early stages. For a company whose entire function is the uninterrupted daily movement of medicine, an IT disruption is an operational risk of a different character than it is for most businesses. Scope, financial impact and remediation cost were not quantified on the sources fetched.

The buyback — quantify it before you credit the EPS growth. McKesson repurchased $4.8B of stock in FY2026 and a further $2.5B in fiscal Q1 2027 alone, including a $2.25B accelerated share repurchase executed at roughly $755 per share. Diluted share count fell from 128.1M in FY2025 to 124.1M in FY2026 (−3.1%). Over a longer window the effect is much larger: FY2023 diluted shares of about 142.2M have become 124.1M — a 12.7% reduction in three years. Over that same span net income grew from $3,560M to $4,762M, +33.8%, while diluted EPS grew from $25.03 to $38.38, +53.3%. Roughly 19.5 points of that 53.3-point EPS gain — about 37% of it — came from owning a smaller denominator, not from earning more money. That is not a criticism; retiring stock at a 31.75% ROIC business is a rational use of cash, and it is precisely what a negative-equity balance sheet is a symptom of. But an investor underwriting "13–16% long-term EPS growth" should be clear that several points of it are financial engineering funded by free cash flow, and that it requires the buyback to continue. As of March 31, 2026 there was $2.7B remaining on the authorisation; the board added $5.0B in April 2026, bringing the total to $7.7B.

Notable One-Off Items. Three matter. (1) The European exit: the Norwegian retail and distribution sale to NorgesGruppen closed January 30, 2026 and generated approximately $480M of pre-tax gain — a one-time item inflating FY2026 GAAP results, and the completion of a multi-year withdrawal from Europe that removes an entire reporting segment from year-over-year comparisons. (2) Opioid-related charges: a $108M FY2026 claims-and-litigation charge plus a $114M nine-month charge for third-party payor opioid claims. (3) The Medical-Surgical separation: the unit is reportedly to operate as Wellverse from January 2027 with an approximately 13% minority partner, which will make FY2027 segment comparisons non-comparable. FY2026 GAAP net income of $4,762M and diluted EPS of $38.38 include the Norway gain; management's adjusted EPS framework and its $44.20–$45.00 FY2027 guidance exclude items of this kind.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the September 2021 monthly close of $199.38 to the September 4, 2026 close of $907.99, MCK returned +355.4% in price alone — a compound annual rate of roughly 35.4%, before a small dividend. For a "boring" 1.5%-margin logistics business with a beta of 0.31, that is an extraordinary outcome, and it happened with far less drama than most of the market: the largest peak-to-trough monthly-close drawdown in the entire window was −24.8%, from the February 2026 close of $987.37 to the May 2026 close of $742.44. Compare that with the violence in high-beta growth names over the same period. The return came from three stacked sources — operating profit growth, a shrinking share count, and a multiple that expanded as the market re-rated distributors from "opioid-litigation defendants with 1% margins" to "specialty healthcare infrastructure with 31% ROIC."

Relative performance. A verified S&P 500 and NASDAQ-100 total-return series over the identical 61-month window was not obtained from the sources fetched, so no precise relative figure is stated. What can be said from verified data on this page: a +355% five-year price return with a 0.31 beta is, on its face, a very large amount of return per unit of market risk — the S&P 500 has not returned anything close to 35% annualised over five years, and MCK achieved its result while moving less than a third as much as the index on any given day. Against its own sector the comparison is cleaner and equally favourable: over the last twelve months MCK is +31.43%, while Cencora's market cap grew 10.4% and Cardinal Health's grew 59.9% — so McKesson has been the middle performer of the triopoly on a one-year view, with Cardinal Health's larger move coming off a far smaller base and a much higher resulting P/E (34.19 versus MCK's 24.22).

08 Summary & Key Risks

The whole argument, in the right units. Start where most coverage starts and immediately go wrong: $410.98B of revenue, 1.57% operating margin. Stop. That ratio is a description of the business model, not a measure of its quality, and it has been roughly 1.5% for five straight fiscal years (1.57%, 1.56%, 1.48%, 1.38%, 1.56% from FY2022 to FY2026). It carries no information. Now do it properly. Operating profit dollars: $4,147M → $4,315M → $4,574M → $4,939M → $6,288M across FY2022–FY2026. That is +51.6% over four years, and +27.3% in FY2026 alone against 12.4% revenue growth. And the capital required to produce it barely moved, which is why ROIC is 31.75% and why the accounting equity is negative — the business consumes so little capital that it has been able to return more than it retains for years. A 1.5% margin earned on someone else's working capital is a far better business than a 20% margin earned on a factory.

Where the profit growth comes from — and it is not where the revenue is. In FY2026, North American Pharmaceutical was 83.7% of revenue and grew operating profit 10%. Oncology & Multispecialty was 12.0% of revenue and grew operating profit 53%. RxTS was 1.4% of revenue and grew operating profit 17% at a 18.97% margin. Roughly half of the company's operating profit growth came from segments representing about one-seventh of its sales. The specialty and oncology platform — specialty distribution plus the US Oncology Network's ~3,400 providers plus the Core Ventures services business — is the actual growth engine, and it works because a community oncology practice that joins the network becomes a distribution customer, a services customer and a data source simultaneously. In fiscal Q1 2027 that segment grew revenue 33% and operating profit 41%; organically, excluding Core Ventures, roughly 24% and 15%. The organic number is the one to underwrite. The gap between the two is the acquisition, and acquisitions have to keep coming for the reported number to hold.

The GLP-1 mirage. $15B of GLP-1 distribution revenue in a single quarter, growing 24%, representing about 40% of the company's entire revenue increase — and contributing, on any reasonable arithmetic, a rounding error to operating profit. At the North American Pharmaceutical segment's 1.03% average operating margin, the incremental $3B of GLP-1 revenue would generate roughly $31M, against a $143M increase in segment operating profit; and branded fee-for-service distribution earns below that average. The practical consequences: (i) revenue growth is a corrupted signal for this company right now, (ii) consolidated gross and operating margin percentages will keep drifting down as GLP-1 mix rises even if every underlying business improves, and (iii) the quarter GLP-1 volumes dip sequentially — as they did in fiscal Q4 2026 — the company will "miss" on revenue while beating on earnings, which is exactly what happened in May 2026 and cost the stock 25%. That mispricing is, arguably, the opportunity.

The two cash claims on the story. First, opioid settlement payments — a multi-year, contractually fixed cash outflow arising from historical controlled-substance distribution, which sits below adjusted operating profit and consumes free cash flow that would otherwise fund buybacks. McKesson recorded a $108M claims-and-litigation charge in FY2026 and a $114M nine-month charge relating to third-party payor opioid claims. The annual payment amount and remaining liability under the national settlement could not be verified from the sources fetched and are not stated here rather than estimated — an investor should size this from the Commitments and Contingent Liabilities note of the FY2026 Form 10-K before underwriting the free cash flow. Second, the buyback: $4.8B in FY2026, $2.5B in fiscal Q1 2027 alone, share count down 12.7% over three years, and about 37% of the last three years' EPS growth attributable to the shrinking denominator. These two items are linked — they compete for the same free cash flow — and the honest version of the story is that McKesson is using a 31% ROIC business's cash to simultaneously pay for its past and shrink its share count.

Valuation. At $907.99 the stock trades at 24.2× trailing GAAP EPS of $37.48, 19.5× the reported forward figure, and 20.3× the $44.65 FY2027 consensus (20.2–20.5× on management's own $44.20–$45.00 guidance). Enterprise value of $112.49B is 15.9× EV/EBITDA and roughly 17.5× the $6.42B of trailing free cash flow. Against the peer set it is the cheapest of the three distributors on trailing earnings (24.22 versus Cencora's 24.59 and Cardinal Health's 34.19) while carrying the highest net margin (1.12% versus 0.79% and 0.67%) and, on FY2026 evidence, the fastest operating profit growth. The PEG of 1.53 is the honest counterweight: this is no longer a discarded stock trading at 10× earnings, and a market that has already re-rated it from ~$199 to ~$908 in five years has priced in a good deal of the oncology story. The question is not whether the business is good — the ROIC settles that — but whether 20× forward earnings for 13–16% EPS growth, several points of which are buyback, is an adequate margin of safety.

Bull Case

A near-recession-proof oligopoly earning a 31.75% return on invested capital with a 0.31 beta, growing operating profit 27% on 12% revenue growth, and shrinking its share count 3% a year with $7.7B of authorisation in hand. The growth is coming from the right places: Oncology & Multispecialty compounded operating profit 53% in FY2026 and 41% in Q1 FY2027, and RxTS earns a 19% margin on 1.4% of revenue. Europe is gone, guidance has been raised, the dividend is up 15%, and the stock trades at 20× forward earnings against a 13–16% long-term EPS target — cheaper than both distributor peers on trailing earnings while out-earning them on margin.

Bear Case

Roughly 37% of the last three years' EPS growth came from buying back stock, not from earning more, and that engine competes for cash with fixed multi-year opioid settlement payments. Two-thirds of Q1's oncology profit growth came from an acquisition rather than organically. Medical-Surgical operating profit fell 20% and is being separated rather than repaired. The whole model rests on percentage-of-list branded distribution fees at exactly the moment the Inflation Reduction Act is cutting list prices, and a 1.57% operating margin leaves no absorption capacity for a lost national contract. Add an unquantified August 25 cybersecurity incident to a business that cannot afford a delivery interruption, and a stock that has quadrupled in five years to 20× forward earnings has very little room for error.

Top 3 Risks

01
Customer concentration against a 1.57% operating margin. The mathematics of thin-spread distribution are unforgiving: losing or repricing one large national pharmacy chain contract removes profit that took years of mix improvement to build. There is no cost line to cut that would absorb it, because the cost line is the business.
02
Drug pricing policy compressing the fee base. Branded distribution fees are largely a percentage of manufacturer list price. IRA Medicare negotiation lowers list prices on the highest-volume drugs, and management has already attributed a revenue shortfall in part to manufacturer price declines. This shrinks the numerator of the spread on the drugs with the most volume — a slow, structural, policy-driven headwind rather than a single event.
03
The oncology growth rate normalising toward organic. Reported Oncology & Multispecialty operating profit grew 41% in Q1 FY2027; organically, excluding Core Ventures, it grew about 15%. Management's own long-term target for the segment is 13–16%. If the acquisition pipeline slows, the reported number converges on the organic one — and since this segment is doing the heavy lifting for the whole company's growth, a convergence from 41% to 15% would be felt in the multiple long before it showed up in the EPS.
  Industry Commentary
Percentage-of-List Fees Meet Government Price-Setting
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The entire branded side of pharmaceutical distribution is compensated as a negotiated percentage of the manufacturer's list price. The Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare negotiation programme sets lower prices on the highest-volume drugs in the system — which means the fee base shrinks on exactly the products that generate the most throughput. McKesson explicitly cited "manufacturer price declines" and the IRA's first pricing wave among the causes of its fiscal Q4 2026 revenue shortfall, when revenue of $96.3B missed a $101.6B estimate. This is not a demand problem — volumes are fine — it is a repricing of the unit of compensation, and it applies identically to Cencora and Cardinal Health. The industry's structural answer is to shift mix toward generics (where the spread is a buy-side margin, not a list-price fee) and toward specialty and services, which is precisely what all three are doing.
GLP-1s Are Inflating Every Distributor's Top Line
ONGOING HEADWIND
$15B of GLP-1 distribution revenue in one McKesson quarter, up 24% year over year, is roughly 14% of the company's total revenue arriving at close to zero incremental profit. Across the triopoly — $998B of combined trailing revenue — this drug class is simultaneously the largest source of reported revenue growth and one of the smallest sources of profit growth. The consequences are systematic and predictable: reported gross and operating margin percentages compress mechanically as GLP-1 mix rises, revenue "misses" occur whenever volumes dip sequentially (McKesson's May 2026 25% drawdown began with exactly such a miss, on a quarter where adjusted EPS still beat), and any investor screening this sector on revenue growth or margin trend is reading noise. The correct screen is segment operating profit dollars and ROIC.
The Race to Own Community Oncology
EMERGING TAILWIND
All three distributors have reached the same conclusion: the profit in this industry is migrating from moving pills to owning the relationship with the specialist physician who prescribes them. McKesson's version is the US Oncology Network (~3,400 providers) plus Core Ventures; Cencora and Cardinal Health are building comparable specialty and physician-services platforms, and both have seen the result in earnings — Cencora's net income grew 38.3% and Cardinal Health's 9.8% on trailing twelve months. Bull framing: McKesson has the largest and longest-established network, its Oncology & Multispecialty segment already earns a 2.89% operating margin against 1.04% in wholesale, and it grew segment operating profit 53% in FY2026 — if that mix shift continues, the consolidated margin percentage stops being the relevant metric and the company re-rates toward a healthcare services multiple. Bear framing: three well-capitalised buyers competing for the same finite pool of community oncology practices is a formula for rising acquisition prices, and roughly two-thirds of McKesson's Q1 oncology profit growth was bought rather than grown. A bidding war among the triopoly would transfer value from all three of their shareholders to the practices they are buying.
