01 Company Overview

McKesson is the largest pharmaceutical distributor in North America and, at $418.79B of trailing-twelve-month revenue, one of the largest companies in the world by sales — while ranking only #97 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $122.48B. Fiscal 2026 (year ended March 31, 2026) revenue was $403.43B, up 12.4% from $359.05B in FY2025. It buys drugs from manufacturers, warehouses them, and delivers them to roughly every pharmacy, hospital, oncology practice and physician office in the United States and Canada — plus a technology business that sits between manufacturers, payers and patients, and a medical-surgical products business.

The central thing to understand about this business, before anything else, is that its margin percentage is meaningless as a quality signal. McKesson earned $14.53B of gross profit on $403.43B of revenue in FY2026 — 3.6 cents on the dollar — and $6.29B of operating income, or 1.56 cents on the dollar. A retailer or a software company with those numbers would be a disaster. A distributor with those numbers is doing exactly what it is supposed to do: earning a thin, contractually-negotiated spread on a vast and largely non-discretionary volume of goods it does not own for very long. The company is not paid to have a fat margin; it is paid to move pills. Nobody who understands the model looks at 1.56%. They look at two things instead: the dollar growth of operating profit (which grew 27.3% in FY2026, more than twice the 12.4% revenue growth), and return on invested capital (30.42%), because the whole trick of distribution is doing it with almost no capital tied up. This report is organised around that lens — and around where inside the business the profit growth is actually coming from, which is not where the revenue is.

The core wholesale business: buying branded and generic drugs from manufacturers and delivering them daily to retail pharmacy chains, independents, hospitals and health systems across the US and Canada. Economics work in two distinct layers. On branded drugs the distributor earns a fee that is largely a negotiated percentage of the manufacturer's list price — high revenue, very low margin, and exposed to manufacturer price actions and to the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare price-setting. On generics, McKesson buys through a global sourcing joint venture and captures a genuine buy-side spread — far higher margin on far lower revenue. That mix is why the segment can grow revenue 11% and operating profit 10% in the same year without either number telling you much on its own. In fiscal Q2 2027 (quarter ended September 30, 2026) the segment did $91.4B of revenue, up 6%, and $1.04B of adjusted operating profit, up 22% (the release credits specialty distribution to health systems and strategic accounts) — profit growing almost four times faster than revenue is the signature of mix improving underneath a flat-ish spread. Management's own long-term target for this segment is 5–8% annual operating profit growth; it is running well ahead of that.

The GLP-1 distortion: GLP-1 distribution revenue was $16B in fiscal Q2 2027 alone, up about 26% year over year (CFO Kenny Cheung on the November 4 earnings call) — see the discussion below the segment cards. That is 14.4% of total company revenue ($16B ÷ $110.96B) from one drug class.

This is the reason to own the stock, and McKesson broke it out as its own segment precisely so investors would stop burying it inside the wholesale line. The platform has three parts. First, specialty distribution — the high-cost, cold-chain, limited-distribution oncology and multispecialty drugs that carry structurally better economics than a bottle of generic lisinopril. Second, the US Oncology Network, an affiliated network of community oncology practices — around 3,400 providers — for which McKesson provides practice management, group purchasing, clinical pathways and research infrastructure, and from which it earns service revenue as well as distribution volume. Third, Core Ventures, the business and administrative services organization established by Florida Cancer Specialists, in which McKesson bought a roughly 70% controlling interest for about $2.49B in 2025. The logic: every practice added is simultaneously a distribution customer, a services customer, and a source of real-world clinical data that manufacturers pay for. Segment operating margin is 2.96% — nearly triple North American Pharmaceutical's 1.03%, on drugs whose per-unit prices are enormous. In fiscal Q2 2027 the segment did $14.98B of revenue, up 24%, and $427M of adjusted operating profit, up 8%; CFO Kenny Cheung said on the call that excluding $51M of net gains in the US Oncology Network in the prior-year quarter, profit rose 23%. For the first half the segment earned $832M, up 22% from $684M. Management's long-term target here is 13–16% annual operating profit growth. Two acquisitions completed in early November 2026 now feed this segment, both per the 10-Q: Precision Medicine Group (about $2.25B in cash) and a controlling interest in Spectrum Vision Partners (about $525M), whose results report in the segment from the third quarter of fiscal 2027.

Why it matters: this segment is 12% of revenue but 20% of the four segments' operating profit, and it grew profit 53% in FY2026 against the company's 27%. It is the piece that decides the whole argument.

The software-and-services layer, and the highest-margin thing McKesson owns at 19.45% operating margin. It runs prior-authorisation automation, patient affordability and copay-assistance programmes, adherence tools, and the third-party network plumbing that connects pharmacies, manufacturers and payers. Its economics are the opposite of the distribution business: negligible revenue per transaction, but no inventory, no trucks, and no working capital. The clearest recent example of what this segment does is the CMS Medicare GLP-1 Bridge programme, which McKesson launched support for in July 2026 and on which it told investors in August that 95% of prior-authorisation requests get a determination within 30 minutes of reaching the payer. In fiscal Q2 2027 the segment did $1.52B of revenue (+10%) and $307M of adjusted operating profit (+18%), which the release credits to access solutions. Long-term target: 10–13% operating profit growth. If you want the single line that explains why a 1.5%-margin company earns a 30.42% return on invested capital, it is this one.

Medical supplies, equipment and lab products distributed to physician offices, surgery centres, extended-care facilities and home health — a genuinely different business from drug wholesaling, with an 8.94% operating margin and, at 2.9% of revenue, 15% of the four segments' operating profit. It is also the only segment going backwards: FY2026 revenue and operating profit both grew just 1%, and fiscal Q2 2027 adjusted operating profit fell 10% year over year, to $225M, on 5% revenue growth, to $3.10B, which the release attributes to ambulatory care product mix. The business is being repositioned rather than fixed quietly: McKesson plans to separate the unit, which expects to start operating under the name Wellverse in January 2027; funds managed by affiliates of Apollo have held a stake of roughly 13% since June 1, 2026. Investors should treat FY2027 segment comparisons here as non-comparable and read the separation terms carefully when they are disclosed.

Watch item: funds managed by Apollo bought an approximately 13% interest in the unit for $1.25B of convertible preferred equity, at about a $13B enterprise value, closing June 1, 2026 (10-Q for the quarter ended September 30, 2026; Apollo's April 2026 release for the valuation). McKesson introduced Wellverse as the unit's future name on August 5, 2026 and expects to start using it in January 2027.

The GLP-1 effect, quantified. This deserves its own paragraph because it is the single biggest reason McKesson's income statement is currently misread. In fiscal Q2 2027, GLP-1 distribution revenue was about $16B, up about 26% year over year — an increase of about $3B (CFO Kenny Cheung on the November 4 call). Total company revenue in the quarter was $110.96B, up $7.81B. So one drug class accounted for about 40% of the entire company's revenue growth, and 14.4% of its total revenue. Now the profit side. North American Pharmaceutical's adjusted operating margin is 1.14% in that quarter ($1,040M on $91.36B). If that extra $3B of GLP-1 revenue had carried the segment's average margin, it would have produced about $34M of operating profit — against a segment adjusted operating profit increase of $189M. And branded, high-list-price drugs of this type earn less than the segment average, not the average, because the distributor is paid a small negotiated fee rather than keeping a spread on what it buys. The honest reading: GLP-1s inflate the top line enormously and contribute very little to profit. Anyone forecasting McKesson from revenue growth is forecasting the wrong thing — and anyone alarmed when the margin percentage falls as GLP-1 volumes surge is watching a change in what it sells, not a weaker business. The $34M figure above is our arithmetic, an upper-bound illustration, not a number the company reports.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
TRIOPOLY STRUCTURE SCALE COST ADVANTAGE GENERIC SOURCING JV NEGATIVE WORKING CAPITAL DEA-REGULATED BARRIER ONCOLOGY NETWORK LOCK-IN RxTS SWITCHING COSTS

The moat is structural, not clever. Three companies — McKesson, Cencora and Cardinal Health — distribute roughly the entire US pharmaceutical supply. Between them they carry about $1,014B of trailing revenue ($418.79B + $337.40B + $257.75B). A new entrant would need national warehousing, next-morning delivery to every pharmacy in the country, DEA controlled-substance licensing in every state, and enough purchasing volume to negotiate a spread — and would then be competing for a business that earns one and a half cents on the dollar. That is an unattractive proposition even for a very well-capitalised attacker, which is why the structure has been stable for decades.

The financial expression of the moat is the balance sheet, and it is worth pausing on because it looks alarming until you understand it. McKesson has negative stockholders' equity of −$3.95B (at September 30, 2026) and a current ratio of 0.90. It gets paid by pharmacies faster than it pays manufacturers, so it funds its inventory with its suppliers' money — negative working capital. Combine that with years of buying back stock at prices above book value and you get a company whose accounting equity is below zero while it generates $7.11B of free cash flow a year (trailing twelve months, stockanalysis.com). This is why the ROIC of 30.42% is the number that matters and the ROE is not just unhelpful but mathematically meaningless — a positive profit divided by negative equity produces a negative ratio that means nothing. Any screen or comparison that shows McKesson with a negative or absurd ROE should be discarded on sight.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer concentration. A distributor with a 1.5% operating margin has essentially no cushion against the loss or repricing of a large national pharmacy chain contract. Contract renewals with the biggest customers are the highest-stakes recurring events in the business — the November 4 release says the CVS Health distribution agreement was extended through June 2032 — and the concentration cuts both ways — a chain that consolidates or shrinks takes volume with it.
▸
Opioid litigation and the compliance overhang. McKesson remains a defendant in and a payer under opioid-related settlements arising from its historical controlled-substance distribution. It recorded a $108M claims-and-litigation charge in FY2025 for opioid-related claims; in FY2026 that line was a $3M net credit (Form 10-K). The settlements are a multi-year, contractually fixed cash outflow that reduces free cash flow available for buybacks without ever appearing in adjusted operating profit, and they remain a real drag on the capital-return story rather than a closed chapter.
▸
Drug pricing policy. Branded distribution fees are largely a percentage of manufacturer list price. The Inflation Reduction Act lets Medicare set lower prices on selected high-volume drugs. McKesson says list-price cuts land on revenue and have not had a meaningful effect on segment operating profit (CFO Kenny Cheung, November 4 call), with more than 95% of its branded drugs fee-for-service. January 2026 list-price decreases cut North American Pharmaceutical revenue growth by about 4% in fiscal Q2 2027. Management said on the August 5 call that the IRA change for Part D drugs under discussion does not take effect until January 2028 and that much about it is unknown; the 10-K says the IRA's ongoing implementation may significantly affect the pharmaceutical value chain.
▸
The cybersecurity incident of August 25, 2026. McKesson's 8-K of August 28, 2026 says it discovered a cybersecurity incident affecting its information systems on August 25, that the investigation was in its early stages and that it had not determined the incident to be material. On September 21 the company said on its cybersecurity update page that its investigation had confirmed the unauthorized access and data theft were limited to certain third-party applications, affecting a subset of customers in Oncology & Multispecialty, with the data primarily business contact and order data in the Medical-Surgical business. It raised its full-year guidance on November 4. For a company whose entire function is the uninterrupted daily movement of medicine, an IT disruption is an operational risk of a different character than it is for most businesses.

The buyback — quantify it before you credit the EPS growth. McKesson repurchased $4.8B of stock in FY2026 and a further $3.4B in the first half of fiscal 2027 (release) — $2.5B of it in Q1, including a $2.25B accelerated share repurchase (the bank delivers the shares up front) at an initial average price of about $755 per share. Average diluted shares were 116.7M in fiscal Q2 2027 against 124.4M a year earlier (−6.2%), and management plans about $5B of repurchases for the full fiscal year (CFO Kenny Cheung on the November 4 call). Diluted share count fell from 128.1M in FY2025 to 124.1M in FY2026 (−3.1%). Over a longer window the effect is much larger: FY2023 diluted shares of about 142.2M have become 124.1M — a 12.7% reduction in three years. Over that same span net income grew from $3,560M to $4,762M, +33.8%, while diluted EPS grew from $25.03 to $38.38, +53.3%. Roughly 19.5 points of that 53.3-point EPS gain — about 37% of it — came from owning a smaller denominator, not from earning more money. That is not a criticism; retiring stock at a 30.42% ROIC business is a rational use of cash, and it is precisely what a negative-equity balance sheet is a symptom of. But an investor counting on the company's 13–16% long-term EPS growth target should be clear that several points of it come from shrinking the share count with free cash flow, and that it requires the buyback to continue. As of March 31, 2026 there was $2.7B remaining on the authorisation; the board added $5.0B in April 2026, bringing the total to $7.7B before the first half's buying.

Notable One-Off Items. Four matter. (1) The European exit: the Norwegian retail and distribution sale to NorgesGruppen closed January 30, 2026 and generated approximately $480M of pre-tax gain — a one-time item inflating FY2026 GAAP results, and the completion of a multi-year withdrawal from Europe that removes an entire reporting segment from year-over-year comparisons. (2) Opioid-related charges: a $108M claims-and-litigation charge in FY2025, against a $3M net credit on that line in FY2026. (3) The Medical-Surgical separation: the unit is to operate as Wellverse from January 2027, with Apollo funds holding an approximately 13% interest, which will make FY2027 segment comparisons non-comparable. (4) The gap between GAAP and adjusted EPS: for the first six months of fiscal 2027, GAAP diluted EPS was $15.53 against $15.16 (+2.4%) while adjusted EPS was $21.59 against $18.12 (+19.2%). Adjusted EPS is the measure management guides to ($45.60–$46.40 for FY2027) and it excludes items of this kind. FY2026 GAAP net income of $4,762M and diluted EPS of $38.38 include the Norway gain. Two deals are not yet in the numbers: the October 6 agreement to take about 49% of Option Care Health for about $1.4B (McKesson will record its share of Option Care's profit on one line below operating profit, so it stays outside segment margins; closing expected in the first half of calendar 2027), which management says the guidance excludes, and Precision Medicine Group, completed in early November.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 monthly close of $216.76 to the November 6, 2026 close of $1,060.84, MCK returned +389.4% in price alone — a compound annual rate of roughly 38% over 4.93 years, before a small dividend. For a "boring" 1.5%-margin logistics business with a beta of 0.28, that is an extraordinary outcome, and it happened with far less drama than most of the market: the largest peak-to-trough monthly-close drawdown in the entire window was −24.8%, from the February 2026 close of $987.37 to the May 2026 close of $742.44. The stock has since closed above that February level (October 30: $994.58; November 6: $1,060.84), 42.9% above the May close. The return came from three stacked sources: operating profit growth, a shrinking share count, and a higher price paid for each dollar of earnings.

Relative performance. A +389% five-year price return with a 0.28 beta is, on its face, a very large amount of return per unit of market risk. Over the last twelve months (closes of November 6, 2025 to November 6, 2026, price only) MCK returned +23.55%, against +37.2% for Cardinal Health, −7.4% for Cencora and +9.7% for SPY, the S&P 500 index fund (Yahoo Finance closes) — so McKesson has been the middle performer of the three distributors on a one-year view, with a trailing P/E of 27.4 against Cardinal Health's 35.2 and Cencora's 18.6.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The whole argument, in the right units. Start where most coverage starts and immediately go wrong: $418.79B of trailing revenue, 1.59% operating margin. Stop. That ratio is a description of the business model, not a measure of its quality, and it has been roughly 1.5% for five straight fiscal years (1.57%, 1.56%, 1.48%, 1.38%, 1.56% from FY2022 to FY2026). It carries no information. Now do it properly. Operating profit dollars (stockanalysis.com; McKesson's own GAAP figure for FY2026 is $6,212M): $4,147M → $4,315M → $4,574M → $4,939M → $6,288M across FY2022–FY2026. That is +51.6% over four years, and +27.3% in FY2026 alone against 12.4% revenue growth. And the capital required to produce it barely moved, which is why ROIC is 30.42% and why the accounting equity is negative — the business consumes so little capital that it has been able to return more than it retains for years. A 1.5% margin earned on someone else's working capital is a far better business than a 20% margin earned on a factory.

Where the profit growth comes from — and it is not where the revenue is. In FY2026, North American Pharmaceutical was 83.7% of revenue and grew operating profit 10%. Oncology & Multispecialty was 12.0% of revenue and grew operating profit 53%. RxTS was 1.4% of revenue and grew operating profit 17% at a 19.45% margin. About two-thirds of the four segments' FY2026 adjusted operating profit growth ($667M of $981M) came from these two segments, which represent about one-seventh of sales. The specialty and oncology platform — specialty distribution plus the US Oncology Network's approximately 3,400 providers plus the Core Ventures services business — is the actual growth engine, and it works because a community oncology practice that joins the network becomes a distribution customer, a services customer and a data source simultaneously. In fiscal Q2 2027 that segment grew revenue 24% and adjusted operating profit 8% (23% excluding $51M of prior-year net gains in the US Oncology Network, per the CFO on the call); for the first half, profit rose 22%. Revenue growth there leans on acquisitions, and they have to keep coming for the reported number to hold.

The GLP-1 mirage. About $16B of GLP-1 distribution revenue in a single quarter, growing about 26%, representing about 40% of the company's entire revenue increase — and contributing, on any reasonable arithmetic, a rounding error to operating profit. At the North American Pharmaceutical segment's 1.14% adjusted operating margin, the extra $3B of GLP-1 revenue would generate roughly $34M, against a $189M increase in segment adjusted operating profit; and branded fee-for-service distribution earns below that average. The practical consequences: (i) revenue growth is a corrupted signal for this company right now, (ii) consolidated gross and operating margin percentages will keep drifting down as GLP-1 mix rises even if every underlying business improves, and (iii) the quarter GLP-1 volumes dip sequentially — as they did in fiscal Q4 2026 — the company will "miss" on revenue while beating on earnings, which is what happened with the May 7, 2026 release (the shares fell 2.5% the next session; most of the 24.8% slide from the February to the May month-end close had already happened by then). That gap between revenue and profit is, arguably, the opportunity.

The two cash claims on the story. First, opioid settlement payments — a multi-year, contractually fixed cash outflow arising from historical controlled-substance distribution, which sits below adjusted operating profit and consumes free cash flow that would otherwise fund buybacks. McKesson recorded a $108M claims-and-litigation charge for opioid-related claims in FY2025 and a $3M net credit on that line in FY2026. Second, the buyback: $4.8B in FY2026, $3.4B in the first half of fiscal 2027, average diluted shares 6.2% lower in Q2 than a year earlier, share count down 12.7% over the three fiscal years to FY2026, and about 37% of the last three fiscal years' EPS growth attributable to a smaller share count. These two items are linked — they compete for the same free cash flow — and the honest version of the story is that McKesson is using a 30% ROIC business's cash to simultaneously pay for its past and shrink its share count.

Valuation. At $1,060.84 the stock trades at 27.4× trailing GAAP EPS of $38.75, 23.1× the $46.00 midpoint of management's raised FY2027 adjusted EPS guidance ($45.60–$46.40, or 22.9–23.3×) and 21.0× the $50.63 FY2028 non-GAAP consensus. Enterprise value — market value plus $11.2B of total debt including leases, less $6.5B of cash, per stockanalysis.com — is about $127B: 17.5× EV/EBITDA and roughly 17.9× the $7.11B of trailing free cash flow. Against the peer set it sits between Cencora (18.6×) and Cardinal Health (35.2×) on trailing earnings while carrying the highest net margin (1.12% versus 1.03% and 0.71%). The PEG of 1.66 is the honest counterweight: this is no longer an overlooked stock, and a stock that has gone from about $217 to about $1,061 in five years has priced in a good deal of the oncology story. The question is not whether the business is good — the ROIC settles that — but whether 23× this year's guided earnings for 13–16% long-term EPS growth (guidance for FY2027 itself implies 17–19%), several points of which are buyback, is an adequate margin of safety.

Bull Case

A near-recession-proof oligopoly earning a 30.42% return on invested capital with a 0.28 beta, growing operating profit 27% on 12% revenue growth in FY2026, and shrinking its share count with about $5B of repurchases planned for FY2027. The growth is coming from the right places: Oncology & Multispecialty grew operating profit 53% in FY2026 and 22% in the first half of FY2027, and RxTS earns a 19.45% margin on 1.4% of revenue. Europe is gone, guidance has been raised again (to $45.60–$46.40 adjusted EPS), the quarterly dividend is $0.94 against $0.82 a year ago, and the stock trades at 23× guided earnings against a 13–16% long-term EPS target — with a higher net margin than either distributor peer.

Bear Case

Roughly 37% of the three fiscal years' EPS growth to FY2026 came from buying back stock, not from earning more, and that engine competes for cash with fixed multi-year opioid settlement payments. Oncology & Multispecialty profit rose only 8% in Q2 on a reported basis against a 13–16% long-term target. Medical-Surgical adjusted operating profit fell 10% and is being separated rather than repaired. Branded list prices are falling and Medicare price-setting is still rolling out, so distribution fees could come under pressure at renewal even though management says segment profit has not been meaningfully hit so far, and a 1.6% operating margin leaves no cushion for a lost national contract. Add the August 25 cybersecurity incident to a business that cannot afford a delivery interruption, and a stock that has nearly quintupled in five years to 23× guided earnings has very little room for error.

This segment is doing the heavy lifting for the whole company's growth, so a slowdown toward the target would show in the share price before it showed in EPS.
  Industry Commentary
Falling List Prices Meet Government Price-Setting
KEY RISK
Branded pharmaceutical distribution is paid largely as a negotiated fee, and list prices are moving. Manufacturer price declines on certain products cut McKesson's fiscal Q4 2026 revenue growth by about 3 percentage points, in a quarter when revenue of $96.3B missed the Zacks consensus by 5.5%; January 2026 list-price decreases cut North American Pharmaceutical revenue growth by about 4% in fiscal Q2 2027. McKesson says these cuts land on revenue and have not had a meaningful effect on segment operating profit, with more than 95% of its branded drugs fee-for-service. Medicare price-setting under the Inflation Reduction Act is the next test: McKesson's 10-K says its ongoing implementation may significantly affect the pharmaceutical value chain. Cencora and Cardinal Health operate under the same rules. The industry's answer is to sell more generics (where the distributor keeps a spread on what it buys, not a list-price fee) and more specialty and services, which is what all three are doing.
GLP-1s Are Inflating Every Distributor's Top Line
ONGOING HEADWIND
About $16B of GLP-1 distribution revenue in one McKesson quarter, up about 26%, is roughly 14% of the company's total revenue arriving at close to zero incremental profit. Across the three distributors — $1,014B of combined trailing revenue — this drug class is simultaneously the largest source of reported revenue growth and one of the smallest sources of profit growth. The consequences are systematic and predictable: reported gross and operating margin percentages compress mechanically as GLP-1 mix rises, revenue "misses" occur whenever volumes dip sequentially (McKesson's May 2026 release was exactly such a miss: revenue fell 5.5% short of the Zacks consensus while adjusted EPS beat it), and any investor screening this sector on revenue growth or margin trend is reading noise. The correct screen is segment operating profit dollars and ROIC.
The Race to Own Community Oncology
EMERGING TAILWIND
All three distributors have reached the same conclusion: the profit in this industry is migrating from moving pills to owning the relationship with the specialist physician who prescribes them. McKesson's version is the US Oncology Network (about 3,400 providers) plus Core Ventures; Cencora and Cardinal Health are building comparable specialty and physician-services platforms, and their trailing-twelve-month net income to September 30, 2026 was $3.48B at Cencora (against $1.55B a year earlier) and $1.82B at Cardinal Health (against $1.60B). Bull framing: McKesson's 10-K calls its network one of the nation's largest, its Oncology & Multispecialty segment already earns a 2.96% operating margin against 1.03% in wholesale, and it grew segment operating profit 53% in FY2026 — if that mix shift continues, the consolidated margin percentage stops being the relevant metric and investors may value the company more like a healthcare services business. Bear framing: three well-capitalised buyers competing for the same finite pool of community oncology practices is a formula for rising acquisition prices, and McKesson's Q2 oncology adjusted operating profit rose 8% (23% excluding prior-year net gains) against a 13–16% long-term target. A bidding war among the triopoly would transfer value from all three of their shareholders to the practices they are buying.
