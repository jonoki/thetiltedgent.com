01 Company Overview
Medtronic plc is the world's largest pure-play medical device manufacturer by revenue, selling implantable and interventional therapies across four portfolios — Cardiovascular, Neuroscience, Medical Surgical and Diabetes — into roughly 150 countries. Fiscal 2026 (ended April 24, 2026) revenue was $36.364 billion, up 8.4% reported and 5.8% organic, which management described as the strongest annual top-line growth in a decade; GAAP diluted EPS was $3.73 against non-GAAP EPS of $5.53. The company ranks #196 globally by market capitalization and #4 among medical device companies ($117.9B), behind Abbott, Intuitive Surgical and Stryker — despite generating more device revenue than any of them, a valuation gap that sits at the center of the value-versus-value-trap debate around this stock.
The investment story in 2026 is about reversal of a decade of underperformance. Two engines are doing the work: a cardiac ablation franchise built on pulsed field ablation (PFA) that grew 78% globally and 124% in the U.S. in FY2026, and the FDA clearance of the Hugo robotic-assisted surgery system in December 2025. Sitting behind both is Elliott Investment Management, which surfaced as one of Medtronic's largest shareholders in August 2025 and has already driven board expansion and a commitment to separate the Diabetes business.

Medtronic's moat is a genuine one, and it is why the stock rarely trades like a broken business even when growth disappoints. Implantable cardiac and neurological devices require pre-market approval trials that run years and cost hundreds of millions; once a physician is trained on a mapping platform, a stimulator programmer or a navigation system, switching means retraining, revalidating protocols and re-papering hospital contracts. The installed base economics are the core asset — Affera consoles, StealthStation navigation towers, Hugo robots and MiniMed pumps all pull recurring high-margin consumables, which is how the company sustains a gross margin of 65.3% across a hardware-heavy portfolio. Scale compounds this: at $36.4B of revenue, Medtronic is the largest device supplier to hospital group purchasing organizations, which lets it bundle low-growth staples with new technology in a way sub-scale rivals cannot. And the PFA franchise demonstrates the moat still functions offensively — Medtronic took eight U.S. share points in a single year in cardiac ablation, a market where it competes against Boston Scientific and J&J, because Affera pairs mapping and ablation in one catheter and the installed base gave it distribution on day one.
Key Vulnerabilities

Structurally sub-par returns on capital. ROIC of 8.25% and ROE of 9.84% are low for a franchise with 65% gross margins. That gap is the fingerprint of a goodwill-heavy balance sheet built through decades of acquisitions (Covidien, HeartWare, Mazor, Affera) where the purchase price was not fully earned back. With ROIC sitting at or near most estimates of the cost of capital, incremental M&A destroys value unless execution improves — which is precisely the activist thesis.

Self-cannibalization in the growth engine. PFA's 78% growth came alongside a 40% decline in cryoablation. Cardiovascular's organic growth (+9.3%) is 270bps below reported (+12.0%), and consolidated organic (+5.8%) is 260bps below reported (+8.4%). The reported number that anchors the "best growth in ten years" headline is flattered by an $819M favorable currency benefit. Strip currency and the underlying business is growing high-single-digits in one portfolio and low-single-digits in two others.

Robotics is a fight against an entrenched incumbent. Hugo's December 2025 FDA clearance covers urology only; general surgery and gynecology are filed but not cleared. Intuitive Surgical has a twenty-year lead, $11.0B of TTM revenue growing 20.7%, and thousands of installed da Vinci systems with trained surgeons. Every quarter Hugo spends in limited-indication rollout is a quarter Medical Surgical's $6.8B stapling and energy franchise keeps bleeding to robotic procedures Medtronic isn't in.

Currency, tariffs and geographic exposure. An Irish-domiciled company with roughly half its revenue outside the U.S. carries meaningful translation risk in both directions — the $819M FY2026 tailwind becomes a headwind on any dollar strengthening. Tariff regimes on medical devices and cross-border component sourcing add an unquantified cost line that management has flagged in guidance but not isolated in the disclosures reviewed here.

Notable One-Off Items

Currency was the largest single distortion in FY2026. Foreign exchange contributed a $819 million favorable benefit to full-year net sales. That is roughly 2.4 points of the 8.4% reported growth — the difference between reported (+8.4%) and organic (+5.8%). Medtronic's Irish domicile and roughly half-international revenue base make this a recurring, two-sided item; anyone modelling FY2027 off the reported FY2026 base is starting from an inflated number.

Restructuring charges of $249 million ran through FY2026, covering employee termination benefits, facility costs and contract terminations. This is a persistent line at Medtronic rather than a true one-off — it has appeared in some form for several consecutive years, which argues for treating it as an ongoing cost of the portfolio-reshaping program rather than excluding it.

Diabetes separation costs. Medtronic is incurring transaction, carve-out and stand-up costs for the MiniMed separation. The specific dollar amount was not disclosed in the FY2026 Q4 materials reviewed for this page — verify against the 10-K and subsequent 10-Qs before modelling. These costs are excluded from non-GAAP EPS.

The GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is $1.80 per share — $3.73 GAAP versus $5.53 non-GAAP for FY2026, roughly $2.3 billion pre-tax. The bulk is amortization of acquisition-related intangibles (a real economic cost of the M&A that produced the low ROIC), with restructuring, separation costs, litigation and certain tax items making up the balance. Investors should be clear which number they are valuing: 24.3x on GAAP is a market multiple, 16.6x on FY26 non-GAAP is not.

Product actions. A catheter system recall was reported in headline coverage in August 2026. Scope, financial impact and affected product lines were not verified for this page — flagged for follow-up rather than quantified.

RSI measures how much of recent price movement has been upward versus downward. Above 70 means the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 means it has been beaten down and may be due a bounce. It says nothing about whether the business is good.

The five-year record is the bear case, written down. On dividend-adjusted closes, MDT went from $107.70 (Sep 2021) to $92.09 (Aug 2026) — a -14.5% total return over five years, against an S&P 500 that compounded at a double-digit annual rate over the same window (VOO's since-inception average is 14.93% annually and it returned 20.65% in the past year alone). An investor who bought Medtronic at the start of this window and reinvested every one of those "50 consecutive years of dividend growth" payments still has less money than they started with, before inflation. That is not a drawdown; it is a lost half-decade, and it is precisely the record that brought Elliott to the register.

The shape of the loss matters more than the size. The stock did not fall in a straight line — it de-rated through 2022 on supply chain and FX, bottomed at $64.48 in October 2023 as the market decided GLP-1 obesity drugs would structurally reduce cardiac and orthopedic procedure volumes, then spent two years rebuilding to a $102.79 high in November 2025 on PFA momentum and activist optimism. It then gave almost all of it back, collapsing 29% to $73.15 by May 2026 during the mid-2026 medtech multiple compression that also drove TD Cowen and Leerink to slash targets. The last three months have been a sharp +25.9% recovery off that low — which means the stock now trades near the middle of a five-year range it has repeatedly failed to escape.

Relative performance is unambiguously poor. Over the trailing twelve months MDT's price is -1.00% while the S&P 500 returned +20.65% — roughly 2,150 basis points of underperformance in a single year. Against medtech peers the gap is structural rather than cyclical: Boston Scientific compounded revenue at 13.5% and Intuitive at 20.7% while Medtronic managed 8.4% reported / 5.8% organic, and the market has priced that difference into the multiples (MDT 24.3x versus ISRG 44.9x and SYK 34.3x). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 24.3x trailing / 15.2x forward earnings and collecting a 3.13% yield backed by 50 consecutive years of increases, for the largest medical device business in the world at the exact moment its growth inflects. FY2026 delivered the best revenue growth in a decade (+8.4% reported, +5.8% organic) with every portfolio growing, driven by a pulsed field ablation franchise that grew 78% globally and took eight U.S. share points in a >$12B market compounding in the mid-20s. Hugo's FDA clearance opens the largest untapped pool in surgical devices, and Elliott's presence has already produced board change, a committed Diabetes separation and a management team publicly promising more investment behind growth. If FY2027 guidance of 6.75%–7.25% organic growth is met, the multiple gap to Stryker (34.3x) and Abbott (36.5x) has no defensible basis.

Bear Case

Medtronic has produced a -14.5% total return over five years and -1.00% over the last twelve months against a +20.65% S&P 500. ROIC of 8.25% and ROE of 9.84% from a 65%-gross-margin business are the arithmetic proof that decades of acquisitions were overpaid for, and nothing in the current plan changes the installed capital base. The headline growth is flattered by $819M of currency benefit; organic growth of 5.8% is still below the medtech peer median, and two of four portfolios grew 3.1% and 2.9% organically. A 2.88 PEG says you are not actually buying growth cheaply. Meanwhile the company is spinning off its fastest-growing reported segment, Hugo faces a twenty-year-entrenched incumbent, and Abbott's Volt is about to become the fourth entrant in PFA. Ten of 29 analysts sit on Hold for a reason.

Top 3 Risks
01
PFA share erosion as the market's fourth entrant arrives. Medtronic's ~56% share of U.S. PFA procedures is the load-bearing assumption in the entire growth story, and it was won in a two-competitor market. Abbott's Volt targets U.S. approval in 2H 2026, joining Boston Scientific's Farapulse (~33%) and J&J's Varipulse (~10%). Share loss or price compression in cardiac ablation would immediately expose the 3.1% and 2.9% organic growth in Neuroscience and Medical Surgical.
02
The Diabetes separation destroys more than it creates. Structure is still undecided — a split-off retires shares and is EPS-accretive, a straight spin is not. Removing a $3.1B business growing 7.9% organically mechanically lowers RemainCo's growth rate below the FY2027 guidance range. If the transaction slips, prices poorly, or the valuation lands materially below the ~$7.9B third-party estimate, the central pillar of the activist-driven re-rating comes out.
03
The return-on-capital problem is structural, not cyclical. An 8.25% ROIC at or near the cost of capital means Medtronic has historically not created value with the $29.1B of debt and decades of acquisitions on its balance sheet. Restructuring charges have recurred for years ($249M in FY2026) and the $1.80/share GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is dominated by amortization of intangibles from that same M&A. Unless ROIC moves meaningfully above 10%, the "cheap multiple" is a fair price for a business that converts revenue growth into shareholder returns poorly — the definition of a value trap.
  Industry Commentary
Pulsed Field Ablation Is a Category Reset, Not a Product Cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
PFA uses high-voltage electrical pulses instead of heat (radiofrequency) or cold (cryo) to ablate cardiac tissue, sparing the esophagus and phrenic nerve and cutting procedure times materially. Adoption has been violent by medtech standards: PFA went from 39% of atrial fibrillation procedures in 2024 to ~49% in 2025, while radiofrequency fell 40%→33% and cryo 21%→18% (Citi survey of 72 physicians). The addressable electrophysiology ablation market exceeds $12B and is growing in the mid-20% range. Medtronic is the share leader at ~56% of PFA procedures (~45% Affera, ~11% PulseSelect), with PFA already 75% of its cardiac ablation revenue. The catch is that this is a whole-category replacement: Medtronic's own cryoablation business fell 40% in the same period. Share leadership in the new technology is not optional — it is the only thing offsetting the destruction of the old one.
Procedure Volumes, GLP-1s and the Medtech Multiple
ONGOING HEADWIND
Medtech has spent three years being repriced on a demand question rather than an earnings one. The October 2023 low of $64.48 — Medtronic's five-year trough — coincided with the market's conviction that GLP-1 obesity drugs would structurally shrink the population needing cardiac, bariatric, sleep-apnea and orthopedic intervention. Procedure volumes have since held up, but the multiple never fully recovered, and the mid-2026 drawdown (MDT -27.7% from January to May 2026) was again a sector de-rating rather than a company event — Leerink and TD Cowen both cut targets by 11–16% while explicitly keeping constructive ratings and citing "broader medical device valuation adjustments." The global device market is still growing 5.5%–6.9% annually to ~$623B in 2026. The industry's fundamentals are fine; its multiple is the variable.
Portfolio Simplification Under Activist Pressure — Bull vs Bear
STRUCTURAL SHIFT
Conglomerate medtech is being taken apart across the industry: Medtronic is separating Diabetes (~$7.9B estimated value), J&J is exploring a separation of its orthopedics franchise (reported at over $20B), and Elliott and peers are pressing the argument that scale in devices no longer earns a premium.

Bull framing: a smaller, faster Medtronic focused on Cardiovascular and Neuroscience, with capital redeployed from a low-margin consumer-hardware business into PFA and robotics, is exactly the fix for an 8.25% ROIC. Elliott has already extracted two board seats, two new committees and an explicit management commitment to invest more behind growth drivers — and R&D spending rose nearly 8% in the quarter Martha made that commitment.

Bear framing: activist-driven separations are financial engineering that improve optics faster than they improve returns. Medtronic is divesting the segment with its fastest reported growth (12.9%) and keeping two portfolios growing 3.1% and 2.9% organically. If RemainCo's growth rate falls after the separation and ROIC stays below 10%, the company will have paid transaction costs to become a smaller version of the same problem — and the stock will still be the one that lost 14.5% over five years.
