01 Company Overview
Medtronic plc is the world's largest pure-play medical device manufacturer by revenue, selling implantable and interventional therapies across four portfolios — Cardiovascular, Neuroscience, Medical Surgical and Diabetes — into roughly 150 countries. Fiscal 2026 (ended April 24, 2026) revenue was $36.364 billion, up 8.4% reported and 5.8% organic, which management described as the strongest annual top-line growth in a decade; GAAP diluted EPS was $3.73 against non-GAAP EPS of $5.53. Fiscal 2027 started faster: Q1 revenue of $9.756 billion, up 13.7%, although an extra selling week supplied about 670bp of that. At the September 21 close the company is worth about $117.4B (our calculation). At the previous edition it ranked #196 globally by market capitalization and #4 among medical device companies, behind Abbott, Intuitive Surgical and Stryker — despite generating more device revenue than any of them, a valuation gap that sits at the center of the value-versus-value-trap debate around this stock.
The investment story in 2026 is about reversal of a decade of underperformance. Two engines are doing the work: a cardiac ablation franchise built on pulsed field ablation (PFA) that grew 78% globally and 124% in the U.S. in FY2026, and the FDA clearance of the Hugo robotic-assisted surgery system in December 2025. Sitting behind both is Elliott Investment Management, which surfaced as one of Medtronic's largest shareholders in August 2025 and has already driven board expansion. The Diabetes separation it pushed for is now under way. MiniMed Group listed on Nasdaq (MMED) in March 2026 with Medtronic keeping about 90%, and on September 14, 2026 Medtronic launched the exchange offer meant to split off the rest. The two engines accelerated in Q1 FY2027: Cardiac Ablation Solutions grew 88% and passed $2 billion of trailing-twelve-month revenue, and management expects Hugo to pass 50,000 completed procedures by fiscal year-end.

Medtronic's moat is a genuine one, and it is why the stock rarely trades like a broken business even when growth disappoints. Implantable cardiac and neurological devices require pre-market approval trials that run years and cost hundreds of millions; once a physician is trained on a mapping platform, a stimulator programmer or a navigation system, switching means retraining, revalidating protocols and re-papering hospital contracts. The installed base economics are the core asset — Affera consoles, StealthStation navigation towers, Hugo robots and MiniMed pumps all pull recurring high-margin consumables, which is how the company sustains a gross margin of 65.3% across a hardware-heavy portfolio. Scale compounds this: at $36.4B of revenue, Medtronic is the largest device supplier to hospital group purchasing organizations, which lets it bundle low-growth staples with new technology in a way sub-scale rivals cannot. And the PFA franchise demonstrates the moat still functions offensively — Medtronic took eight U.S. share points in a single year in cardiac ablation, a market where it competes against Boston Scientific and J&J, because Affera pairs mapping and ablation in one catheter and the installed base gave it distribution on day one.
Key Vulnerabilities

Structurally sub-par returns on capital. ROIC of 8.59% and ROE of 10.65% (trailing twelve months, both a little better than at FY2026) are low for a franchise with 65% gross margins. That gap is the fingerprint of a goodwill-heavy balance sheet built through decades of acquisitions (Covidien, HeartWare, Mazor, Affera) where the purchase price was not fully earned back. With ROIC sitting at or near most estimates of the cost of capital, incremental M&A destroys value unless execution improves — which is precisely the activist thesis.

Self-cannibalization in the growth engine. PFA's 78% growth came alongside a 40% decline in cryoablation. Cardiovascular's organic growth (+9.3%) is 270bps below reported (+12.0%), and consolidated organic (+5.8%) is 260bps below reported (+8.4%). The reported number that anchors the "best growth in ten years" headline is flattered by an $819M favorable currency benefit. Strip currency and the underlying business is growing high-single-digits in one portfolio and low-single-digits in two others. Q1 FY2027 shows a similar flattering effect from a different source. The 13.7% headline includes an extra selling week worth about 670bp, and management put underlying growth at its best in nearly eight years. That is a real improvement, but it is about 7%, not 14%.

Robotics is a fight against an entrenched incumbent. Hugo's December 2025 FDA clearance covers urology only; general surgery and gynecology are filed but not cleared. Intuitive Surgical has a twenty-year lead, $11.0B of TTM revenue growing 20.7%, and thousands of installed da Vinci systems with trained surgeons. Every quarter Hugo spends in limited-indication rollout is a quarter Medical Surgical's $6.8B stapling and energy franchise keeps bleeding to robotic procedures Medtronic isn't in.

Currency, tariffs and geographic exposure. An Irish-domiciled company with roughly half its revenue outside the U.S. carries meaningful translation risk in both directions — the $819M FY2026 tailwind becomes a headwind on any dollar strengthening, and it had already shrunk to a $57M benefit in Q1 FY2027. Tariffs on medical devices and cross-border component sourcing add a cost line that management has not isolated. On the Q1 call it said tariff refunds left the quarter "a slight net negative," and that the rest-of-year guidance does not assume further refunds.

Notable One-Off Items

Currency was the largest single distortion in FY2026. Foreign exchange contributed a $819 million favorable benefit to full-year net sales. That is roughly 2.4 points of the 8.4% reported growth — the difference between reported (+8.4%) and organic (+5.8%). Medtronic's Irish domicile and roughly half-international revenue base make this a recurring, two-sided item; anyone modelling FY2027 off the reported FY2026 base is starting from an inflated number.

Restructuring charges of $249 million ran through FY2026, covering employee termination benefits, facility costs and contract terminations. This is a persistent line at Medtronic rather than a true one-off — it has appeared in some form for several consecutive years, which argues for treating it as an ongoing cost of the portfolio-reshaping program rather than excluding it.

Diabetes separation costs. Medtronic is incurring transaction, carve-out and stand-up costs for the MiniMed separation. The specific dollar amount was not disclosed in the FY2026 Q4 materials reviewed for this page — verify against the 10-K and subsequent 10-Qs before modelling. These costs are excluded from non-GAAP EPS.

The GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is $1.80 per share — $3.73 GAAP versus $5.53 non-GAAP for FY2026, roughly $2.3 billion pre-tax. The bulk is amortization of acquisition-related intangibles (a real economic cost of the M&A that produced the low ROIC), with restructuring, separation costs, litigation and certain tax items making up the balance. Investors should be clear which number they are valuing. At the September 21 close, 22.6x trailing GAAP EPS is a market multiple; 15.4x FY2027 consensus non-GAAP EPS is not.

Q1 FY2027 had a 14th week. Fiscal 2027 is a 53-week year, and management estimates the extra selling week added about $570 million, or 670bp, to Q1 organic growth. Year-over-year comparisons for this quarter overstate underlying growth by roughly half. The same quarter, Medtronic completed the acquisitions of Scientia Vascular and SPR Therapeutics, made a strategic investment in Pi-Cardia and signed a robotics partnership with Cornerstone Robotics. None of these was sized in the release.

Product actions. A catheter system recall was reported in headline coverage in August 2026. Scope, financial impact and affected product lines were not verified for this page — flagged for follow-up rather than quantified.

RSI measures how much of recent price movement has been upward versus downward. Above 70 means the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 means it has been beaten down and may be due a bounce. It says nothing about whether the business is good.

The five-year record is the bear case, written down. On actual (non-dividend-adjusted) month-end closes, MDT went from $125.35 (Sep 2021) to $91.75 (Sep 21, 2026), a −26.8% price return over five years (about −6.1% annualized; dividends, which would offset part of this, are excluded). Over the same window the S&P 500 compounded at a double-digit annual rate (at the previous edition, VOO's since-inception average was 14.93% a year). An investor who bought Medtronic at the start of this window and reinvested every one of those "50 consecutive years of dividend growth" payments would have offset only part of that price decline. That is not a drawdown; it is a lost half-decade, and it is precisely the record that brought Elliott to the register.

The shape of the loss matters more than the size. The stock did not fall in a straight line — it de-rated through 2022 on supply chain and FX, bottomed at $70.56 in October 2023 as the market decided GLP-1 obesity drugs would structurally reduce cardiac and orthopedic procedure volumes, then spent two years rebuilding to a $105.33 high in November 2025 on PFA momentum and activist optimism. It then gave almost all of it back, collapsing 28% to $73.81 by May 2026 during the mid-2026 medtech multiple compression that also drove TD Cowen and Leerink to slash targets. It then recovered +24.3% off that low to $91.75. A beat-and-raise quarter on September 1 did not move it further, so the stock still trades near the middle of a five-year range it has repeatedly failed to escape.

Relative performance is unambiguously poor. On our monthly series, MDT is −3.7% from the September 2025 month-end to this close. At the previous edition, the S&P 500's trailing one-year return was +20.65%, which was not re-verified for this date. Against medtech peers the gap is structural rather than cyclical: Boston Scientific compounded revenue at 13.5% and Intuitive at 20.7% while Medtronic managed 8.4% reported / 5.8% organic, and the market has priced that difference into the multiples (MDT 22.6x now; ISRG 44.9x and SYK 34.3x at the previous edition). Medtronic's one clear relative win is downside protection — a 0.57 beta and a 3.14% yield mean it falls less than the index in drawdowns, which is a real service in a defensive allocation and cold comfort in a bull market.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 22.6x trailing / 15.4x forward earnings and collecting a 3.14% yield backed by 50 consecutive years of increases, for the largest medical device business in the world at the exact moment its growth inflects. FY2026 delivered the best revenue growth in a decade (+8.4% reported, +5.8% organic) with every portfolio growing, driven by a pulsed field ablation franchise that grew 78% globally and took eight U.S. share points in a >$12B market compounding in the mid-20s. Q1 FY2027 kept going: Cardiac Ablation Solutions grew 88%, underlying growth was the best in nearly eight years, and guidance went up. Hugo's FDA clearance opens the largest untapped pool in surgical devices. Elliott's presence has already produced board change, a MiniMed IPO and a split-off now under way, and a management team publicly promising more investment behind growth. If raised FY2027 guidance of 7.25%–7.75% organic growth is met, the multiple gap to Stryker (34.3x) and Abbott (36.5x at the previous edition) has no defensible basis. The stock has not moved since the raise.

Bear Case

Medtronic has produced a −26.8% price return over five years and about −3.7% over the last twelve months on our monthly series. ROIC of 8.59% and ROE of 10.65% from a 65%-gross-margin business are the arithmetic proof that decades of acquisitions were overpaid for, and nothing in the current plan changes the installed capital base. The headline growth is flattered by $819M of currency benefit; organic growth of 5.8% is still below the medtech peer median, and two of four portfolios grew 3.1% and 2.9% organically. Q1's 13.7% is roughly half extra week. A PEG of about 3.8 says you are not actually buying growth cheaply. Meanwhile the company is splitting off a Diabetes business that grew 14.9% organically last quarter. Hugo faces a twenty-year-entrenched incumbent, and Abbott's Volt was targeting U.S. approval in 2H 2026 as a fourth PFA entrant. Nine of 28 analysts sit on Hold for a reason.

Top 3 Risks
01
PFA share erosion as the market's fourth entrant arrives. Medtronic's ~56% share of U.S. PFA procedures is the load-bearing assumption in the entire growth story, and it was won in a two-competitor market. Abbott's Volt targets U.S. approval in 2H 2026, joining Boston Scientific's Farapulse (~33%) and J&J's Varipulse (~10%). Share loss or price compression in cardiac ablation would immediately expose the 3.1% and 2.9% organic growth in Neuroscience and Medical Surgical.
02
The Diabetes separation destroys more than it creates. The structure is now set: MiniMed listed in March 2026, and a split-off exchange offer runs to October 9, 2026, with any unexchanged stake to be divested later. Holders who tender hand back Medtronic shares, which is what makes a split-off shrink the share count where a straight spin would not. Management puts the growth effect of losing Diabetes at about 20bp and the operating-margin gain at about 100bp. The risks are execution and value. If take-up is weak, if MMED trades down into the pricing window (Medtronic's ~90% stake was worth about $5.3B at the September 21 close, our calculation), or if the leftover stake overhangs the stock, the central pillar of the activist-driven re-rating weakens.
03
The return-on-capital problem is structural, not cyclical. An 8.59% ROIC at or near the cost of capital means Medtronic has historically not created value with the $28.2B of debt and decades of acquisitions on its balance sheet. Restructuring charges have recurred for years ($249M in FY2026) and the $1.80/share GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap is dominated by amortization of intangibles from that same M&A. Unless ROIC moves meaningfully above 10%, the "cheap multiple" is a fair price for a business that converts revenue growth into shareholder returns poorly — the definition of a value trap.
  Industry Commentary
Pulsed Field Ablation Is a Category Reset, Not a Product Cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
PFA uses high-voltage electrical pulses instead of heat (radiofrequency) or cold (cryo) to ablate cardiac tissue, sparing the esophagus and phrenic nerve and cutting procedure times materially. Adoption has been violent by medtech standards: PFA went from 39% of atrial fibrillation procedures in 2024 to ~49% in 2025, while radiofrequency fell 40%→33% and cryo 21%→18% (Citi survey of 72 physicians). The addressable electrophysiology ablation market exceeds $12B and is growing in the mid-20% range. Medtronic is the share leader at ~56% of PFA procedures (~45% Affera, ~11% PulseSelect), with PFA already 75% of its cardiac ablation revenue. The catch is that this is a whole-category replacement: Medtronic's own cryoablation business fell 40% in the same period. Share leadership in the new technology is not optional — it is the only thing offsetting the destruction of the old one.
Procedure Volumes, GLP-1s and the Medtech Multiple
ONGOING HEADWIND
Medtech has spent three years being repriced on a demand question rather than an earnings one. The October 2023 low of $70.56 — Medtronic's five-year trough — coincided with the market's conviction that GLP-1 obesity drugs would structurally shrink the population needing cardiac, bariatric, sleep-apnea and orthopedic intervention. Procedure volumes have since held up, but the multiple never fully recovered, and the mid-2026 drawdown (MDT -28.3% from January to May 2026) was again a sector de-rating rather than a company event — Leerink and TD Cowen both cut targets by 11–16% while explicitly keeping constructive ratings and citing "broader medical device valuation adjustments." The global device market is still growing 5.5%–6.9% annually to ~$623B in 2026. The industry's fundamentals are fine; its multiple is the variable.
Portfolio Simplification Under Activist Pressure — Bull vs Bear
STRUCTURAL SHIFT
Conglomerate medtech is being taken apart across the industry: Medtronic is splitting off Diabetes (MiniMed, listed on Nasdaq since March 2026; Medtronic's ~90% stake was worth about $5.3B at the September 21 close, our calculation), J&J is exploring a separation of its orthopedics franchise (reported at over $20B), and Elliott and peers are pressing the argument that scale in devices no longer earns a premium.

Bull framing: a smaller, faster Medtronic focused on Cardiovascular and Neuroscience, with capital redeployed from a low-margin consumer-hardware business into PFA and robotics, is exactly the fix for an 8.59% ROIC. Elliott has already extracted two board seats, two new committees and an explicit management commitment to invest more behind growth drivers — and R&D spending rose nearly 8% in the quarter Martha made that commitment.

Bear framing: activist-driven separations are financial engineering that improve optics faster than they improve returns. Medtronic is divesting the segment with the fastest FY2026 reported growth (12.9%) and keeping two portfolios that grew 3.1% and 2.9% organically that year. Q1 FY2027 was better, at 9.3% and 10.2% organic including the extra week. If RemainCo's growth rate falls after the separation and ROIC stays below 10%, the company will have paid transaction costs to become a smaller version of the same problem — and the stock will still be the one that lost 26.8% over five years.
