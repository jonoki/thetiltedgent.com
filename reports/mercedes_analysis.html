01 Company Overview

Mercedes-Benz Group is the company behind the three-pointed star: luxury and premium cars (Mercedes-Benz, Mercedes-AMG, Mercedes-Maybach, the G-Class), premium vans (Sprinter, Vito, the new VLE) and a captive lender, Mercedes-Benz Financial Services, that finances and leases those vehicles. It was called Daimler AG until February 1, 2022; two months earlier, on December 10, 2021, its truck and bus business started trading separately as Daimler Truck. In 2025 it sold 1,801,291 cars and 359,136 vans and reported revenue of €132.2B, down 9.2%, with EBIT of €5.8B (down 57%) and EPS of €5.34, against €10.19 in 2024.

The share price tells a harsher story than the income statement. At the October 30, 2026 close of €40.68, the stock was down 32.3% in 2026 from its €60.07 year-end close and 34.7% below its 52-week high of €62.34 (January 5). On 962.9 million issued shares that is a market value of €39.17B (our calculation) — while the industrial business alone held net liquidity of €31.0B at September 30, 2026. Put plainly, the market is paying about €8.2B for everything beyond that cash pile: the car and van franchises, a lender with €123.7B of contracts, and stakes in Daimler Truck and Beijing Benz.

The latest news is a profit warning followed by results. On October 27 Mercedes cut its 2026 outlook: the Cars division's adjusted return on sales guide fell to 2–3% from 3–5%, group EBIT (operating profit before interest and tax) is now expected at last year's level instead of significantly above it, and industrial free cash flow is now expected significantly below 2025 instead of slightly below. The stock fell 3.9% that day. The Q3 results on October 28 were better than expected on profit — group EBIT of €1.77B against a consensus of €1.6B, a Cars margin of 2.5% against 2.4% (consensus figures as listed in the company's October 27 release) — but industrial free cash flow was only €172M against €422M expected. Management blamed a steeper China decline and higher fourth-quarter costs, including materials. The debate: is this a luxury brand in a cyclical trough, priced at 7.1x trailing earnings with an 8.6% trailing dividend yield, or a company whose most profitable market is being taken by Chinese brands for good?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

140-year luxury brand Top-End pricing power (S-Class, Maybach, G, AMG) Captive finance arm (€123.7B book) €31.0B industrial net cash Double-digit van margins Global plant network

The moat is the badge and what it lets Mercedes charge at the top of the range. The company steers to a 14–15% share of Top-End models (S-Class, Maybach, G-Class, AMG) in its sales mix — 14.3% in H1 2026 — and in Q2 the new S-Class started in Europe while new V8 AMG models and the Maybach GLS debuted. The finance arm deepens it: a lease ends with the customer back at a Mercedes dealer, the natural moment to sell the next car. The balance sheet is the other wall — €31.0B of industrial net cash lets the company fund the biggest launch programme in its history and still pay out. What the moat does not protect against is a market where the badge stops commanding a premium. That is what is happening in China.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
China is shrinking, and maybe for good. Q2 car sales there fell 30%, China was about 20% of Q3 Cars sales on management's account, and the group took €752M of impairments on Chinese equity-method investments in Q2, €704M of them in Cars (H1 2026 report). Its H1 outlook expects the Chinese market to be significantly below last year and says intense price competition, especially from local manufacturers, should weigh on unit sales for many foreign brands. Deutsche Bank's Tim Rokossa wrote on September 29 that China remains the biggest challenge, and a structural rather than merely cyclical one (dpa-AFX).
02
The German cost base. In June 2026 the board launched a "productivity offensive" for Germany: faster processes, leaner structures, more on-site presence and more working hours without extra pay, with a transformation payment due in July 2026 pushed to 2027. Further effects depend on talks with employee representatives — a negotiation that has to happen while the product launch programme is running at full tilt. On October 28 the company said it will restart a voluntary severance programme for indirect staff in Germany in December, with most of the earnings effect expected in 2027 (Reuters).
03
The car margin is thin for a luxury maker. Cars' adjusted return on sales was 2.5% in Q3, against 4.8% a year earlier, and on October 27 the full-year guide was cut to 2–3% from 3–5%, citing China and higher fourth-quarter costs including materials. The CFO told the call to expect the Cars division to be around break-even in Q4 (Investing.com transcript).
04
Two Chinese strategic holders. BAIC and Li Shufu together hold close to 20% of the shares. Both have agreed to stay below 10% each, but a company whose largest car market is under pressure from Chinese competitors also counts Chinese automotive investors as its two largest holders in the 2025 shareholder table.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end of €71.31 (spin-adjusted) to the October 30, 2026 close of €40.68, the share price fell 43.0%. Dividends change the picture a lot: Mercedes paid €5.00, €5.20, €5.30, €4.30 and €3.50 a share from 2022 to 2026 — €23.30 in all, 57.3% of today's price — and with them reinvested the five-year total return is −16.6%. Over the same window the DAX, which counts dividends, returned +64.6%. Mercedes has been a cash machine with a shrinking share price.

The shape has three acts. 2022 to early 2024 was a range between the low €50s and the low €70s, peaking at €73.81 in March 2024. From there the price stepped down as China weakened and margins fell, with a brief rally to €60.07 at the end of 2025. 2026 took it to new lows, with September's €40.595 the lowest month-end close of the window (October ended at €40.68, up 0.2%): three of the five worst months in the series are in 2026 (March −11.2%, June −15.8%, September −14.0%). The 6-period monthly RSI is 26.3, in oversold territory, and the 10-month moving average of about €49.01 is far above the price. The trend is still down; the RSI only says it has been unusually one-sided.

Relative performance

Against its home index, Mercedes lost badly: −16.6% total return vs +64.6% for the DAX over five years, and −27.9% vs +4.3% on price over the last year (−22.8% vs +7.0% with dividends). Against its German peers it looks less bad: BMW fell 29.0% and Volkswagen's preference shares 22.9% over the same year, while Porsche AG was flat (+0.4%) and Stellantis lost 53.2%. The market is marking down the whole European car industry, not just Mercedes. What sets Mercedes apart is the cushion underneath: an 8.6% trailing dividend yield, a running buyback, and industrial net cash worth about four-fifths of its market value.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At €40.68 you pay 7.1x trailing earnings and 0.43x book for a company with €31.0B of industrial net cash, a 7.5% free-cash-flow yield and a €1B buyback running. The car business is at a trough margin (2.5% in Q3) while the biggest model launch in its history is still ramping: Q3 battery-electric sales hit a record 68,428, up 60.5%, and the electric GLC and C-Class only reach customers in the second half. Vans earns 9–10% margins and Financial Services is running in the upper half of its 12–14% return guide. Q3 group EBIT of €1.77B beat the consensus of €1.6B listed in the company's own release, and management is steering to restart severance in Germany and review China. If China simply stops shrinking, earnings have room to recover from today's depressed base, and the average target of €51.16 sits 26% above the price.

Bear Case

The cheap multiple prices earnings that are still falling: EPS dropped from €10.19 in 2024 to €5.34 in 2025, and Cars' margin was 2.5% in Q3 against 4.8% a year earlier, with the 2026 guide cut to 2–3% and Q4 expected around break-even. China, 30.6% of 2025 car units and about 20% of Cars sales in Q3, keeps shrinking, and Deutsche Bank calls the problem structural. Industrial free cash flow was €172M in Q3 against €422M expected, and the 2026 guide is now significantly below 2025. The 8.6% yield rests on a FY2025 dividend that took 66% of earnings, above the 40% policy; on 2026 profit, a smaller payout is possible. The German productivity offensive still has to be negotiated with labour, and VW, BMW and now Mercedes have cut forecasts this year. A stock near its five-year low with targets 26% above it can also mean the targets are late, not that the price is wrong.

Top 3 Risks

01
China volume and pricing. A 30% Q2 sales fall, China down to about 20% of Cars sales in Q3, €752M of Q2 impairments on Chinese equity investments and a company outlook calling for significantly weaker unit sales for many foreign makers. The Beijing Benz joint venture means the damage shows up in volumes and equity income before it shows up in revenue.
02
Cars margin below the new guide. 2.5% in Q3 against a 2–3% full-year guide that was itself cut on October 27, with the CFO expecting a break-even Q4 for Cars. A further miss would put the cash flow that funds dividends and buybacks in question.
03
German restructuring and labour relations. The productivity offensive asks for longer hours without extra pay and defers a scheduled payment to 2027. Its further effects depend on talks with employee representatives, the nine months already carried about €1.1B of restructuring cash out, and a new severance programme restarts in December.
Industry Commentary
China's luxury car market has turned against foreign brands
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For a decade China was the profit engine of the German luxury makers. Mercedes' H1 2026 outlook now expects the Chinese car market to be significantly below last year, and the Chinese premium and luxury segment to stay in intense price competition led by local manufacturers. Mercedes' Q2 sales there fell 30%, it wrote down Chinese equity investments by €752M in the quarter, and on the Q3 call the CEO said the Chinese market has shrunk by more than 20% this year while management is reviewing its China business (Investing.com transcript; Reuters). Bernstein's Stephen Reitman sees premium brands like Mercedes as better protected than mass-market brands as Chinese makers move into Europe, but protection in Europe does not replace lost profit in China.

A sector-wide profit reset in Germany
ONGOING HEADWIND

In 2026 the German car industry cut forecasts in turn. Jefferies tied Mercedes' June slide to BMW's profit warning; on September 18 Volkswagen cut its 2026 operating margin outlook to at most 1% from 4–5.5%, and Mercedes fell 4.8% that day. On October 27 Mercedes cut its own outlook, the last of the big three German carmakers to lower its 2026 forecast after BMW and Volkswagen (AFP), and the stock fell 3.9%. Mercedes' own fixed costs are already about 25% below 2019, and it is now asking German staff for more hours without extra pay. The common thread is high fixed costs meeting lower volumes in China and a slow, expensive shift to electric cars.

Electric demand is finally showing up in Europe
EMERGING TAILWIND

Mercedes' European BEV sales rose 87% in Q2 and European BEV order intake more than doubled. Q3 battery-electric sales were a record 68,428. The new CLA and GLB are already contributing, and the electric GLC and C-Class come next. The electrified share guide was raised to 23–25% for 2026. If the new electric models sell at margins close to the combustion cars they replace, the launch programme ends the period when electric cars dragged down group profit. If they need discounts to move, it extends it.
