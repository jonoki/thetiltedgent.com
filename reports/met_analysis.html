01 Company Overview

MetLife is one of the world's largest life and employee-benefits insurers, selling group life, disability and dental coverage to employers, annuities and pension risk transfer to retirement plans, and individual life and accident & health products across Asia, Latin America and EMEA. It is not a property-casualty insurer — there is no combined ratio here, and any report that quotes one for MetLife is quoting the wrong industry's metric. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $77.08B, up 8.59% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue is $79.40B, up 8.74%. At a $60.7B market value MetLife ranks #197 among US public companies and #420 globally by market capitalization.

The business is really two businesses bolted together, and the report below keeps them apart. The spread business: MetLife collects premiums today and pays claims decades from now, investing the float in between — net investment income (NII) was $6.7B in 2Q26, up 18%, and the rate at which maturing bonds are reinvested is the single biggest earnings lever. The underwriting business: Group Benefits sells coverage to employers, and profit there is a function of employment levels and the benefit ratio — claims as a share of premium. In 2Q26 the group life mortality ratio ran at 79% against a target range of 83%–88%, i.e. mortality experience was materially better than priced.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EMPLOYER DISTRIBUTION 163-YEAR BRAND MORTALITY DATA SCALE REGULATORY CAPITAL BARRIER JAPAN FRANCHISE IN-HOUSE ASSET MGMT SWITCHING FRICTION

MetLife's moat is distribution and data, not product. Group benefits are sold to employers on multi-year contracts administered through payroll and HR systems; ripping out an incumbent carrier means re-enrolling every employee, which is why persistency is high and why the incumbent sees renewal pricing first. Underneath that sits 163 years of mortality and morbidity experience (founded 1863) — pricing a group life book accurately requires a claims dataset that a new entrant simply cannot assemble. Third, regulation itself is a moat: writing long-duration promises requires risk-based capital (RBC) that most challengers cannot fund — MetLife carried $16.4B of US statutory adjusted capital at 2Q26, up 1% from Q1, with holding-company cash of $3.4B, inside its own target range. Finally, running MIM in-house means MetLife earns the asset-management margin on its own float rather than paying it away, which structurally widens the spread relative to a carrier that outsources.

Employment cyclicality. Group Benefits is the largest earner and its premium base is a function of how many people are employed at client companies. A recession that cuts payrolls cuts insured lives and premium simultaneously, with no offsetting benefit — this is the segment's structural weakness, and it is why the "steady" earner is not actually recession-proof.

Mortality experience is not controllable. The 79% group life mortality ratio in 2Q26 sits well below the 83%–88% target band, and management itself flagged normalization. Mortality can swing the other way — a bad flu season, a pandemic, or adverse selection in a renewal cohort — and the P&L absorbs it directly. The +25% Group Benefits growth should not be extrapolated.

Rate and credit exposure on the spread book. Net investment income of $6.7B is the largest single earnings input. A sustained fall in reinvestment rates compresses the spread on maturing assets; a credit cycle impairs the corporate and structured holdings backing long-dated liabilities. The variable investment income (VII) line is the visible symptom — $231M pretax in 2Q26, below the quarterly run rate, on private equity returns averaging just 0.8%. VII is the line every analyst on the call asks about, and it can move quarterly earnings by hundreds of millions without any change in the underwriting business.

Currency translation on two-fifths of earnings. Asia, Latin America and EMEA together produced 45.9% of 2Q26 segment adjusted earnings ($796M of $1,733M) and are earned in yen, pesos, reais and other non-dollar currencies. Management reports sales in constant currency precisely because translated dollar results can diverge sharply from local performance. A dollar rally is a direct hit to reported earnings that no amount of underwriting discipline offsets.

Why book value excluding AOCI is the number the market uses

This is the single most important accounting idea in a life insurer, and it explains why MetLife's book value per share is quoted two different ways — $38.59 reported versus $57.71 adjusted, a $19.12 per-share gap.

AOCI (accumulated other comprehensive income) is where unrealized gains and losses on the bond portfolio sit. Those marks bypass the income statement and land straight in equity. Here is the problem: accounting rules mark the asset side of the balance sheet to market, but the corresponding liabilities — the policy obligations those bonds were bought to fund — are not marked in the same way or to the same degree. When interest rates rise, the bonds fall in value and AOCI goes deeply negative, so reported book value collapses. But the liabilities those bonds back also became cheaper to fund in economic terms, and MetLife holds the bonds to maturity against those liabilities and collects par. Nothing economic happened. The reverse is equally misleading: a rate rally inflates AOCI and reported book value while the economics are, if anything, slightly worse because reinvestment rates just fell.

So reported book value moves violently for reasons that have nothing to do with how the business performed, and price-to-book computed on it — 2.50× here — is close to meaningless as a valuation signal or a time series. Strip AOCI out and you get a measure of the capital actually deployed in the business, which is why every insurance analyst quotes book value ex-AOCI ($57.71, P/B 1.67×) and computes adjusted ROE (17.0%) on that same denominator. Judge MetLife on 1.67× and 17% — not on 2.50× and 11.5%. If you take one thing from this report, take that.

Notable One-Off Items

The GAAP-versus-adjusted gap is the headline distortion. 2Q26 net income was $705M ($1.09/share) while adjusted earnings were $1.6B ($2.43/share) — adjusted earnings were more than double GAAP. On a full-year basis the same wedge shows up between TTM GAAP EPS of $5.22 and the FY2026 consensus of $9.84 (an adjusted-basis estimate, up 11.43% from an $8.83 FY2025 adjusted base against GAAP FY2025 EPS of just $4.71). The difference is overwhelmingly derivative and hedging marks: MetLife hedges rate and currency exposure, the hedges are marked through income while the hedged liabilities are not, and GAAP net income swings accordingly. This is why the trailing P/E of 18.49 and the forward P/E of 9.81 are not measuring the same earnings and should never be compared to each other.

Favorable items inside 2Q26 that will not repeat at the same magnitude: group life mortality at 79% against an 83%–88% target band; the Chilean encaje return at an elevated 5.6%; and a record Latin America quarter. Unfavorable: variable investment income of $231M pretax came in below run-rate on private-equity returns of only 0.8% — a headwind that, if it reverses, is a tailwind to future quarters. GAAP EPS history also carries visible distortion: FY2023 EPS collapsed to $1.81 (−71%) before rebounding 228% to $5.94 in FY2024, a swing driven by marks rather than operating performance.

In plain English: RSI compares the size of recent monthly gains to recent monthly losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It measures momentum, not value.

Trajectory and total return. On dividend-adjusted monthly closes, MET went from $53.44 (Sep 2021) to $96.51 (Aug 2026) — a total return of +80.6%, roughly 12.8% annualized over the five years. For comparison, the S&P 500's total return from Dec 31, 2021 through Aug 28, 2026 was +73.0% (2021 +28.71%, 2022 −18.11%, 2023 +26.29%, 2024 +25.02%, 2025 +17.88%, 2026 YTD +13.51%), about 12.5% annualized over that slightly shorter 4.66-year window. Measured on the matching Dec 2021 start, MET returned +77.1% ($54.50 → $96.51) against the index's +73.0%. So a low-beta life insurer has roughly matched — very slightly beaten — the S&P 500 over five years, which is not the usual shape of that comparison and is worth sitting with.

The path, however, was nothing like the index's. MET went essentially nowhere from late 2021 to mid-2023, spent 2024 grinding higher on rising net investment income, chopped sideways through 2025, dropped to $70.18 in March 2026 — and then rallied 37.5% in five months to the August close. Nearly the entire five-year outperformance was earned in 2026. Note also that the May 2023 print of $45.24 in the source series is a sharp outlier against $55.47 in April and $51.62 in June; it is reproduced here as the source published it rather than smoothed, but readers should treat that single point with suspicion.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Both engines are firing at once, which is rare. The spread side delivered net investment income up 18% to $6.7B while variable investment income was running below trend at $231M — meaning the recurring, rate-driven component is doing the work and there is upside if alternatives normalize. The underwriting side produced a 79% group life mortality ratio and +25% Group Benefits earnings. Adjusted ROE hit 17.0%, the top of the 15%–17% target, and the stock trades at 1.67× book excluding AOCI and 9.81× FY2026 consensus EPS. Add a $3B buyback on a $61.3B cap with share count already down 4.7% year over year, and a 2.46% dividend yield above both the peer group and the index.

Bear Case

Much of 2Q26's beat is experience, not franchise. Mortality at 79% against an 83%–88% target is four points of pure favorability that management itself said will "gradually normalize," and the Chilean encaje at 5.6% is a mark, not a business. Strip those and the growth rate looks ordinary. The stock has already run 37.5% off its March low to the top of its 52-week range; consensus of $105.06 implies only 8.86% upside and Barclays' $97 implies none. At 18.49× trailing earnings MET trades at a 34% premium to the 13.82× life-insurance aggregate. Meanwhile GAAP earnings remain less than half of adjusted — $5.22 TTM versus a $9.84 FY2026 estimate — so the cheap forward multiple depends entirely on accepting management's adjustments.

Mortality and benefit-ratio normalization. The 79% group life mortality ratio sits four points below the bottom of the 83%–88% target band, and management flagged that renewals will give this back over time. Group Benefits is 29% of segment earnings and grew 25% largely on this; a reversion toward 85% removes a meaningful chunk of that growth with no change in the business.
02
Reinvestment rates and credit on the spread book. Net investment income of $6.7B is the largest earnings input, and it depends on reinvesting maturing bonds at or above the yields rolling off. A sustained decline in rates compresses that spread progressively as the portfolio turns over, and a credit cycle would impair the corporate and structured assets backing decades-long liabilities. The 0.8% private-equity return already visible in the $231M VII line is an early read on how quickly alternatives can turn from tailwind to drag.
03
Currency translation on 45.9% of segment earnings. Asia, Latin America and EMEA together produced $796M of $1,733M in 2Q26 segment adjusted earnings, all earned in non-dollar currencies. Management reports sales in constant currency because translated results diverge from local performance; a dollar rally converts genuine 17% Asian sales growth into flat or negative reported dollars, and there is no operational lever that offsets it.
  Industry Commentary
The Rate Regime Is the Whole Story
EMERGING TAILWIND
A life insurer earns the difference between what it makes on invested assets and what it owes policyholders. After more than a decade of near-zero rates, bonds bought in the 2010s are maturing and being reinvested at materially higher yields — which is why net investment income rose 18% to $6.7B in a single quarter without MetLife writing any more business. This is a multi-year mechanical tailwind that persists as long as reinvestment rates exceed roll-off rates. It is also the reason a "boring" insurer matched the S&P 500 over five years. The corollary is uncomfortable: an aggressive easing cycle reverses it just as mechanically.
Alternatives Marks and the VII Problem
ONGOING HEADWIND
Life insurers pushed into private equity and real estate during the zero-rate years to manufacture yield. Those holdings are now the volatile part of the P&L. MetLife's variable investment income was $231M pretax in 2Q26, below the quarterly run rate, on private-equity returns averaging 0.8%. That is a rounding error of a return on assets meant to deliver double digits. VII is the first question on every earnings call for a reason: it can move a quarter by hundreds of millions with zero connection to underwriting quality, and a prolonged drought in private-market realizations keeps it suppressed across the whole sector.
Bull vs Bear: What You Are Actually Buying
FRAMING
The bull owns 1.67× book ex-AOCI generating a 17.0% adjusted ROE, with employment-linked group premium, a structural reinvestment tailwind, currency-hedged growth in Asia and Brazil, and a $3B buyback shrinking an already 4.7%-smaller share count. Return on book above the cost of equity at a modest multiple of book is the entire value proposition, and it is presently satisfied.

The bear notes the multiple has already expanded — 37.5% off the March low, 18.49× trailing versus a 13.82× peer aggregate, only 8.86% to consensus — and that the quarter that drove it leaned on 79% mortality and a 5.6% encaje, neither of which is a franchise. The honest question is not whether MetLife is a good business. It is whether you are paying for normalized earnings or for one unusually kind quarter.
