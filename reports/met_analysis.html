01 Company Overview

MetLife is one of the world's largest life and employee-benefits insurers, selling group life, disability and dental coverage to employers, annuities and pension risk transfer to retirement plans, and individual life and accident & health products across Asia, Latin America and EMEA. It is not a property-casualty insurer — there is no combined ratio here, and any report that quotes one for MetLife is quoting the wrong industry's metric. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $77.08B, up 8.59% year over year, and trailing-twelve-month revenue is $82.56B after the third quarter of 2026. At a $61.8B market value MetLife ranks #197 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization.

The business is really two businesses bolted together, and the report below keeps them apart. The spread business: MetLife collects premiums today and pays claims decades from now, investing that money (the float) in between — net investment income (NII) was $6.0B in 3Q26 on an adjusted basis, up 10%, and the rate at which maturing bonds are reinvested is the single biggest earnings lever. The underwriting business: Group Benefits sells coverage to employers, and profit there is a function of employment levels and the benefit ratio — claims as a share of premium. In 3Q26 the group life mortality ratio ran at 79.5% (CFO John McCallion on the 3Q26 earnings call) against a 2026 target range of 83%–88%, i.e. mortality experience was materially better than priced.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EMPLOYER DISTRIBUTION 163-YEAR HISTORY MORTALITY DATA SCALE REGULATORY CAPITAL BARRIER JAPAN FRANCHISE IN-HOUSE ASSET MGMT SWITCHING FRICTION

MetLife's moat is distribution and data, not product. Group benefits are sold to employers on multi-year contracts administered through payroll and HR systems; ripping out an incumbent carrier means re-enrolling every employee, which is why customers tend to stay and why the incumbent sees renewal pricing first. Underneath that sits more than 150 years of mortality and morbidity experience (the company traces its roots to 1863 and took the Metropolitan Life name in 1868) — pricing a group life book accurately requires a claims dataset that a new entrant simply cannot assemble. Third, regulation itself is a moat: writing long-duration promises requires risk-based capital (RBC) that most challengers cannot fund — MetLife ended 3Q26 with holding-company cash and liquid assets of $3.4B, inside its own target range. Finally, running MIM in-house means MetLife earns the asset-management margin on its own float rather than paying it away, which widens the spread relative to a carrier that outsources.

Employment cyclicality. Group Benefits is the second-largest earner (27.2% of 3Q26 segment earnings) and its premium base is a function of how many people are employed at client companies. A recession that cuts payrolls cuts insured lives and premium simultaneously, with no offsetting benefit — this is the segment's structural weakness, and it is why the "steady" earner is not actually recession-proof.

Mortality experience is not controllable. The 79.5% group life mortality ratio in 3Q26 sits well below the 83%–88% target band, and management has said the favorable trend will normalize gradually. Mortality can swing the other way — a bad flu season, a pandemic, or adverse selection in a renewal cohort — and the P&L absorbs it directly. The +15% Group Benefits growth should not be extrapolated.

Rate and credit exposure on the spread book. Adjusted net investment income of $6.0B in 3Q26 is the largest single earnings input. A sustained fall in reinvestment rates compresses the spread on maturing assets; a credit cycle impairs the corporate and structured holdings backing long-dated liabilities. The variable investment income (VII) line is the visible symptom — $720M pretax in 3Q26, up 49% on a year earlier, against a company estimate of at least $600M given in a September 24 8-K and full-year guidance of about $1.6B, which the CFO said on the 3Q26 call MetLife now expects to exceed. It can move quarterly earnings by hundreds of millions without any change in the underwriting business.

Currency translation on about half of earnings. Asia, Latin America and EMEA together produced 48.1% of 3Q26 segment adjusted earnings ($925M of $1,924M) and are earned in yen, pesos, reais and other non-dollar currencies. The release reports growth in constant currency beside the reported figure precisely because translated dollar results can diverge from local performance. A dollar rally is a direct hit to reported earnings that no amount of underwriting discipline offsets.

Why book value excluding AOCI is the number the market uses

This is the single most important accounting idea in a life insurer, and it explains why MetLife's book value per share is quoted two different ways — $30.73 reported versus $59.41 adjusted, a $28.68 per-share gap.

AOCI (accumulated other comprehensive income) is where unrealized gains and losses on the bond portfolio sit. Those marks bypass the income statement and land straight in equity. Here is the problem: accounting rules mark the asset side of the balance sheet to market, but the corresponding liabilities — the policy obligations those bonds were bought to fund — are not marked in the same way or to the same degree. When interest rates rise, the bonds fall in value and AOCI goes deeply negative, so reported book value collapses. But the liabilities those bonds back also became cheaper to fund in economic terms, and MetLife holds the bonds to maturity against those liabilities and collects par. Nothing economic happened. The reverse is equally misleading: a fall in rates inflates AOCI and reported book value while the economics are, if anything, slightly worse because reinvestment rates just fell.

So reported book value moves violently for reasons that have nothing to do with how the business performed, and price-to-book computed on it — 3.20× here — is close to meaningless as a valuation signal or a time series. Strip AOCI out and you get a measure of the capital actually deployed in the business, which is why every insurance analyst quotes book value ex-AOCI ($59.41, P/B 1.65×) and computes adjusted ROE (19.2%) on that same denominator. Judge MetLife on 1.65× and 19.2% — not on 3.20× and 42.7%. Reported book value fell 22% in the year to 3Q26 while adjusted book value rose 5%, which is the distortion in action. If you take one thing from this report, take that.

Notable One-Off Items

The GAAP-versus-adjusted gap is the headline distortion. 3Q26 net income was $2.4B ($3.67/share) while adjusted earnings were $1.8B ($2.78/share) — this quarter GAAP ran about a third above adjusted, the reverse of 2Q26 ($705M, $1.09/share, against $2.43 adjusted). On the call CFO John McCallion said net income exceeded adjusted earnings "mainly due to a non-economic accounting gain following the rise in interest rates in the quarter"; the release counts $551M after tax of net derivative gains, on embedded derivatives, as a result of higher U.S. interest rates. TTM GAAP EPS is $7.63 against an FY2026 consensus of $10.22 (an adjusted-basis estimate). The difference is derivative and hedging marks: MetLife hedges rate and currency exposure, the hedges are marked through income while the hedged liabilities are not, and GAAP net income swings accordingly. This is why the trailing P/E of 12.88 and the forward P/E of 9.62 are not measuring the same earnings and should never be compared to each other.

Favorable items inside 3Q26 that will not repeat at the same magnitude: variable investment income of $720M pretax, up 49% and above the company's September estimate of at least $600M (the release credits it, with volume growth and underwriting, for the earnings rise), and group life mortality at 79.5% against an 83%–88% target band. Unfavorable: total notable items of −$46M, mainly the annual actuarial assumption review. GAAP EPS history also carries visible distortion: FY2023 EPS collapsed to $1.81 (−71%) before rebounding 228% to $5.94 in FY2024, a swing driven by marks rather than operating performance.

Trajectory and return. On actual (non-dividend-adjusted) closes, MET went from $62.49 (Dec 2021 month-end) to $98.27 (Nov 6, 2026) — a price return of +57.3%, roughly 9.8% annualized over 4.85 years (dividends, which would add several points, are excluded). On that like-for-like basis MET is ahead of the index by roughly 2.5 points, with a much lower beta.

The path, however, was nothing like the index's. MET went essentially nowhere from late 2021 to mid-2023, spent 2024 grinding higher on rising net investment income, moved sideways through 2025, dropped to $70.72 in March 2026 — and then rallied 41.4% in seven months to the $100.02 October close, before closing at $98.27 on Nov 6 after falling 3.05% on the first session after results and 1.48% the next. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Both engines are firing at once. The spread side delivered adjusted net investment income up 10% to $6.0B and variable investment income of $720M, up 49%. The underwriting side produced a 79.5% group life mortality ratio and +15% Group Benefits earnings, and all six segments grew revenue. Adjusted ROE hit 19.2% (19.6% excluding notable items), above the 15%–17% target, and the stock trades at 1.65× book excluding AOCI and 9.62× FY2026 consensus EPS. Add a $3B buyback authorization on a $61.8B cap with share count down 4.55% year over year, and a 2.41% dividend yield above the life-insurance aggregate.

Bear Case

Much of the earnings strength is experience, not franchise. VII of $720M against a $400M quarterly pace in the full-year guidance, and mortality at 79.5% against an 83%–88% target band that management itself said will normalize, are favorable swings, not run-rate. Reported growth also flatters some lines: Latin America's 45% is 3% excluding notable items, and the release credits higher VII, alongside volume, for Retirement & Income Solutions' 12% rise on that basis. The stock is up 39.0% from its March month-end close of $70.72 and fell 4.5% over the two sessions after the print. Consensus of $108.88 implies 10.80% upside. At 12.88× trailing earnings MET trades above the 11.94× life-insurance aggregate, and GAAP EPS of $7.63 TTM against a $10.22 FY2026 adjusted estimate means the cheap forward multiple depends on accepting management's adjustments.

01
Mortality and benefit-ratio normalization. The 79.5% group life mortality ratio sits well below the bottom of the 83%–88% target band, and management has said renewals will give this back over time. Group Benefits is 27% of segment earnings and grew 15%; a reversion toward 85% removes a meaningful chunk of that growth with no change in the business.
02
Reinvestment rates and credit on the spread book. Adjusted net investment income of $6.0B is the largest earnings input, and it depends on reinvesting maturing bonds at or above the yields rolling off. A sustained decline in rates compresses that spread progressively as the portfolio turns over, and a credit cycle would impair the corporate and structured assets backing decades-long liabilities. The swing in VII from $231M in 2Q26 to $720M in 3Q26 shows how quickly alternatives can move earnings in either direction.
03
Currency translation on 48.1% of segment earnings. Asia, Latin America and EMEA together produced $925M of $1,924M in 3Q26 segment adjusted earnings, all earned in non-dollar currencies. Asia grew 11% reported against 14% in constant currency; a dollar rally converts genuine local growth into flat or negative reported dollars, and there is no operational lever that offsets it.
  Industry Commentary
The Rate Regime Is the Whole Story
EMERGING TAILWIND
A life insurer earns the difference between what it makes on invested assets and what it owes policyholders. After more than a decade of near-zero rates, bonds bought in the 2010s are maturing and being reinvested at materially higher yields — which is why adjusted net investment income rose 10% to $6.0B in 3Q26 from a year earlier. This is a multi-year mechanical tailwind that persists as long as reinvestment rates exceed roll-off rates. It is also a reason a "boring" insurer kept pace with the S&P 500 over five years on price alone (+57.3% against +54.8% for SPY). The corollary is uncomfortable: an aggressive easing cycle reverses it just as mechanically.
Alternatives Marks and the VII Swing
SWING FACTOR
Life insurers pushed into private equity and real estate during the zero-rate years to manufacture yield. Those holdings are now the volatile part of the P&L. MetLife's variable investment income was $720M pretax in 3Q26, up 49% from a year earlier, after $231M in 2Q26 — a threefold swing in one quarter. Private equity returns averaged 4.2% in 3Q26 against 0.8% in 2Q26, the CFO said on the two earnings calls. VII can move a quarter by hundreds of millions with zero connection to underwriting quality, and private-market realizations that dry up keep it suppressed across the whole sector.
Bull vs Bear: What You Are Actually Buying
FRAMING
The bull owns 1.65× book ex-AOCI generating a 19.2% adjusted ROE, with employment-linked group premium, a multi-year lift from reinvesting at higher rates, growth in Asia and Latin America, and a $3B buyback authorization shrinking an already 4.55%-smaller share count. A return on book value above what shareholders require, at a modest price relative to book, is the whole appeal, and today it holds.

The bear notes the stock has already run up — 39.0% above the March month-end close, 12.88× trailing versus an 11.94× peer aggregate, only 10.80% to consensus — and that the quarter leaned on a $720M VII line and 79.5% mortality, neither of which is a franchise. The honest question is not whether MetLife is a good business. It is whether you are paying for normalized earnings or for unusually kind quarters.
