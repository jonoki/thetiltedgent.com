01 Company Overview

On June 1, 2026 a shareholder that already owned about a quarter of MGM Resorts put a number on it: $48.30 a share, in cash. People Inc. — Barry Diller's company, formerly IAC — held about 26.1% of the stock and proposed to buy the rest. Sixteen weeks later the stock closed at $38.73, 24.7% below that number and 11.3% below the $43.67 it closed at on May 29, the last trading day before the proposal. The market is not pricing a deal. It is pricing something worse than the business it was pricing before anyone offered to buy it.

The business itself is a hotel-and-casino operator that has sold much of the real estate it runs. MGM runs four reporting segments — Las Vegas Strip Resorts, Regional Operations, MGM China and MGM Digital — plus a 50% stake in the BetMGM joint venture. The company began as Grand Name Co. in 1986, became MGM Grand, Inc. in 1987, MGM Mirage after buying Mirage Resorts in 2000, and MGM Resorts International in 2010. Between 2020 and 2022 it sold much of its real estate to landlords including VICI Properties and Blackstone (per Wikipedia) in sale-leasebacks. That is why the balance sheet carries $23.9B of long-term lease liabilities against only $6.07B of long-term debt, and why the cash raised has gone largely into buybacks: $9.4B of repurchases over FY2021–FY2025 (our sum of StockAnalysis annual figures), roughly the company's entire current market value. FY2025 revenue was $17.54B (+1.72%), GAAP EPS $0.76.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Licensed, scarce gaming real estate Strip room & convention scale Macau concession via MGM China Loyalty data across properties Brand portfolio (Bellagio, Aria, MGM Grand) Osaka project (opening targeted 2030)

The moat is regulatory and physical: gaming licences are scarce, and a Strip resort cannot be copied by software — the point Diller made himself. But MGM separated the moat from the ownership. Much of the real estate now belongs to landlords, and the operator keeps the licences, the brands and the customer relationships while paying fixed rent. That turns a cyclical, high-operating-leverage business into one with even higher operating leverage: in a good year the rent is small relative to EBITDAR; in a soft year it is not.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Fixed rent against variable demand. Q2 2026 segment EBITDAR was roughly $1.24B; consolidated Adjusted EBITDA was $610M, down 6% YoY. With Strip RevPAR down 4% and MGM China EBITDAR down 15%, small declines at the property level become large ones below the rent line. StockAnalysis puts interest coverage at 2.82x and debt/EBITDA (lease-inclusive) at 6.74x.
02
A conflicted bidder sets the ceiling and the floor. The bidder already owns ~26.1% and its chairman sits on MGM's board (recused from deliberations on the deal). A special committee of independent directors is reviewing the proposal; as of the Q2 call, nothing more had been disclosed. A controlling holder that knows the business well can wait, and minority shareholders have limited ways to force a better price.
03
Macau profit is falling faster than revenue. Flat revenue and a 14.8% EBITDAR decline in Q2 means the cost of holding share has risen. About 44% of that segment's economics belongs to MGM China's other shareholders.
04
Operational and cyber risk. The September 2023 cyberattack, attributed to the Scattered Spider group, cost about $100M (per Wikipedia); the stock's month-end close fell 16.4% that month. An integrated resort runs its hotels, payments and gaming floors on the same systems.

Notable One-Off Items

Q2 2026 GAAP profit was driven by a $286.7M net gain on property transactions, worth $1.13 per share in the company's reconciliation. The release does not say what produced it; the MGM Northfield Park disposal (closed April 21, 2026) is the likely source — our inference, not a company statement. It was partly offset by a goodwill impairment worth $0.37 per share and foreign-exchange items. That is why diluted EPS ($1.11) was nearly double adjusted EPS ($0.59), and why adjusted EPS actually fell 25% YoY while GAAP net income attributable to MGM rose to $292M from $49M. Read the TTM P/E with that in mind.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $43.15 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $38.73, MGM lost 10.2% of its price over five years. It paid essentially no dividends in that time, so price return is close to total return. Over the same years the company spent about $9.4B on buybacks and cut its share count sharply — and the share price still went nowhere. The range tells the story: every month-end close in the window sits between $28.95 (June 2022) and $50.77 (July 2023). Five years of a stock oscillating in a $22 band while the company changed its capital structure underneath it.

The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 79.7 in June 2026 — the proposal month and the most overbought reading in the series — and has since fallen to 45.6, neutral. The low of the series was 24.4 in March 2025. The 10-month moving average is about $39.90, just above the current price: the stock has now given back the entire run that the bid created and returned to its long-run average.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The best-informed shareholder in the company — one with a board seat — offered $48.30 in cash, and the stock is at $38.73. The whole analyst range ($43–$60) sits above the price. On our calculation MGM trades at a 15.3% trailing free-cash-flow yield after rent, the Strip has grown revenue for two consecutive quarters, same-store regional revenue set a record, digital revenue is growing 20%, and Osaka adds a long-dated growth leg. If the committee negotiates a deal anywhere near the proposal, the upside is about 25%; if not, a company that shrank its share count 9% in the past year, buying at these prices, compounds per-share value on its own.

Bear Case

The price below the pre-bid close says the market does not believe the $48.30. Property-level profit is falling where it matters — MGM China EBITDAR −15%, Strip RevPAR −4% — while $23.9B of lease liabilities fix the rent bill. Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA fell 6% in a record-revenue quarter, adjusted EPS fell 25%, and the TTM P/E of 24 rests on a one-off property gain. A 10.4% short float and a $46 UBS target cut in September point the same way. A controlling holder with a board seat can wait for a lower price.

Top 3 Risks

01
The proposal fails or is repriced lower. It is non-binding and needs a definitive agreement plus gaming and competition approvals. The share price already sits below the undisturbed level, so the downside of failure may be partly priced — but not measurably so from the data here.
02
Operating leverage through fixed rent. Lease liabilities of $23.9B and lease-inclusive debt/EBITDA of 6.74x mean a small drop in Las Vegas or Macau demand falls straight through to earnings and free cash flow.
03
Macau margin erosion. MGM China held revenue flat while EBITDAR fell 14.8% in Q2 2026. If that is the price of keeping share, it is a recurring cost, not a one-off.
Industry Commentary
The asset-light casino operator
STRUCTURAL

MGM's 2021–2022 sale-leasebacks converted owned real estate into cash and a long-term rent obligation. The equity became smaller and more leveraged: shareholders' equity is $3.38B including $0.86B of minority interest — $9.99 per share of common equity — against $30.16B of lease-inclusive debt. The cash went mostly to buybacks — $9.4B over five fiscal years. Whether that trade was good for shareholders depends on the price paid for the shares, and the five-year chart gives an unflattering answer: the repurchases were made while month-end closes ranged between $28.95 and $50.77 — the same band the stock trades in today.

Why a controlling holder bids in a soft tape
DEAL DYNAMICS

People Inc.'s letter framed the offer against 30- and 90-day average prices that had spent most of 2025 and early 2026 in the $30s. A holder with 26.1% and a board seat bids when it thinks the market price is below its value, not when the market agrees. The special committee's job is to get a higher price; the market's current verdict is that it may get no deal at all. On August 3, 2026 Yakira Capital Management, a People Inc. shareholder, published a letter urging People's board to "withdraw the proposal" and buy back its own shares instead (Yakira release via PR Newswire).
