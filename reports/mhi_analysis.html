01 Company Overview

Mitsubishi Heavy Industries builds the machines that sit underneath a country: power-station gas turbines, nuclear plant equipment, warships, missiles, defense aircraft, the H3 rocket, metals machinery, ships, industrial engines and air-conditioners. It dates its founding to July 7, 1884. In the fiscal year to March 2026 (MHI calls it FY2025) it booked ¥7.65 trillion of new orders, up 19.5%, recorded ¥4.97 trillion of revenue, up 14.1%, and earned business profit of ¥432.2 billion and net income of ¥332.1 billion (¥98.86 a share). MHI says orders, business profit, net income and free cash flow were all records.

The order book is the whole story. At September 30, 2026 MHI was sitting on ¥14.23 trillion of signed work — about 2.6 years of revenue — after a first half in which orders rose 9.0% to ¥3.61 trillion and outran revenue 1.4 to 1. Two engines drive it. The first is GTCC — large gas-turbine power plants, wanted by US utilities and, in MHI's own words, driven by "high electricity demand - especially from data centers". The second is Japan's defense build-up, which has given MHI roughly ¥1.5 trillion of defense orders a year for three years. The stock rose about twelve-fold on the back of both, from a split-adjusted ¥256.1 at the November 2021 month-end to ¥3,151 — but it has been falling since February. It closed November 10, 2026 37% below its February 2026 month-end peak of ¥5,014 and 27.8% lower than a year earlier, while the Nikkei 225 rose 18.8% over the same twelve months. On November 6 MHI reported a first half in which business profit rose 45% and net income 77%, raised its full-year targets for orders, revenue, profit and cash flow, and announced a share buyback of up to ¥300 billion. The stock had fallen five sessions in a row before the release; it closed that day down 4.6% in a market that fell 6.0%, then gained 8.2% over the next two sessions. The price is what has come down: the question is whether a 25x trailing P/E on a long-cycle machinery maker is now a fair price for a cycle that management says is running stronger than it planned, particularly in gas turbines, nuclear and defense.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

¥14.2T signed backlog Large-frame gas turbine franchise Long-term service agreements Japan's defense industrial base H3 launch vehicle Nuclear equipment know-how Net cash ¥1.22T

MHI's moat is capacity and know-how that take years to build. In large gas turbines, management said in August that demand is "broadly in line with, or slightly above" the strong level it planned for, and it is adding production capacity rather than booking more orders than it can deliver; in November it raised its full-year gas-turbine plant order forecast to ¥3.0 trillion from ¥2.4 trillion. The American turbine orders come with long-term service agreements, which tie MHI to each plant for years of maintenance after delivery. Defense works the same way with one main customer: MHI is the biggest by market value of the three heavy-industry companies the Japanese financial press groups together as the core defense names (with Kawasaki Heavy and IHI), and its Aircraft, Defense & Space backlog stood at ¥3.7 trillion in September. The weakness is the other side of the same coin: long contracts carry long execution risk. FY2025 carried losses on some thermal power projects, and the first half of FY2026 a charge of about ¥70 billion for replacing components at two coal-gasification plants delivered five years ago.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Project execution. Fixed-price, multi-year plants can go wrong years after they are signed. MHI's own summary of FY2025 says business profit grew in spite of losses on some thermal power projects and a goodwill impairment in Industrial Power Solutions. In the first half of FY2026 it set aside about ¥70B for warranty costs at the Nakoso and Hirono coal-gasification plants it delivered in 2021: its latest technical assessment found that certain components will need replacing periodically to keep the plants running reliably. The raised full-year profit guide also holds a ¥20B buffer for further risks.
02
One product cycle carries the group. Energy Systems made 52% of segment profit in 1H FY2026, even after the coal-gasification charge, and gas-turbine plants (GTCC) drive most of its growth. Analysts on the August call were already asking about softening demand for small and mid-sized turbines. The CFO's answer then was that the large-frame market MHI focuses on, with core US utility customers, has seen "no change" in supply and demand or in improving order margins.
03
The yen flatters the numbers. FY2026 guidance assumes ¥150 to the dollar; the yen closed November 10 at 157.98. MHI booked first-half revenue at an average ¥158.1 to the dollar, against ¥146.4 a year earlier; the weaker yen added ¥22B to 1H business profit compared with a year earlier, and foreign-exchange gains helped net income too. A stronger yen would take both back.
04
Guidance leaves out the Middle East. MHI's FY2026 forecast "excludes impact from the situation in the Middle East"; the company says the direct effect on orders and the supply chain is currently limited and it is monitoring. It booked a major Engineering order in the Middle East in the second quarter, and Saudi boiler contracts are part of the book.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the latest figures. First, a one-off charge of about ¥70 billion in Energy Systems: a provision for warranty costs at the Nakoso and Hirono coal-gasification plants, where MHI now expects certain components to need periodic replacement. Gains on asset sales added ¥5B to business profit compared with a year earlier. Second, currency: the weaker yen added ¥22B to first-half business profit compared with a year earlier, finance income rose to ¥20.7B from ¥10.8B, and MHI credits foreign-exchange gains and finance income for part of the jump in net income. Third, the Logisnext exit, completed May 1, 2026: discontinued operations contributed ¥5.3B of first-half profit, against a ¥10.1B loss a year earlier, and the sale proceeds turned investing cash flow positive (+¥73.8B). MHI's raised FY2026 guidance of ¥440B of net income implies ¥236.7B in the second half, 9% more than the ¥217.2B of the second half of FY2025, and its ¥600B business profit guide includes a ¥20B buffer for risks.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 10, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of ¥256.1 (¥2,561 as traded, before the 10-for-1 split that took effect April 1, 2024) to the November 10, 2026 close of ¥3,151, MHI's price rose 1,130.4% — about twelve times the money, before dividends. The Nikkei 225 rose 117.3% over the same window. Most of the climb came between mid-2023 and February 2026, the years in which annual orders rose to ¥6.41 trillion in FY2024 and ¥7.65 trillion in FY2025.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how extreme that run was. It read above 90 in ten months of 2024 and reached 96.4 in January 2025. Since the February 2026 peak it has slid, and it now reads 31.8, its lowest in the window and just above the 30 line that marks oversold. The price is 20.0% below its 10-month moving average of about ¥3,938, which is falling: after three years of trend, the stock has fallen in six of the nine months since February (counting November to date), giving back more than a third of its value from the peak.

Relative performance

Against its home benchmark MHI has been two different stocks. Over five years it beat the Nikkei 225 by a mile: +1,130.4% against +117.3% (November 2021 month-end to November 10, 2026, both price only). Over the last year it has lagged badly: −27.8% against +18.8% (November 10, 2025 to November 10, 2026), and in calendar 2026 −17.9% against +20.1% for the index (December 2025 close 50,339.48). The business did not slow in that time — in the first half of FY2026 orders rose 9%, business profit 45% and net income 77%, and guidance went up twice. What changed is what investors would pay: a stock that had been priced at a large premium fell while its earnings grew. Among its peers MHI's ¥10.59T market value is about 4.1 times IHI's and 6.2 times Kawasaki Heavy's, and roughly a third of GE Vernova's in dollars.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

MHI has ¥14.2 trillion of work signed, 83 large gas turbines under contract and a customer base — US utilities building for data-center load, and a Japanese government that has been ordering about ¥1.5 trillion of defense equipment a year — that is still asking for more. On November 6 it raised its full-year targets for orders (to ¥8 trillion), business profit (¥600 billion), net income (¥440 billion), free cash flow (¥800 billion) and ROE (14%), and started a buyback of up to ¥300 billion. The CFO says MHI is "beginning to see the benefits of improved pricing on new unit projects, lower loss-related costs, and greater production efficiency", so margins should keep rising as the better-priced orders reach revenue. Customers are paying up front, leaving ¥1.22 trillion of net cash at September 30, before the buyback. The price has fallen 37% from its February month-end peak while profit grew: the peak price was 50.7 times what FY2025 went on to earn, today's is 25.2 times trailing earnings. If the cycle runs to 2030 as the order book implies, profits are catching up with the share price.

Bear Case

At 25x trailing earnings and 3.3 times book, MHI is still priced as a growth company, but it is a long-cycle machinery maker whose profits come in waves. The turbine boom is the earnings story — Energy Systems made 52% of segment profit in the first half — and capital-goods cycles end. The first half was helped by a yen averaging ¥158 to the dollar, weaker than the ¥150 guidance assumes, and hurt by a ¥70 billion bill for plants delivered five years ago: fixed-price projects can cost money long after they are booked. Defense & Space orders fell to ¥153.8 billion from ¥427.6 billion in the half. Much of the cash pile is customers' money, owed back as work, and management says it will need it from next year. A stock that has fallen 37% from its February peak while the Nikkei is up 20% this year is telling you many buyers have moved on, and brokers cut targets after the print (to ¥3,600 at CLSA, per Asset Alive): a stock that has got cheaper relative to its earnings can get cheaper still.

Top 3 Risks

01
The gas-turbine cycle turns. GTCC is the main source of orders, margin growth and advance payments. If US power demand or utility capital plans cool, orders, cash flow and the expected margin expansion all weaken together — and the share price assumes they won't.
02
Execution on a record backlog. MHI is doubling turbine capacity and adding defense capacity while delivering a ¥14.2 trillion book. Late deliveries or cost overruns on fixed-price contracts have hurt it before: FY2025 carried losses on some thermal projects, and the first half of FY2026 a ¥70 billion warranty provision on two coal-gasification plants.
03
Valuation and currency. A 25x P/E leaves little room for a miss, and the yen at ¥158 to the dollar is flattering results against a ¥150 plan. A stronger yen and a lower P/E at the same time is the bad combination.
Industry Commentary
The gas-turbine super-cycle
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

MHI's FY2025 results deck puts the demand down to three things: the "transition away from coal- and oil-fired thermal power, need for peaking power to offset renewable energy intermittence, and high electricity demand - especially from data centers". The result is a seller's market for large gas turbines. MHI booked 35 large-frame units in FY2025 and 19 more in the first half of FY2026; in August the CFO said turbines being ordered were generally scheduled for 2028–2030, and projects still under discussion for around 2030–2031. The CFO's answers, in MHI's published summary of the Q1 briefing (dated August 6), are the bull case in management's own words: "Order pricing has not declined, and the profitability of newly booked projects continues to improve gradually," and for core US utility customers there has been no change in the strong supply-demand environment. MHI is not chasing every order — it says it is selective and is building the book in step with a capacity expansion: in November the CEO said it aims to roughly double gas-turbine plant production capacity by FY2030 from FY2024, and in October Mitsubishi Power announced a $490 million expansion of its Savannah, Georgia turbine works. The risk is the one every capital-goods cycle carries: utilities order in waves, and the waves end.

Japan's defense build-up
POLICY-DRIVEN

MHI's defense orders have run at about ¥1.5 trillion a year for three years, according to the CFO in August, and it now forecasts ¥1.52 trillion of Defense & Space orders for FY2026. Revenue lags: Defense & Space revenue was ¥1,144.5B in FY2025 and is now guided to ¥1,320B, so work keeps piling up. Exports have opened up too, with Australia's three-frigate order. Policy is the swing factor. In mid-September Japanese press reports, relayed by Kabutan, said the government was considering a defense-spending target of 3.5% of GDP; on November 10 Minkabu Press relayed a report that the defense ministry puts the cost of its FY2027–2031 buildup program at ¥70–80 trillion, alongside a market view that the US midterm results could mean a smaller US defense budget. As Minkabu Press noted, a bigger Japanese budget helps most the companies with the largest sales to the defense ministry — which is why MHI, Kawasaki Heavy and IHI tend to move together on these headlines.

The price investors will pay, both ways
VALUATION DEBATE

MHI went from a sleepy conglomerate to a stock that rose about twelve-fold in five years because the market started valuing it on its order book rather than its history. That premium is now being tested. The bull framing: the business is fine and only the price investors will pay per yen of profit has come down — profit grew, the share price fell; the February 2026 month-end price of ¥5,014 was 50.7 times what FY2025 went on to earn (¥98.86 a share), and today's ¥3,151 is 25.2 times trailing earnings and 24.1 times the company's raised FY2026 forecast, with a buyback now under way. The bear framing: 25 times earnings is still a full price for cyclical heavy industry, a one-off plant charge showed that old contracts can bite, the stock still sits only 3% above where it closed before the November print, and it has lagged its index by more than 45 points over twelve months. The November 6 print delivered what the bulls asked for — raised guidance and a use for some of the cash — so the next judge is the 2027 plan: a credible capacity path and clear returns on the ¥1 trillion-plus of planned investment would argue the premium is earned.
