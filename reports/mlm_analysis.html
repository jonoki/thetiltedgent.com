01 Company Overview

Martin Marietta is a natural-resource-based building materials company: it owns quarries, digs up crushed stone, sand and gravel, and sells the tons into road, non-residential and residential construction, alongside downstream cement, ready mixed concrete and asphalt operations and a specialty magnesia and lime business. Third-quarter 2026 revenue was $2,369M, up 28% from $1,846M a year earlier; revenue for the nine months to September 30 was $5,678M, up 23% (from $4,617M). Over the trailing twelve months it was about $7.21B (FY2025: $6,150M). At roughly $36.9B of market value the company ranks #276 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation.

The company reported Q3 2026 on November 4 and, for the first time, folded in Lhoist North America (LNA), the limestone-and-lime combination announced at about $13.5B that closed on August 21, 2026 for total consideration of $12.9B ($7.0B in cash and about $5.9B in stock, against the $6.5B of stock announced), financed with $5.5B of new senior notes and a $1.5B term loan. Since the closing date LNA added $216M of revenue and $51M of gross profit. The headline: adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, with one-offs removed) rose 21% to $808M, but adjusted earnings per share fell 11% to $5.47, GAAP (as-reported) EPS fell 34% to $3.94, and the average aggregates selling price fell 4% as reported. On the company's own measure that strips out acquisitions and geography, organic mix-adjusted price rose 2.7%, but organic shipments declined modestly. Guidance was reset to include Lhoist: 2026 revenue of $7.75–7.85B (it was $7.2–7.4B without Lhoist) and adjusted EBITDA of $2.45–2.50B (it was $2.36–2.50B). The stock closed at $518.15 on November 6, up 3.1% from the $502.47 of the previous edition, but 6.9% below its November 4 close of $556.56, the day of the release.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
RESERVE IRREPLACEABILITY FREIGHT-RADIUS MONOPOLY ZONING & PERMITTING BARRIER 2BN+ TONS LIMESTONE COASTAL DISTRIBUTION NETWORK PRICE-OVER-VOLUME DISCIPLINE PUBLIC-SPEND DEMAND ANCHOR

The moat here is geology plus freight arithmetic, and it is one of the most durable structures in industrial America. Crushed stone is a low-value, high-weight commodity: trucking it any meaningful distance costs more than the stone itself, so a quarry effectively holds a local franchise within a short trucking distance. You cannot compete by building a better quarry, because you cannot get a new one permitted — zoning, environmental review and community opposition make greenfield sites in growth markets close to unobtainable. The result is a business where the incumbent sets price and the customer pays it, which the organic data still shows: organic mix-adjusted ASP rose 2.7% in Q3 2026, even while organic shipments dipped and the reported price fell 4% on acquisition mix. The Lhoist combination extends that logic — the same limestone deposit that yields construction aggregates yields high-calcium lime, so 2 billion-plus tons of reserves now serve two kinds of customer (construction and industrial/steel/environmental) whose demand does not rise and fall together. Note the trend, though: the underlying price gain slowed from 3.7% in Q2 to 2.7% in Q3.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Volume cyclicality is not hedged by pricing power. Pricing is durable; tonnage is not. Organic shipments declined modestly in Q3 2026, which the company ties to softer interest-rate-sensitive private construction; the 16% headline growth came from acquisitions. A residential or non-residential downturn shows up in tons immediately and in price only with a long lag, which means earnings fall before the moat does.
▸
Balance sheet risk from the Lhoist financing. The company priced $5.5B of senior notes across five tranches maturing 2029–2056 in August 2026, alongside a $1.5B term loan. At September 30, 2026 total debt was about $12.8B against $140M of cash (Q3 release), versus $5.95B at June 30, 2026, before the deal financing (Q2 release). Management said on the Q3 call it is committed to returning to a target of 2 to 2.5 times net debt to adjusted EBITDA, with roughly $500M of debt repayment expected over the following weeks. Any integration stumble now lands on a company carrying far more debt.
▸
Accounting is obscuring the operating trend. The gain from the QUIKRETE exchange now sits in discontinued operations: nine-month consolidated net earnings of $2,020M include $1,428M from discontinued operations, against $592M from continuing operations. TTM EPS of $37.33 is therefore not a number to value the company on — see Section 04. Adjusted EPS of $5.47 in Q3 was 11% below a year earlier even as revenue rose 28%, and GAAP EPS of $3.94 was 34% lower.
▸
Public-infrastructure demand concentration. A large share of aggregates tonnage flows to highway and public infrastructure work, which ties demand to federal and state spending decisions rather than to private investment cycles. That steadies demand in a recession and makes each renewal of the federal highway law a risk. On the Q3 call management said the current surface-transportation programmes have been extended through December 11, 2026, and that work continues toward a longer extension and the next authorisation. The size and timing of that law is the outside factor most worth tracking on this stock.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the December 2021 monthly close of $440.52 to the November 6, 2026 close of $518.15, MLM returned +17.6% in price over about 4.9 years — roughly 3.4% a year, plus a dividend yielding well under 1% a year (price return, month-end close to the banner-date close). That is a thin result for a business whose operating economics improved over the same window: gross margin went from 24.91% in FY2021 to 30.80% in FY2025 (FY2025 on the continuing-operations basis, without the businesses later exchanged with QUIKRETE). The path was anything but linear. The stock bottomed at a monthly close of $299.24 in June 2022 as rate fears hit construction-linked stocks, then ran +105% over the following twenty-one months to $613.94 in March 2024, chopped sideways through 2024–2025, reached its highest month-end close, $676.57, in February 2026 (the highest daily close was $708.11 on February 10), and has since given back 23.4%. Most of that fall came after the June 29 Lhoist announcement: the stock closed at $616.06 on June 26. Its lowest close of the past 52 weeks, $471.10, came on October 14.

Relative performance. Against the October 31, 2025 month-end close of $613.10 the stock is down 15.5% (price return), while its closest peer, Vulcan Materials, fell 2.3% ($289.50 to $282.72) and the SPY S&P 500 fund rose 7.8% ($682.06 to $735.29) over the same window; beta to the S&P 500 is 1.03. Since June 26, the last close before the Lhoist announcement, Martin Marietta is down 15.9%, Vulcan down 9.2% and SPY up 0.9%. The fall came alongside revenue growth of 23% over nine months and adjusted EBITDA growth of 17% (to $1,809M), so the reported numbers do not show a collapse in demand; but organic shipments are no longer growing, which is the part of the argument the bears can point to.

Momentum read. The price sits below its 10-month moving average, which has rolled over since the summer, and above its low of the October 14 session; the November 4 close ($556.56) was the high point of the rebound. Momentum and fundamentals are pointing in different directions on this name right now: RSI cannot see that trailing EPS contains $1,428M of discontinued-operations earnings, or that Lhoist North America is in the numbers for only six weeks of the quarter.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The pricing machine is intact and the market is reading the wrong line: organic mix-adjusted aggregates ASP grew 2.7% in Q3 2026 while the reported figure showed −4%, adjusted EBITDA grew 21% to $808M, and aggregates cash gross profit hit a quarterly record of $696M. Guidance now includes Lhoist and lifts 2026 revenue to $7.75–7.85B, with 2 billion-plus tons of limestone reserves serving both construction and industrial demand curves, plus a $350M annualised cash-flow improvement programme and a plan to cut debt toward 2–2.5 times EBITDA. You are buying an irreplaceable reserve base 27% below its 52-week high, with 22.5% of upside to the average analyst target and even the lowest target ($500) only 3.5% below the price.

Bear Case

Strip out the noise and you are paying 34.5× forward earnings for a company earning 4.81% on invested capital and 6.02% on equity — returns below most estimates of what its investors and lenders require — with $12.8B of debt against $140M of cash after the Lhoist deal. Organic shipments declined in Q3; the 16% increase was bought. Adjusted EPS fell 11% and GAAP EPS 34% even as revenue rose 28%, the underlying price gain slowed from 3.7% to 2.7%, and 2026 aggregates ASP is guided down 2–3% as reported. The stock fell 5.2% in the session after the print, and 29% of the analyst panel is at Hold.

Top 3 Risks

01
Lhoist integration and leverage. A $12.9B transaction (announced at $13.5B) funded with $5.5B of new notes and a $1.5B term loan, closed August 21, 2026, leaving about $12.8B of debt at September 30. If synergies disappoint or the lime end markets (steel especially) soften, the debt does not care.
02
Organic volume stagnation. The 16% Q3 shipment growth came from acquisitions; organic shipments dipped. If construction starts roll over — residential, non-residential or a pause in public appropriations — the acquisitions will have bought volume into a falling market, and price increases cannot offset a fall in tons in the same year.
03
Returns below what the capital costs. ROIC of 4.81% and ROE of 6.02% on a business trading at 34.5× forward earnings is a mismatch that resolves one of two ways: returns rise, or the share price falls relative to earnings. The Lhoist purchase raises the capital base before its earnings fully arrive.
Industry Commentary
Infrastructure spending is doing the volume work
EMERGING TAILWIND

Martin Marietta now guides 2026 revenue to $7.75–7.85B, including Lhoist North America, and aggregates shipments to grow 12.5–13.5%, with data-center project shipments more than doubling in Q3 (per the call transcript). Public infrastructure work is the demand least sensitive to price that this company sees: highway contracts are awarded on specification and proximity, and the aggregate is a small fraction of total project cost, so the customer does not shop on stone price. Against that, the company attributed the modest decline in organic shipments to softer interest-rate-sensitive private construction.

Consolidation is now the sector's main event
ONGOING HEADWIND

In nine months Martin Marietta executed an asset exchange with QUIKRETE, the New Frontier Materials bolt-on, the $12.9B combination with Lhoist North America (announced at about $13.5B) and the Frazier Quarry purchase. That is the aggregates industry's structural answer to the fact that you cannot permit new quarries. The consequence for investors is that reported financials become harder to read — $1,428M of discontinued-operations earnings, a reported-versus-organic ASP gap of nearly seven percentage points (−4% vs +2.7%), and the disappearance of separate cement, ready mixed and asphalt disclosure. The stock's 23.4% drawdown since the February monthly peak is consistent with a market that discounts what it cannot easily read.

Bull vs bear, framed on one number
THE DECIDING VARIABLE

Everything on this page reduces to which ASP series you believe describes the future. The bear takes the reported line: −4% pricing, $7.62 of gross profit per ton (down 17%), 26.74% TTM gross margin, 4.81% ROIC, GAAP EPS down 34%, a capital-intensive cyclical paying too much for growth at the wrong point in the cycle, at 34.5× forward. The bull takes the organic line: +2.7% mix-adjusted pricing, a record $696M of aggregates cash gross profit, 21% adjusted EBITDA growth, and 2 billion tons of reserves that will still be there in 2050. Both readings are honestly derived from the same quarter. The fourth-quarter report, the first with a full quarter of Lhoist, is where the argument gets more data.
