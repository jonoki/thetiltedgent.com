01 Company Overview

Mosaic digs two things out of the ground that no farm can do without: potash from Saskatchewan and phosphate from Florida's Bone Valley — and it sells both, plus imported product, through a large distribution business in Brazil. The company was formed in October 2004 by merging IMC Global (founded 1909) with Cargill's crop nutrition division; Cargill distributed its stake to its own shareholders in May 2011, and Mosaic joined the S&P 500 on September 26, 2011. FY2025 revenue was $12.05B, up 8.4%, with diluted EPS of $1.70. Over the twelve months to September 2026, revenue is $11.90B and the company is loss-making: −$1.12B net, −$3.54 per share.

Here is the hand Mosaic is playing. A phosphate producer is a spread business: it buys sulfur and ammonia, reacts them with rock, and sells the result. In Q3 2026 the selling price rose again — DAP realised $838/tonne against $714 a year earlier — and the Phosphate segment still lost $194M at the operating line, because the inputs ran further ahead. Sulfur cost Mosaic $715 per long ton in the quarter, and the plants it cannot supply at an affordable price are simply off: Louisiana phosphate production is idled, Bartow and Brazil are curtailed, and the South Fort Meade and Miski Mayo mines have been idled to match. Potash earned $284M of adjusted EBITDA, 76% of the three segments' total, but it had its own miss: lower ore grades at Esterhazy cut 2026 production to 8.6Mt from about 9.0Mt. On the November 4 call the CFO said "Q4 is gonna be a trough in terms of EBITDA," with sulfur already contracted at $615 for the quarter. The stock, at $18.42 before the release, closed that day up 17.37% and stood at $22.36 on November 6, still 35.5% below its September 2025 month-end.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Long-life Saskatchewan potash reserves Integrated Florida phosphate rock Brazil distribution footprint Mine permits & capital intensity Low potash cash cost ($73/t) Non-discretionary end demand

The moat is geology and permits. Nobody builds a new potash mine or phosphate complex quickly, and farmers cannot skip potassium and phosphorus indefinitely. That protects Mosaic's existence. It does not protect its margin: fertiliser is a commodity, the price is set globally, and the producer takes it. The Q3 2026 numbers show the distinction plainly — owning the rock did not stop Phosphates losing $194M when sulfur spiked, because a phosphate plant's rock is not its only input.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Sulfur is the swing input. Sulfur cost $715 per long ton in Q3 and the Q4 contract settled at $615, but contracts cover only about 65–66% of production needs going into 2027, per Bodine on the Q3 call. On the Q2 call he attributed the tight sulfur market to the ongoing closure of the Strait of Hormuz and a blockade affecting Kazakhstan (Motley Fool transcript). Mosaic cannot hedge its way out of a physical shortage; its lever is to curtail production, and with 70–75% of phosphate costs fixed, by Bodine's count, each idled plant raises the cost of every tonne still made.
02
Canadian potash is now a political asset. On September 21, 2026 the US President posted that Washington was negotiating a potash deal with Belarus priced "substantially less than we are currently paying to Canada" (quoted by Wealth Professional). The US Treasury removed Belaruskali and Belarusian Potash Company from its sanctions list on March 26, 2026 (OFAC). Belarus's own president responded the same day that its output is already fully contracted (European Pravda, Sep 22, 2026). By October 14 a first 30,000-tonne cargo was expected at New Orleans (Al Jazeera, citing Kpler ship-tracking data).
03
The balance sheet is doing more work. Total debt rose from $5.05B at December 31, 2025 to $5.91B at September 30, 2026, against $273M of cash. Nine-month free cash flow was −$403M and the trailing-twelve-month figure is −$808M; Q3 alone was about break-even. Mosaic still pays $0.22 a quarter. In August it sold $2.0B of new notes due 2031–2036 and retired $1.4B of older debt, so all long-term maturities previously due in 2027 and 2028 are refinanced and its $2.5B credit line remains fully available. That buys time; it does not lower the debt.
04
Operating reliability. The October 2025 phosphate production miss (Riverview, Bartow), the 2026 curtailments and now the Esterhazy ore-grade shortfall mean both main businesses have missed their own production plans in about a year. Restarting idled assets costs money, and Bodine said Mosaic would move in a measured way and only with confidence that sulfur prices will not swing back.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a potash franchise that earned $284M of adjusted EBITDA in a single quarter at $73/t cash cost, plus a phosphate business priced as if the damage is permanent, at 0.63x book with a 3.94% dividend. The phosphate problem is an input-cost problem, not a demand problem: DAP realised $838/t, management says its Q4 phosphate is largely committed, and the CFO has named Q4 as the earnings trough. The $615 Q4 sulfur contract helps cash at once and earnings later. If sulfur normalises, the spread reopens on idled capacity Mosaic has said it will restart once prices look durable. Add capital spending targeted below $1B in 2027, overhead below $500M, the Araxá sale and long-term debt maturities refinanced through 2028, and the cash-flow line can turn without much help from the cycle. In potash, Baltic shipping limits are removing Russian and Belarusian tonnes in Q4, and Belarus itself said its output is fully contracted.

Bear Case

A commodity producer with negative trailing EPS (−$3.54), negative free cash flow (−$808M TTM), debt up $0.86B in nine months and a dividend the business has not covered over the last year is not cheap at 0.63x book — it is showing you what the book is worth. Phosphates lost $194M in a quarter when the product price rose 17%; Fertilizantes volumes fell by a third. Management's own guide says Q4 is worse before it is better, with sulfur in costs at $800–850. The one healthy segment, potash, just cut its production guide and guided Q4 prices to $255–275/t, below Q3's $276, and it faces a US government seeking cheaper non-Canadian supply. Consensus FY2026 EPS is −$0.05, and the analyst range runs down to $17. After a 21.6% first week of November, the stock has already priced in part of the recovery.

Top 3 Risks

Sulfur and ammonia stay expensive. Mosaic's contract sulfur covers only about 65–66% of production needs going into 2027, and management will not restart idled plants until spot sulfur improves and looks likely to stay there. If input costs keep outrunning DAP, the phosphate curtailments stay in place and their fixed costs keep landing in the income statement.

Leverage and the dividend. Total debt $5.91B against $273M of cash and negative trailing free cash flow. Management expects positive free cash flow in the second half; if Q4 falls short, the choice between the dividend and the balance sheet becomes explicit.
Industry Commentary
The sulfur squeeze on phosphate producers
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Phosphate fertiliser needs sulfuric acid, and sulfuric acid needs sulfur — mostly a by-product of oil and gas refining, so its supply follows energy flows rather than farm demand. On the Q3 2026 call Mosaic put its realised sulfur cost at $715 per long ton against a market average of about $1,100, its Q4 contract at $615, and the figure reaching Q4 costs at $800–850, because higher-priced inventory is used first. That leaves its stripping margin, the gap between the fertiliser price and its raw-material cost, in the mid-$300s per ton for Q4. Mosaic's response across 2026 has been to curtail: Louisiana idled, Bartow and Brazil cut back, Florida running in the low- to mid-60s percent, and two mines idled so rock does not pile up. That preserves cash per tonne but shrinks volume. Bodine said spot sulfur is improving but is still too high to restart curtailed production.

Potash: balanced market, political supply
ONGOING HEADWIND

Potash is a concentrated industry. US Geological Survey data put Canada's share of US potash imports at 79% over 2021–2024, with Russia at 12% (via Wealth Professional, September 22, 2026), and the US Treasury removed Belaruskali and Belarusian Potash Company from its sanctions list on March 26, 2026. Mosaic's MOP price was $276/t in Q3, up from $271, and on the Q3 call management put 2026 global demand at about 75Mt, near a record, with China opening 2027 contract talks earlier than usual. A US-Belarus deal would not change world supply much — Belarus says its output is already contracted — but it could redirect flows into Mosaic's home market. RBC's Andrew Wong said any effect on realised prices from a shift in supply would be moderate unless Belarusian potash replaces Canadian potash in large volumes (Wealth Professional).

Self-help: a smaller, cheaper Mosaic
EMERGING TAILWIND

What Mosaic controls, it is cutting: Carlsbad sold, Araxá in a sale process, capital spending guided to $1.2B for 2026 and below $1B for 2027, overhead guided to $510–530M for 2026 and below $500M for 2027, and a Biosciences business expected to double to $135M of net sales this year. The bull framing: the company is sitting out the bad hands and keeping its chips for when sulfur normalises, with idled capacity ready to restart. The bear framing: deferring waste-disposal and upkeep spending on idled sites only pushes that bill out, as Bodine acknowledged on the call, and selling assets at the bottom of a cycle locks in a smaller business just before the recovery. The deciding data points are reported every quarter: phosphate operating result, sulfur cost per long ton, and free cash flow after the dividend.
