01 Company Overview

Marsh & McLennan is the world's largest insurance and reinsurance broker and one of its largest professional-services firms, operating in 130 countries through two reported segments: Risk and Insurance Services (Marsh Risk in insurance broking, Guy Carpenter in reinsurance broking) and Consulting (Mercer in health, wealth and career; Oliver Wyman in management consulting). Fiscal 2025 revenue was $26.98 billion, up 10.3% YoY on a reported basis but only +4% on an underlying (organic, constant-currency, acquisition-adjusted) basis — the gap is almost entirely the McGriff acquisition. Adjusted operating margin expanded to 27.1% from 26.8%, and adjusted EPS rose 9% to $9.75.

The business model is asset-light and fee-based: brokers earn commissions calculated as a percentage of the insurance premium placed, plus fees. That makes revenue recurring and capital-light — but it also means the direction of property-and-casualty (P&C) insurance pricing feeds straight through to organic growth. With commercial rates down 5% in Q3 2026 (6% in Q2) and global property down 10%, this is the central debate on the stock. At $89.6 billion of market capitalisation MMC ranks #134 in the S&P 500 by market value, and it is by a wide margin the largest company in the listed Insurance Brokers industry (~29% of the group's $96.8 billion of combined revenue).

Latest quarter. Third-quarter 2026 results, reported October 15: revenue $6.9 billion, up 8% (5% underlying); adjusted EPS $2.03, up 10%, against a $1.96 consensus; GAAP EPS $1.69 after a $144 million charge to settle claims over Marsh's work as insurance broker for the failed finance firm Greensill Capital, with no further charges expected. Marsh Risk revenue grew 5% underlying, Reinsurance fell 6%, People and Investments grew 5% and Management Consulting (Oliver Wyman) grew 14%. Management raised its Thrive savings target (a multi-year cost-cutting programme) to $600 million from $400 million.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOURCES OF MOAT
GLOBAL SCALE — 130 COUNTRIES PROPRIETARY PLACEMENT DATA STICKY RENEWAL BOOK REGULATORY & LICENSING COMPLEXITY CROSS-SELL ACROSS 4 BUSINESSES SERIAL M&A ROLL-UP MACHINE ASSET-LIGHT, NO UNDERWRITING RISK

MMC's moat is distribution scale layered on top of information asymmetry. A broker that places tens of billions of dollars of premium across 130 countries sees pricing, terms and claims outcomes no single insurer or client can see, and that data advantage is what lets Marsh Risk defend commission rates when carriers push back. Renewal retention in commercial broking is structurally high — the switching cost of moving a complex global insurance programme mid-cycle is real, and the incumbent broker controls the placement history. The roll-up strategy is itself part of the moat: MMC has spent $7.75bn on McGriff (2024) and £4.3bn on Jardine Lloyd Thompson (2019), plus a continuous stream of smaller tuck-ins (on July 1, 2026, for example, it bought the 60% of the Spanish broker Asterra it did not already own), each plugged into a larger carrier-negotiation footprint. Because the model requires no underwriting capital, incremental revenue converts at high margin: ROE of 27.2% and ROIC of 15.0% on a business generating $4.15 billion of free cash flow. That recurring, capital-light cash stream is why brokers have historically traded at a premium multiple to the broader financials sector — and why the current 22.6x trailing / 16.8x forward P/E, below the 27.5x industry weighted average, reads as a cycle derating rather than a moat impairment.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Commission revenue is levered to P&C premium rates. Brokers get paid a percentage of premium. Commercial insurance rates fell 5% in Q1 2026 — the seventh consecutive quarterly decline — 6% in Q2 and 5% in Q3, when global property fell 10% and casualty rose 1% (Marsh's global insurance market index). Guy Carpenter's property-cat rate-on-line index fell 16% at the July 2026 renewal. Rate declines do not automatically shrink revenue (exposure growth and new business offset), but they remove the tailwind behind underlying growth of 9% in 2023 and 7% in 2024; it is 4–5% now. This is the single most important variable in the model.

2. Leverage taken on for McGriff. Total debt stands at $22.33 billion including $1.80 billion of lease liabilities ($20.53 billion of borrowings at September 30, 2026), against $1.84 billion of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.50 and enterprise value of $110.05 billion versus $89.56 billion of market cap. The company also carries a current ratio of only 1.09 and a quick ratio of 0.50. Deleveraging competes directly with the $5.5 billion 2026 capital-deployment target and with further M&A.

3. Fiduciary investment income declines with rates. Brokers hold client premium in fiduciary accounts and keep the interest. That income was $403 million in FY2025 and $100 million in Q3 2026 — down $9 million from $109 million a year earlier — with management guiding to roughly $94 million in Q4. Every Fed cut erodes a high-margin, zero-cost revenue line that flattered 2023–25 earnings.

4. Integration, restructuring and legal drag. FY2025 carried $215 million of McGriff integration costs and $222 million of "Thrive" restructuring charges; 2026 has added $182 million of Thrive charges in nine months ($79 million in Q3), and management raised the programme's savings target to $600 million from $400 million. Separately, Greensill-related charges totalled $569 million in the first nine months: $425 million in Q1 and a $144 million settlement charge in Q3, after which the company said it does not expect further charges. The move to a single Marsh brand, begun in January 2026 and completed in the third quarter, retired names such as Guy Carpenter and Mercer that carried independent value, which adds brand-equity risk.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

The gap between MMC's GAAP GAAP results and its adjusted results is unusually wide right now, and every component is identifiable:

Greensill charges — $569 million in the first nine months of 2026. Legal and settlement costs (net of insurance recoveries) tied to claims over Marsh's work as insurance broker for the failed finance firm Greensill Capital: $425 million in Q1 and $144 million in Q3, when the company settled and said it does not expect further charges. They are the largest driver of the GAAP TTM EPS of $8.35 sitting below the FY2025 GAAP EPS of $8.43 despite revenue growth.

McGriff integration costs — $215 million in FY2025. Non-recurring integration spend associated with folding a 3,500-person, $1.3bn-revenue broker into Marsh McLennan Agency. The company said in its January 2026 results release that the McGriff integration was complete.

"Thrive" restructuring — $222 million in FY2025, $79 million in Q3 2026 ($182 million in nine months). A multi-year cost programme; on the Q3 call management raised its savings target to $600 million from $400 million, with most actions completed by the end of 2028 and about $750 million of total costs. It is the explicit engine behind the expected margin expansion; treat the charges as recurring-while-the-programme-runs rather than truly one-off.

Currency and acquisition effects. The 6-point spread between FY2025 reported revenue growth (+10.3%) and underlying growth (+4%) is almost entirely acquisition-driven. Any assessment of the business should anchor on underlying growth. McGriff passed its first anniversary in November 2025, and reported and underlying growth nearly converged in Q2 2026 (+6% and +5%); in Q3 they diverged again (+8% reported, +5% underlying), with currency and acquisitions the items between them.

Fiduciary interest income — $403 million FY2025. Not a one-off, but worth flagging as a quality-of-earnings item: it is pure interest on client money, it carries no cost, and it is now shrinking ($100m in Q3 2026, down $9m from $109m a year earlier).

Five-year trajectory. From a November 2021 month-end close of $164.02 to $188.35 on October 16, 2026, MMC returned +14.8% on price alone over nearly five years — roughly 2.9% annualised before dividends, with the current yield at 2.10%. That headline number understates the round trip inside it. The stock rose 63% off the September 2022 month-end low of $149.29 to $244.03 in March 2025 (the highest month-end close in the window), as the hard P&C market delivered underlying growth of 9% in 2023 and 7% in 2024 and rising rates inflated fiduciary income. It then fell 34.4% peak-to-trough (month-end closes) to $159.97 in May 2026 as the pricing cycle turned, reinsurance rates collapsed, fiduciary income began rolling off and the market repriced a 4%-organic-growth broker. Since then it has recovered 17.7% from that low, with a dip to $170.73 at the end of September and a 4.6% jump on the day of Q3 results. Net of everything: the window's gain peaked at +48.8% in March 2025 and about 70% of it has since been given back.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the world's largest insurance broker — an asset-light, no-underwriting-risk toll booth on global commercial premium — at 22.6x trailing and 16.8x forward earnings, below both the 27.5x broker-industry weighted average and the 26.0x S&P 500 trailing multiple. The model still throws off $4.15bn of free cash flow at 93% conversion, earns 27.2% ROE and 15.0% ROIC, and pays a 2.10% yield after a 10% dividend increase in July. Margin expansion is the plan: FY2025 adjusted margin of 27.1% (up from 26.8%), 29.3% in Q2 2026 (29.5% a year earlier) and 22.7% in Q3 (flat on a year earlier), with management expecting another year of margin expansion and a Thrive savings target raised to $600 million. Meanwhile Consulting — 36% of revenue and uncorrelated to P&C pricing — is accelerating, with Oliver Wyman up 14% underlying in Q3. Every listed broker derated together, which argues this is a cycle trough being priced, not a franchise in decline.

Bear Case

Broker revenue is a percentage of premium, and premium is falling. Commercial rates kept falling through 2026 (−5% in Q1, −6% in Q2, −5% in Q3; global property −10% in Q3), and Guy Carpenter's property-cat index fell 16% at the July 2026 renewal — the steepest in 25 years. Reinsurance broking is already shrinking (−6% underlying in Q3, −2% in Q2). Underlying growth has fallen from 9% in 2023 and 7% in 2024 to 4–5%, yet the PEG ratio is 2.39. The reported growth optics flatter the business: FY2025's +10.3% was almost entirely McGriff, and that roll-up was funded with cash and debt, leaving $22.3bn of debt including leases, 1.50x debt-to-equity and a 1.09 current ratio. Fiduciary income — pure-margin interest on client money — is in mechanical runoff as rates fall. Add $569m of Greensill charges through nine months, $222m of restructuring in FY2025 (and $182m more so far in 2026), $215m of integration costs and a rebrand that retired franchise names like Guy Carpenter and Mercer, and the sell side's 65% Hold weighting looks like the right call.

01
A prolonged soft P&C market. Commissions scale with premium. If rates keep falling at 5–6% a year through 2027, exposure growth and new business will not fully offset, and underlying growth could break below 3% — at which point a 17x forward multiple on a low-single-digit grower is no longer cheap. 02
Leverage constrains the roll-up. $22.33bn of debt including leases against $1.84bn of cash and an enterprise value $20.5bn above market cap means deleveraging, the $5.5bn capital-return target and continued M&A are competing for the same cash. Earnings quality erosion. The GAAP-to-adjusted gap is wide: $569m of Greensill charges through nine months, $222m of Thrive (FY2025) plus $182m so far in 2026, $215m of McGriff integration (FY2025), and fiduciary income down $9m year-over-year in Q3. TTM GAAP EPS of $8.35 sits below FY2025's $8.43 despite revenue growth (+7% for nine months). If the market stops crediting adjusted numbers, the effective multiple is materially higher than 22.6x.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Matters Right Now
The P&C Pricing Cycle Has Turned — and Brokers Get Paid on Premium
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the whole debate, and it deserves to be stated plainly: an insurance broker's commission is a percentage of the premium it places, so when premium rates fall, broker revenue per unit of risk falls with them. The 2019–2023 hard market — driven by catastrophe losses, social inflation and constrained capacity — handed MMC a multi-year tailwind: underlying growth was 9% in 2023 and 7% in 2024. That has reversed. Marsh's own global rate index showed commercial rates down 5% in Q1 2026 (the seventh consecutive quarterly decline), 6% in Q2 and 5% in Q3, with global property down 10% in Q3 and only casualty still rising (+1%). Reinsurance is worse: Guy Carpenter's global property-catastrophe rate-on-line index fell 16% at the July 1, 2026 renewal, which management called the steepest year-over-year decline observed in 25 years, and Reinsurance (Guy Carpenter) revenue duly fell 2% underlying in Q2 and 6% in Q3. Brokers are not defenceless — exposure growth, rising insured values, new client wins and higher-value advisory work all offset — which is why MMC still printed 5% underlying growth in Q2 and Q3 rather than a decline. But the mechanism is real, it is currently running against the company, and it is why underlying growth has slowed to 4–5% and why 15 of 23 analysts sit on Hold.
Consolidation: the Roll-Up Is the Growth Strategy
ONGOING HEADWIND
Insurance broking is consolidating hard, and the numbers show why: the five largest listed brokers generate $77.9bn of the $96.8bn earned by all 24 listed brokers — 80% of the public universe. MMC alone is 29% of it. Consolidation is genuinely accretive here: scale improves carrier negotiating leverage, spreads compliance costs, and lets acquirers buy sub-scale agencies at lower multiples than their own. MMC paid $7.75bn for McGriff in late 2024 for ~$1.3bn of revenue and 3,500 people; Arthur J. Gallagher (+30.8% TTM revenue growth) and Brown & Brown (+34.6%) are running the same playbook even harder. The headwind is what it costs and what it hides. Reported growth flattered by acquisitions masks decelerating organic growth; MMC's FY2025 +10.3% reported versus +4% underlying is exactly that. And the debt is permanent while the revenue is not guaranteed: $20.5bn of borrowings ($22.3bn including leases), 1.50x debt-to-equity, $215m of integration costs in FY2025 alone. Management's own commentary on the Q2 call — that H1 2026 M&A slowed on gaps between strategic and financial-sponsor valuations — suggests private-equity competition is now setting the clearing price for the very assets MMC needs to keep buying.
Rate Cuts Cut Both Ways — Fiduciary Income vs. the Multiple
EMERGING TAILWIND
Brokers hold client premium in fiduciary accounts between collection and remittance and keep the interest earned. In a 5% policy-rate world that was a windfall — $403m in FY2025, effectively 100%-margin. As rates fall it reverses: Q3 2026 fiduciary interest income was $100m, down $9m from a year earlier, with roughly $94m guided for Q4. Every 100bp of cuts removes a meaningful, high-quality slice of pre-tax income with no offsetting cost. Bull framing: falling rates also lift the present value of a long-duration, recurring, 0.58-beta cash stream, and MMC currently trades at 16.8x forward earnings against 20.3x for the S&P 500 — a defensive compounder derating into a rate-cutting cycle is historically an attractive setup, and the $4.15bn of free cash flow plus a 10%-growing dividend gets paid while you wait. Bear framing: fiduciary income runoff, a soft P&C cycle and a shrinking reinsurance unit are three independent headwinds hitting the same P&L simultaneously, while $22.3bn of debt including leases limits the buyback that would otherwise cushion EPS. The resolution depends almost entirely on one number — whether underlying revenue growth holds at 4–5% or slips toward 3%.
