01 Company Overview

Marsh & McLennan is the world's largest insurance and reinsurance broker and one of its largest professional-services firms, operating in 130 countries through two reported segments: Risk and Insurance Services (Marsh Risk in insurance broking, Guy Carpenter in reinsurance broking) and Consulting (Mercer in health, wealth and career; Oliver Wyman in management consulting). Fiscal 2025 revenue was $26.98 billion, up 10.3% YoY on a reported basis but only +4% on an underlying (organic, constant-currency, acquisition-adjusted) basis — the gap is almost entirely the McGriff acquisition. Adjusted operating margin expanded to 27.1% from 26.8%, and adjusted EPS rose 9% to $9.75.

The business model is asset-light and fee-based: brokers earn commissions calculated as a percentage of the insurance premium placed, plus fees. That makes revenue recurring and capital-light — but it also means the direction of property-and-casualty (P&C) insurance pricing feeds straight through to organic growth. With commercial rates down 6% in Q2 2026 and global property down 12%, this is the central debate on the stock. At $90.3 billion of market capitalisation MMC ranks roughly #272 globally and #196 in the United States by market value, and it is by a wide margin the largest company in the listed Insurance Brokers industry (~30% of the group's $89.4 billion of combined revenue).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOURCES OF MOAT
GLOBAL SCALE — 130 COUNTRIES PROPRIETARY PLACEMENT DATA STICKY RENEWAL BOOK REGULATORY & LICENSING COMPLEXITY CROSS-SELL ACROSS 4 BUSINESSES SERIAL M&A ROLL-UP MACHINE ASSET-LIGHT, NO UNDERWRITING RISK

MMC's moat is distribution scale layered on top of information asymmetry. A broker that places tens of billions of dollars of premium across 130 countries sees pricing, terms and claims outcomes no single insurer or client can see, and that data advantage is what lets Marsh Risk defend commission rates when carriers push back. Renewal retention in commercial broking is structurally high — the switching cost of moving a complex global insurance programme mid-cycle is real, and the incumbent broker controls the placement history. The roll-up strategy is itself part of the moat: MMC has spent $7.75bn on McGriff (2024) and £4.3bn on Jardine Lloyd Thompson (2019), plus a continuous stream of tuck-ins — Astera in Spain closed July 1, 2026, with Altmark M and an alternatives-manager deal pending — each acquired at a smaller multiple than MMC's own and immediately plugged into a larger carrier-negotiation footprint. Because the model requires no underwriting capital, incremental revenue converts at high margin: ROE of 25.9% and ROIC of 14.5% on a business generating $4.77 billion of free cash flow. That recurring, capital-light cash stream is why brokers have historically traded at a premium multiple to the broader financials sector — and why the current 23.2x trailing / 17.2x forward P/E, below the 29.0x industry weighted average, reads as a cycle derating rather than a moat impairment.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Commission revenue is levered to P&C premium rates. Brokers get paid a percentage of premium. Commercial insurance rates fell 5% in Q1 2026 — the seventh consecutive quarterly decline — and 6% in Q2 2026, with global property down 12%. Guy Carpenter's property-cat rate-on-line index fell 16% at the July 2026 renewal. Rate declines do not automatically shrink revenue (exposure growth and new business offset), but they remove the tailwind that drove 7–8% organic growth in 2023–24 down to ~4% now. This is the single most important variable in the model.

2. Leverage taken on for McGriff. Total debt stands at $22.38 billion against $1.70 billion of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.45 and enterprise value of $110.97 billion versus $90.29 billion of market cap. The company also carries a current ratio of only 1.14 and a quick ratio of 0.53. Deleveraging competes directly with the $5.5 billion 2026 capital-deployment target and with further M&A.

3. Fiduciary investment income declines with rates. Brokers hold client premium in fiduciary accounts and keep the interest. That income was $403 million in FY2025 and $88 million in Q2 2026 — down $11 million year-over-year — with management guiding to roughly $95 million in Q3. Every Fed cut erodes a high-margin, zero-cost revenue line that flattered 2023–25 earnings.

4. Integration, restructuring and legal drag. FY2025 carried $215 million of McGriff integration costs and $222 million of "Thrive" restructuring charges; Q2 2026 added $58 million of Thrive charges ($103 million year-to-date). Separately, a $425 million Greensill-related charge was recorded in Q1 2026. The 2027 consolidation of all four businesses under the single Marsh brand adds execution and brand-equity risk to businesses (Oliver Wyman, Mercer) whose names carry independent value.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

The gap between MMC's GAAP GAAP results and its adjusted results is unusually wide right now, and every component is identifiable:

Greensill charge — $425 million, Q1 2026. A legal/settlement charge tied to the long-running Greensill Capital matter, recorded in the first quarter. This is the single largest driver of the GAAP TTM EPS of $8.17 sitting below the FY2025 GAAP EPS of $8.43 despite revenue growth, and it explains the depressed Q1 2026 GAAP operating margin of 24.34%.

McGriff integration costs — $215 million in FY2025. Non-recurring integration spend associated with folding a 3,500-person, $1.3bn-revenue broker into Marsh McLennan Agency. Management has not published a formal synergy target on the calls reviewed.

"Thrive" restructuring — $222 million in FY2025, $58 million in Q2 2026 ($103 million year-to-date). A three-year programme targeting savings through automation and operating-model change. It is the explicit engine behind the guided margin expansion; treat the charges as recurring-while-the-programme-runs rather than truly one-off.

Currency and acquisition effects. The 10-point spread between FY2025 reported revenue growth (+10.3%) and underlying growth (+4%) is almost entirely acquisition-driven. Any assessment of the business should anchor on underlying growth; reported growth will decelerate sharply as McGriff annualises out of the comparison — visible already in Q2 2026, where reported (+6%) and underlying (+5%) had nearly converged.

Fiduciary interest income — $403 million FY2025. Not a one-off, but worth flagging as a quality-of-earnings item: it is pure interest on client money, it carries no cost, and it is now shrinking ($88m in Q2 2026, −$11m YoY).

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 suggest the stock has run hot and may be due for a pause; readings below 30 suggest it has been beaten down and may be due for a bounce. It says nothing about whether the business is good — only about the recent balance of buying and selling pressure.

Five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 close of $151.43 to $189.20 on August 19, 2026, MMC returned +24.9% on price alone over five years — roughly 4.6% annualised before dividends, with the current yield at 2.09%. That headline number badly understates the round trip inside it. The stock compounded steadily from 2022 through 2024, more than doubling off the September 2022 low of $149.29 to an all-time-area high of $244.03 in March 2025, as the hard P&C market delivered 7–8% organic growth and rising rates inflated fiduciary income. It then fell 34.4% peak-to-trough to $159.97 in May 2026 as the pricing cycle turned, reinsurance rates collapsed, fiduciary income began rolling off and the market repriced a 4%-organic-growth broker. The last three months have retraced roughly 18% of that decline. Net of everything: the entire five-year gain was earned in 2023–24 and half of it has been given back.

Relative performance. Over the trailing twelve months MMC's market capitalisation fell 11.4% and the share price fell 8.89%. Within the listed broker group that is middle-of-the-pack: Willis Towers Watson −3.4%, Aon −6.6%, MMC −11.4%, Arthur J. Gallagher −12.6%, Brown & Brown −23.9% (all market-cap changes over twelve months). The whole industry derated together, which is the strongest evidence that this is a cycle event, not a company-specific problem. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the world's largest insurance broker — an asset-light, no-underwriting-risk toll booth on global commercial premium — at 23.2x trailing and 17.2x forward earnings, below both the 29.0x broker-industry weighted average and the 29.7x S&P 500. The model still throws off $4.77bn of free cash flow at 94% conversion, earns 25.9% ROE and 14.5% ROIC, and pays a 2.09% yield growing 10% a year for the 17th consecutive year. Margin expansion is real and structural, not cyclical: FY2025 adjusted margin of 27.1% (up from 26.8%) rose again to 29.3% in Q2 2026 on the back of the Thrive programme, and management guides to more. Meanwhile Consulting — 36% of revenue and uncorrelated to P&C pricing — is accelerating, with Oliver Wyman up 13% underlying in Q2, its fastest in over two years. Every listed broker derated together, which argues this is a cycle trough being priced, not a franchise in decline.

Bear Case

Broker revenue is a percentage of premium, and premium is falling. Commercial rates have now declined for seven-plus consecutive quarters (−5% in Q1 2026, −6% in Q2, global property −12%), and Guy Carpenter's property-cat index fell 16% at the July 2026 renewal — the steepest in 25 years. Reinsurance broking is already shrinking (−2% underlying in Q2). Underlying growth has halved from 7–8% to ~4%, yet the PEG ratio is 2.58. The reported growth optics flatter the business: FY2025's +10.3% was almost entirely McGriff, and that roll-up was funded with cash and debt, leaving $22.4bn of debt, 1.45x debt-to-equity and a 1.14 current ratio. Fiduciary income — pure-margin interest on client money — is in mechanical runoff as rates fall. Add a $425m Greensill charge, $222m of restructuring, $215m of integration costs and a full rebrand of four storied franchises in 2027, and the sell side's 65% Hold weighting looks like the right call.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
A prolonged soft P&C market. Commissions scale with premium. If rates keep falling at 5–6% a year through 2027, exposure growth and new business will not fully offset, and underlying growth could break below 3% — at which point a 17x forward multiple on a low-single-digit grower is no longer cheap. Watch the January 1, 2027 reinsurance renewal as the first read.
02
Leverage constrains the roll-up. $22.38bn of debt against $1.70bn of cash and an enterprise value $20.7bn above market cap means deleveraging, the $5.5bn capital-return target and continued M&A are competing for the same cash. Management has already flagged that H1 2026 deal activity slowed on valuation gaps — the acquisition engine that has papered over slowing organic growth may not be available at the same pace.
03
Earnings quality erosion. The GAAP-to-adjusted gap is wide and widening: $425m Greensill, $222m Thrive (FY2025), $215m McGriff integration, plus fiduciary income declining $11m year-over-year in a single quarter. TTM GAAP EPS of $8.17 sits below FY2025's $8.43 despite 8.3% revenue growth. If the market stops crediting adjusted numbers, the effective multiple is materially higher than 23x.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Matters Right Now
The P&C Pricing Cycle Has Turned — and Brokers Get Paid on Premium
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the whole debate, and it deserves to be stated plainly: an insurance broker's commission is a percentage of the premium it places, so when premium rates fall, broker revenue per unit of risk falls with them. The 2019–2023 hard market — driven by catastrophe losses, social inflation and constrained capacity — handed MMC a multi-year tailwind that produced 7–8% underlying growth. That has reversed. Marsh's own global rate index showed commercial rates down 5% in Q1 2026 (the seventh consecutive quarterly decline) and 6% in Q2 2026, with global property down 12% and only casualty still rising (+2%). Reinsurance is worse: Guy Carpenter's global property-catastrophe rate-on-line index fell 16% at the July 1, 2026 renewal, which management called the steepest year-over-year decline observed in 25 years, and Guy Carpenter revenue duly fell 2% underlying in Q2. Brokers are not defenceless — exposure growth, rising insured values, new client wins and higher-value advisory work all offset — which is why MMC still printed 5% underlying growth in Q2 rather than a decline. But the mechanism is real, it is currently running against the company, and it is why underlying growth has halved and why 15 of 23 analysts sit on Hold.
Consolidation: the Roll-Up Is the Growth Strategy
ONGOING HEADWIND
Insurance broking is consolidating hard, and the numbers show why: the five largest listed brokers generate $76.4bn of the $89.4bn earned by all 22 listed brokers — 85% of the public universe. MMC alone is 30% of it. Consolidation is genuinely accretive here: scale improves carrier negotiating leverage, spreads compliance costs, and lets acquirers buy sub-scale agencies at lower multiples than their own. MMC paid $7.75bn for McGriff in late 2024 for ~$1.3bn of revenue and 3,500 people; Arthur J. Gallagher (+30.8% TTM revenue growth) and Brown & Brown (+34.6%) are running the same playbook even harder. The headwind is what it costs and what it hides. Reported growth flattered by acquisitions masks decelerating organic growth; MMC's FY2025 +10.3% reported versus +4% underlying is exactly that. And the debt is permanent while the revenue is not guaranteed: $22.4bn of borrowings, 1.45x debt-to-equity, $215m of integration costs in FY2025 alone. Management's own commentary — H1 2026 M&A slowed on "gaps" between strategic and financial-sponsor valuations — suggests private-equity competition is now setting the clearing price for the very assets MMC needs to keep buying.
Rate Cuts Cut Both Ways — Fiduciary Income vs. the Multiple
EMERGING TAILWIND
Brokers hold client premium in fiduciary accounts between collection and remittance and keep the interest earned. In a 5% policy-rate world that was a windfall — $403m in FY2025, effectively 100%-margin. As rates fall it reverses: Q2 2026 fiduciary interest income was $88m, down $11m year-over-year, with roughly $95m guided for Q3. Every 100bp of cuts removes a meaningful, high-quality slice of pre-tax income with no offsetting cost. Bull framing: falling rates also lift the present value of a long-duration, recurring, 0.58-beta cash stream, and MMC currently trades at 17.2x forward earnings against a 29.7x index — a defensive compounder derating into a rate-cutting cycle is historically an attractive setup, and the $4.77bn of free cash flow plus a 10%-growing dividend gets paid while you wait. Bear framing: fiduciary income runoff, a soft P&C cycle and a shrinking reinsurance unit are three independent headwinds hitting the same P&L simultaneously, while $22.4bn of debt limits the buyback that would otherwise cushion EPS. The resolution depends almost entirely on one number — whether underlying revenue growth holds at 4–5% or slips toward 3%.
