01 Company Overview

Munich Re is the insurer's insurer. Its biggest business is reinsurance: when an insurer in Florida, Tokyo or Sydney wants to cap what a hurricane, earthquake or a bad run of life claims can cost it, Munich Re takes a slice of that risk for a slice of the premium. Around that core sit a specialty-insurance arm (Global Specialty Insurance, which includes the US cyber-focused specialist HSB), a large primary insurer in Germany and Europe (ERGO), and an asset manager (MEAG); the group's investment portfolio stood at €223.2bn on September 30. In 2025 the group booked €60.4bn of insurance revenue and a record €6.1bn net result (€47.15 per share), the fifth year in a row it beat its own profit guidance.

The 2026 story has moved from a paradox to a test the company has so far passed. Profits are at records — €5.667bn in the first nine months, up 9.5% from €5.176bn — and in the third quarter Munich Re earned €1.742bn against the €1.479bn analysts expected (the mean of 13 analysts' estimates cited by Munich Re), then raised its 2026 profit target from €6.3bn to €6.6bn. The shares closed November 20 at €580.00, 3.2% above the €562.20 they ended 2025 at and 14.0% above the €508.60 of the previous edition (October 2). The cycle is still the question. Reinsurance is priced once a year at "renewals", and in 2026 prices have slipped at every one: −2.5% in January, −3.1% in April, −5.5% in July (risk-adjusted). Munich Re has answered by walking away from business it considers underpriced — the volume it wrote fell 7.8%, 18.5% and 9.1% at those three renewals — and its 2026 group revenue outlook of €62bn, cut from €64bn in August, was left unchanged in November. The poker read: this is a player folding more hands because the pots have got smaller, and for now the market is treating that as discipline rather than shrinkage.

The third quarter also put the "luck" argument to a test. Catastrophe losses came back — major losses were €646m, 14.9% of premium against 2.9% a year earlier — yet stayed under the 18% the company budgets for, so the property-casualty reinsurance combined ratio (claims and costs as a share of premium; below 100% means underwriting alone made money) rose to 80.0% from 62.7% and the quarter's profit still beat expectations.

The two numbers that matter for a reinsurer

A reinsurer earns money twice — once on underwriting (pricing risk above its eventual cost) and once on investing the premiums until claims are paid. And it must hold capital against the day both go wrong.

Solvency II Ratio (Sep 30, 2026)
301%
Up from 298% at year-end 2025 (304% at June 30). Solvency II requires 100%; Munich Re's own target is above 200%. At 301% it holds roughly three times the regulatory requirement — the spare capital that funds the €2.25bn buyback and the dividend. Hannover Re reported 242% at September 30.

Major-loss expenditure was €646m in Q3 — 14.9% of net insurance revenue against an expected 18% — of which €514m natural catastrophes and €132m man-made. Q2 was 4.9% and Q1 3.5%, so the quarter was the first in 2026 to land near budget. One large hurricane or earthquake closes the gap quickly.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Capital strength (Solvency II 301%) Scale & diversification across perils and regions Risk expertise since 1880 Long client relationships €223bn investment portfolio (MEAG) Primary-insurance ballast (ERGO)

A reinsurer sells a promise to pay in the worst year its client will ever have, so the product is the balance sheet. Clients want a large, well-capitalised counterparty that will still be there after the worst event. Munich Re's scale lets it spread risk across perils, regions and lines that rarely go wrong together, which is why it can hold three times the regulatory capital and still return roughly €5bn a year to shareholders (the €3.0bn dividend paid in 2026 plus the €2.25bn buyback). The vulnerability is that the moat protects the franchise, not the price: in a soft market, competitors' capital competes on price, and even the biggest player has to choose between writing less or earning less on what it writes. In 2026 Munich Re has chosen to write less.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The pricing cycle has turned. Risk-adjusted prices fell at all three 2026 renewals, by 5.5% in July. Jefferies cut its target on September 28 on the view that reinsurance prices had been falling faster (it raised it from €550 to €555 on November 18 after the Q3 numbers). Property-casualty reinsurance revenue was down 9.0% in the first nine months, and the reinsurance revenue outlook was cut from €40bn to €38bn in August.
02
2026 profits are flattered by catastrophe losses running below budget. Major losses ran at 4.2% of premium in the first half and 14.9% in Q3, against an 18% budget; the Q3 combined ratio already rose to 80.0% from 62.7%. Management's own normalised combined ratio for Q2 (its adjustment for catastrophe losses at their expected level and other one-offs) was about 82.0% against the reported 68.9%. A heavier fourth quarter — or one large US hurricane or earthquake — would take reported margins further toward that level, and Munich Re said it may strengthen its balance sheet further in Q4 depending on major-loss developments.
03
Currency and markets move the result. Munich Re carries large US-dollar exposure and reports in euros; the 2025 currency result was −€1,425m, chiefly from the weak dollar, and currency translation cut reported revenue again in 2026. Q2's investment result leaned on €1,174m of fair-value gains, which can reverse when equity markets fall.
04
Growth is being bought. Ambition 2030 depends on specialty and primary insurance growing faster than reinsurance shrinks. ERGO NEXT (2025) and At-Bay ($575m, closing 2027) are tech-heavy US acquisitions; integration and pricing risk in unfamiliar lines is the price of diversification.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the 2026 figures. First, catastrophe luck: major losses in property-casualty reinsurance were €130m in Q1, €191m in Q2 and €646m in Q3 (€118m in Q3 2025), all below the 18% budget; Q1 also carried about €90m of claims from the Iran war (about €60m in Global Specialty Insurance, €30m in property-casualty reinsurance). Second, tax: the Q3 effective tax rate was 27.6% against 32.9% a year earlier (Q2: 19.0% against 27.2%), and FY2025 carried a one-off charge at ERGO Germany from the cut in German corporation tax from 2028. Third, currency: the currency result swung from −€189m in Q3 2025 to +€74m in Q3 2026 (−€602m to −€2m in Q2), so part of the year-on-year profit comparison is the absence of last year's dollar losses rather than better business. The buyback also lifts per-share figures: the share count in circulation fell from 127,961,889 at year-end to 125,814,467 at June 30, and the programme has bought back a further 491,051 shares since October 2 on our arithmetic (3,434,311 shares in total by November 18, against 2,943,260 by October 2).

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 20, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of €236.90 to the November 20, 2026 close of €580.00, Munich Re's share price rose 144.8%; with dividends reinvested the return was 198.5%, against 62.5% for the DAX (itself a dividends-included index). The shape has three parts. From the July 2022 low of €221.10 to the April 2025 month-end high of €601.60 the stock compounded almost without pause, rising in 24 of those 33 months. From there it went sideways-to-down, to €452.30 at the May 2026 month-end. Since then it has recovered: the October 30 month-end close was €567.00, and November 20 sits 3.6% below that April 2025 month-end peak (the intraday high on November 5 was €602.80), with profits at records.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 11.1 times trailing earnings and 2.2 times book for a group earning a 21.9% return on equity, holding three times its regulatory capital, and returning about €5bn a year — a 4.1% dividend yield plus a buyback worth another 3.1% of the market value. Q3 profit beat consensus by about 18% even with catastrophe losses back, the €6.6bn target is 86% achieved after nine months, and management is walking away from underpriced business rather than chasing volume, which is what protects margins through a soft market. Meanwhile the less cyclical businesses are growing: life & health technical result €1,521m in nine months, ERGO profit €819m, and At-Bay adds a cyber franchise. If January 2027 pricing holds, as Munich Re expected at the half-year, the stock still looks reasonable against its own earnings power.

Bear Case

The cycle has turned and the numbers are flattered. Renewal prices fell 2.5%, 3.1% and 5.5% through 2026, property-casualty reinsurance revenue fell 9.0% in the first nine months, and the revenue outlook was cut by €2bn in August. Reported margins rest on catastrophe losses running below budget — Q3's 14.9% of premium was under the 18% budget, and in Q2 the normalised combined ratio was about 82% against the reported 68.9%. When losses return to budget while prices keep falling, profit growth stalls, and Ambition 2030's 8% EPS growth target depends on buybacks and acquisitions. After a 14% rise since early October the average analyst target in this report (about €582) is barely above the share price and nine of twelve firms rate it Hold or Neutral, so the market already pays for much of the good news.

Top 3 Risks

01
A large catastrophe. Munich Re budgets 18% of property-casualty reinsurance premium for major losses and spent 4.2% in the first half and 14.9% in Q3. A major US hurricane or earthquake would put the full-year target and the buyback pace back in play; for scale, the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires cost about €0.8bn on their own.
02
Falling reinsurance prices. Three negative renewals in 2026, and Jefferies' September note, which cut its target on faster price declines (it raised it slightly after the Q3 numbers). If January 2027 brings another cut of the July size, the €38bn reinsurance revenue figure becomes a ceiling and the normalised margin narrows.
03
Markets and the dollar. Q2's investment result was lifted by €1,174m of fair-value gains, and the 2025 currency result was −€1,425m, chiefly from the weak dollar. An equity sell-off or another leg down in the dollar would hit reported profit even with good underwriting.
Industry Commentary
The reinsurance cycle is softening
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

After big premium increases in earlier years, reinsurance cover for primary insurers has been getting gradually cheaper since early 2025, dpa-AFX reported from the industry's September gathering. Munich Re's risk-adjusted prices fell 2.5% in January, 3.1% in April and 5.5% in July 2026. At that Rendez-Vous de Septembre meeting in Monte Carlo, dpa-AFX reported that the three market leaders had accepted average price declines of about 5% since the start of 2026, with similar pressure expected into 2027. Munich Re's response — write less, keep the margin — is the textbook answer for a capital-rich player, but it shrinks the top line. The half-year release put it this way: "Overall, the price level for Munich Re’s portfolio remains good, despite a 5.5% decline."

Frequent "mid-sized" catastrophes
ONGOING HEADWIND

The losses that hurt primary insurers are increasingly hail, wildfire and severe convective storms rather than single giant hurricanes. dpa-AFX's report from Monte Carlo cited industry losses of about $104bn from below-catastrophe events in 2025, and Munich Re raised its major-loss budget to 18% of premium for 2026 from 17%. The first half of 2026 was light for Munich Re — €54m of natural-catastrophe major losses in Q2 — and the third quarter was heavier: €514m of natural-catastrophe major losses against a €47m release a year earlier, though total major losses (14.9% of premium) stayed under the 18% budget. The budget increase is management's own signal that the long-run loss level is rising.

Diversification as the answer to the cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND

Munich Re's strategy is to make the cycle matter less: Ambition 2030 grows the share of profit from life & health reinsurance, specialty insurance and ERGO, which Jurecka calls "rising profit contributions from our less volatile business segments." The pieces are moving — the €4bn longevity transaction, ERGO NEXT in the US, At-Bay in cyber, and control of ERGO China Life. In the first nine months these three businesses produced €2.64bn of the €5.67bn net result (47%), already close to half. The bull framing: a more stable earnings stream deserves a higher multiple than a pure reinsurer gets. The bear framing: diversification by acquisition into US insurtech is how reinsurers have destroyed value before, and the payoff will not be visible until well after the cycle has played out.
