01 Company Overview

Munich Re is the insurer's insurer. Its biggest business is reinsurance: when an insurer in Florida, Tokyo or Sydney wants to cap what a hurricane, earthquake or a bad run of life claims can cost it, Munich Re takes a slice of that risk for a slice of the premium. Around that core sit a specialty-insurance arm (Global Specialty Insurance, which includes the US cyber-focused specialist HSB), a large primary insurer in Germany and Europe (ERGO), and an asset manager (MEAG); the group's investment portfolio stood at €225.2bn on June 30. In 2025 the group booked €60.4bn of insurance revenue and a record €6.1bn net result (€47.15 per share), the fifth year in a row it beat its own profit guidance.

The 2026 story is a paradox. Profits are at records — €3.925bn in the first half, up from €3.178bn, and the second quarter beat the analyst consensus that Munich Re itself collected by about 24% — yet the shares closed October 2 at €508.60, 9.5% below the €562.20 they ended 2025 at. The reason is the cycle. Reinsurance is priced once a year at "renewals", and in 2026 prices have slipped at every one: −2.5% in January, −3.1% in April, −5.5% in July (risk-adjusted). Munich Re has answered by walking away from business it considers underpriced — the volume it wrote fell 7.8%, 18.5% and 9.1% at those three renewals — and in August it cut its 2026 group revenue outlook from €64bn to €62bn while keeping the €6.3bn profit target. The poker read: this is a player folding more hands because the pots have got smaller, and the market is deciding whether that is discipline or shrinkage. This year's profits have also been helped by a very quiet first half for catastrophes, which flatters the numbers.

The two numbers that matter for a reinsurer

A reinsurer earns money twice — once on underwriting (pricing risk above its eventual cost) and once on investing the premiums until claims are paid. And it must hold capital against the day both go wrong.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Capital strength (Solvency II 304%) Scale & diversification across perils and regions Risk expertise since 1880 Long client relationships €225bn investment portfolio (MEAG) Primary-insurance ballast (ERGO)

A reinsurer sells a promise to pay in the worst year its client will ever have, so the product is the balance sheet. Clients want a large, well-capitalised counterparty that will still be there after the worst event. Munich Re's scale lets it spread risk across perils, regions and lines that rarely go wrong together, which is why it can hold three times the regulatory capital and still return roughly €5bn a year to shareholders (the €3.0bn dividend paid in 2026 plus the €2.25bn buyback). The vulnerability is that the moat protects the franchise, not the price: in a soft market, competitors' capital competes on price, and even the biggest player has to choose between writing less or earning less on what it writes. In 2026 Munich Re has chosen to write less.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The pricing cycle has turned. Risk-adjusted prices fell at all three 2026 renewals, by 5.5% in July. Jefferies cut its target on September 28 on the view that reinsurance prices have recently been falling faster. Property-casualty reinsurance revenue was down 15.3% in the first half, and the reinsurance revenue outlook was cut from €40bn to €38bn in August.
02
2026 profits are flattered by a quiet first half for catastrophes. Major losses ran at 4.2% of premium in the first half against an 18% budget. Management's own normalised combined ratio for Q2 was about 82.0% against the reported 68.9%. A normal second half — or one large US hurricane — would bring reported margins back toward that level.
03
Currency and markets move the result. Munich Re carries large US-dollar exposure and reports in euros; the 2025 currency result was −€1,425m, chiefly from the weak dollar, and currency translation cut reported revenue again in 2026. Q2's investment result leaned on €1,174m of fair-value gains, which can reverse when equity markets fall.
04
Growth is being bought. Ambition 2030 depends on specialty and primary insurance growing faster than reinsurance shrinks. ERGO NEXT (2025) and At-Bay ($575m, closing 2027) are tech-heavy US acquisitions; integration and pricing risk in unfamiliar lines is the price of diversification.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the 2026 figures. First, catastrophe luck: major losses in property-casualty reinsurance were €130m in Q1 and €191m in Q2, far below budget; Q1 also carried about €90m of claims from the Iran war (about €60m in Global Specialty Insurance, €30m in property-casualty reinsurance). Second, tax: the Q2 effective tax rate was 19.0% against 27.2% a year earlier, and FY2025 carried a one-off charge at ERGO Germany from the cut in German corporation tax from 2028. Third, currency: the currency result swung from −€602m in Q2 2025 to −€2m in Q2 2026, so part of the year-on-year profit growth is the absence of last year's dollar losses rather than better business. The buyback also lifts per-share figures: the share count in circulation fell from 127,961,889 at year-end to 125,814,467 at June 30.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of €236.90 to the October 2, 2026 close of €508.60, Munich Re's share price rose 114.7%; with dividends reinvested the return was 161.7%, against 65.3% for the DAX (itself a dividends-included index). The shape has two halves. From the July 2022 low of €221.10 to the April 2025 month-end high of €601.60 the stock compounded almost without pause, rising in 24 of those 33 months. Since then it has gone sideways-to-down: October 2 sits 15.5% below that April 2025 peak, even though the 2025 and first-half 2026 profits were records.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows the change of regime. It spent most of 2023 and early 2024 above 70, peaking at 89.9 in November 2023; it touched 27.3 at the May 2026 low, the only reading below 30 in the series, and has since recovered to 47.6 — neutral. The 10-month moving average, about €511, sits just above the price: the stock has regained its May losses but not yet turned the trend.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 9.5 times trailing earnings and 1.9 times book for a group earning a 23% return on equity, holding three times its regulatory capital, and returning about €5bn a year — a 4.7% dividend yield plus a buyback worth another 3.6% of the market value. Profit beat consensus by about 24% in Q2, the €6.3bn target is 62% achieved by mid-year, and management is walking away from underpriced business rather than chasing volume, which is what protects margins through a soft market. Meanwhile the less cyclical businesses are growing: life & health technical result €1,028m in the half, ERGO profit up, and At-Bay adds a cyber franchise. If January 2027 pricing holds, as Munich Re expects, the stock looks cheap against its own earnings power.

Bear Case

The cycle has turned and the numbers are flattered. Renewal prices fell 2.5%, 3.1% and 5.5% through 2026, property-casualty reinsurance revenue fell 15.3% in the half, and the revenue outlook has been cut by €2bn. Reported margins rest on a first half in which major losses cost a fraction of budget — normalised, Q2's 68.9% combined ratio was about 82%. When losses return to normal while prices keep falling, profit growth stalls, and Ambition 2030's 8% EPS growth target depends on buybacks and acquisitions. A Hold consensus with targets clustered just above the price says the market sees limited upside until the cycle turns.

Top 3 Risks

01
A large catastrophe. Munich Re budgets 18% of property-casualty reinsurance premium for major losses and spent 4.2% in the first half. A major US hurricane or earthquake would put the full-year target and the buyback pace back in play; for scale, the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires cost about €0.8bn on their own.
02
Falling reinsurance prices. Three negative renewals in 2026 and an analyst (Jefferies) cutting his target on faster price declines. If January 2027 brings another cut of the July size, the €38bn reinsurance revenue figure becomes a ceiling and the normalised margin narrows.
03
Markets and the dollar. Q2's investment result was lifted by €1,174m of fair-value gains, and the 2025 currency result was −€1,425m, chiefly from the weak dollar. An equity sell-off or another leg down in the dollar would hit reported profit even with good underwriting.
Industry Commentary
The reinsurance cycle is softening
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

After big premium increases in earlier years, reinsurance cover for primary insurers has been getting gradually cheaper since early 2025, dpa-AFX reported from the industry's September gathering. Munich Re's risk-adjusted prices fell 2.5% in January, 3.1% in April and 5.5% in July 2026. At that Rendez-Vous de Septembre meeting in Monte Carlo, dpa-AFX reported that the three market leaders had accepted average price declines of about 5% since the start of 2026, with similar pressure expected into 2027. Munich Re's response — write less, keep the margin — is the textbook answer for a capital-rich player, but it shrinks the top line. The half-year release put it this way: "Overall, the price level for Munich Re’s portfolio remains good, despite a 5.5% decline."

Frequent "mid-sized" catastrophes
ONGOING HEADWIND

The losses that hurt primary insurers are increasingly hail, wildfire and severe convective storms rather than single giant hurricanes. dpa-AFX's report from Monte Carlo cited industry losses of about $104bn from below-catastrophe events in 2025, and Munich Re raised its major-loss budget to 18% of premium for 2026 from 17%. The first half of 2026 was light for Munich Re — €54m of natural-catastrophe major losses in Q2 — and the half-year report noted severe wildfires in Southern Europe since July whose cost could not yet be estimated. The budget increase is management's own signal that the long-run loss level is rising.

Diversification as the answer to the cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND

Munich Re's strategy is to make the cycle matter less: Ambition 2030 grows the share of profit from life & health reinsurance, specialty insurance and ERGO, which Jurecka calls "rising profit contributions from our less volatile business segments." The pieces are moving — the €4bn longevity transaction, ERGO NEXT in the US, At-Bay in cyber, and control of ERGO China Life. In the first half these three businesses produced €1.83bn of the €3.93bn net result (47%), already close to half. The bull framing: a more stable earnings stream deserves a higher multiple than a pure reinsurer gets. The bear framing: diversification by acquisition into US insurtech is how reinsurers have destroyed value before, and the payoff will not be visible until well after the cycle has played out.
