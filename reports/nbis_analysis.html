01 Company Overview

Nebius is what the market now calls a "neocloud": it buys Nvidia GPUs by the tens of thousands, houses them in data centers it builds or leases, and rents the compute to AI labs and hyperscalers under multi-year contracts. It is the rump of Yandex — the Russian search company sold its Russian businesses in 2024, kept the Dutch parent, a Finnish data center and a few venture bets, and relisted on Nasdaq as Nebius on October 21, 2024. FY2025 revenue was $529.8M, up 479% from $91.5M in FY2024; trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $1.36B, up 507%, and the company exited Q2 2026 at a $3.0B annualized run-rate. At $62.05B of market capitalization it ranks roughly #447 globally (companiesmarketcap.com, est.), which is remarkable for a business that generated $35M of quarterly revenue two years ago.

The stock does not trade on earnings — it has none that matter — it trades on contracted capacity and the cost of funding it. Microsoft signed for $17.4B (up to $19.4B with options) in September 2025; Meta added $3B in November 2025 and then $12B firm plus up to $15B more in March 2026; Nvidia bought an 8.3% stake for $2B the same month. Contracted backlog now exceeds $40B. The debate this page works through is the neocloud debate itself: whether renting depreciating GPUs financed with $10.2B of debt, $6B of customer prepayments and a $2.8B-a-quarter share-issuance program ever returns more than its cost of capital, or whether Nebius is simply a leveraged, concentrated pass-through for Nvidia's product cycle. The clue that it is not settled: ROIC is −4.1%, and 24.2% of the float is sold short.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

$40B+ contracted backlog Nvidia allocation + 8.3% Nvidia stake In-house server & rack design Secured power (5 GW contracted target) Customer prepayments fund 50–60% of capex Full software stack (cloud, inference, MLOps) European data-sovereignty positioning

The honest description of a neocloud moat is that it is time and access, not technology. Nebius's advantage is that it has power, land, Nvidia allocation and anchor contracts today, while demand for Blackwell-class capacity exceeds supply — the capacity auction clearing 15% above any prior Blackwell price is the evidence. Its genuine differentiators versus the other neoclouds are vertical integration (it designs its own hardware, which management credits for the 50% core EBITDA margin) and the prepayment structure: roughly 70% of Q2 2026 deals came with upfront cash covering 50–60% of the associated capex, which shortens payback to a stated 1 year 10 months and pushes financing risk onto investment-grade customers. That is a better balance sheet model than CoreWeave's ($51.6B of debt on $7.6B of revenue), and it is why Nebius's debt-to-equity is 0.99 rather than 10.3.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Customer concentration. Microsoft and Meta together account for something like $34–46B of a $40B+ backlog (the ranges overlap because the Meta conditional tranche is "up to"). Both are building their own data centers at hundreds of billions of dollars a year; both rent from neoclouds to bridge timing gaps. When the bridge is no longer needed, renewals reprice — and Microsoft's contract runs only five years.
▸
GPU depreciation life. D&A was 45% of Q2 revenue. If Vera Rubin obsoletes Blackwell faster than the 5–6 year lives the industry assumes, every neocloud's EBITDA margin is overstated and the residual value assumption behind asset-backed lending weakens. Contracts of 1–3 years against assets depreciated over 5–6 years is a duration mismatch the market currently forgives.
▸
Funding dependence. Capex guidance is $20–25B for 2026 against revenue guidance of $3–3.4B. That gap is filled by $9B+ of prepayments, $10.2B of debt (interest expense was $119M in Q2 alone), and equity: shares outstanding rose 22% year-on-year and the ATM program still has 12.3M shares of authorisation. If the share price falls, the cheapest funding source gets expensive precisely when it is needed.
▸
Hyperscaler pricing power. Oracle, AWS, Azure and Google can bundle GPU capacity with software and credits, and can accept lower returns on compute as a customer-acquisition cost. Short-term neocloud pricing of $40–50M/MW reflects scarcity, not a durable price. When supply catches up — and Nvidia is investing to make sure it does — mid-term rates of $20–25M/MW are the ceiling, not the floor.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The neocloud arithmetic, using only management's own numbers. Start with the unit: one megawatt of contracted capacity yields $20–25M of annual revenue on a 1–3 year contract. Management says the core business runs at a ~50% adjusted EBITDA margin and that a deal now pays back in 1 year 10 months. Payback of 22 months at 50% EBITDA margin implies roughly $18–23M of capex per MW — call it a 1:1 ratio of capex to first-year revenue. Cross-check at the company level: the 2026 guide is $20–25B of capex for a year-end ARR of $7–9B, which is closer to $2.5–3.0 of capex per dollar of run-rate revenue — worse than the per-deal math, because capacity is being built ahead of the contracts that will fill it. Take the midpoint, about $2 of capex per $1 of revenue, and a 50% EBITDA margin gives an EBITDA yield on invested capital of ~25%.

Now subtract what EBITDA ignores. If a GPU cluster is depreciated over six years, depreciation alone is ~17% of the capex per year; over four years it is 25%. That is why D&A was 45% of Q2 revenue even with capacity still filling. So the 25% EBITDA yield becomes an EBIT yield of roughly 0–8%, depending entirely on the useful-life assumption — before interest expense of $119M a quarter on $10.2B of debt, before ~$100M a quarter of stock compensation, and before tax. Management's own medium-term target is a 20–30% EBIT margin; on $2 of capital per $1 of revenue that is a 10–15% pre-tax ROIC, which clears a reasonable cost of capital for a business with investment-grade counterparties and prepaid contracts — but only if pricing holds through the Blackwell-to-Rubin transition and utilisation stays high after the current contracts roll. Today's reported ROIC is −4.1%. The stock at 8.4× forward sales is pricing the target, not the present.

What makes Nebius different from CoreWeave — and what does not. The prepayment model is real: ~$6B of deferred revenue, $9B+ of expected 2026 prepayments, and customers funding 50–60% of the capex behind their own contracts. That is why Nebius carries $10.2B of debt against CoreWeave's $51.6B, and why its debt-to-equity is 0.99 versus 10.27. It is also why Nebius's operating cash flow (+$5.26B TTM) looks healthy while free cash flow is −$5.88B: the prepayments arrive as cash before the GPUs are delivered. What is not different is the exposure. Both companies rent one supplier's product to a handful of customers who are also building their own supply, both depend on capital markets staying open — Nebius issued 12.7M shares for $2.8B in a single quarter and grew its share count 22% in a year — and both are valued on backlog that converts to revenue only as fast as utilities connect power.

Concentration, stated plainly. Of the $40B+ backlog, Microsoft accounts for up to $19.4B and Meta up to $27B. Those two names are between roughly 85% and 100% of the disclosed backlog depending on how the conditional Meta tranche is counted. The four "landmark" Q2 deals averaging over $1B each and the named smaller customers (Reflection, Cohere, a quant firm) are the diversification story, and it is early. A five-year Microsoft contract signed in September 2025 comes up for renewal in 2030, when Rubin-successor GPUs will be two generations on and Microsoft's own data centers will be built. The price at which that renews is unknowable, and it is the single most important number for the terminal value.

Hidden assets, and what they are worth. The ~25% ClickHouse stake is carried at roughly $3.75B on the January 2026 round (derived estimate); the Toloka stake and Avride are additional. Together they might be worth 6–8% of the market cap — enough to matter in a drawdown, not enough to underwrite the valuation. They also account for every dollar of positive GAAP net income in the last two years.

Bull Case

Nebius is the best-financed neocloud: $40B+ of investment-grade backlog, 50% core EBITDA margins from vertically integrated hardware, customer prepayments covering half of capex, a debt-to-equity of 0.99 while CoreWeave's is 10×, and Nvidia as an 8.3% shareholder guaranteeing allocation. Revenue is guided to grow ~6× in 2026 to $3.0–3.4B with ARR reaching $7–9B; capacity auctions clearing 15% above prior highs say pricing power is rising, not falling. Execute to the 20–30% EBIT target and the stock at 8.4× forward sales is a growth-adjusted bargain against $28B of cumulative 2026–28 revenue analysts already forecast.

Bear Case

ROIC is −4.1%, free cash flow is −$5.9B, D&A consumes 45% of revenue and interest another 20%, and the only GAAP profit in two years came from marking up a database company. Capex of $20–25B against $3–3.4B of revenue is funded by a share count growing 22% a year, $10B of debt and prepayments from two customers who are building their own supply. Contracts run 1–3 years; GPUs are depreciated over 5–6. With 24% of the float short, a $271 spread in analyst targets and a 32-month gap in its own price history, this is a leveraged option on Nvidia's product cycle trading at 45× enterprise value to sales.

Top 3 Risks

01
Funding window closes. The model needs roughly $20–25B a year of external and customer capital. A 30% drawdown in the stock (three have happened in 23 months) raises the cost of ATM equity and convertible funding exactly when capex commitments are fixed. The Altman Z-score of 1.49 is in the distress zone on a mechanical reading — misleading for a prepaid business, but a reminder that the balance sheet is a bet on continuity.
02
GPU useful life proves shorter than modelled. Rubin's arrival in 2027 will set the market price of Blackwell capacity. If mid-term rates fall from $20–25M/MW toward the low teens as supply catches up, six-year depreciation schedules turn into write-downs and the 50% EBITDA margin becomes an accounting artefact.
03
Anchor renewal risk and concentration. Microsoft and Meta are up to ~100% of disclosed backlog; both are the largest builders of their own AI data centers on earth. Hyperscalers rent neocloud capacity to bridge timing gaps, and bridges get dismantled. A non-renewal or repricing in 2029–2030 would reset the terminal value the current multiple assumes.
  Industry Commentary
Power, Not GPUs, Is the Binding Constraint
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every neocloud can buy Nvidia chips if it pays; none can conjure a grid connection. Nebius exited 2025 with 170 MW connected and is guiding 800 MW–1 GW by year-end 2026 — a five-to-six-fold increase in twelve months that depends on utilities, permits and, increasingly, on behind-the-meter generation ("hundreds of megawatts" secured, Bloom fuel cells at Vineland after a local hearing was adjourned without a vote). The 5 GW contracted-power target is the right metric to watch because it is the one the company cannot buy its way to. Miss the low end of connected power and ARR misses with it, regardless of demand.
Hyperscalers Are Customers Today and Competitors by Design
ONGOING HEADWIND
Microsoft and Meta rent from Nebius, CoreWeave and Oracle because their own build-outs cannot keep pace with model demand. That is a timing arbitrage, and it is why neocloud contracts run one to five years rather than fifteen. Oracle's OCI has shown the hyperscaler version of the same trade — accept thin compute margins to win the workload. The sector's revenue is therefore real but its duration is short: the market is paying 8–12× forward sales across the group for cash flows whose renewal terms will be set by the customers, in 2028–2030, in a market with far more supply. Nebius's prepayment structure improves the funding of that bet; it does not change its nature.
Inference Is Where Neoclouds Become Software
EMERGING TAILWIND
Training clusters are rented by the megawatt; inference is sold by the token, to many more customers, on stickier integrations. Nebius's "Token Factory" and its capacity auction are early attempts to move from one product (dedicated GPU time) toward a marketplace with pricing power — the auction clearing 20% above Blackwell pipeline pricing is the first data point. Bull framing: if inference demand diversifies the customer base beyond two hyperscalers and lifts utilisation on ageing GPUs, the depreciation problem shrinks and the multiple migrates toward software. Bear framing: inference is exactly where AWS, Azure and Google can bundle and subsidise, and where the ClickHouse-sized valuations of AI labs — Nebius's other customers — are most exposed if the model-economics boom cools. The −4.1% ROIC is the number that has to move before either framing is proven.
