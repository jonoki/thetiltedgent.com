01 Company Overview

Nasdaq, Inc. is no longer primarily a stock exchange. It describes itself as "a technology company that serves capital markets and other industries," and the reported numbers back the description: in the second quarter of 2026, the trading business — Market Services — produced $340M of the company's $1,500M in net revenue, roughly 23%. The other 77% came from index licensing, listings, data, anti-financial-crime software, regulatory reporting technology and capital-markets infrastructure software. Full-year 2025 net revenue was $5,249M, up 12.9% from $4,649M in 2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 30, 2026 is $5,614M, up 14.2%. At a $51.43B market capitalisation the company ranks #315 globally by market value.

A point of order, because the ticker invites confusion: NDAQ is listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market but is not a member of the Nasdaq-100 index, which excludes financial companies by construction — so the company that owns and licenses the index is not in it. The benchmark used throughout this page is the S&P 500, with the Nasdaq-100 referenced only as a business driver. The central debate is this: Nasdaq has spent the last several years converting a cyclical, volume-dependent exchange into a recurring-revenue software and index business, and the market has awarded it a P/E of 26.8× against a peer median near 22.8×. The question is whether the recurring mix justifies that premium, or whether the $8.76B of debt and the gap between 57% non-GAAP and 47% GAAP operating margins mean investors are paying a software multiple for an acquisition-assembled one.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
NASDAQ-100 BRAND $1.114T INDEX ETP AUM $3.26B ARR BASE REGULATORY LICENCE LISTINGS NETWORK EFFECT PROPRIETARY MARKET DATA BANK SWITCHING COSTS

The core advantage is not the trading venue — order flow is fickle and venues compete on fractions of a cent. It is that Nasdaq owns a brand that funds flow into. The Nasdaq-100 is one of a small handful of index brands worldwide that retail and institutional money will buy by name, and the fee on it is a basis-point charge against $1.114 trillion of tracking assets that cost essentially nothing more to service as they grow. That is why Index revenue grew 38% while Data & Listing Services grew 10% and Workflow & Insights grew 5% in the same quarter. Layered underneath is a second, less glamorous moat: $3.26B of ARR in compliance-critical software sitting inside banks' regulatory and trade-processing stacks — systems that are audited, examined and painful to rip out. Neither moat depends on winning a price war for order flow.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Index revenue is a leveraged bet on equity market levels. A basis-point fee on $1.114T of ETP assets falls when those assets fall, and it falls without any operational failure by the company. The line that grew 38% and drove most of the segment's outperformance is the same line that would contract fastest in a drawdown — and because it carries near-100% incremental margin, the operating-profit impact is amplified in both directions.
02
Leverage from an acquisition-built software segment. Total debt was $8.76B against $520M of cash at June 30, 2026, with a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.78 and a current ratio of 1.01. The company repaid a net $162M in Q2 while also spending $530M on dividends and buybacks — deleveraging is real but deliberate rather than urgent. ROIC of 10.43% against ROE of 16.52% is the visible signature of that borrowed capital.
03
A 10-point GAAP-to-adjusted margin gap. Q2 2026 GAAP operating margin was 47%; non-GAAP was 57%. GAAP diluted EPS was $0.89 (+14%) while non-GAAP EPS was $1.07 (+25%). Almost every valuation you will see quoted on this name uses the larger number. The forward P/E of 21.02× versus the trailing 26.83× is that same gap expressed as a multiple.
04
Regulatory dependence cuts both ways. Exchange status is a licence granted by the SEC, and market-data pricing, access fees and the consolidated-tape economics are all subject to regulatory review. The same rulemaking that creates demand for Regulatory Technology can compress the economics of Market Services and proprietary data. Nasdaq is unusually exposed to the direction of US market-structure policy in both directions at once.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 split-adjusted monthly close of $69.96 to the September 10, 2026 close of $92.01, NDAQ appreciated +31.5% over five years — roughly 5.6% a year in price alone, plus a dividend currently yielding 1.35%. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the mix, because that is the whole argument. Of $1,500M of Q2 2026 net revenue, $340M — 22.7% — came from trading. The rest came from index licensing, listings, data, and software sold to banks. Total ARR is $3,258M, split $1,870M in Financial Technology and $1,388M in Capital Access Platforms, growing 11% reported and 12% organic, with annualised SaaS revenue at 38% of ARR growing 15% organically. Against TTM net revenue of $5,614M, that ARR base covers roughly 58% of the company's revenue on a contracted, recurring footing. That is the number that separates Nasdaq from a pure exchange and it is the number that justifies a 26.8× trailing P/E against a 22.8× peer median.

Now the part that complicates it. The fastest-growing revenue line in the company is Index, at +38%, and Index is not really recurring in the software sense — it is a basis-point fee on $1.114 trillion of exchange-traded product assets. It recurs as long as the assets remain and the market does not fall. Strip Index out and Capital Access Platforms grew at Data & Listing's 10% and Workflow & Insights' 5%. So the segment carrying the "recurring, high-quality revenue" banner is disproportionately driven by the one line most levered to equity market levels. The bull case and the cyclicality live in the same place.

The valuation, on three bases. (i) On trailing GAAP EPS of $3.43, NDAQ trades at 26.8× — the fully-loaded number, charging the company for the amortisation of what it paid to build the Financial Technology segment. (ii) On 2026 consensus EPS of $4.14, it trades at 22.2×, and the published forward P/E of 21.02× implies a slightly higher or later earnings base. (iii) On trailing free cash flow of $1.95B, it trades at about 26× equity value, or roughly 31× on the $60.19B enterprise value — the EV multiple is the honest one, because it charges for the $8.76B of debt that the equity multiple ignores. Against a peer set trading at 22.0–23.2× (ICE, CME, Cboe) with MSCI at 30×, Nasdaq's premium is real but modest, and the direction of travel — toward MSCI's index-and-data profile and away from ICE's exchange profile — is the implicit bet.

What the balance sheet is doing. $8.76B of debt against $520M of cash and $27.34B of total assets, a 0.78 debt-to-equity ratio, ROE of 16.52% against ROIC of 10.43%. In the quarter, management sent $530M to shareholders and $162M to lenders. That is a deliberate statement: the leverage is regarded as comfortable rather than pressing. It is also why the forward multiple looks cheaper than the trailing one — deleveraging plus amortisation roll-off mechanically lifts GAAP EPS over time without any operating improvement. Investors who like the forward number should be clear that a meaningful part of it is financial, not operational.

Bull Case

The mix shift is done and it worked: 77% of revenue now comes from outside trading, $3.26B of ARR covers roughly 58% of revenue on a contracted basis, and all three segments grew double digits in Q2 2026 with Financial Technology at +18% for the half. Index ETP assets crossed $1.114 trillion with $109B of twelve-month inflows, SpaceX chose Nasdaq for the quarter's largest IPO, and non-GAAP operating margin of 57% shows the operating leverage is real. Fourteen of seventeen analysts rate it Buy with a $109.87 average target, 19.4% above the current price, and short interest of 1.86% of float says almost nobody is betting against it.

Bear Case

You are paying a 17% P/E premium to exchange peers for a recurring-revenue story whose fastest-growing component — Index at +38% — is a basis-point fee on $1.114T of assets that will shrink with the market, not a subscription. Ex-Index, the rest of Capital Access Platforms grew 5–10%. GAAP operating margin is 47% against the 57% everyone quotes, and GAAP EPS grew 14% against the 25% non-GAAP figure; the difference is permanent amortisation from an acquisition-built segment that also left $8.76B of debt, a 1.01 current ratio and ROIC six points below ROE. The stock has returned 8.41% annualised over five years — behind Cboe's 19.25% and CME's 12.23% — and is down 3.02% over the past year.

Top 3 Risks

01
Index AUM is the single point of leverage. A basis-point fee on $1.114 trillion carries near-100% incremental margin on the way up and near-100% decremental margin on the way down. A 20% equity drawdown would cut the fastest-growing, highest-margin revenue line by roughly the same proportion, with no offsetting cost relief — and would do so in exactly the quarter that listings and index flows also dry up. The three exposures are correlated, not diversifying.
02
The multiple depends on the non-GAAP bridge holding. At 26.8× trailing GAAP earnings versus a 22.8× peer median, Nasdaq is priced as a software-and-data company. If ARR growth decelerates from the current 11–12% toward the 5–10% growth rates in Data & Listing Services and Workflow & Insights, the premium has no support. No earnings miss is required for that de-rating — a single soft ARR print would do it.
03
Regulatory and market-structure risk on both sides of the business. Exchange status, market-data pricing and access-fee economics are all subject to SEC rulemaking, and the same policy environment that creates demand for Regulatory Technology can compress the margins in Market Services and proprietary data. With a 1.01 current ratio and $8.76B of debt, the company has less balance-sheet cushion to absorb an adverse ruling than its 0.97 beta implies.
  Industry Commentary
Passive Flows Are the Exchange Industry's Real Growth Engine
EMERGING TAILWIND
The structural shift from active management to index products has turned index ownership into the most valuable franchise in capital-markets infrastructure — better than trading, better than data. Nasdaq's index ETP assets crossed $1.114 trillion with $109 billion of net inflows in twelve months, and the resulting Index revenue of $271M grew 38% while the rest of its segment grew 5–10%. The economics are extraordinary: a basis-point fee that requires no additional headcount as assets grow. This is the same dynamic that supports MSCI's 30× P/E, the highest in the peer group. The catch is that it is a fee on a stock of assets, so it inherits the market's cycle wholesale.
The IPO Window Reopened — and the Quality Bar Moved
ONGOING HEADWIND
Q2 2026 brought 68 Nasdaq IPOs, of which only 26 were operating companies — the balance largely vehicles and funds. That ratio is the quiet story: headline listing counts recovered faster than the underlying pipeline of real businesses. And listings monetise thinly in any case: Data & Listing Services revenue grew just 10% in the quarter that produced SpaceX's $86 billion IPO, the largest of the period. A marquee listing is worth more as a franchise signal — index inclusion candidacy, data volume, credibility with the next issuer — than as a revenue line. Exchanges compete hard for listings they barely profit from directly.
Everyone Is Buying the Same Software Business
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Exchange operators have spent the past several years converting trading franchises into financial-software companies, and they have done it with debt. Nasdaq carries $8.76B of borrowings against $520M of cash, an enterprise value $8.76B above its market cap, and a 10-point gap between 47% GAAP and 57% non-GAAP operating margin that is essentially permanent acquisition amortisation. Bull framing: the strategy worked — 77% of revenue now sits outside trading, ARR of $3.26B covers ~58% of revenue, and the company can deleverage out of $1.95B of annual free cash flow while still returning $530M a quarter to shareholders. Bear framing: every peer bought the same kind of asset, which means the differentiation investors are paying a 17% P/E premium for is not obviously proprietary — and the only fully unique asset in the portfolio, the Nasdaq-100 brand, is levered to the very market cycle the software was supposed to diversify away from.
