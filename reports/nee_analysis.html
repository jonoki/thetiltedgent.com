01 Company Overview

NextEra Energy is a two-engine electric power company: Florida Power & Light (FPL), a vertically integrated regulated utility serving roughly 6 million customer accounts across Florida, and NextEra Energy Resources (NEER), the largest competitive developer, owner and operator of wind, solar and battery storage assets in North America. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $27.41B, up 10.74% year over year, with TTM revenue through June 2026 of $28.70B (+10.8%).

At a $179.84B market capitalization NextEra is the largest utility in the United States by market value — roughly 70% larger than #2 Southern Company ($106.2B) — and ranks #67 among all US public companies and #119 globally. The company has raised its dividend for 30 consecutive years and currently yields 2.89%. In May 2026 NextEra announced a proposed combination with Dominion Energy, a transaction management expects to close in the second half of 2027 and which would lift the pro-forma adjusted EPS growth target to 9%+ annually through 2032.

FPL is the regulated engine: a vertically integrated Florida utility that earns a regulated return on invested capital ("rate base"). Regulatory capital employed grew approximately 9.3% YoY in Q2 2026 — the single most important number in the utility half of the story, because a regulated utility's earnings grow roughly in line with rate base times allowed return. Q2 2026 segment net income was $1.412B ($0.67/share) versus $1.275B ($0.62) a year earlier, +10.7% YoY. Capital expenditure was $2.8B in the quarter with $12–13B expected for full-year 2026.

In November 2025 the Florida Public Service Commission unanimously approved a four-year base rate agreement covering 2026 through 2029, negotiated with a coalition of customer groups rather than litigated — a materially de-risked outcome that removes rate-case uncertainty through the end of the decade. The typical 1,000-kWh residential bill rises about 2% in 2026 (to $136.64 from $134.14), with management guiding to roughly 2% annual bill increases through decade-end. Residential bills sit ~30% below the national average, non-fuel O&M is over 70% better than the industry average, and reliability is top-decile (~59–60% better than national average). That cost advantage is the political capital that lets FPL keep spending.

The newest growth vector is large load: FPL raised its target from 6 GW to 8 GW of large-load (principally data center) demand by 2032, having identified ~21 GW of interest with ~12 GW in advanced discussions and potential service beginning 2028. Each gigawatt is projected to require roughly $2B of capital expenditure — i.e. 8 GW implies on the order of $16B of incremental rate base. Management expects to announce at least one large-load transaction by year-end 2026.

NEER develops, builds, owns and operates competitive (non-rate-regulated) wind, solar, battery storage, nuclear and gas generation, selling output under long-term PPAs (power purchase agreements) to utilities, corporates and hyperscalers. The signed-contract backlog is the headline growth metric: NEER added 3.6 GW of new originations in Q2 2026 — including 2 GW of battery storage — and, after placing 1.1 GW into service, ended the quarter with a backlog of approximately 35.1 GW. That backlog is essentially a pre-sold multi-year earnings stream; it converts to revenue as projects reach commercial operation.

Q2 2026 GAAP segment net income was $1.634B ($0.78/share) versus $983M ($0.48) a year prior, but that figure is inflated by non-qualifying hedge mark-to-market. On an adjusted basis NEER earned $1.291B ($0.62/share) versus $1.091B ($0.53), +18.3% YoY — the cleaner read.

Strategically NEER is repositioning from a pure renewables developer into an all-of-the-above generation supplier for AI load growth. Management describes four origination channels and a base case of 15 GW of new generation for large load by 2035, with upside of 30 GW or more; a "power hub" program has ~30 potential sites in discussion, targeting 40 by year-end. On nuclear, NextEra secured the final 30% minority interest in the Duane Arnold plant (becoming sole owner), received an Iowa Utilities Commission generating certificate, and targets return to service no later than Q1 2029. On gas, management has been explicit about the boundary: "You're not going to see us participating in LNG" — the gas strategy is vertical integration and marketing (Symmetry acquisition), not export infrastructure. NEER also completed a 137-mile, 345-kV transmission line in New Mexico ahead of schedule and budget, and was selected by MISO as a consortium partner on two large-scale 765-kV transmission projects in Illinois.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
REGULATED MONOPOLY (FL) 4-YR RATE DEAL → 2029 LOWEST-COST OPERATOR 35.1 GW BACKLOG SUPPLY-CHAIN SCALE INTERCONNECTION QUEUE POSITION 30-YR DIVIDEND STREAK A-TIER CAPITAL ACCESS

NextEra's moat is genuinely two moats bolted together, and they reinforce each other. FPL is a legal monopoly — no competitor can serve its territory — operating in one of the fastest-growing states in the country (90,000+ customer additions in a single quarter; 335,000 expected by decade-end). Because FPL's non-fuel O&M is over 70% better than the industry average and its bills sit ~30% below the national average, it enjoys unusual regulatory goodwill; that goodwill is what produced a negotiated, coalition-backed four-year rate settlement in November 2025 rather than a contested rate case. Regulatory predictability through 2029 is worth real multiple points in a sector where adverse commission rulings are the main idiosyncratic risk.

NEER's moat is scale in a business where scale is the whole game. Being the largest buyer of turbines, panels and batteries in North America produces procurement pricing peers cannot match; two decades of development produces land positions, interconnection queue slots and permitting relationships that cannot be bought; and a 35.1 GW signed backlog means the growth is contracted, not hoped-for. Critically, the two halves share a balance sheet: FPL's regulated, rating-agency-friendly cash flows support the group's cost of capital, and cheap capital is the binding constraint on renewables development. The 30-year dividend growth record is both a signal of that stability and a source of a sticky, yield-oriented shareholder base.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Federal clean-energy tax policy has already turned against the core product. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, enacted July 4, 2025), wind and solar projects that begin construction more than twelve months after enactment — i.e. after July 4, 2026 — must be placed in service by December 31, 2027 to claim §45Y production credits or §48E investment credits. That is a brutally short window for utility-scale development. A July 7, 2025 Executive Order further directed Treasury to strictly enforce termination and to narrow the historically generous 5%-of-cost "beginning of construction" safe harbor. Layered on top, Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) rules deny credits to projects with disqualifying Chinese, Russian, Iranian or North Korean ownership, debt or "material assistance" content — material-assistance rules bite for projects starting construction after December 31, 2025, and the supply chain for solar modules and batteries is heavily China-linked. The economics of post-2027 wind and solar development are structurally worse than they were in 2024.

2. Balance sheet leverage and rate sensitivity. Total debt is $110.2B against $2.87B of cash; debt-to-equity is 1.62, current ratio is 0.53, and free cash flow is negative $16.57B on $13.8B of operating cash flow, because the company is spending far more than it earns to build rate base and backlog. That model works beautifully when capital is cheap and works badly when it is not — the 2023 de-rating was precisely this lesson. Enterprise value ($287.2B) is 60% larger than market cap; debtholders own a large share of the asset base.

3. Concentration in a single, physically exposed jurisdiction. Two-thirds of revenue comes from one state. Florida carries hurricane risk (storm restoration costs recovered through regulatory mechanisms, but with timing and political friction), a single utility commission whose composition can change, and a customer-growth story that depends on continued in-migration. A hostile PSC after 2029, or a severe storm season colliding with an unpopular bill increase, is the concentrated downside.

4. Execution and integration risk on the Dominion combination. The proposed Dominion Energy transaction — filed with state and federal regulators July 15, 2026, with Virginia running a statutory six-month review and closing targeted for the second half of 2027 — is the largest strategic bet in the company's history. It brings multi-jurisdiction regulatory risk, a $2.25B shareholder-funded customer bill credit commitment, and a long deal-uncertainty window during which NEE trades partly as merger arbitrage rather than on fundamentals. The stock fell roughly 11% in the month the deal was announced.

Notable One-Off Items

Hedging mark-to-market is the dominant distortion, and it is large. In Q2 2026 NextEra reported GAAP net income of $3.144B ($1.50/share) against adjusted earnings of $2.407B ($1.15/share) — a $737M favorable non-recurring gap, or 23% of reported GAAP profit. NextEra excludes the mark-to-market on non-qualifying hedges (interest rate and commodity derivatives that do not receive hedge accounting) precisely because these swing violently with rates and power prices and reverse over time. Deriving from disclosed segment data: NEER contributed roughly $343M of that gap ($1.634B GAAP vs $1.291B adjusted) and Corporate & Other roughly $394M ($98M GAAP vs approximately −$296M adjusted). FPL, which does not carry a material hedge book, showed no adjustment.

The distortion runs in both directions across periods, which is why single-quarter GAAP comparisons are close to meaningless here. In FY2025 the sign flipped: GAAP EPS was $3.30 while adjusted EPS was $3.71 — a $0.41/share unfavorable GAAP gap, the mirror image of Q2 2026. Note that management's own 8%+ long-term growth target is explicitly measured "off a 2025 base of $3.71," i.e. off the adjusted number. Any valuation built on the TTM GAAP EPS of $4.45 (and therefore on the 19.38 trailing P/E) is overstating the underlying earnings power. The forward P/E of 20.95 — higher than trailing — is the market correctly anticipating that reversal.

Other items to watch, though not separately quantified in the Q2 2026 release reviewed here: storm cost recovery at FPL (Florida utilities recover hurricane restoration costs through regulatory mechanisms with a lag, creating quarter-to-quarter noise and a reserve amortization balance that management can flex within the rate agreement); asset sales and gains on disposal at NEER, historically a recurring source of "non-recurring" earnings; and the Duane Arnold minority-interest acquisition (final 30% stake), which is a purchase-accounting event rather than an operating result. The 2023–24 wind-down of the NextEra Energy Partners (NEP) yieldco relationship also produced several periods of impairment and restructuring noise; that structure is no longer the growth-funding vehicle it once was.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten down and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. NEE closed September 2021 at $69.17 and August 18, 2026 at $86.22 — a +24.6% price return over five years, or roughly +40% total return including the dividend stream (dividend contribution estimated at ~2.5–3%/yr; not independently verified). That is a poor five years in absolute terms for a company whose earnings grew throughout, and a dramatically worse one relative to the S&P 500, which compounded at a far higher rate over the same window on the back of mega-cap technology. The shape matters more than the endpoint: the stock made a cycle high of $82.60 in December 2021, spent two years de-rating to a low of $51.75 in February 2024 (−37% from the high), then rebuilt to a five-year high of $97.17 in April 2026 — a +88% recovery off the bottom — before giving back 11% on the Dominion announcement.

The de-rating and the recovery were both about the same variable: the discount rate. NEE is, in market terms, a long-duration bond with an equity ticker. When the 10-year Treasury repriced upward through 2022–23, a company funding a negative-$16B free cash flow build program with debt and equity was hit twice — once on the multiple, once on the cost of the growth itself. The 2024–26 recovery ran the same mechanism in reverse, amplified by the AI-power narrative. As of August 2026 price ($86.22) sits below both the 50-day ($87.14) and 200-day ($87.79) moving averages, and 6-period monthly RSI has cooled out of the overbought readings it printed in early 2026.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

NextEra owns both halves of the electricity trade at a moment when electricity demand is inflecting for the first time in two decades. FPL delivers ~9.3% regulated rate base growth under a four-year rate agreement locked through 2029, with 8 GW of large-load demand targeted by 2032 at roughly $2B of capex per gigawatt. NEER carries a 35.1 GW contracted backlog growing 3.6 GW a quarter, a restarting nuclear asset in Duane Arnold, and a base case of 15 GW of new large-load generation by 2035 with 30 GW+ upside. Management commits to 8%+ adjusted EPS growth through 2035 off a $3.71 2025 base — 9%+ if Dominion closes — plus ~10% dividend growth through 2026 and ~6%/yr thereafter, on a 30-year growth streak. At 19.4x trailing earnings the stock trades at a discount to a 21.4x utility sector, with a $98.37 consensus target implying 14% upside.

▼ Bear Case

The earnings quality is worse than the headline: TTM GAAP EPS of $4.45 embeds a $737M favorable hedge mark-to-market in Q2 alone, and the forward P/E of 20.95 sitting above the trailing 19.38 is the market pricing that reversal. Free cash flow is −$16.57B, debt is $110.2B against $2.87B cash, D/E is 1.62 and ROIC is 3.17% — a company that must continuously access capital markets to grow, in an era when it can no longer count on federal tax credits. Post-July-2026 construction starts must be in service by December 2027 to earn §45Y/§48E credits, and FEOC content rules threaten the supply chain regardless. Add eighteen months of Dominion deal uncertainty, a $55 low target on the Street, and a stock that is 12.7% below its April high and below both its 50- and 200-day moving averages.

Top 3 Risks
01
Clean-energy tax credit rollback. Wind and solar projects beginning construction after July 4, 2026 must be placed in service by December 31, 2027 to claim §45Y/§48E credits — a window too short for most utility-scale development — and FEOC material-assistance rules apply to projects starting after December 31, 2025. Treasury has been directed to narrow the traditional 5%-of-cost safe harbor. The value of NEER's post-2027 pipeline depends heavily on how much capacity was safe-harbored in time and how strictly the rules are read.
02
Interest rates and the funding model. With $110.2B of debt, negative $16.57B free cash flow and a 0.53 current ratio, NextEra is structurally dependent on cheap capital. The 2022–24 drawdown (−37% peak to trough with no deterioration in operations) is the empirical proof of how this stock behaves when the long end backs up. A renewed rise in long yields hits the multiple and the project economics simultaneously.
03
Dominion merger execution. An eighteen-month close window through H2 2027, multi-state regulatory review including Virginia's statutory six-month process, a $2.25B shareholder-funded bill-credit commitment, and integration risk on the largest transaction in company history. The 11% single-month drawdown on announcement shows the market's initial verdict; a blocked, delayed or repriced deal removes the 9%+ pro-forma growth math that the highest price targets on the Street depend on.
  Industry Commentary — Macro Themes That Matter Most Right Now
AI Data-Center Electricity Demand
EMERGING TAILWIND
US electricity demand is growing again after roughly two decades of flat load, and the marginal buyer is a hyperscaler with an urgent, price-insensitive need for firm power. The evidence in NextEra's own numbers is specific: ~21 GW of large-load interest identified at FPL, ~12 GW in advanced discussions, an official target raised from 6 GW to 8 GW by 2032, and roughly $2B of capital expenditure required per gigawatt — which converts demand directly into regulated rate base, the highest-quality earnings a utility can add. At NEER the framing is a base case of 15 GW of new generation for large load by 2035 with 30 GW+ upside, plus a power-hub program at ~30 sites targeting 40 by year-end 2026. The scarce commodity is not panels; it is interconnection, land and speed — precisely NextEra's accumulated advantages. Watch for the first announced large-load transaction, guided for year-end 2026, as the moment the narrative becomes a contract.
Clean-Energy Policy Reversal
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the largest quantifiable change to NextEra's operating environment in a decade, and it runs directly against the business that made the company. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (enacted July 4, 2025), wind and solar projects beginning construction after July 4, 2026 must be placed in service by December 31, 2027 to qualify for §45Y production or §48E investment tax credits — effectively an eighteen-month build clock on assets that routinely take longer. A July 7, 2025 Executive Order directed Treasury to "strictly enforce the termination" of the credits and to prevent circumvention of "beginning of construction" policy, signalling that the long-standing 5%-of-cost safe harbor will be read far more narrowly, with a requirement that "a substantial portion" of the facility actually be built. Separately, Foreign Entity of Concern rules deny credits where Chinese, Russian, Iranian or North Korean entities hold 25%+ equity, issued 15%+ of debt, appoint officers, or supply an impermissible share of "material assistance" — the last applying to projects starting construction after December 31, 2025, against a module and cell supply chain that is overwhelmingly China-linked. What survives comparatively intact: battery storage under §48E (unchanged eligibility timeline) and nuclear — which is precisely why 2 of the 3.6 GW added to backlog in Q2 2026 was storage, and why Duane Arnold matters more than its megawatts suggest. The strategic pivot is visible in the numbers; the question is whether it is fast enough.
Interest Rates & Utility Valuations — the Bull/Bear Fulcrum
ONGOING HEADWIND
Everything above resolves through the discount rate. With beta of 0.65, a 2.89% dividend yield and $110.2B of debt funding a negative-$16.57B free cash flow build program, NEE is a rate-duration instrument that happens to trade on an equity exchange. The bull framing: if long yields drift lower, the utility complex re-rates, NextEra's cost of capital falls exactly when its 35.1 GW backlog and 8 GW of large-load opportunity need funding, and an 8%+ adjusted EPS growth rate on a 30-year dividend streak becomes scarce and valuable — a $98–$116 target range follows naturally. The bear framing: if the long end stays high or rises, the same leverage that amplifies growth amplifies the damage, and 2022–24 is the template — a 37% drawdown with no operational deterioration whatsoever. Note the current setup: NEE trades at 19.4x trailing versus a 21.4x utility sector (as of Aug 17, 2026), a discount to a group it has historically led. Whether that discount is the market pricing merger risk and hedge-inflated earnings, or an entry point, is the actual investment question — and it will be settled by the yield curve and by whether the first large-load contract lands before year-end.
