Section 01

Company Overview

Netflix is the largest standalone paid streaming service on earth, delivering series, films, games and — increasingly — live events to members in roughly 190 countries, and it is now the most valuable pure-play entertainment company by a wide margin (~$305B versus Disney's ~$170B and Warner Bros. Discovery's ~$65B). Full-year 2025 revenue was $45.18 billion, up 15.9% YoY, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 30, 2026 stands at $48.37 billion, up 16.0%, with TTM net income of $13.65 billion (+33%) and free cash flow of $11.15 billion.

The paradox in front of investors is that operations have rarely looked better while the stock has rarely looked worse. Netflix is 45% below its June 2025 high on a split-adjusted basis and down about 37% over the trailing 52 weeks, even as EPS grew 35%. Essentially the entire drawdown is multiple compression: the trailing P/E has fallen from roughly 45x at the end of 2024 and 37x at the end of 2025 to 22.6x today, as revenue growth decelerated from 16% to 13.4% in Q2 2026 and the market began pricing streaming as a scaled, competitive, capital-hungry business rather than a land-grab. Netflix completed a 10-for-1 stock split on November 17, 2025; every per-share figure in this report is split-adjusted.

Notable Executives & Owners

Co-Chief Executive Officers
Greg Peters & Ted Sarandos

Peters (ex-COO and product/technology chief) owns advertising, pricing, paid sharing and the ads stack; Sarandos owns content and has run programming since the DVD era. Co-founder Reed Hastings moved to Executive Chairman in 2023, leaving this pair to steer the shift from subscriber growth to revenue-and-margin growth.

Chief Financial Officer
Spencer Neumann

CFO since 2019, previously CFO of Activision Blizzard and Disney Parks. He engineered the pivot from cash-burning content spend to durable free cash flow — from −$132M FCF in 2021 to $11.15B TTM — and has been running an aggressive buyback, including a record $4.7B repurchase in Q2 2026.

Key Institutional Investors
Vanguard, BlackRock, Capital Group

Ownership is overwhelmingly institutional — roughly 84.2% of shares are held by institutions versus only 0.58% insider ownership. The register is dominated by index complexes (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street) plus large active growth managers, which means the stock trades on flows and factor rotation as much as on fundamentals.

Section 03

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

Content Scale & Amortisation Global Distribution Brand Power Data & Personalisation IP Local Production Infrastructure Lowest Cost per Viewing Hour Emerging Two-Sided Ad Platform

Moat description. Netflix's advantage is arithmetic before it is creative. It spends roughly $17–18 billion a year on content and spreads that cost across a global membership base of roughly 300 million-plus households, which means its cost per subscriber-hour is structurally below any rival that monetises primarily in one country or one language. That lets it green-light a Korean, Spanish or Polish series at a local cost base, distribute it in 190 countries the same day, and earn a global return on a local budget — something Sky, Globo, or a U.S. network economically cannot do. Layered on top are two reinforcing assets: fifteen years of viewing data feeding a recommendation and commissioning engine that raises hit rates and lowers marketing cost, and a purpose-built global delivery network (Open Connect) that keeps streaming COGS falling as a share of revenue. The result is visible in the P&L: gross margin has gone from 39.4% in 2022 to 49.1% TTM and operating margin from 17.8% to 29.7%, while free cash flow went from $1.6B to $11.2B. Critically, this is not a network-effect or switching-cost moat — it is a scale-and-execution moat, which is more durable than a fad and less durable than a lock-in.

Key Vulnerabilities

1 — No structural switching costs

Cancelling Netflix takes about thirty seconds and costs nothing. There is no bundle, no hardware, no contract, no accumulated library the customer owns, and no social graph to abandon. The entire retention argument rests on the perceived value of the next slate. That works beautifully when hit rate is high, but it means a two-quarter content drought translates directly into churn in a way it never does for Amazon Prime (bundled with shipping) or Apple TV (bundled with hardware and services). It is also why price increases are the highest-risk lever management pulls.

2 — Content-cost and sports-rights inflation

The live-events push that makes the advertising business work also imports the worst economics in media. Sports rights are auctioned, non-exclusive over time, and inflate faster than subscription pricing — and every bidder (Amazon, Apple, YouTube, Disney/ESPN, Comcast) has a deeper or more diversified balance sheet than a single-product streamer. Netflix has so far bought scarcity rather than volume (Christmas NFL, boxing, WWE, a ~$200M Women's World Cup deal), but consensus 2027 EPS growth of just +6.4% against +11.3% revenue growth implies the street already expects a cost step-up that compresses incremental margin.

3 — Consolidation is building rivals with real scale

Paramount Skydance's $110 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery — currently paused by a federal judge on antitrust grounds, with the parties agreeing to hold until June 1, 2027 or resolution of the litigation and a possible trial in November 2026 — would combine HBO Max, CBS, Paramount+, and the WBD film library into a genuine second-scale player. Separately, Roku's pending acquisition by Fox (announced June 2026) puts a major distribution and CTV-ad platform inside a competitor. Netflix has spent a decade competing against subscale rivals; that is changing.

4 — Attention competition from free platforms, and disclosure opacity

Netflix's real competitor for hours is YouTube, which pays nothing for most of its content and already commands a larger share of U.S. TV time. Short-form video (YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Reels) is deflating the value of a viewing hour across the industry. Compounding this for investors, Netflix stopped reporting quarterly paid memberships in 2025 and does not break out advertising revenue — so the two metrics that would most directly validate or invalidate the growth story are no longer observable. Reduced disclosure tends to compress multiples, and arguably already has.

Notable One-Off Items

No large non-recurring charges — write-downs, litigation settlements, acquisition costs or tax windfalls — appear in the reported figures reviewed for this snapshot. Netflix has not made a transformational acquisition, so the P&L is unusually clean for a media company. Four comparability items are worth flagging anyway:

(i) The 10-for-1 stock split of November 17, 2025 makes every pre-November-2025 per-share quote non-comparable at face value; all figures here are split-adjusted. (ii) Buyback distortion: $9.92B of repurchases TTM shrank the share count 1.48% YoY, so roughly a point and a half of the 35.5% EPS growth is mechanical rather than operational. (iii) Content amortisation is an estimate, not a cash cost — Netflix's $33.8B content asset is amortised on management's forecast of viewing curves, so gross margin is more judgement-dependent than it is at a hardware or software company. (iv) Operating expense growth is running ahead of revenue: TTM technology & development +17.5%, marketing +18.0%, and general & administrative +22.7%, versus 13.4% revenue growth in the most recent quarter. If that gap persists, margin expansion stops.

RSI measures how much of a stock's recent movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 suggest the stock has risen a lot in a short time and may be "overbought"; readings below 30 suggest the reverse. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation tool — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time, and monthly RSI is a slow, big-picture signal rather than a trading trigger.

The five-year trajectory has been a round trip, twice. A $10,000 position bought in August 2021 at roughly $56.90 split-adjusted would be worth about $12,890 today — a +28.9% total return over five years, roughly 5.2% annualised, with no dividends. That number conceals two violent cycles: a 75% peak-to-trough collapse from November 2021 to June 2022 as pandemic-era subscriber growth reversed, then a 665% recovery from the June 2022 low of ~$17.49 to the June 2025 high of ~$133.80, and now a 45% drawdown back to $73.33. Netflix has spent the last five years being repeatedly re-rated rather than re-earned: revenue compounded at a steady 10–16% throughout, while the P/E oscillated between 22x and 54x. Investors who confused the multiple for the business got hurt in both directions.

Summary & Key Risks

BULL CASE

You are being offered the best business in entertainment — 49% gross margin, 30% operating margin, 49.5% ROE, 32.4% ROIC, $11.2B of free cash flow — at 22.6x trailing and 20.7x forward earnings, a ~30% discount to both the Entertainment industry average (32.0x) and the NASDAQ-100 (32.1x), for a company still growing revenue at 13–16% while its nearest large rival grows at 3.4% and two others lose money.

The advertising business is the unmodelled call option: not separately disclosed, plausibly ~$4B annualised and roughly doubling, in a $50–60B CTV market growing 15–18%. Management is buying the de-rating aggressively ($4.7B in Q2 alone, $9.92B TTM, ~1.5% of shares a year), and consensus still sees $94.33 — 28.6% above the current quote — with zero sell ratings across 51 analysts.

BEAR CASE

Growth is decelerating in a straight line — 15.9% (FY25) → 16.2% (Q1 26) → 13.4% (Q2 26) — while every operating expense line grows faster than revenue (tech +17.5%, marketing +18.0%, G&A +22.7%). Consensus 2027 EPS growth of just 6.4% on 11.3% revenue growth is the street conceding that margin expansion, which drove the entire 2023–2025 re-rating, is finished.

Meanwhile the moat is scale, not lock-in: churn costs the customer nothing, YouTube already takes more U.S. TV time and pays nothing for content, sports rights inflation is imported directly onto the P&L, and consolidation (Paramount Skydance–WBD at $110B, Fox–Roku) is assembling rivals with real depth. The stock has fallen 42% from its high and every one of those declines came with an analyst still rating it Buy — the ratings have not yet caught up to the targets.

Top 3 Risks

  1. Growth deceleration becomes permanent, not cyclicalTwo consecutive quarters of slowing revenue growth (16.2% → 13.4%) in a business that no longer discloses subscriber counts means investors cannot distinguish a pricing pause from genuine saturation. If growth settles below 10%, a 20x forward multiple is no longer cheap — it is fair, and there is no re-rating left to earn.
  2. Content and sports-rights cost inflation compresses marginThe live-events strategy that powers advertising is bought in competitive auctions against Amazon, Apple, YouTube, Disney and Comcast — all of whom can subsidise rights from other profit pools. Netflix cannot. Consensus 2027 EPS growth of 6.4% already assumes this bites; a worse outcome would break the 30% operating margin.
  3. Competitive consolidation and platform-layer captureA completed Paramount Skydance–WBD merger would create a scaled second player with HBO, CBS and the Warner library, while Fox's acquisition of Roku places CTV home screens, ad inventory and measurement inside a rival. Netflix has thrived against fragmented, subscale competition; it has never been tested against a peer with comparable content depth and superior distribution control.

Industry Commentary

Streaming's Consolidation Endgame

Critical Constraint

The Entertainment industry is 47 listed companies with $821B of combined market cap against $330B of combined revenue and a 7.79% aggregate profit margin — an industry that generates enormous revenue and almost no profit. Netflix alone is 37% of that market cap on 15% of the revenue. Everything else is consolidating to survive: Paramount Skydance's $110B bid for Warner Bros. Discovery is paused in antitrust litigation with a possible November 2026 trial and a standstill to June 1, 2027; Fox has agreed to acquire Roku; WBD's market cap is up 99.4% and Roku's 60.5% over the past year purely on deal news, while Disney is down 20.7% and Spotify down 25.3%. The market is paying for scarcity value in takeover targets and refusing to pay up for organic operators — and Netflix, at $305B, is too big to be acquired and has shown no appetite to be an acquirer.

Content Cost Inflation Meets the Attention Ceiling

Ongoing Headwind

Total television viewing time in developed markets is flat to declining; the only variables are share and price. That converts streaming into a zero-sum fight for hours in which the marginal hour is increasingly supplied by YouTube, which commands roughly 12–13% of U.S. TV time and pays creators a revenue share rather than a production budget. Netflix's answer — live sports and events — solves the advertising problem but imports auction-priced cost inflation from the one part of media where scale advantages do not apply. Netflix's own operating expense lines are already growing faster than revenue (G&A +22.7% TTM against 13.4% Q2 revenue growth). The industry's structural issue is that content is the only differentiator and content is the fastest-inflating input.

The CTV Advertising Migration

Emerging Tailwind

Connected-TV advertising is a $50–60B global market growing 15–18% a year as budgets migrate out of linear television, and it is the only large, fast-growing profit pool available to streamers. Netflix arrived late but arrives with something rivals lack: premium, brand-safe, logged-in, globally consistent inventory and — since moving to its own in-house ad stack — full control of targeting and measurement. Roku's 16.8% revenue growth and Amazon's ad ramp show what the pool can support. If Netflix's advertising line compounds from an estimated ~$4B toward $10B+ by the end of the decade, that alone re-accelerates total revenue growth back above 15% with far higher incremental margin than subscriptions.

How the Bull and Bear Cases Resolve

The Framing

Bull resolution: advertising is real and large, live events lift engagement per dollar rather than just cost, the Paramount–WBD merger is blocked, and Netflix compounds revenue at 13–15% with stable 30% margins. At 25–28x a 2027 EPS of $3.82–4.20 that is a $95–115 stock, consistent with the $94.33 consensus and the UBS/BMO end of the range. Bear resolution: growth settles at 9–11%, sports rights and opex eat the margin, a combined Paramount–WBD takes share, and advertising plateaus near $5B. At 16–18x a flatter $3.60–3.70 EPS that is a $58–67 stock — below the 52-week low of $65.08. The tell to watch is Q3 on October 20, 2026: revenue growth above 13% with operating expense growth below revenue growth points to the first path; the reverse points to the second. Everything else — the moat commentary, the buyback, the target range — is noise around that single number.
