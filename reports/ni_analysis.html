01 Company Overview

NiSource is a regulated gas and electric utility holding company: roughly 3.3 million natural-gas customers and 500,000 electric customers across six states, run through two reporting segments — Columbia Operations (gas distribution in Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, Kentucky and Maryland) and NIPSCO Operations (gas and electric in northern Indiana). The company was known as NIPSCO Industries until April 1999. TTM revenue is $6.88B, up 12.25%, and FY2025 GAAP EPS was $1.95 (adjusted $1.90). A regulated utility does not sell a product so much as rent out a rate base — $21.3B at year-end 2024 — at a return a state commission sets.

That is why the story of 2026 is not in the income statement. The plan management sells is aggressive for a utility: a $28.6B 2026–2030 capital programme ($21.0B base, $7.6B for data-center infrastructure), 9–11% consolidated rate-base growth and a 9–10% adjusted EPS CAGR from 2026 to 2033, powered by special contracts with Amazon and Alphabet in Indiana. The stock priced that plan in fully — a $48.28 month-end close in April 2026 — and then Indiana changed the odds. In August the governor fired a utility commissioner, the commission held technical conferences on the formal investigations it had opened in July into allowed ROE and cost-recovery trackers, it had already rejected NIPSCO's full infrastructure tracker request, and two research houses cut their view of Indiana as a regulatory jurisdiction. The stock closed September 21 at $40.52, down 16.1% from April and 1.1% above its 52-week low. The central question: is the growth plan still worth paying for if the regulator that sets the return on half the rate base has become less predictable?

Customer Savings

NIPSCO Generation ("GenCo") is a separate affiliate built so that, in Utility Dive's words, "NIPSCO's existing customers don't pay for infrastructure needed to serve hyperscalers." The first contract, approved by the IURC on November 19, 2025, supplies Amazon with up to 3 GW (2.4 GW by end-2032) over 15 years, backed by about $7B of investment: two 1.3-GW gas-fired plants and a 400-MW, four-hour battery. An Alphabet contract (~340 MW) was approved in July 2026 and a 400-MW Amazon increase was filed July 17, with a final order sought by November. Together management puts savings for existing customers at ~$1.4B over the contract terms, and says it is in active negotiations on another 3 GW with 2 GW of prospects behind that. This is the piece that lifts consolidated EPS growth from the 6–8% base plan to 9–10%.

Risk shape: contracted, but concentrated — two counterparties, one state, and construction risk on large gas plants.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchise monopoly service territories $21.3B regulated rate base ~85% capital recovery within 12 months Six-state regulatory diversification 15-year hyperscaler contracts Ring-fenced GenCo structure

The moat is legal, not competitive: nobody else is allowed to lay a gas main to a house in Columbus or a wire to a data center in northern Indiana. That makes demand safe and returns negotiated. The flip side is that the moat has exactly one gatekeeper per state, and the gatekeeper is political. NiSource cannot lose customers to a rival; it can lose return to a commission. 2026 is the year the market remembered that the second risk is the one that matters.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Indiana regulatory reset. On August 3, 2026 Governor Mike Braun fired Commissioner Andy Zay; the IURC opened investigations into ROE under multi-year rate plans (Cause 46428) and into the use of trackers (Cause 46429) in July, with technical conferences on August 7. On August 10 Wolfe Research cut Indiana to "below average," writing that "political intervention in the regulatory process and volatility in the regulatory environment has meaningfully increased the risk and ROEs needed for investing in Indiana utilities," and Jefferies said "investor confidence in Indiana has materially deteriorated." Half of NiSource's rate base sits there.
02
Tracker denial. The IURC rejected NIPSCO's full infrastructure tracker request (reported as a $741M plan). Management says the order "doesn't preclude us from seeking recovery ... in future regulatory proceedings." Trackers are what keep regulatory lag short; losing one means spending now and waiting for a rate case to earn on it.
03
Capex outruns cash. TTM free cash flow is −$1.46B, debt/EBITDA 5.74x, interest coverage 2.27x and shares outstanding +2.48% YoY. The plan depends on continued equity issuance and on the 14–16% FFO-to-debt band holding. Sources disagree on the annual equity need (one call summary says $400–600M, another ~$250M), so no figure is relied on here.
04
Labour and weather shocks hit the quarter. The April 2026 lockout of ~1,600 USW-represented NIPSCO employees and record tornado activity turned Q2 adjusted EPS to $0.16 from $0.22. Both are non-recurring in intent; the clerical unit had not ratified a contract as of the last report fetched.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $24.23 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $40.52, NI gained 67.2% on price — before dividends, which rose from $0.895 a share in FY2021 to $1.20 on a trailing basis. For a regulated utility that is a strong half-decade, and almost all of it came in two bursts: the 2024 re-rating after the Blackstone deal closed and the data-center story took hold, and the late-2025 to April-2026 run after the Amazon contract was approved. The last five months have handed back the second burst.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how unusual 2024–2026 was for a utility: it sat above 70 for most of mid-2024 through 2025, peaking at 93.3 in November 2024. It has now fallen to 33.8, close to the 29.5 reading at the September 2023 low, the most oversold point in the window. The 10-month moving average is about $44.79, roughly 10% above the price: the long-term trend has turned down, and momentum has not yet stabilised.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 19.8x this year's consensus EPS for a utility guiding to 9–10% EPS growth through 2033 and 9–11% rate-base growth, with a 2.96% yield growing ~7% a year. The data-center growth is contracted for 15 years with two investment-grade hyperscalers, built through a ring-fenced affiliate that saves existing customers ~$1.4B. Half the rate base is in five non-Indiana gas jurisdictions with ~85% of capital recovered within 12 months. The stock has fallen 16% from its April high on a regulatory scare, not a broken contract, and every one of 15 analysts has a target above today's price. If Indiana's investigations land at a low-9% ROE rather than something worse, the damage is a few basis points of return on part of the base.

Bear Case

The plan only works if regulators let NiSource earn on $28.6B of spending quickly, and the regulator for half of the rate base has just been reshaped for "affordability." The commission rejected NIPSCO's full tracker, is re-examining ROEs and trackers, and a commissioner was fired. Free cash flow is −$1.46B, debt/EBITDA 5.74x, interest cover 2.27x and the share count is growing 2.5% a year, so any slippage in allowed return or recovery speed goes straight to per-share growth and to equity needs. A 21.5x trailing multiple is a growth-utility multiple, and the growth now depends on Indianapolis.

01
Indiana allowed-return and recovery reset. Causes 46428 and 46429 could lower the 9.75% electric ROE and slow recovery on 50.6% of the rate base.
02
Financing a negative-FCF plan. $3.76B of TTM capex against $2.30B of operating cash flow, 5.74x debt/EBITDA and a 14–16% FFO/debt band to defend. A ratings slip or a higher cost of equity compresses the arithmetic behind the 9–10% CAGR.
03
GenCo execution and concentration. Two 1.3-GW gas plants and a 400-MW battery to build on time and on budget for a small number of counterparties; the 400-MW Amazon amendment still needs an IURC order.
Industry Commentary
Affordability politics vs. utility capex
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Indiana is a clean case study of a wider tension: utilities want to spend heavily on grids, gas pipe and generation for new load, while governors and commissions face customer bills. In August 2026 Indiana's governor replaced a commissioner and, per IPM, appointed a successor who emphasised affordability; the IURC opened ROE and tracker investigations as the state moves to multi-year, performance-based rates. Wolfe Research said political intervention "has meaningfully increased the risk and ROEs needed for investing in Indiana utilities." For NiSource the transmission from politics to earnings is direct: allowed ROE times equity ratio times rate base.

Operating shocks: storms and labour
ONGOING HEADWIND

Record tornado activity in 2026 drove outage response costs that were not adjusted out of Q2, and the April NIPSCO lockout added $21.4M of cost that was. Neither changes the rate base; both show how thin a quarter's margin is when the allowed return is fixed and the costs are not.

Data-center load as a utility growth engine
EMERGING TAILWIND

The GenCo structure is NiSource's answer to the affordability problem: hyperscalers pay for the plants built for them, and existing customers get ~$1.4B of savings over the contract terms. The bull framing: that design is exactly what an affordability-focused commission should want, so the Amazon amendment and future contracts (3 GW in negotiation, 2 GW more in prospects) keep moving. The bear framing: the same commission that approved GenCo has just shown it will reopen returns and trackers, and a 15-year contract is only as stable as the regulatory regime around it. The November order on the Amazon amendment is the first test.
