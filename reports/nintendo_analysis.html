01 Company Overview

Nintendo started in Kyoto in 1889 making hanafuda playing cards, and it still sells them. Today it is a video-game platform company: it designs the console, makes most of the best-selling games for it, and earns a growing slice from licensing Mario, Zelda and the rest to film studios, theme parks and phone games. It reports as a single business segment. In the fiscal year to March 2026 (Nintendo calls it FY26) it booked ¥2,313.1 billion of sales, up 98.6%, on the June 2025 launch of the Nintendo Switch 2, and operating profit of ¥360.1 billion, up 27.5%. Profit attributable to shareholders rose 52.1% to ¥424.1 billion. It sold 19.86 million Switch 2 consoles in that first year; by June 30, 2026 the life-to-date total was 23.68 million, against 156.59 million for the original Switch.

The stock tells a different story from the sales line. It closed June 2025, the month the Switch 2 launched, at a record month-end ¥13,880; by June 2026 it was ¥6,815, down 50.9%, and it ended the banner date at ¥7,849. The problem is the second year. Nintendo's own forecast for FY27 (April 2026 – March 2027) is sales down 11.4% to ¥2,050 billion, Switch 2 hardware down 16.9% to 16.5 million units, and profit attributable to shareholders down 26.9% to ¥310 billion, with about ¥100 billion of extra cost from memory-chip prices and US tariffs built into cost of sales. The first quarter of that year still beat expectations: sales of ¥517.8 billion against an LSEG median estimate of ¥444.96 billion (+16.4%) and net profit of ¥147.4 billion against ¥78.30 billion (+88.3%), per CNBC. The quarter included a one-off refund of US tariffs (about $300 million) and a weaker yen, which added about ¥22.2 billion to operating profit (Nintendo); on August 6 Nintendo left the full-year forecast unchanged. To protect margins it raised console prices: the Switch 2 went from ¥49,980 to ¥59,980 in Japan on May 25, 2026, and from $449.99 to $499.99 in the US on September 1. Currency matters too: 76.9% of FY26 sales came from outside Japan (the Americas alone 40.4%), so a weaker yen inflates reported sales and profit. The FY27 forecast assumes ¥150 per dollar; the ECB rate on October 2, 2026 was ¥157.67.

The cash pile

Nintendo's balance sheet is unusual for a company its size. At June 30, 2026 it held ¥1,544.1 billion of cash and deposits plus ¥423.8 billion of short-term securities — ¥1,967.9 billion, about 22% of its market value — and has no borrowings: its liabilities are trade payables, provisions and taxes. Equity was 76.5% of total assets. Cash and securities also earn money: interest income was ¥46.1 billion in FY26 and ¥13.2 billion in Q1 FY27, and foreign-exchange gains on its dollar and euro deposits added ¥44.3 billion and ¥16.8 billion. That is why ordinary profit (¥542.2 billion in FY26) runs well above operating profit.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Mario, Zelda, Donkey Kong IP Hardware + software designed together 100M+ annual playing users Switch 1 library plays on Switch 2 Evergreen first-party catalogue ¥1.97T cash, no borrowings

Nintendo's moat is characters nobody else can use, sold on hardware nobody else makes. Because it controls both the console and the best-selling games on it, it can design a system around a game idea instead of chasing raw processing power, and first-party titles keep selling for years — Mario Kart 8 Deluxe and Nintendo Switch Sports were still steady sellers in FY26. Players' Nintendo Accounts carry over from Switch to Switch 2, which, in Nintendo's account, is what made the transition smooth. The weakness is the flip side of the same model: profits rise and fall with one console cycle and a handful of in-house hits.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Memory chips. The FY27 plan absorbs about ¥100 billion from higher component prices, "particularly for memory", and tariffs. That is 27% of the year's ¥370 billion operating-profit target. Trade press tied the December 2025 slide in the shares to rising RAM costs for the Switch 2.
02
Price rises meet a second-year console. Switch 2 prices went up 20% in Japan (May 2026) and 11% in the US (September 2026) within 15 months of launch, while Nintendo itself forecasts fewer units. Furukawa told analysts on May 8 that the increase "raises the barrier for entry to a certain extent for people deciding whether to make a purchase". If demand proves price-sensitive, the hardware forecast is at risk.
03
Hit dependence. First-party titles were 82.6% of Q1 FY27 software sales. Results ride on Nintendo's own release calendar: the holiday quarter's lead title is a remake (Ocarina of Time, November 5), and the next new mainline Pokémon games are 2027 releases.
04
Yen and tariffs. About 77% of sales are outside Japan. Q1 FY27 had a ¥39.0 billion FX tailwind on sales (about ¥22.2 billion on operating profit) and a $300 million one-off tariff refund; neither is a run-rate. US tariff policy remains an input to the cost forecast. A proposed class action filed for two US buyers by the law firms Emery Reddy and Milberg in federal court in Seattle on April 21, 2026 alleges that Nintendo passed the tariffs on to US buyers and seeks to make it reimburse them; on July 20 Nintendo of America moved to dismiss, arguing the buyers have no legal entitlement to the refunds (Hoffert v. Nintendo of America, W.D. Wash. court filings; Game Developer).

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the trailing figures. First, the US tariff refund: about $300 million of IEEPA tariffs, previously expensed, was booked as a reduction in Q1 FY27 cost of sales; Nintendo says the tariffs had mostly been absorbed rather than passed on in prices. Third, the launch-year base: FY26 sales nearly doubled on the Switch 2 launch, so every year-on-year comparison through June 2026 is against an unusually large hardware quarter.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The stock has halved while the business has grown. At ¥7,849 Nintendo trades on 19.15 times trailing earnings — about the Prime Market average — with ¥1.97 trillion of cash and securities, no debt, and a 60% payout policy. The Switch 2 had shipped 23.68 million units by June 30, 2026, the Switch 1 library plays on it, and software, where the margin is, is gaining share: Q1 operating margin was 27.5%, and Q1 sales and net profit beat the LSEG median estimates by 16.4% and 88.3% (CNBC). The FY27 forecast assumes ¥150 per dollar against ¥157.67 today, and the analyst revenue consensus already sits above guidance. Add a $1 billion Mario film, a Zelda film and new mainline Pokémon games in 2027, and the pipeline beyond the holiday quarter is full.

Bear Case

Nintendo itself is guiding to lower sales, fewer consoles and 26.9% less profit this year, which puts the forward P/E on guidance at 29.19. Memory prices are an industry-wide cost Nintendo cannot design away, and it has already raised Switch 2 prices in Japan, the US, Canada and Europe. The best Q1 numbers leaned on a one-off tariff refund and a weak yen. The holiday slate is led by a remake, and the market marked the stock down 8.7% over the two sessions around the September Directs. UBS now has a Sell and a ¥5,500 target; the range of targets runs from ¥5,000 to ¥21,260.

Top 3 Risks

01
Component costs keep rising. The ¥100 billion memory-and-tariff cost in the FY27 plan was set in May. If memory prices rise further, Nintendo can absorb it (margins fall) or raise prices again (units fall).
02
The second-year console slump deepens. Q1 Switch 2 shipments were down 34.4%; the full-year plan is down 16.9%. A weak holiday quarter after September's price rises would put the 16.5 million forecast — and the software sales that ride on it — at risk.
03
A stronger yen. 76.9% of FY26 sales were outside Japan, and Nintendo holds large dollar and euro deposits whose revaluation runs through ordinary profit. A move from ¥157.67 back toward the ¥150 planning rate would remove the cushion in the forecast.
Industry Commentary
The memory squeeze on game consoles
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Nintendo's FY27 plan names "rising component prices, particularly for memory" as part of a roughly ¥100 billion cost hit. Trade press reports the same squeeze on PC, phone and laptop makers as memory capacity shifts to AI data centers (Outlook Respawn, December 2025, citing TrendForce). For a console maker the choice is between eating the cost, raising prices, or both; Nintendo has done both. The first test of whether consumers accept a $499.99 Switch 2 is the holiday quarter that starts with the September 1 price rise.

Year two of a console cycle
ONGOING SWING FACTOR

Nintendo describes Switch 2 sales as more concentrated in the launch year than its previous systems. The investment case rests on what happens next: the installed base turns into software sales, and software carries the margin. Q1 FY27 showed the mechanism working — hardware down to 55.3% of platform sales, gross margin up to 54.3% — but it needs enough new consoles sold to keep feeding it. Management is not worried yet: on May 8 Furukawa called the pace of adoption "extremely fast even when compared to Nintendo Switch" and said "we do not see any particular concerns about its momentum at this time"; Nintendo's August 6 presentation notes say second-year sales "compare favorably to the adoption trajectory of Nintendo Switch".

Characters beyond the console
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: two Mario films have each passed $1 billion at the box office, and a live-action Zelda film is dated for 2027 — Nintendo's characters now reach audiences who have never bought its hardware, and IP income doubled in Q1. The bear framing: at ¥73.5 billion in FY26, IP income was 3.2% of sales and fell 9.7% in a year without a film. It smooths the cycle; it does not replace it.
