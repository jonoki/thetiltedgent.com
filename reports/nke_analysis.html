01 Company Overview

NIKE, Inc. designs, develops and markets athletic footwear, apparel, equipment and accessories worldwide under the NIKE, Jordan and Converse brands, selling through wholesale partners and its own NIKE Direct channel (owned stores plus Nike.com and the Nike app). It remains the largest athletic footwear company on earth — independent market research puts the global athletic footwear market at roughly $150–153 billion in 2026, of which the top five players (NIKE, adidas, PUMA, New Balance, Under Armour) hold approximately 43% combined, with NIKE the clear leader.

Fiscal 2026 (ended May 31, 2026) revenue was $46.40 billion, essentially flat as reported (+0.2%) and down 2% currency-neutral — a second straight year without growth after FY2025's −9.8% collapse. Net income was $3.11 billion and diluted EPS $2.10, both down about 3%. At $41.05 the stock sits roughly 76% below its November 2021 month-end peak of $169.24 and is down 46.98% over the past 52 weeks, versus a +20.9% total return for the S&P 500 (SPY) over the same window. NIKE has fallen to roughly #416 globally by market capitalization — a company that was a top-50 global name four years ago.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Swoosh Brand Equity Jordan Franchise Athlete & League Endorsements Global Wholesale Network Sourcing Scale Air / Flyknit / Aero-FIT IP Membership Data

NIKE's moat has always been demand-side rather than cost-side: a brand that lets it charge premium prices for commodity-manufactured goods, reinforced by the deepest athlete and federation endorsement portfolio in sport and by the Jordan sub-brand — arguably the most valuable single franchise in consumer products. That moat is intact but visibly narrower than it was in 2021. The evidence is in the numbers: gross margin of 43.2% in FY2026 versus 46.0% in FY2022, a ~275 basis point erosion, and ROE of 22.1% against peers at 24–43%. A brand with unimpaired pricing power does not need to liquidate inventory for two consecutive years. What remains genuinely defensible is scale in wholesale distribution — no challenger can service Foot Locker, Dick's, JD Sports and 40+ country distributor networks simultaneously — and an R&D budget that challengers with $4–5 billion of revenue cannot match. The bear counter is that in running, the category that now drives the market, product superiority no longer requires NIKE's scale.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Greater China structural share loss. China revenue fell 11% currency-neutral in FY2026 and the Q4 decline (−17% cc) was worse than the full year, so the trend is accelerating, not bottoming. This is not purely macro: Anta gained five points of sportswear share (14%→19%) and Li-Ning three (6%→9%) between 2019 and 2023 while NIKE's footwear share drifted from 25% to 24% and adidas's halved. National-brand preference among younger Chinese consumers is a durable, non-cyclical headwind that no amount of Win Now execution in Beaverton addresses.

2. Challenger brands in performance running. On grew revenue 18.5% and quadrupled net income; HOKA-driven Deckers grew 7.9% with a 57.8% gross margin — 14 points above NIKE's. Both are winning in the highest-margin, highest-growth part of the market with narrow assortments and specialty-retail credibility. NIKE's Q3 running growth of 20%+ is off a depressed base and does not yet demonstrate share recapture; it demonstrates that the category was under-served internally for years.

3. Sourcing concentration and tariff whiplash. Roughly 50% of NIKE footwear is made in Vietnam and 27% in Indonesia — about 75% of unit volume in two countries. The February 20, 2026 Supreme Court decision striking down the IEEPA tariffs (which had hit 46% on Vietnam and 32% on Indonesia) produced a $986 million recovery, but replacement Section 122 tariffs at 15% took effect four days later and Section 301/232 duties are untouched; the Yale Budget Lab pegs the residual effective US tariff increase at 6.7 percentage points versus pre-2025. Structural exposure is unchanged. Refunds also require individually filed customs claims and "will not come automatically."

4. Channel transition execution risk. Rebuilding wholesale means handing margin and control back to retailers NIKE spent five years disintermediating. Wholesale is growing (+6%) but NIKE Direct is falling faster in mix terms (−6%, digital −12%), so the company is trading high-margin owned revenue for lower-margin partner revenue and hoping volume recovery more than compensates. On the Q3 call management conceded sell-through trends "remain below targets" even as order books improved — order growth without sell-through is future inventory risk.

Notable One-Off Items

The FY2026 numbers are materially distorted and must be adjusted before use.

1. IEEPA tariff recovery — $986 million, Q4 FY2026. On February 20, 2026 the US Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the executive branch exceeded its authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, vacating tariffs that had reached 46% on Vietnamese and 32% on Indonesian goods. NIKE recognised a $986 million expected recovery in the fourth quarter, worth approximately 900 basis points of Q4 gross margin and $0.52 of the quarter's $0.72 diluted EPS. Reported Q4 gross margin of 49.2% (+890bps) therefore corresponds to an underlying rate near 40%, roughly in line with Q3 FY2026's 40.2%. Q4 net income "up 407%" is almost entirely this item. Strip it and FY2026 EPS is approximately $1.58, which is precisely the base consensus uses for its FY2027 estimate of $1.71 (+8.4%). Recovery timing is uncertain: refunds require individually filed customs claims and are not automatic.

2. Deliberate inventory liquidation. Management quantified the intentional removal of unhealthy classic-franchise inventory as roughly a five-point headwind to reported revenue in Q3 FY2026. This depresses revenue and gross margin but is a management choice, not demand destruction — the offsetting question is whether the cleaned marketplace generates full-price sell-through afterward.

3. Currency. FY2026 revenue was flat reported but −2% currency-neutral, i.e. FX added roughly 2 points. The distortion is largest in EMEA (+3% reported vs −3% currency-neutral, ~600bps) and Greater China (Q4 −12% reported vs −17% currency-neutral). Every reported growth figure in FY2026 flatters the underlying business.

4. Restructuring charges — not verified. We could not verify a discrete FY2026 restructuring or impairment charge line item from the sources reached for this snapshot. NIKE has taken restructuring charges in prior fiscal years; readers should check the FY2026 Form 10-K directly rather than assume none exist. Converse's −31% decline is disclosed as a revenue outcome, not as a charge.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 means the stock has run hot and may be due a pause; below 30 means it has been sold hard and may be due a bounce. It says nothing about whether the business is good.

Five-year trajectory. NIKE closed August 2021 at $164.74 and peaked at a month-end close of $169.24 in November 2021. It closed at $41.05 on August 20, 2026. That is a −75.1% price return over five years from the August 2021 starting point, or −75.7% from the November 2021 peak close. Including roughly $7–8 of dividends received over the period, total return is still worse than −70%. Over the same five years the S&P 500 compounded strongly; over the trailing twelve months alone, SPY returned +20.87% against NKE's −46.98% — a relative underperformance of roughly 68 percentage points in one year. This is not a sector drawdown: On Holding trades at 21x earnings with 18.5% growth, Deckers grew revenue 7.9%, and adidas is down only 3.2% over twelve months. NIKE's decline is company-specific.

The shape matters as much as the magnitude. There were four distinct legs down: the 2022 inventory-glut and China-lockdown de-rating ($169 → $83), a reopening rally that recovered half of it ($83 → $127), a long grinding slide through 2023–2024 as DTC damage compounded, and then the sharpest phase — $77 in August 2025 to $41 in August 2026, a further 47% decline that occurred entirely during the turnaround. That last leg is the crux of the investment debate: the market has now spent two full years watching Elliott Hill execute and has repriced the shares lower every quarter.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The brand is not broken — the distribution model was, and it is being repaired with measurable results: wholesale revenue grew 6% in FY2026, North America grew 5%, running grew over 20%, and Air Force 1 and Air Jordan 1 have stabilised. Gross margin has turned up off the FY2025 trough (42.7% → 42.9%), inventory is flat rather than growing, and the tariff overhang has been legally resolved with a $986M recovery banked. At $41.05 with a 4.0% yield backed by a 24-year growth streak and $9.0B of cash, an investor is paid to wait. Bernstein's $68 target — 66% above spot — reflects the view that a company still generating $46B of revenue and 22% ROE will not be permanently valued at 1.3x sales. The fall 2026 Investor Day is the natural re-rating event.

Bear Case

Two years into the turnaround, every underlying metric is still deteriorating. Currency-neutral revenue fell 2% in FY2026 and 4% in Q4 — worse, not better. Greater China's decline accelerated from −10% in Q3 to −17% currency-neutral in Q4. Free cash flow has fallen 67% in two years to $2.18B, and FCF per share of $1.47 no longer covers the $1.64 dividend. Reported FY2026 EPS of $2.10 includes $0.52 of one-time tariff recovery; on the ~$1.58 operating base the stock trades at 26x, and consensus still expects FY2027 revenue to decline. Forward P/E of 23.96 exceeds trailing P/E — the market is paying up for shrinking earnings while On and Deckers take the profitable end of the market at 58–65% gross margins.

Top 3 Risks

01
Greater China does not stabilise. The Q4 currency-neutral decline of 17% was the worst of the year, and Chinese consumers are structurally rotating to Anta and Li-Ning. If China revenue halves again from $5.8B, roughly $3B of NIKE's highest-margin revenue disappears — enough on its own to push consolidated gross margin below 42% and make the current dividend indefensible from cash flow.
02
Gross margin recovery is structurally capped, not cyclically delayed. Underlying Q4 gross margin ex-tariff-recovery was near 40%, versus 44.7% in FY2024 and 46.0% in FY2022. The channel mix is moving toward lower-margin wholesale by design, and residual tariffs still add roughly 6.7 points to the effective US import rate. If 43% is the new ceiling rather than a waypoint back to 46%, the earnings power the bull case assumes does not exist.
03
The dividend is not covered by free cash flow. FCF per share of $1.47 against a $1.64 annual dividend and a 78.1% payout ratio means the 24-year growth streak is being funded from the balance sheet. NIKE has $9.0B of cash and can sustain this for years, but a token increase — or worse, a cut — would remove the single largest reason non-believers still hold the stock, and would likely trigger forced selling from dividend-growth mandates.
  Industry Commentary
Chinese Consumer Demand and Local Brand Preference
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
China's sportswear market is roughly $61 billion (2023), about 15% of global demand, with athletic footwear alone at ~$15.2B in 2026E. The problem is not market size — it is who is winning it. Between 2019 and 2023, Anta's sportswear share rose from 14% to 19% and Li-Ning's from 6% to 9%, while adidas's footwear share collapsed from 19% to 10% and NIKE's slipped from 25% to 24%. NIKE's FY2026 China revenue of $5.8B fell 11% currency-neutral, with Q4 down 17% — an accelerating decline, not a bottoming one. Anta has also built a Western-brand portfolio (Fila China, Descente, Amer Sports) that competes with NIKE at the premium end using domestic distribution. Meanwhile Lululemon quadrupled its China share since 2019 and On holds under 1%, so the share NIKE is losing is going overwhelmingly to national champions. This is the one headwind on this page with no obvious management fix.
Tariffs on Asian Footwear Sourcing
ONGOING HEADWIND
NIKE sources roughly 50% of footwear from Vietnam and 27% from Indonesia — about 75% of volume in two countries. The February 20, 2026 Supreme Court ruling in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump vacated IEEPA tariffs that had reached 46% on Vietnam, 49% on Cambodia and 32% on Indonesia, producing a $986M recovery for NIKE. But Section 122 replacement tariffs at 15% took effect four days later on February 24, and Section 232 and Section 301 China duties were untouched. The Yale Budget Lab estimates the effective US tariff rate still runs 6.7 percentage points above pre-2025. Refunds require individually filed customs claims — over 1,000 corporate entities have started the process, and the National Retail Federation has had to lobby for a workable refund mechanism. Practically: the accounting benefit is booked, the cash is uncertain, the structural cost is permanent, and the supply-chain diversification and price increases taken during the high-tariff period remain in place. Sportswear stocks surged then slumped on the ruling — the market has already worked this out.
The Athletic Footwear Landscape — Scale vs. Challengers
STRUCTURAL SHIFT — CUTS BOTH WAYS
The global athletic footwear market is $150–153B in 2026 growing 3.9–5.5% annually toward $212–235B by 2034/35, with running shoes alone over 37% of the market and the top five players holding ~43% combined. The decade's defining change is that scale stopped being a prerequisite for product superiority: On Holding built a $4.0B revenue business at a 64.8% gross margin with 18.5% growth, and HOKA carried Deckers to $5.5B at 57.8% — both roughly 15–20 points above NIKE's 43.2%. They win in specialty running, the exact category NIKE ceded while chasing lifestyle classics on Nike.com.

Bull framing: combined, On and Deckers are under $10B of revenue against NIKE's $46.4B; NIKE's Q3 running growth of 20%+ and sold-out Nike Mind launch show that when Beaverton actually points its R&D and endorsement machine at a category, it still moves the market — and no challenger can service global wholesale at NIKE's scale. Bear framing: those challengers are compounding at 8–19% with double NIKE's margin structure and roughly a quarter of its capital intensity (Deckers ROIC 90.6% vs NIKE 19.6%), and they are taking the premium consumer first. The market has already voted: NIKE trades at 24x forward earnings that are expected to fall, while On trades at 16.7x forward with a PEG of 0.68. Being the largest player in a growing market is worth very little if you are the only one not growing.
