01 Company Overview

Northrop Grumman is one of five US defence primes, and the most concentrated of them in the two hardest engineering problems the Pentagon is currently paying for: the B-21 Raider stealth bomber and Sentinel, the replacement for the entire land-based leg of the nuclear triad. FY2025 revenue was $41.95B, up 2.25% from $41.03B, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $42.89B, up 5.90%. At a $73.16B market capitalisation it is roughly the #336 most valuable listed company in the world — the smallest of the four US primes covered on this site by market cap, and the cheapest on trailing earnings at 16.35×.

The central debate is a single sentence long. Northrop's two most strategically indispensable programmes are both fixed-price problems: B-21 has already absorbed roughly $2.0 billion of cumulative losses on its first five low-rate production lots, and Sentinel breached Nunn-McCurdy with cost growth of about 81%, to an estimated $141 billion. Neither programme can plausibly be cancelled — one is the only new penetrating bomber in the Western world, the other is the ICBM leg of the triad. So the company holds contracts with enormous strategic leverage and, so far, negative accounting leverage. The stock has priced that ambiguity harshly: −9.92% over the past year against Lockheed Martin +20.00%, RTX +28.88% and General Dynamics +13.30%, and it sits 33% below its 52-week high of $774.00. Everything below is an attempt to work out whether that gap is a judgement about the programmes or about the calendar.

The two programmes, and why fixed price hurts on a contract nobody can cancel

Why a fixed-price development contract can lose money on a programme the customer cannot cancel: under a fixed-price contract the government agrees to pay a set amount regardless of what the work actually costs, so once the contractor's estimated cost to complete exceeds that price, accounting rules require it to book the entire expected loss immediately — and the customer's inability to cancel does not raise the price by a cent, it merely guarantees the contractor must keep building at that loss.

B-21 Raider. Northrop took a $1.56 billion pre-tax charge announced January 25, 2024 (about $1.19B after tax, spread over years at roughly $200M annually after tax), covering the first five low-rate initial production lots, after inflation assumptions and actual manufacturing costs from building the first aircraft came in above plan. Management stated at the time that each of the first five LRIP lots will be performed at a loss. In April 2025 a further $477 million pre-tax ($397M after tax) charge followed — this one caused by deliberate manufacturing process changes to enable a higher production rate, plus underestimated material costs. Cumulative programme losses are therefore approximately $2.0 billion, and the Q2 2026 10-Q shows roughly $1.0 billion of remaining loss accrual in current liabilities. Q1 2026 carried a further $157M unfavourable adjustment, offset by reduced contingency accruals following the capacity-expansion agreement. The path to profitability is not a promise of retroactive recovery on those five lots — it is that lots six onward are negotiated separately, on volumes and a learning curve the earlier lots paid for. The Pentagon provided about $60 million of inflation assistance in 2023 and Northrop has said it is not counting on more. The funding that matters instead is the February 2026 agreement for a 25% production capacity increase under a multi-year contract framework, against roughly $2.5 billion of Northrop capital investment (about $200M of it in 2026). On the Q2 2026 call Kathy Warden said "B-21, there's been good execution throughout the year," described margins as headed north, and said the Air Force would reach a conclusion by year-end on accelerating into a programme of record larger than 100 aircraft.

Sentinel (LGM-35A, the Minuteman III replacement). Sentinel triggered a critical Nunn-McCurdy breach in 2024 — the statutory trigger that forces the Office of the Secretary of Defense to review and either certify or terminate a programme whose unit cost has run far over baseline. Estimated total programme cost has risen roughly 81% above the original projection, to approximately $141 billion. The dominant driver was not the missile: it was the flawed initial assumption that existing Minuteman III silos could be refurbished, which turned into a requirement to build largely new ground infrastructure, including replacing on the order of 7,500 miles of copper cabling with fibre. The programme was certified to continue and restructured. As of February 2026 the Air Force stated the restructure would complete by year-end alongside regained Milestone B certification, with first flight test targeted for 2027 and initial operating capability in the early 2030s. A September 2025 GAO report flagged persistent cost and schedule risk, and the Air Force has considered opening elements of the ground infrastructure to competition. Commercially, the Q2 2026 quarter was the good news: further definitisation and authorisation added $7.6 billion to backlog with contract incentives that improved profitability, an acoustic test validated silo launch conditions, solid rocket motors for the first five flight tests are in production, and ground broke on a new facility in Utah for the production phase later this decade.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOLE-SOURCE B-21 SOLE-SOURCE SENTINEL SOLID ROCKET MOTOR DUOPOLY CLASSIFIED PORTFOLIO $104.7B BACKLOG SECURITY CLEARANCE BASE LOW-OBSERVABLE IP

The moat is irreplaceability, not pricing power — and the distinction is exactly why the stock behaves the way it does. There is no second source for a penetrating stealth bomber, no second source for the ICBM recapitalisation, and effectively one alternative supplier of large solid rocket motors in the Western world. That means Northrop cannot be competed away from its two anchor franchises across a horizon measured in decades. What it does not mean is that Northrop can set its own price: on a fixed-price development contract the customer's dependence produces political durability, not margin. Backlog of $104.7 billion at June 30, 2026 — a record, up 17% year over year and 9% above the $95.7B at end-2025 — converts at roughly 35% into revenue over the next 12 months and 55% over 24 months, which is close to the best revenue visibility available in any listed industrial. Q2 net awards were $20.0 billion for a 1.84× book-to-bill. The moat shows up in the order book with total clarity, and in the margin line with none.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Fixed-price contract structure. Two of the four segments have taken unfavourable estimate-at-completion adjustments in the most recent quarter alone — $68M on SiAW in Defense Systems, $91M on the GEM 63XL booster in Space Systems — and B-21 has absorbed roughly $2.0B. On a fixed-price contract, the entire projected loss must be booked the moment it is foreseen, which means one revised cost estimate can wipe out several quarters of segment profit with no cash leaving the building that day. The customer's dependence does not repair this.
▸
Programme concentration. B-21 and Sentinel between them define the equity story, the capital plan and the multiple. Both are single-customer, single-country programmes subject to congressional appropriation and to Nunn-McCurdy-style review. Sentinel has already been through that process once; a second restructuring, a rescoping of the ground segment, or competition for ground infrastructure would hit revenue and margin at the same time.
▸
Capital intensity crowding out returns. Capex is guided to ~$1.85B in 2026 and roughly 4.5% of sales in 2027–28 to fund the B-21 ramp — against $2.5B of committed multi-year investment. The consequence is already visible: Northrop repurchased only $68 million of stock in the first half of 2026 while paying $684 million of dividends. A defence prime that stops buying back its own shares while the multiple is compressed is telling you where the cash has to go.
▸
Budget-cycle and political exposure. Essentially all revenue is US government or allied-government funded. The FY2027 environment looks strong — House and Senate Armed Services Committees supporting a $1.1 trillion base DoD budget, nearly 10% above FY2026 — but a continuing resolution, a change of administration priorities, or a decision to trim the nuclear modernisation portfolio would hit a company with no meaningful commercial hedge.

Notable One-Off Items. This company's income statement is unusually distorted by non-recurring programme accounting, and reading it without adjusting is a mistake. FY2023 shows operating income of just $2,645M on $39.29B of revenue — a 6.73% margin against 17.32% the year before — because the $1.56B B-21 charge announced in January 2024 landed in that fiscal year; net income fell to $2,056M and EPS to $13.53 before recovering to $28.34 in FY2024. FY2021 is also not comparable: operating income of $7,495M on $35.67B (21.01%) is far above anything since, and the drivers were not verified from the sources fetched. In the current period, Q2 2026 carried $159M of unfavourable EAC adjustments ($68M SiAW, $91M GEM 63XL) against a favourable Sentinel adjustment, and Q1 2026 carried a $157M unfavourable B-21 adjustment offset by reduced contingency accruals. The company reports "MTM-adjusted" EPS, which excludes mark-to-market pension effects — the $28.60–$29.10 guidance range is on that basis, not GAAP.

RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has risen persistently enough to be called overbought; below 30, persistently enough to be called oversold. It describes momentum, not value — an overbought stock can keep rising and an oversold one can keep falling.

Five years of monthly closes describe a stock that spent four years grinding higher on the post-Ukraine defence re-rating, spiked violently in early 2026, and then gave back nearly the entire spike. From $360.15 at the end of September 2021 to $514.98 on September 4, 2026, the price return is +43.0% — a real but unremarkable five years, and dividends are on top of that. The path matters more than the endpoints. NOC ran from $369.90 in January 2022 to $549.01 by October 2022 as Europe rearmed, then spent 2023 and most of 2024 range-bound between roughly $430 and $530 while the B-21 charge worked through the numbers. The move that defines the chart is the ramp from $499.98 in June 2025 to $724.38 in February 2026 — a 45% advance in eight months on budget optimism and the B-21 capacity-expansion agreement — followed by a 29.7% drawdown to $509.31 by June 2026 as the segment margin problems reasserted themselves. The stock has been chopping between $510 and $545 since.

Relative performance. Over the last twelve months NOC returned −9.92% while Lockheed Martin returned +20.00%, RTX +28.88% and General Dynamics +13.30% — verified the same day from the same source. That is a roughly 30-point gap to LMT and nearly 40 points to RTX inside a sector that has been in favour, which means the market is not selling defence, it is selling this defence company. The comparison with General Dynamics is the sharpest one available: GD trades at 21.92× trailing earnings on 9.13% revenue growth and a 10.38% operating margin, NOC at 16.35× on 5.90% growth and a 13.36% operating margin. NOC earns the better margin and trades at the worse multiple. The difference is that GD's problem segment (Marine Systems, 7.1% margin) is a known, slow, shipyard-throughput problem, while NOC's problem segments produce sudden nine-figure EAC charges that arrive without warning on the day of the print. Markets pay for predictability, not for margin. A verified S&P 500 total return for the identical window was not fetched, so no index comparison figure is stated here.

Awaiting the year-end programme decisions
08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A record $104.7B backlog at a 1.84× book-to-bill, converting 35% into revenue within twelve months, on the two programmes the United States is least able to walk away from — bought at 16.35× trailing earnings, the cheapest of the four primes, with a 13.36% operating margin that is better than General Dynamics'. The B-21 loss accrual is already booked and roughly $1.0B remains to work off; lots six onward are negotiated separately, on a production base the Air Force agreed in February 2026 to expand by 25%. Sentinel added $7.6B of backlog in Q2 with a favourable EAC adjustment attached. Meanwhile Defense Systems sits on a record $35B backlog and up to $10B of multi-year missile opportunity over seven years, into an allied restocking cycle and a $1.1 trillion FY2027 base budget.

Bear Case

The company has taken unfavourable estimate-at-completion adjustments in three different segments across the last two quarters — $2.0B cumulative on B-21, $91M on GEM 63XL, $68M on SiAW, $157M on B-21 again in Q1 2026 — and the market has correctly concluded it cannot forecast the next one. Forward P/E of 17.60 sits above trailing 16.35 because guided FY2026 EPS is below TTM EPS: earnings go down before they go up. Free cash flow is guided to $3.1–$3.5B while capex climbs to ~4.5% of sales in 2027–28, and the buyback has effectively stopped — $68M of repurchases in the first half of 2026. Sentinel's cost has already grown 81% to ~$141B and the GAO still flags cost and schedule risk. A cheap multiple on a company that keeps surprising itself is not obviously cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
A further B-21 loss provision. Roughly $1.0B of loss accrual remains on the books and Q1 2026 already produced a $157M unfavourable adjustment. Each of the first five LRIP lots is contracted at a loss; any further increase in the estimate to complete must be recognised in full, immediately, in the quarter it is identified.
02
Sentinel restructuring slipping or rescoping. The Air Force targets restructure completion and Milestone B recertification by year-end 2026, first flight in 2027. A September 2025 GAO report flagged persistent cost and schedule risk, and the Air Force has considered competing elements of the ground infrastructure. Because the ground segment is where the 81% cost growth originated, that is precisely the part most exposed to being taken away or re-baselined again.
03
The capital plan crowding out shareholder returns. Capex of ~$1.85B in 2026 and ~4.5% of sales in 2027–28 against $3.1–$3.5B of adjusted free cash flow leaves little room. H1 2026 buybacks of $68M against $684M of dividends show the trade-off is already being made. If the FCF ramp promised for the fourth-quarter update disappoints, the equity loses both its cheapness argument and its capital-return argument at once.
Industry Commentary
Fixed-price contracting has become the sector's defining risk
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

After two decades of cost-plus development, the Pentagon pushed development risk onto contractors through fixed-price contracts — and then inflation arrived. Northrop is the clearest case study: $2.0 billion of cumulative B-21 losses, of which $1.56B was recognised in one January 2024 charge and $477M in April 2025, plus $68M on SiAW and $91M on the GEM 63XL in a single recent quarter. The customer cannot cancel B-21 — there is no other penetrating bomber programme in the Western world — and that changes nothing about the accounting. Note the asymmetry the sector now lives with: on a fixed-price contract the contractor absorbs 100% of the overrun and captures only a negotiated share of any underrun. Northrop received about $60M of Pentagon inflation assistance in 2023 and has said it is not counting on more. Every prime now prices development risk differently as a result, which is a slow structural improvement — but only for contracts signed from here.

Munitions and solid rocket motors: the allied restocking cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND

Three years of expenditure in Ukraine and the Middle East drained Western munitions stockpiles faster than they could be replaced, and the binding constraint turned out to be solid rocket motor capacity — the propulsion units at the heart of nearly every tactical and strategic missile, made in the West by essentially two suppliers. Northrop's Promontory, Utah operation is one of them, feeding Sentinel, space launch and tactical missiles simultaneously. The commercial consequences are now visible: ten multi-year missile acceleration agreements in work with up to $10 billion of sales opportunity over seven years, qualification as a PAC-3 supplier with production awards expected later in 2026, and a $2 billion solid rocket motor framework agreement with the DoD and Lockheed Martin — a direct competitor on platforms, a customer on propulsion. NATO's pledge of $50 billion of additional investment, including Triton commitments, extends the same demand outside the US budget. The catch is capacity: the same Promontory constraint that makes this valuable also makes the GEM 63XL redesign expensive, which is how a demand tailwind produced a $91M charge.

A generational budget up-cycle, and what each side does with it
ONGOING HEADWIND / TAILWIND

The demand environment is about as favourable as a defence prime can ask for: bipartisan committee support for a $1.1 trillion base DoD budget, nearly 10% above FY2026, a $67 billion supplemental request, and a $350 billion reconciliation package. The bull reading is that Northrop is the cheapest way to own it — 16.35× trailing earnings, a 1.84× book-to-bill, a record backlog, and margin per dollar of revenue better than General Dynamics — while the two programmes that scare people are precisely the two the government is least free to stop funding. The bear reading is that a rising budget does not fix a fixed-price contract, and that the last twelve months are the evidence: NOC fell 9.92% while LMT rose 20.00%, RTX 28.88% and GD 13.30%, inside that same up-cycle. Budget growth lifts revenue; it does not lift a programme whose price was set years ago. Which reading wins is decided by three disclosures between now and January — the Q3 print on October 20, the Q4 free cash flow update for 2027–28, and the year-end decisions on B-21 programme size and Sentinel restructure completion.
