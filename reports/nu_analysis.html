01 Company Overview

Nu — Nubank to its customers — calls itself the largest digital bank in Latin America: 138.9 million customers at June 30, 2026, of whom almost 118 million are in Brazil, 15.8 million in Mexico and more than 5 million in Colombia. It has no branches. It makes money three ways: interest on credit cards and personal loans, a spread on deposits ("float"), and fees. In Q2 2026 (quarter ended June 30, 2026) it booked gross revenue of $5.88B, up 39% FX-neutral, and net income of $1.06B, the first billion-dollar quarter in its history, up 49% FX-neutral. ROE was 33%. At a $67.9B market value it ranks #3 among Brazil-based companies on companiesmarketcap's country ranking, behind Petrobras and Itaú Unibanco, and #367 globally. It is not in the S&P 500; the home-market index is the Ibovespa, but Nu's only listing is the NYSE.

The brief's premise, "a digital bank at emerging-market scale", holds, and the numbers show why the model works. A traditional bank pays for branches and staff for every customer. Nu spends about $1.0 a month to serve an active customer and earns about $17.1 a month from one (ARPAC). That gap of roughly 17 to 1 is the business. The risk sits on the other side of the ledger. Most of the revenue is unsecured consumer credit to Brazil's mass market, and the 90+ day NPL ratio is 6.9%. That is the price of lending at 22.9% NIM. The stock has fallen 26% from its 52-week high of $18.98, although earnings are growing at more than 40%. The market is asking whether those earnings survive a Brazilian credit cycle, and whether the Mexico and US build-outs add to returns or dilute them.

FX exposure: Nu reports in US dollars, but almost all of its revenue is earned in Brazilian reais, Mexican pesos and Colombian pesos. Brazil holds $36.4B of the $45.3B deposit base. A weaker real cuts dollar-reported results, and a stronger one flatters them. In Q2 2026, reported USD revenue grew 55.8% YoY against 39% FX-neutral. The gap is currency.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

~$1/month cost to serve 139M-customer scale Primary-bank relationships in Brazil Proprietary credit data (NuFormer) Deposits at 88% of interbank Bank licences: Mexico live, Brazil pending

The moat is cost plus data. With no branches, Nu can profitably serve customers an incumbent would lose money on, and each of those customers adds transaction history that improves underwriting for the next. The company says it leads Brazil in primary banking relationships, and that customers who bank primarily with Nu default at about half the portfolio average. Those two claims show how the moat reaches the credit line. AI is also cutting costs: agents now handle more than 60% of customer-support conversations in Brazil. The weakness is that cheap distribution makes it cheap to lend, and lending to Brazil's mass market is a cyclical business however good the model is.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Unsecured consumer credit in one country. Cards and personal loans make up $36.3B of the $39.4B book, and Brazil holds about 85% of customers. The 90+ NPL of 6.9% is already up 0.4pp YoY, and management is deliberately moving into higher-risk segments. A rise in Brazilian unemployment would hit the largest revenue line and the cost of credit at the same time.
02
Rate and currency sensitivity. Float income depends on Brazil's high policy rate. Results are translated into USD from the real and the peso. In Q2 2026 reported growth beat FX-neutral growth by about 17 points. That tailwind can reverse.
03
Founder control. Vélez holds 74.4% of the votes on a much smaller economic stake. Class A holders cannot change strategy, the board or capital allocation. Class B holders also have pre-emptive rights to keep their roughly 25% of shares in any new issue.
04
Expansion spend. Mexico now runs as a full bank, Colombia is still scaling, the US launched on September 10, 2026 through a partner bank, and Brazil is adding a banking licence by acquisition. Each carries upfront cost. The efficiency ratio's 1.9pp QoQ rise was partly international investment.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 19.3x trailing and about 15.8x forward earnings, you get a bank earning a 33% ROE that grew net income 49% FX-neutral in its latest quarter. ARPAC is $17.1 against a $1.0 cost to serve, and the gap is widening. The deposit base costs 88% of interbank rates, and the bank serves 139 million customers with about 10,000 employees. Mexico is now a licensed bank, and its customers monetise faster than Brazil's did. Consensus looks for a PEG of 0.42 and EPS growth of +41% then +28%. If 90+ NPLs stay near 7% while ARPAC compounds, the current price is about 12x 2027 consensus EPS for one of the most profitable banks listed anywhere.

Bear Case

A P/B of about 5x prices in a ~30% ROE indefinitely, on a book that is 92% unsecured consumer credit in an emerging market. Both NPL buckets are higher YoY, and management is deliberately lending into riskier segments. Q2's jump in risk-adjusted NIM was partly seasonal. Float income depends on Brazil's high rates, and reported growth is inflated by currency. On top of that, the company is paying to enter the US, a market where it has no data advantage. The founder holds 74% of the votes, so Class A holders cannot stop any of it. The stock has fallen 21% from its January month-end high despite record earnings, which suggests the marginal buyer already believes this case.

Top 3 Risks

01
Brazilian consumer-credit cycle. The 90+ NPL is 6.9% and the 15–90 is 4.8%, both up YoY. Credit is 61% of revenue, and cost of credit was $1.69B in a single quarter. A macro shock in Brazil hits revenue, provisions and ROE together.
02
International expansion dilutes returns. The Mexico bank conversion, Colombia, the US partner-bank launch and Nu Global all add cost ahead of revenue. The efficiency ratio rose 1.9pp QoQ, partly from this. The December 8 Investor Day should show whether management sets return hurdles for each market.
03
Rates, currency and governance. Lower Brazilian rates compress float income. A weaker real cuts USD-reported results. The dual-class structure leaves outside shareholders no say in either response.
Industry Commentary
Unsecured credit growth vs. the delinquency curve
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Nu's Q2 2026 release describes "intentional expansions into higher-risk, higher-return segments" alongside a 180bps rise in NIM to 22.9%. That is a deliberate trade: more yield for more loss. It is working so far. Risk-adjusted NIM rose to 12.4% from 9.9% a year earlier, even though the 90+ NPL rose from 6.5% to 6.9%. The company also says 90+ delinquency has improved in every income band since July 2025 while peer banks' segments worsened. The two statements fit together if the higher portfolio NPL comes from mix, meaning more lending to riskier bands, rather than from deterioration within any band. That can hold until the cycle turns. In a lending business, the vintages written in the good years are the ones that show losses in the bad years.

From fintech to bank: licences in three countries
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

In 2026 Nu is turning itself into a regulated bank across its markets. In Mexico, the CNBV granted operating authorisation as a multiple bank (6-K, July 10, 2026), and Nu launched there in August, becoming, in its words, Mexico's largest digital bank. In Brazil it agreed to buy Banco Porto Real to add a banking licence under Joint Resolution No. 17. In the US it has preliminary conditional approval for a national bank charter from the OCC, granted January 2026, and runs on a partner bank in the meantime. Bank licences mean cheaper deposits and a wider product range. They also mean more capital regulation and supervisory cost. Nu's cost advantage was built outside that regime and will now be tested inside it.

AI in underwriting and service — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

NuFormer, Nu's foundation model for financial behaviour, underwrites cards in Brazil and Mexico and unsecured loans in Brazil, with SME and Colombian cards in testing. AI agents handle more than 60% of support conversations in Brazil at or above human parity, according to the company. The bull framing: a decade of transaction data from more than 100 million customers is a proprietary training set that incumbents cannot easily copy, and it pushes down both cost to serve and credit losses. The bear framing: better models allowed the move into riskier segments, and model-driven credit has no track record through a full Brazilian recession at this scale. The test is the 90+ NPL of the 2026 vintages in 2027, and it cannot be seen yet.
