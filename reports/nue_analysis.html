01 Company Overview

Nucor is the largest steel producer in the United States and North America's largest recycler of any material. It runs electric arc furnace (EAF) minimills — furnaces that melt scrap steel with electricity rather than smelting iron ore with coke in a blast furnace — across sheet, plate, bar and structural products, plus a downstream fabrication business and an upstream raw-materials arm. FY2025 revenue was $32.49B, up 5.7% from $30.73B in FY2024; the trailing-twelve-month figure through the quarter ended October 3, 2026 is $38.83B, up 21.8%. At a $61.66B market capitalisation it ranks #204 in the S&P 500 by market value.

Read one number and you have read most of this company: the metal spread — what a ton of steel sells for minus what the scrap to make it costs. In Q3 2026 Nucor's steel mills sold to outside customers at $1,233 per ton against an average scrap and scrap-substitute cost of $421 per gross ton, a spread of roughly $812. A year earlier the same two numbers were $1,038 and $391, a spread of $647. Mill shipments over the same year rose about 12%; the spread rose about 25%; steel mill pre-tax earnings rose 125%, from $793M to $1,788M. Volume is the smaller lever. Spread is the business.

The central debate of this page: how much of the 2026 spread is policy and how much is demand. Section 232 tariffs at a 50% headline rate have suppressed imports and lifted domestic prices; separately, data-centre, grid and reshoring construction has genuinely tightened order books. The stock is +78.8% over 52 weeks (price, October 28, 2025 close to October 28, 2026 close) and now trades at 17.3x trailing earnings that are themselves still recovering off a trough. If the spread is structural, about 13x analysts’ average 2027 earnings estimate is cheap. If it is a tariff-widened peak, the trailing multiple is the wrong one to look at in either direction.

This is the melting operation and the reason the whole company works differently from an integrated mill. An EAF minimill melts scrap and direct-reduced iron with electricity. Capital cost per ton is far lower than a blast furnace; more importantly, the cost base is variable rather than fixed. A blast furnace cannot be economically banked and restarted, so an integrated producer facing weak demand keeps melting and eats the price. Nucor can idle a furnace for a shift and its largest single input cost — scrap — simply stops being purchased. That is the mechanical reason Nucor stays profitable through cycles that put integrated mills into losses: in FY2025, a trough year, Nucor still earned $1.74B net, while Cleveland-Cliffs, blast-furnace-weighted, lost $1.48B.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EAF variable cost base Scrap flow ownership (DJJ) DRI + gas vertical hedge Downstream fabrication capture Non-union, profit-sharing pay model Balance sheet: D/E 0.29 Melted-and-poured domestic status

The core advantage is cost structure shape, not cost level. Nucor is not always the lowest-cost ton in the world — imported steel from subsidised capacity often is, which is why tariffs matter. What Nucor has is a cost curve that bends with volume. Its two biggest inputs, scrap and electricity, are both bought by the ton melted. Add a compensation model in which a large share of mill pay is production-and-profit bonus, and labour flexes with output too. The result: when steel prices fall, Nucor's costs fall alongside them and margin compresses rather than inverting. FY2025 is the proof — the trough year still produced $1.74B of net income and $7.52 of EPS, and the dividend was raised for a 53rd straight year. Secondary moats: owning the scrap supply chain, having DRI as a scrap substitute, capturing the downstream fabrication margin, and a conservative balance sheet that lets the company build capacity counter-cyclically while levered competitors cannot.

Key vulnerabilities

01
Tariff dependence is real and it is policy, not moat. The Section 232 regime at a 50% headline rate is the single largest reason domestic hot-rolled prices sit where they do. Tariff rates are set by proclamation and can be changed by proclamation — the structure was rewritten on April 2, 2026 (effective April 6) into a tiered system and amended again by a June 1, 2026 proclamation. The wall is also not airtight: Nucor's own Q3 2026 slides show finished-steel imports down about 13% year over year through Q3, but the import share of the market rising from about 14% in Q4 2025 to about 17% in Q3 2026. A future administration, an adverse trade ruling, or negotiated country carve-outs could compress the spread without any change in Nucor's operations.
02
Scrap is a cost Nucor does not control. The input side of the spread is a globally traded commodity. Scrap rose from $391 to $421 per gross ton year over year while the mills' selling price rose much faster ($1,038 to $1,233) — a favourable sequence. Reverse it and the leverage runs the other way with the same violence. Rising EAF capacity across the industry is itself a source of scrap demand, and prime scrap in particular is structurally tight.
03
The capital programme is large, front-loaded and unproven. FY2026 capex guidance on the Q2 2026 call was $2.5B, about 60% for growth; nine-month spending was $1.86B. The West Virginia sheet mill made its first heat in September 2026; Nucor's Q3 slides say production begins in 2027, with a phased ramp to ~50% utilisation by year-end 2027. Pre-operating and start-up costs were $124M in Q3 2026 alone ($0.42 a share), after $120M in Q2, and CFO John Sullivan said on the Q2 call that these costs would stay elevated through the rest of 2026 and throughout 2027. That is a real earnings drag arriving regardless of where prices go, and it adds domestic sheet supply into a market whose tightness is partly policy-created.
04
Cyclicality makes valuation genuinely hard. There is no "normal" P/E for this business. FY2022 EPS was $28.79; FY2025 was $7.52 — a 3.8x swing in three years with no change in the assets. Any multiple quoted on a single year is a statement about where in the cycle you think that year sits, not about the price of the company.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — and in a commodity cyclical it tends to track the underlying steel price more than anything about the company.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $111.65 to $271.79 on October 28, 2026, NUE returned +143.4% on price alone — a compound annual rate of roughly 19.5% over the 5.0-year window, before a dividend that has been raised every year of it. Over the same dates SPY returned +68.2% and QQQ +87.9% on price. That is an excellent outcome, and it was not a straight line. The path contains a 2022 commodity spike to $154.78 (April 2022) followed by a 33% collapse to $104.41 by June 2022; a grinding recovery to a March 2024 close of $197.90; then a −44.7% bear market into the May 2025 close of $109.36 as spreads compressed and FY2024–25 earnings fell from $8.46 to $7.52 of EPS; and finally a +148.5% advance off that low, which took the October 28 close above every month-end on this chart. About two-thirds of that final leg came in 2026 ($163.11 at the December 2025 close to $271.79).

Relative performance. Over the last 52 weeks NUE is +78.8% on price (October 28, 2025 close to October 28, 2026 close), against +12.4% for SPY, an S&P 500 fund, and +14.6% for QQQ, a Nasdaq-100 fund, over the same dates. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The metal spread has widened from ~$647 to ~$812 per ton year over year while utilisation went from 85% to 89%; Q3 beat the top of Nucor's own guidance, and management expects Q4 in line with or modestly higher — so the earnings recovery has not rolled over. Consensus 2027 EPS of $21.02 puts the stock at ~12.9x on a business with 14.87% ROIC, 0.29 debt-to-equity, 53 straight years of dividend increases and a steady buyback. The West Virginia sheet mill, the new Berkeley galvanizing line and the tower expansions add earning assets into demand that management describes as broad across energy, infrastructure, data centres and reshoring, with border-wall shipments that management said on the Q2 call would continue into 2028.

Bear Case

The spread is where it is largely because a 50% tariff wall keeps out imports that would otherwise set the price — a policy input, not an operating one, rewritten twice already in 2026 (April and June) — and Nucor's own slides show the import share of the market creeping back up to about 17%. Buy at 97% of the 52-week high, after a 78.8% run, against a consensus target implying 6.7% upside, and you are paying for spreads near their best. Meanwhile the company is spending about $2.5B this year, carrying start-up costs of $124M in Q3 alone, and adding domestic sheet supply into the same tight market that created the spread. A tariff softening, a scrap spike, or a construction slowdown compresses this at a 1.85 beta.

Top 3 risks

01
Policy reversal on Section 232. The dominant swing factor in domestic steel pricing is set by executive proclamation and has already been rewritten twice in 2026 (April 2 and June 1). Carve-outs, bilateral deals or a rate reduction would compress the selling-price side of the spread with no offsetting change on the cost side.
02
Scrap cost inflation into new EAF supply. Nucor's input is a globally traded commodity it does not control, and the industry-wide shift to electric arc furnaces — including Nucor's own new capacity — increases competition for prime scrap. A scrap move that outruns pricing collapses the spread quickly; the $421/gross ton figure (Q3 2026) is the number to watch each quarter.
03
Execution and timing on the capital programme. The West Virginia mill begins production in 2027 with a phased ramp to ~50% utilisation by year-end 2027, and start-up costs ran $124M in Q3 2026. If the cycle turns before the ramp completes, Nucor will be bringing a new sheet mill into a falling price — the classic way cyclicals destroy returns on otherwise sound projects.
Industry Commentary
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Tariff policy is the price-setter
Section 232 went to a 50% global rate in June 2025, and on April 2, 2026 (effective April 6) was restructured into a tiered schedule: 50% on most steel articles, 25% on a second list of goods containing steel, 10% on goods made abroad entirely from U.S.-melted-and-poured steel, and capped transitional rates on some equipment until January 1, 2028 — with duty now assessed on the full customs value of a product rather than only its metal content. A June 1, 2026 proclamation (effective June 8) amended it again: lower rates on some agricultural and HVAC equipment, some goods dropped from coverage, and a 10% rate on certain foreign-made equipment containing at least 85% U.S.-melted-and-poured steel or U.S. aluminum by weight. Nucor's Q3 slides show the effect and its limits: finished-steel imports down about 13% year over year through Q3 2026, but import market share up from about 14% to about 17% since Q4 2025, and imports from South Korea up 57%. Nucor's monthly price chart turned within a month of the June 2025 rate rise and made its largest single monthly gain (+33.2%) in April 2026. That correlation is the honest framing of this stock: it is a bet on continued protection as much as on industrial demand.
EMERGING TAILWIND
Data centres, grid and reshoring
Management describes demand as broad across energy, infrastructure, data centres and reshoring; its Q3 slides say data-centre construction spending doubled from 2024 to 2026 and that North American transmission spending passed about $65B in 2025. Nucor has been building directly into it — utility transmission tower plants, with a $165M expansion in Indiana and Alabama announced in October 2026 and a towers backlog running into Q1 2028. This matters because it is the one demand source that is not policy-dependent: a data centre needs structural steel, rebar, and grid steel regardless of tariff rates. Flat-rolled shipments to automotive customers are up 4% year to date (Q3 slides). If the tariff regime softens, the question becomes whether this demand is deep enough to hold the spread on its own.
ONGOING HEADWIND
Consolidation and new domestic supply
Nippon Steel completed its acquisition of U.S. Steel on June 18, 2025 at $55 a share in cash, and U.S. Steel's shares stopped trading that day; the U.S. government holds a "golden share" with consent rights over major decisions, and Nippon has committed to invest $11B in the U.S. by 2028. A well-capitalised foreign parent now stands behind a large domestic integrated producer. At the same time Nucor, Steel Dynamics and others are commissioning new domestic sheet capacity into a market whose tightness is partly tariff-created. Domestic supply is going up while the import wall is what holds the price up — a combination that historically ends spread cycles.
BULL VS BEAR — HOW TO FRAME IT
Is $812 the new floor or the old ceiling?
Everything reduces to one question. The bull says a ~$812 spread at 89% utilisation is the new mid-cycle, because the import wall is structural, the demand is grid- and data-centre-driven, and Nucor's EAF cost curve means it earns well even if the spread reverts toward $650. The bear says $812 is a policy-inflated peak, that FY2025's $7.52 EPS is closer to true mid-cycle than the $19.53 analysts expect for 2026, and that paying 17.3x trailing near a 52-week high for a 1.85-beta cyclical is the textbook cycle-top error. Note that both cases are consistent with the same verified facts. The discriminator is not in the financials — it is in the tariff schedule and the scrap price, and both are observable quarter by quarter.
