01 Company Overview

NVIDIA designs the accelerated-computing platform — GPUs, the CUDA software stack, NVLink interconnect and networking silicon — on which essentially all frontier AI training and most large-scale AI inference currently runs. In fiscal 2026 (ended January 25, 2026) the company reported revenue of $215.9 billion, up 65.5% year over year, with GAAP net income of $120.1 billion and diluted EPS of $4.90. The most recent quarter — Q3 FY2027, ended October 25, 2026 and reported November 17 — brought revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% year over year and 20% sequentially, taking trailing-twelve-month revenue to $361.8 billion. At a $3.52 trillion market capitalization on November 19, down from $5.12 trillion at the previous edition, it is about a third (32.6%) of the $10.81 trillion StockAnalysis "Semiconductors" industry aggregate.

Two questions now sit on top of the stock. The first is old: whether the capex cycle funding NVIDIA is durable or self-feeding. Gross margin is near 75% and the company holds more cash than debt, but it now also carries $390 billion of purchase commitments and $118 billion of guarantees, $105 billion of them credit support for one data-center campus that will be leased to OpenAI (Q3 call). Four customers accounted for 61% of revenue in the October 2025 quarter, and management expects the major frontier labs to supply about 15% of Data Center revenue this year. The second question is new: who runs the company. Jensen Huang, co-founder and CEO since 1993, resigned on November 12, 2026, four days after the board opened an investigation into reports that he had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort; CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO while the board looks for a permanent one inside and outside the company. "Our momentum is real," she said in the Q3 release, and the numbers bear her out. The share price, down 28.7% in November to the 19th, is pricing the second question.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
CUDA Software Lock-In NVLink Scale-Up Fabric Rack-Scale Systems Integration CoWoS & HBM Supply Priority Annual Product Cadence ~5M Developer Installed Base Networking Attach Rate

The moat is CUDA, not the silicon. Most bull and bear arguments get this wrong in opposite directions. On raw silicon, NVIDIA's lead is real but bounded — AMD's MI355X ships 288GB of HBM3e and Google's TPU v7 Ironwood delivers 4.6 PFLOPS of FP8 per chip, both credible on paper. What competitors cannot replicate in a product cycle is nineteen years of accumulated CUDA-native software: the kernel libraries, the compiler toolchain, the framework integrations, the cluster orchestration, and — most durably — the several million engineers whose fingers already know the API. Migration cost is not a one-time port; it is an ongoing tax on every new model architecture. NVIDIA has spent the last three years extending this from a chip-level moat to a rack-level one: NVLink/NVSwitch turns 72 GPUs into a single coherent memory domain, which means the competitive unit is no longer "a GPU" but "a rack that a competitor must reproduce end to end, including the interconnect and the software that schedules across it." Networking revenue above $21 billion in Q3 FY2027 (per the call), up from $14.8 billion in Q1, is the financial signature of that shift. Layered on top is supply: NVIDIA is TSMC's priority CoWoS advanced-packaging customer and a first-call buyer of HBM capacity, which functions as a real constraint on how fast any challenger can scale even with a competitive design.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. A company built by its founder now has an interim CEO and an open investigation. Jensen Huang co-founded NVIDIA in 1993 and ran it for 33 years; he drove the decade-long CUDA bet that built the moat. He resigned as President, CEO and director on November 12, 2026, four days after the board opened an independent investigation, overseen by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel, into reports that he had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort; the board said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised. Colette Kress is interim CEO and CFO at once, Heidrick & Struggles is searching inside and outside the company, and the board has added a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee. The Q3 release's risk language now lists the leadership transition, the committee's investigation and "government and regulatory inquiries"; on November 17 The Wall Street Journal reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that the SEC has opened a civil probe examining in part whether NVIDIA's disclosures misled investors, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent asked whether NVIDIA had knowingly sold advanced chips to China to lift its revenue (InvestmentNews). Kress told the call it is "business as usual", that customers, suppliers and partners are "100% behind us", and that NVIDIA has "secured memory" and advanced packaging. The risks are slower decisions, departures of senior engineers, and a successor without Huang's standing with customers and with Washington.

2. Customer concentration is extreme and getting worse. In Q3 FY2026 (the quarter ended October 2025), four direct customers each exceeded 10% of revenue: 22%, 15%, 13% and 11% — 61% combined (NVIDIA's disclosure, as tabulated by The Motley Fool). A year earlier only three customers were above 10%, and the largest was 12%. For the full fiscal year 2026, one direct customer was 22% of revenue and another 14% (10-K). Receivables more than doubled from $38.5 billion at January 25, 2026 to $81.0 billion at October 25, 2026 (Q3 FY2027 release). In NVIDIA's disclosure, "direct customer" often means a contract manufacturer or system integrator rather than the ultimate hyperscaler, which makes the true end-customer concentration harder to see, not lower. A single large buyer deferring a build by one quarter is a multi-billion-dollar revenue event.

3. Circular financing: NVIDIA's money in its own customers. The bear case here is specific, not vague. NVIDIA has taken equity positions in and made commitments to companies that are themselves large buyers of its chips. As of the February 2026 close of OpenAI's funding round, NVIDIA's OpenAI stake was finalized at $30 billion — down from the up-to-$100 billion structure announced in September 2025 — alongside roughly $10 billion committed to Anthropic, a ~$3 billion CoreWeave stake, and several billion more across NVentures portfolio companies. At the peak of the announced (not funded) commitments, Tom Tunguz's October 2025 analysis put direct investments at $110 billion plus $15 billion of GPU-backed debt exposure — 67% of then-LTM revenue, versus Lucent's 1999 vendor-financing book at 24% of revenue. Two observations cut against the alarm: NVIDIA's OpenAI commitment was reduced by roughly 70% rather than expanded, and equity-securities gains fell to $1.8 billion in Q3 FY2027. Two cut with it: the support outside the balance sheet has grown — by the Q3 call, purchase commitments stood at $390 billion, neocloud commitments at $32 billion and guarantees at $118 billion, including $105 billion of credit support for land, power and buildings at the PORTS-Pike campus, which will host NVIDIA systems under a 20-year lease to OpenAI — and the largest customers' AI spending is partly financed by capital markets rather than by cash from AI products. Management says it will stay "disciplined and selective" in that support. Put plainly: part of the demand depends on the same enthusiasm that sets NVIDIA's own share price.

4. Hyperscaler custom silicon and the inference split. NVIDIA's largest customers are simultaneously its most motivated competitors. Anthropic is deploying more than one million Google TPU v7 Ironwood chips for Claude inference. AWS is assembling Trainium 3 into UltraClusters of up to one million units and claiming ~50% cost reduction versus equivalent NVIDIA instances on supported workloads. Microsoft's Maia 200 entered service in early 2026, and Meta's MTIA v3 was due in production in mid-2026. Broadcom's OpenAI partnership targets 10GW of deployed capacity by 2029. Training and inference are different competitive markets: NVIDIA's share exceeds 90% in training, where flexibility across rapidly changing model architectures is worth paying for, but is estimated at 60–75% in inference, where workloads are stable, cost-per-token dominates, and a fixed-function ASIC can win. As the industry's compute mix shifts from training toward inference — which it must, if AI is to generate revenue — the contestable share of the market grows. One third-party projection has NVIDIA's inference-specific share falling to 20–30% by 2028 (est.). Custom ASICs are modelled to compound at ~44.6% versus ~16.1% for general-purpose GPUs.

5. China: policy risk without policy revenue. NVIDIA's Q4 guide again assumes zero Data Center compute revenue from China, so there is little left in the model to lose. Q3 did include $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data-center sales to China; management says those shipments have stopped. On November 16 the Commerce Department issued a rule requiring licences for advanced AI chips sent to data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, and for remote access by China-headquartered companies (Fortune). The company has already absorbed one large China-related inventory charge (see One-Off Items below). See the Industry Commentary section for the rules.

Notable One-Off Items

The gap between GAAP and non-GAAP earnings nearly closed in Q3 FY2027. GAAP net income was $65.287 billion against non-GAAP $64.870 billion, and GAAP diluted EPS $2.70 against non-GAAP $2.68. The main difference is the line "Gains from equity securities, net": $1.767 billion pre-tax in Q3, after $15.936 billion in Q1 and $7.771 billion in Q2 — $25.5 billion for the nine months, against $3.4 billion a year earlier. These are mark-ups on NVIDIA's stakes in other companies passing through the income statement, and they are part of the trailing EPS of $9.31 ($7.91 at the previous edition): Q3's gain was small, but it replaced a $1.4 billion gain in the year-earlier quarter, so the twelve-month total did not shrink. Since Q1 FY2027 NVIDIA's non-GAAP figures include stock-based compensation ($2.09 billion in Q3), so the two measures now differ mainly by these gains, acquisition-related costs and their tax effect. The feedback loop remains: if AI company valuations fall, NVIDIA's reported GAAP earnings fall with them, at the moment its operating results would also be under pressure.

The China H20 charge is fully in the base. The Q3 FY2027 release footnotes net H20 charges of $4.3 billion in the first nine months of fiscal 2026 and an insignificant amount in fiscal 2027. That charge pulled full-year FY2026 GAAP gross margin to 71.1% against a 75.0% Q4 exit rate, and it is part of why nine-month GAAP gross margin rose 5.6 points, from 69.3% to 74.9%. From Q4 the comparisons are clean, and the guided 71–72% is a real step-down.

Capital return, and two large uses of cash. In September the board added $150 billion to the repurchase authorization, without expiration; Q3 FY2027 then returned $28.1 billion ($22.1 billion in buybacks, $6.0 billion in dividends), leaving about $227.3 billion of authorization at quarter-end, which management plans to use by the end of fiscal 2028 (Q3 call). Diluted shares were 24.19 billion in Q3, down 1.2% from 24.48 billion a year earlier. Separately, NVIDIA agreed on September 2 to acquire Hugging Face for about $11.9 billion plus up to about $1.0 billion of retention equity, expected to close in the first half of 2027 (8-K, September 3), and it raised $24.9 billion of new debt in the first nine months of the fiscal year.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are being asked to pay 8.8× fiscal 2028 consensus earnings (15.7× trailing) for a business that grew revenue 103% in the latest quarter, guided the next one to $130 billion, earns a 75% gross margin, generated $149 billion of free cash flow over twelve months and holds more cash than debt. The November fall was about leadership and export rules, not the numbers: Q3 beat its own guide by $7.8 billion, Vera Rubin brought in nearly $20 billion in its first quarter, networking passed $21 billion, and management raised its fiscal 2028 growth outlook to about 75% with no China compute revenue assumed. Management says customers, suppliers and partners are "100% behind us", and the buyback has $227 billion of authorization left — about 6.5% of the market value — to be used by the end of fiscal 2028. CUDA and the rack-level system (NVLink, Spectrum-X, now Vera CPUs) still bind every competitor. If the company runs as well without its founder as the Q3 numbers suggest, a 40% fall from the 52-week high is the opportunity, not the warning.

Bear Case

A founder-led company lost its founder in four days, with a board investigation still open, a reported SEC inquiry and a political fight over export controls; the next CEO will not have Jensen Huang's standing with customers, suppliers or Washington, and the search has no deadline. Four customers were 61% of revenue in the October 2025 quarter, receivables have more than doubled since January to $81 billion, and NVIDIA's own commitments now run to $390 billion of purchases and $118 billion of guarantees, $105 billion of it credit support for one OpenAI campus. Gross margin is guided down to 71–72%. At a 2.25 beta, a hyperscaler pushing a build one quarter to the right is a double-digit revenue miss. Meanwhile custom silicon is deployed at scale — over a million TPU v7 chips at Anthropic alone, Trainium 3 UltraClusters, Maia 200 — and it is concentrated in inference, the workload that must grow fastest for AI to pay for itself. NVIDIA's share exceeds 90% in training but is estimated at 60–75% in inference, with projections of 20–30% by 2028. And the stock has shown how far it can fall: 62.9% from November 2021 to September 2022.

Top 3 Risks
01
Leadership and the investigation. Jensen Huang's resignation removed the founder who set NVIDIA's strategy for 33 years. Until a permanent CEO is named and the independent committee reports, the company is run by an interim CEO who is also its CFO, and the Q3 release now lists the transition, the investigation and government and regulatory inquiries among its risks. A successor unfamiliar to customers, or a finding that touches export compliance, would hit both orders and the price investors pay for each dollar of earnings.
02
Customer concentration converting a capex pause into a revenue collapse. With 61% of revenue from four direct customers in the October 2025 quarter, and receivables up from $38.5 billion in January to $81.0 billion in October, NVIDIA has no diversification cushion. The risk is step-function, not gradual erosion: a single large buyer deferring a build by one quarter is a multi-billion-dollar revenue event.
03
Self-feeding demand: NVIDIA's money behind its customers. NVIDIA's $40 billion of stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic, plus CoreWeave and NVentures positions, mean that a fall in AI company valuations hits reported GAAP earnings directly; nine-month equity gains were $25.5 billion. Beyond the stakes, the Q3 call put purchase commitments at $390 billion and guarantees at $118 billion, most of it credit support for one data-center campus leased to OpenAI. It runs both ways, and the downside version has never been tested.
  Industry Commentary
The AI Capex Cycle: Durable or Self-Feeding?
ONGOING HEADWIND
Early in 2026, combined hyperscaler capital expenditure for the year was tracking at $660–690 billion, roughly 75% of it AI infrastructure, and Alphabet has since raised its own 2026 range to $195–205 billion in July, up from a top end of $190 billion (Bloomberg). Consensus has NVIDIA's FY2027 revenue at $423.9 billion (+96.3%, StockAnalysis), which means the sell side expects NVIDIA to capture a very large share of that incremental spend — the Q3 call put the cloud industry's backlog at $2.5 trillion and NVIDIA's own guarantees at $118 billion, and the Q2 FY2027 release added a new channel: independent compute-financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR intended to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital, "subject to definitive agreements." The durability question is whether hyperscaler AI spending is funded by AI revenue or by balance-sheet capacity and enthusiasm. The answer today is: partly both. What makes this self-feeding rather than merely cyclical is that NVIDIA's own $40 billion in customer-adjacent equity, its GAAP earnings marked to private AI valuations, and its share price are all functions of the same sentiment variable. Two things to watch, both more informative than the capex headline: depreciation schedule extensions (lengthening useful-life assumptions on GPU fleets is how a capex slowdown gets hidden for a year), and whether hyperscalers begin financing purchases with debt structured against the GPUs themselves.
Custom Silicon Reaches Deployment Scale
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
2026 is the year hyperscaler ASICs stopped being a slide and became a deployment. Anthropic is running more than one million Google TPU v7 Ironwood chips for Claude inference, on TSMC 3nm at 4.6 PFLOPS FP8 per chip. AWS is building Trainium 3 UltraClusters of up to one million units and claiming ~50% cost reduction versus equivalent NVIDIA instances on supported workloads. Microsoft's Maia 200 shipped in early 2026 at over 10 PFLOPS FP4. Meta's MTIA v3 was due in production in mid-2026, with MTIA v4 sampling late in the year. Broadcom's OpenAI partnership targets 10GW by 2029. Aggregate custom-silicon spend runs ~$25 billion in 2026 against ~$8 billion in 2024, compounding at a modelled 44.6% versus 16.1% for general-purpose GPUs. NVIDIA's overall accelerator share is variously estimated at "over 90%" and at 75–81% depending on whether captive silicon is counted — the disagreement between those two figures is the debate. The training/inference mix decides it: NVIDIA holds >90% of training and an estimated 60–75% of inference, and inference is where the volume must ultimately be.
China: Zero in the Model, Optionality in the Stock — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND
Current state of the rules: on November 16, 2026 the Commerce Department issued an interim final rule requiring licences for advanced AI chips sent to data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, and for remote access by China-headquartered companies; Fortune reported that Reuters counted roughly 600,000 H200s sitting in Hong Kong while Beijing withholds import approval. NVIDIA sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data-center products to China in Q3 FY2027 and says it has stopped. Earlier, BIS revised its license review policy on January 13, 2026, following a December 8, 2025 presidential announcement, moving H200-class parts (and AMD's MI325X) from presumption of denial to case-by-case review subject to three conditions — no reduction in capacity available to US customers, documented Chinese-purchaser export compliance procedures, and independent third-party testing in the US. Third-party analysis puts the ceiling near 850,000 H200-equivalent units capped at 50% of cumulative US sales, with a 25% Section 232 tariff applied. Blackwell-class parts remain under presumption of denial. Bloomberg reported a small approved H200 license on February 26, 2026. The AI OVERWATCH Act (H.R. 6875), advanced 42–2 on January 21, 2026, would statutorily ban Blackwell exports for two years.

Bull framing: NVIDIA guides China Data Center compute revenue at zero. There is nothing in the model to lose and a claimed ~$50 billion annual China AI chip market to gain. ByteDance-led 2026 orders reportedly total upward of $14 billion. Any partial reopening is upside to consensus, and the stock prices none of it.

Bear framing: A market permanently closed to the highest-performance parts is a market where Huawei, Cambricon and domestic alternatives are being actively subsidized into existence — creating a credible non-US competitor with a captive billion-user market and no CUDA dependency. NVIDIA has already absorbed $4.3 billion of net H20 charges in the first nine months of fiscal 2026 (Q3 FY2027 release footnote) — large enough to pull the full-year FY2026 gross margin to 71.1% against a 75.0% exit rate. The optionality is real but it is not free: the longer the door stays shut, the more of the moat gets rebuilt on the other side of it.
