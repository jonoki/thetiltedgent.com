01 Company Overview

Most people hear "News Corp" and picture a newspaper. The company that trades under NWSA is closer to a holding company with four roughly equal legs: Dow Jones (The Wall Street Journal, Barron's, MarketWatch, plus B2B risk-and-compliance and energy data), Digital Real Estate Services (a majority stake in Australia's REA Group and the US site Realtor.com), Book Publishing (HarperCollins), and News Media (The Times and The Sun in the UK, the New York Post, and the Australian mastheads). Fiscal 2026 — the year to June 30, 2026 — produced revenue of $9.03B, up 7%, and Total Segment EBITDA of $1.63B, up 15%.

The company exists in its current form because of a split. On June 28, 2013 the original News Corporation renamed itself 21st Century Fox and spun off the publishing assets as the "new" News Corp, which took the old company's S&P 500 seat the same day. It lists two share classes on Nasdaq: Class A (NWSA), the larger line and the subject of this report, and Class B (NWS), the voting line through which the Murdoch family controls the company. Both are S&P 500 index lines — the second was added in September 2015 — and they share one set of accounts, so every business figure here applies to both. At the September 21 close the voting B shares cost 10.6% more than the A shares. That gap is the market's price for a vote.

The story the market keeps coming back to is a recombination. The Murdochs split News Corp from Fox, tried to put them back together in 2022, and dropped the idea in January 2023. Court documents from the family succession case, reported on August 27, 2026, show the family discussed it; Fox said on August 28 that the references concerned a merger considered in 2022 and that there have been no merger discussions since. The family trust settlement of September 8, 2025 left a new trust controlled by Lachlan Murdoch with 33% of News Corp's Class B shares. NWSA holders get the economics but, outside a few limited circumstances, no vote, so any deal will be decided by other people.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

WSJ / Barron's brands 6.7M paying news subscribers REA's Australian listings position HarperCollins backlist Proprietary archives for AI licensing Net cash ($2.10B cash vs $1.99B borrowings)

The moat is content that people pay for and that nobody else owns. The WSJ subscriber base, the HarperCollins backlist and REA's listings network all took decades to build and none can be copied. The AI licensing strategy rests on the same asset: News Corp has signed partnerships with OpenAI and Meta and is suing Perplexity and Brave over alleged unauthorised use of its content. Where the moat is weaker is the ad-funded tabloids in News Media, which have large audiences but thin margins, and Realtor.com, which is growing revenue while losing users.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
You own the economics, not the votes. NWSA holders do not control the company. The trust created in the September 2025 settlement holds 33% of the Class B stock, and court documents reported in August 2026 show the family has discussed recombining with Fox as recently as 2022. If that comes back, the terms will be set by people whose interests may not match yours.
02
The biggest profit engine depends on housing. Digital Real Estate made $741M of segment EBITDA, the largest of any segment. It depends on listing volumes in Australia and the US, both of which move with mortgage rates, and the CFO has already flagged that risk.
03
AI may buy the content or may copy it. Licensing deals are a new source of revenue, but the lawsuits against Perplexity and Brave show the other possibility: AI answers that use the reporting and pay nothing for it. If they win, News Media, with a 6% margin, has little room to absorb lost traffic.
04
Reported earnings are hard to read. Minority interests in REA, discontinued Foxtel income in the prior year, and write-offs in publishing all distort the reported figures. StockAnalysis shows EPS down 50% while the company reports continuing-operations EPS up 22.6%. Both are correct because they measure different things, and a reader who only sees the headline figure can easily misjudge the year.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, Foxtel: the Australian pay-TV business was classified as discontinued, and the prior-year Q4 included $690M from it, per the results release. That explains why StockAnalysis shows FY2025 EPS of $2.07 and FY2026 EPS "down" 50%. FY2022 revenue of $10.39B also appears to still include Foxtel (likely, not verified), so the −22.9% FY2023 revenue decline is a change in presentation, not a collapse. Second, buybacks: News Corp repurchased $643M of stock in FY2026, versus $150M in FY2025. The CFO said this was helped by about $380M of Foxtel shareholder-loan repayments, so that pace may not be repeatable. Third, publishing write-offs: $16M of inventory and a $13M receivable, which explain HarperCollins' 3% full-year EBITDA decline.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The market still prices this as a newspaper company. Dow Jones grew EBITDA 13% with Q4 margins at 28.1%, Risk & Compliance grew 16%, REA grew revenue 21% in the fourth quarter, and Total Segment EBITDA rose 15% to $1.63B. Gross margin has risen four years running. The balance sheet is effectively net cash, buybacks quadrupled to $643M, and there is no crowded short (2.13%). AI licensing turns the archive into revenue. A Fox recombination could crystallise value, and the Aug 27 report moved News Corp up, not down. At 22.5x forward earnings with consensus targets 22% higher, the valuation is fair for a business whose profit mix keeps improving.

Bear Case

At 28.8x trailing EPS this is not cheap, and a large part of the profit belongs to REA's other shareholders. The strongest segment depends on housing, and the CFO is already warning about mortgage rates. The FY2026 buyback pace used about $380M of one-off Foxtel loan repayments, so it will not recur at that size. News Media earns a 6% margin on huge audiences that AI answers may divert. NWSA holders have no votes, so any recombination terms will be set by the family. The stock is back at its September 2025 high, which already reflects much of the good news.

Top 3 Risks

01
Governance and a recombination on the family's terms. The Class B trust (33%) and the chairman decide. The 2022 plan to re-merge with Fox failed after investors balked (Reuters), and court records show the idea could come back.
02
Housing-cycle exposure in the largest profit segment. Digital Real Estate made $741M of EBITDA. A drop in Australian or US listings would hit the segment that has been driving group growth.
03
AI disintermediation of news traffic. Licensing deals and lawsuits are the defence. If AI summaries cut referral traffic faster than licensing revenue grows, News Media's thin 6% margin has little room before it turns to a loss.
Industry Commentary
Publishers and AI: licence or lawsuit
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

News Corp is pursuing both options against AI companies at once. It licenses its content to some (OpenAI, Meta — of which Thomson said "These are not merely transactional arrangements; they are partnerships") and sues others (Perplexity, Brave). The outcome will decide whether decades of journalism and a large book backlist become a licensing income stream or free training data. The company's public position, per its CEO, is that without professional content AI output is "a slimy sea of AI slop." Management says it is in advanced talks for more deals. The revenue from licensing is not broken out in the sources fetched, so its size cannot yet be measured.

Two share classes, one family
STRUCTURAL

The S&P 500 holds both of News Corp's lines, NWSA and NWS, and splits the company's index weight between them. Only three S&P 500 companies have two listed share classes in the index: Alphabet, Fox and News Corp. The Class B line closed at $32.86 against $29.70 for Class A on September 21, 2026, a 10.6% premium for the vote. The premium reflects the market's view on control events: a recombination, a buyout or another trust dispute would be decided by Class B holders.

Sum of the parts
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull argument, set out in a June 2026 TIKR note, is that "a business compounding EBITDA at double-digit rates...should not trade at 9x NTM EBITDA." Dow Jones' professional-data lines, a majority stake in a listed Australian property portal and a large publisher are each worth more to a specialist buyer than they are inside a conglomerate run by a family. The bear response is that the discount exists precisely because of that control structure, and it will stay unless the family chooses to break up or sell. That makes governance, not earnings, the factor most likely to move the stock.
