01 Company Overview

Realty Income is the world's largest net-lease REIT, owning 15,588 properties leased to 1,798 clients across 92 industries, spanning 353 million square feet in the United States, the United Kingdom and continental Europe. The business is built on triple-net leases (leases where the tenant, not the landlord, pays property taxes, building insurance and maintenance), which strips operating variability out of the rent stream and produces near-bond-like cash flow: adjusted EBITDA margin runs around 95%. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $5.76 billion, up 9.2% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue is $6.07 billion (+10.7%). The company is an S&P 500 constituent, ranks #8 among global REITs by market capitalisation and #424 among all public companies worldwide, and has declared 674 consecutive monthly dividends with 115 consecutive quarterly increases.

The correct earnings metric here is not EPS but AFFO per share. GAAP EPS of $1.37 is suppressed by roughly $3 billion of annual non-cash depreciation on buildings that in practice are not losing economic value; AFFO per share was $1.09 in Q2 2026 (+3.8% YoY) and $2.22 for the first half (+5.2%), with full-year 2026 guidance raised to $4.44–$4.45. At $63.17 that is roughly 14.2x forward P/AFFO and an AFFO dividend payout of about 73%.

Portfolio Mix

The core franchise: single-tenant, freestanding retail buildings leased on long-duration triple-net terms to service-oriented and non-discretionary retailers. The tenant pays taxes, insurance and maintenance, so Realty Income collects rent with almost no property-level operating drag — this is why gross margin runs above 92% and adjusted EBITDA margin near 95%. The largest industries by annualized rent are grocery (11.1%), convenience stores (9.4%) and home improvement (6.4%); the largest individual clients are Dollar General (3.3%), 7-Eleven (3.1%) and Walgreens (3.0%). No single client exceeds 3.5% of rent, which is the practical answer to concentration risk. Re-leasing economics remain positive: the blended rent recapture rate (new rent on a re-let or renewed space as a percentage of the old rent) was 102.7% in Q2, with renewals at 104.6% and international re-lets at 112.9% — meaning expiring leases are being replaced at higher, not lower, rents. Long-run recapture has averaged 104% on renewals since 2013 and 108% on new clients since 2020.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE — 15,588 PROPERTIES A-RATED BALANCE SHEET ACCESS MONTHLY DIVIDEND BRAND 115 CONSECUTIVE QUARTERLY RAISES GLOBAL SOURCING — $32.5B/YR EUROPEAN FIRST-MOVER PRIVATE CAPITAL PLATFORM

The moat is almost entirely a cost-of-capital and sourcing moat, and understanding this is the whole investment case. A net-lease REIT does not manufacture anything; it buys buildings at a cap rate and funds them with a blend of equity and debt. The spread between the acquisition yield and the weighted-average cost of that capital is the earnings growth. Realty Income's advantages are therefore: (1) a balance sheet that borrows cheaply — 91.8% of debt at fixed rates, 5.8-year weighted-average maturity, fixed-charge coverage of 4.7x, net debt to annualized pro forma adjusted EBITDAre of 5.4x (5.2x including unsettled ATM forwards), and net debt of just 34.3% of enterprise value; (2) sourcing scale no competitor can match — $32.5 billion of opportunities sourced in 2026 against roughly $10 billion of guided investment, an 8% selectivity rate that lets the company decline mispriced deals rather than chase them; (3) the ability to write a single cheque for a $2.6 billion quarter, including sale-leasebacks and portfolio deals that smaller peers simply cannot underwrite; and (4) a retail shareholder base bonded to the monthly dividend, which historically supports a valuation premium and therefore a lower equity cost. The equity-cost point cuts both ways and is discussed below. Note the structural sensitivity: the 2026 guided investment volume of $10 billion at a ~7.3% initial cash yield against a $59.75 billion equity base means growth is highly dependent on the price at which new capital is raised.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
The cost-of-capital trap. Net-lease REITs grow by issuing equity at or above NAV and deploying it into property at a positive spread. When the share price falls, the implied cost of equity rises; if it rises above the achievable acquisition cap rate, accretive external growth stops entirely. Realty Income has been living this since 2022 — public equity has fallen to just 18% of year-to-date funding versus a 47% three-year average. The private-capital and insurance-capital workarounds are genuinely clever, but they are a response to a constraint, not evidence the constraint is gone. If the shares de-rate again, the $10 billion investment guidance is the first thing that gets cut.
B
Interest-rate sensitivity is structural, not cyclical. Because triple-net rent is contractual, long-dated (~8.6-year WALT) and escalates slowly (same-store rent growth of just 1.0–1.2%), the security behaves like a long-duration corporate bond with a small inflation kicker. Its price is therefore dominated by the level of long-term real yields rather than by operating performance. The stock fell from $59.91 (Jul 2022) to $40.88 (Oct 2023) — a 31.8% drawdown — while occupancy never left the high 98s. Nothing about the portfolio explains that move; the 10-year Treasury does.
C
Tenant credit in structurally challenged retail. The top three clients — Dollar General (3.3%), 7-Eleven (3.1%) and Walgreens (3.0%) — sit in discount retail, convenience and pharmacy, three formats under real pressure from e-commerce, store-footprint rationalisation and, in pharmacy, a well-documented industry contraction. Only 34% of annualized rent comes from investment-grade rated clients. Management carries a watch list in the "high 5% area" covering 137 tenants and guides to roughly 40 bps of credit loss on rental revenue. First-half 2026 provisions for impairment were $144.4 million.
D
Slow organic growth and dilution risk from scale. Same-store rent growth of 1.0–1.2% means essentially all AFFO growth must be bought. At $59.75 billion of equity value, moving the needle requires ever-larger deployment — $10 billion this year, sourced from a market where cap rates compress whenever rates fall. The law of large numbers is real: AFFO per share growth guidance of ~4% for 2026 is respectable but is roughly half what the company delivered in its mid-cap years, and the new adjacencies (data centres, gaming, Europe) exist substantially to fight that decay.

Notable One-Off Items

Several non-recurring items materially distort reported GAAP results and should be stripped out when assessing run-rate earnings — which is precisely what the AFFO adjustment does:

1
Provisions for impairment — $54.2M in Q2, $144.4M for the first half. Non-cash write-downs on properties where carrying value exceeds recoverable value, typically driven by tenant credit deterioration or vacancy in weaker retail formats. This is the single largest drag on the GAAP net-income line and the main reason net margin (20.9%) looks poor for a business with 92.7% gross margin.
2
Merger, transaction and other costs — $2.1M in Q2, $12.8M for the first half. Residual integration and transaction expense associated with the Spirit Realty combination and the ongoing joint-venture and fund formations (Cloud Capital, Apollo, GIC, US Core Plus Fund). Small in absolute terms but a reminder that the platform build-out carries real friction cost.
3
Foreign currency and derivative losses — $(8.8)M in Q2, $(25.8)M for the first half. A direct consequence of the 20.5% of the portfolio sitting in the UK and continental Europe. The company hedges, but translation and mark-to-market noise now flows through results every quarter and will grow with the European book.
4
Gains on sales of real estate — $38.3M in Q2, $73.9M for the first half. Offsetting the impairments. Realty Income sold 80 properties for $160.7M in Q2 and 177 properties for $348.6M in the first half — active portfolio pruning, not distress. Gains are excluded from AFFO.
5
Lease termination income — guided at $45–$50M for 2026, only ~$1M booked in Q2. Lumpy by nature. Because the full-year guide is unchanged, the implication is that most of it is expected in the second half, which flatters the H2 AFFO comparison relative to H1.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 close of $49.13 to $63.17 on August 20, 2026, the shares delivered a price return of +28.6%, or roughly 5.2% annualised. Adding dividends — an estimated $15 or so per share over the period, based on the current $3.25 annualised rate and the company's ~4% dividend growth CAGR — lifts the simple (non-reinvested) total return to approximately +59%, or about 9.7% a year. The dividend figure is an estimate, not a verified cumulative distribution total. Against the S&P 500, which by any measure compounded far faster over this window on the back of the mega-cap technology and AI trade, O materially underperformed — the index return was not verified in this session and no specific figure is asserted here.

The shape of the chart is the story, and it is almost entirely a rates story. The stock made its cycle high of $59.91 in July 2022, fell 31.8% to $40.88 by October 2023 as long-term Treasury yields peaked, and has spent nearly three years climbing back — regaining $59.91 only in January 2026. Across that entire 31.8% drawdown, occupancy never left the high-98% range, the dividend was raised every quarter, and rent recapture stayed above 100%. Nothing in the operating business explains the move. That is the clearest possible demonstration of the point made in Section 03: a triple-net portfolio with an 8.6-year weighted-average lease term and 1% same-store growth is a long-duration bond with an equity ticker, and its price is set by the discount rate.

Against the S&P 500, it has been a persistent laggard through a five-year window dominated by AI capital expenditure and mega-cap technology multiple expansion; that is the expected outcome for a 0.72-beta income vehicle in a growth-led market. The counterweight is drawdown behaviour: across the 13 S&P 500 declines of 10% or more since Realty Income's 1994 listing, the stock's average return was -2.6% versus -22.6% for the index, outperforming in 11 of 13. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the highest-quality rent stream in listed real estate — 15,588 properties, 98.8% occupancy, 8.6-year weighted-average lease term, 102.7% rent recapture — at 14.2x forward AFFO with a 5.15% monthly-paid yield covered at a 73% AFFO payout. The cost-of-capital constraint that has capped the stock since 2022 is being engineered around: private capital and insurance capital supplied 73% of 2026 equity funding, lifting effective cap rates from 7.5% to 10.1% and ROE from 8.8% to 12.8% on fund investments, while traditional public equity fell to 18% of funding from a 47% three-year average. Investment guidance was raised to $10B at a 7.3% initial cash yield, and the data-centre and gaming platforms open a >$1.4T incremental TAM at multiples the market values far above dollar-store real estate. If long rates fall, the multiple re-rates and you are paid 5% while you wait.

Bear Case

This is a bond wearing an equity costume, and the coupon grows at 1%. Same-store rent growth of 1.0–1.2% means essentially all AFFO growth is bought, and buying it requires capital priced off a share price the company does not control — the definition of a business without pricing power over its own growth. At $59.75B of market cap, ~4% AFFO growth requires $10B of annual deployment into a market where cap rates compress the moment rates fall, so the good scenario for the multiple is the bad scenario for the spread. Meanwhile the tenant base is concentrated in three formats under genuine structural pressure — pharmacy, dollar stores, convenience — with only 34% investment-grade rent, a watch list in the "high 5% area" covering 137 tenants, and $144.4M of first-half impairments. Fifteen of 24 analysts sit on Hold for a reason: at 14.2x AFFO, the stock already prices a benign rate path.

Top 3 Risks

1
A renewed rise in long-term interest rates. This is not one risk among several — it is the dominant variable. The 2022–23 episode produced a 31.8% drawdown with zero operational deterioration, and a repeat would simultaneously compress the multiple, raise the cost of debt as the 5.8-year weighted-average maturity book rolls, and raise the cost of equity to the point where the $10B investment programme becomes dilutive rather than accretive. The 91.8% fixed-rate debt profile buys time; it does not confer immunity.
2
A large tenant credit event in discount retail or pharmacy. Dollar General (3.3%), 7-Eleven (3.1%) and Walgreens (3.0%) together represent 9.4% of annualized base rent, and the grocery / convenience / home-improvement industry cluster is 26.9%. Guidance assumes ~40 bps of credit loss. A mass store-closure programme or a Chapter 11 filing at any of the top handful of clients would push actual losses above that, force re-leasing at a time when recapture rates are only ~103%, and trigger further impairments on top of the $144.4M already taken in the first half.
3
Execution risk in the new adjacencies — data centres, gaming and Europe. The Cloud Capital JV, the Apollo and GIC vehicles and the European build-out are all recent, all involve partners, and all carry underwriting profiles unlike the freestanding-retail deals that built the track record. Data-centre residual value, hyperscale tenant re-leasing risk and technological obsolescence are genuinely different problems from re-letting a dollar store. Foreign-currency and derivative losses already run at $25.8M per half-year. Diversification that expands the TAM also expands the ways to be wrong, and a stumble in a headline JV would damage the one thing this company cannot afford to lose — the market's belief that its cash flow is boring.
  Industry Commentary
Interest Rates and the Investment Spread
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Net-lease REIT growth is arithmetic: acquisition cap rate minus weighted-average cost of capital, multiplied by volume. Realty Income deployed at a 7.3% weighted-average initial cash yield in Q2 (7.4% US, 7.0% Europe) against a balance sheet levered 5.4x net debt/EBITDAre with 91.8% fixed-rate debt. When long yields rise, both sides of that equation move against the company at once — the cost of new debt rises immediately, and the share price falls, raising the implied cost of equity. That is exactly why the stock lost 31.8% between July 2022 and October 2023 while occupancy stayed above 98%. It is also why public equity has been cut to 18% of 2026 year-to-date funding from a 47% three-year average: management is refusing to issue shares at a price it considers below intrinsic value, and is sourcing growth capital elsewhere instead. Every element of the current strategy — private capital funds, insurance capital, EUR bonds, 3.5%-coupon convertibles — is a response to this single constraint.

Tenant Credit in Discount Retail and Pharmacy
ONGOING HEADWIND

The formats that made freestanding net lease attractive — dollar stores, convenience, pharmacy, quick-service restaurants — are the same formats now facing e-commerce encroachment, footprint rationalisation and, in pharmacy, structural reimbursement pressure. Realty Income's answer is granularity: 1,798 clients across 92 industries with no single tenant above 3.3% of rent, plus a deliberate shift of new capital toward industrial (75% of Q2 US deployment) and investment-grade credit (34% of rent, up 2pp in a quarter; 40% of new investment in the first half). The evidence so far is that this is working — blended rent recapture of 102.7% means space is being re-let at higher rents, not lower. But the watch list sits in the "high 5% area" across 137 tenants, first-half impairments totalled $144.4M, and credit-loss guidance of 40 bps has no cushion in it for a large bankruptcy.

The Search for Yield — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND

Realty Income's shareholder register is unusual for a $60B company: institutional ownership of 79.95% is below what a comparable large cap would show, because a substantial retail base owns it specifically for the 674 consecutive monthly dividends, 115 consecutive quarterly increases and 22 straight years of growth. That base is sticky and price-insensitive in a way that supports the multiple. Bull framing: if long-term rates decline, income capital rotates back into 5%-yielding real assets, the multiple expands from 14.2x AFFO toward its historical range, and the cheaper equity simultaneously reopens accretive external growth — the two effects compound, which is why the stock rose 19.9% in the two months to February 2026. Bear framing: the same base is the marginal seller when cash and short-duration credit offer a competitive yield without duration risk. In that regime the multiple compresses, the equity window shuts, investment volume gets cut, AFFO growth decays toward the ~1% same-store rate, and the dividend increases shrink to token amounts — which is precisely the loop that produced the 2022–23 de-rating. The 15 Hold ratings out of 24 are the Street declining to pick a side.
