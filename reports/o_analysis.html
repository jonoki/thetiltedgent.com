01 Company Overview

Realty Income is a net-lease REIT, far larger by market value than the net-lease peers compared below, owning 15,536 properties leased to 1,842 clients across 96 industries, spanning 357 million square feet in the United States, the United Kingdom and continental Europe. The business is built on triple-net leases (leases where the tenant, not the landlord, pays property taxes, building insurance and maintenance), which keeps the rent steady and makes the cash flow behave much like a bond's: gross margin runs about 93%. Revenue for the nine months to September 30 was $4.72 billion, up 10.7% YoY (Q3 alone: $1.62 billion, +10.3%), and trailing-twelve-month revenue is about $6.2 billion. The company is a member of the S&P 500, ranks #216 in the S&P 500 by market value, and has declared 676 consecutive monthly dividends with 116 consecutive quarterly increases; it describes itself as a member of the S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats index for having raised its dividend for over 32 consecutive years.

The correct earnings metric here is not EPS but AFFO per share. GAAP EPS of $1.45 (trailing twelve months) is held down by non-cash depreciation on buildings that in practice are not losing economic value; AFFO per share was $1.12 in Q3 2026 (+3.7% YoY) and $3.35 for the nine months (+5.0%), with full-year 2026 guidance raised to $4.45–$4.46 from $4.44–$4.45. At $59.22 that is roughly 13.3x P/AFFO on the guidance midpoint ($4.455) and an AFFO dividend payout of about 73% (72.6% in Q3).

Portfolio Mix

The core franchise: single-tenant, freestanding retail buildings leased on long triple-net terms to retailers selling services and everyday necessities. The tenant pays taxes, insurance and maintenance, so Realty Income collects rent with almost no building-running costs — this is why gross margin runs above 92%. The largest industries by annualized rent are grocery (10.6%), convenience stores (9.2%) and home improvement (6.4%); the largest individual clients are Dollar General (3.3%), 7-Eleven (3.0%) and Walgreens (2.9%), and the top 20 clients together are 34.3%. No single client exceeds 3.3% of rent, which is the practical answer to concentration risk. Re-leasing economics remain positive: the blended rent recapture rate (new rent on a re-let or renewed space as a percentage of the old rent) was 103.5% in Q3 and 103.1% for the nine months — meaning expiring leases are being replaced at higher, not lower, rents. Same-store rent growth (rent from the same buildings a year earlier) was 1.1%.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE — 15,536 PROPERTIES A-RATED BALANCE SHEET ACCESS MONTHLY DIVIDEND BRAND 116 CONSECUTIVE QUARTERLY RAISES $7.9B INVESTED IN 9 MONTHS 20.4% OF RENT IN UK & EUROPE PRIVATE CAPITAL PLATFORM

The advantage is almost entirely cheap funding and the ability to find deals, and understanding this is the whole investment case. A net-lease REIT does not manufacture anything; it buys buildings at a cap rate (the first-year rent as a percentage of the purchase price) and funds them with a blend of equity and debt. The gap between the rent yield on what it buys and the average cost of that money is the earnings growth. Realty Income's advantages are therefore: (1) a balance sheet that borrows cheaply — 94.4% of debt at fixed rates, 5.1-year weighted-average maturity, fixed-charge coverage of 4.6x, net debt to annualized adjusted EBITDAre of 5.4x, counting recent deals as if owned for the whole period (5.2x including unsettled ATM forwards), and net debt of 37.9% of total enterprise value, counting only its own share of joint ventures; (2) sourcing scale — $7.9 billion invested in the first nine months against a $10.0 billion full-year target; (3) the ability to buy $2.6 billion of property in a single quarter, including sale-leasebacks (buying a building from a company and renting it back) and portfolio deals that smaller peers simply cannot take on; and (4) individual shareholders who own it for the monthly dividend, which historically supports a higher share price and therefore a lower equity cost. The equity-cost point cuts both ways and is discussed below. Note the sensitivity: nine-month investment of $7.9 billion came at a 7.1% initial cash yield (6.8% in Q3) against a $56.3 billion equity value at the Nov 4 close, so growth is highly dependent on the price at which new capital is raised.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
The cost-of-capital trap. Net-lease REITs grow by selling new shares at or above NAV and buying property that yields more than that money costs. When the share price falls, raising money by selling shares gets more expensive; if that cost rises above the rent yield on available property, buying more stops adding to earnings per share. Realty Income has been living this since 2022 — the shares fell from $61.28 at the end of August to $54.30 at the end of September 2026, and Q3 equity came in at an average $60.49 a share, with another 18.5 million shares still to settle at $58.97. The private-capital partnerships (the KKR Europe venture, the Core Plus Fund) and convertible debt are genuinely clever workarounds, but they are a response to a constraint, not evidence the constraint is gone. If the share price falls again, the $10 billion investment target is the first plan under pressure.
B
Interest-rate sensitivity is built in. Because triple-net rent is contractual, runs for years (~8.5-year weighted average lease term) and rises slowly (same-store rent growth of just 1.1%), the share behaves like a long-dated corporate bond with a small built-in rent increase. Its price is therefore dominated by the level of long-term interest rates rather than by operating performance. The stock fell from $73.99 (Jul 2022) to $47.38 (Oct 2023) — a 36.0% drawdown (month-end closes) — while occupancy stayed above 98%. Nothing about the portfolio explains that move; the 10-year Treasury does.
C
Tenant credit in structurally challenged retail. The top four clients — Dollar General (3.3%), 7-Eleven (3.0%), Walgreens (2.9%) and Family Dollar (2.5%) — sit in discount retail, convenience and pharmacy, formats under real pressure from e-commerce, store closures and, in pharmacy, a well-documented industry contraction. Only 34.9% of annualized rent comes from investment-grade rated clients. Property impairments were $111.6 million in Q3 and $256.0 million for the nine months (against $144.4 million at the half-year), and provisions for credit losses were $14.2 million in Q3 and $60.6 million for the nine months.
D
Slow organic growth and dilution risk from scale. Same-store rent growth of 1.1% means essentially all AFFO growth must be bought. At $56.3 billion of equity value, making a difference requires ever-larger purchases — $10 billion this year, sourced from a market where cap rates fall whenever interest rates fall. AFFO per share grew 3.7% in Q3 and 5.0% over nine months, and the new adjacencies (data centres, gaming, Europe) exist substantially to fight the drag of size.

Notable One-Off Items

Several non-recurring items materially distort reported GAAP results and should be stripped out when assessing run-rate earnings — which is precisely what the AFFO adjustment does:

1
Provisions for impairment — $111.6M in Q3, $256.0M for the nine months. Non-cash write-downs on properties where carrying value exceeds recoverable value, typically driven by tenant credit deterioration or vacancy in weaker retail formats. Q3's $111.6M compares with $144.4M for the whole first half. This is the largest of the one-off charges listed here, and the company's GAAP earnings guidance excludes any further impairments.
2
Merger, transaction and other costs — $2.7M in Q3, $15.5M for the nine months; executive severance — $1.5M in Q3, $3.3M year to date. Deal-related costs plus severance, both added back in AFFO. In March 2026 the company said its Chief Legal Officer would stay in the role through September 2 and then leave under its executive severance plan. Small in absolute terms, but a reminder that building the platform has a cost.
3
Foreign currency and derivative losses — $2.5M in Q3, $28.4M for the nine months. A direct consequence of the 20.4% of the portfolio sitting in the UK and continental Europe. The company hedges, but currency swings and changes in the value of its hedges now flow through results every quarter and will grow with the European book.
4
Gains on sales of real estate — $113.9M in Q3, $187.8M for the nine months. Offsetting the impairments. Realty Income sold 182 properties for $526.6M of net proceeds in Q3 and 359 properties for $875.2M in the nine months, a steady programme of selling properties. Gains are excluded from AFFO.
5
Lease termination income — $45M booked in the nine months, against 2026 guidance of $45–$50M. Lumpy by nature: payments from tenants who end a lease early. Most of the full-year guide has already landed, so it gives little extra lift to the fourth-quarter AFFO comparison.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 month-end close of $67.92 to $59.22 on November 4, 2026, the shares delivered a price return of roughly −12.8% (price only; the window starts after the November 12, 2021 spin-off of Orion Office REIT, so the spin does not affect it). Dividends are not in that figure: at today's annualized $3.258 a share the dividend is 5.50% of the $59.22 price, so a holder's total return was better than the price alone, but this page does not compute it. Over the same window the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) rose from $455.56 to $766.81, or +68.3% on price alone, so O materially underperformed the index on price.

The shape of the chart is the story, and it is almost entirely a rates story. The stock made its cycle high of $73.99 in July 2022, fell 36.0% to $47.38 by October 2023 as long-term Treasury yields peaked, and climbed back to $67.00 in February 2026 (9.4% below that high) before sliding again: the September 2026 close of $54.30 was 19.0% below February's. Across the 2022–23 drawdown, occupancy never left the high-98% range, the dividend was raised every quarter, and rent recapture stayed above 100%. Nothing in the operating business explains the move. That is the clearest possible demonstration of the point made in Section 03: a triple-net portfolio with an 8.5-year weighted-average lease term and about 1% same-store growth behaves like a long-dated bond with a stock-market ticker, and its price is set by interest rates.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a broad, long-lease rent stream — 15,536 properties, 98.8% occupancy, 8.5-year weighted-average lease term, 103.5% rent recapture — at 13.3x guided AFFO with a 5.50% monthly-paid yield covered at a 73% AFFO payout. The high cost of raising money, which has held the stock back since 2022, is being worked around: the Core Plus Fund reached $3.52 billion of assets with $2.70 billion from outside investors, KKR-advised capital paid €528 million for 49% of a European venture, and Q3 equity was raised at an average $60.49 a share. Investment guidance of $10B is on track ($7.9B done at a 7.1% initial cash yield), AFFO guidance was raised again, and the data-centre and gaming platforms give it more places to invest. If long rates fall, the share price rises and you are paid 5.5% while you wait.

Bear Case

This is a bond wearing an equity costume, and the coupon grows at about 1%. Same-store rent growth of 1.1% means essentially all AFFO growth is bought, and buying it requires capital priced off a share price the company does not control — the definition of a business without pricing power over its own growth. At $56.3B of market cap, AFFO growth of 4–5% requires $10B of annual deployment into a market where cap rates fall the moment interest rates fall, so the good scenario for the share price is the bad scenario for the gap between rents and funding costs. Meanwhile the tenant base is concentrated in formats under genuine structural pressure — pharmacy, dollar stores, convenience — with only 34.9% investment-grade rent, and impairments of $256.0M in nine months, $111.6M of them in Q3; GAAP earnings guidance was cut. Seventeen of 25 analysts sit on Hold for a reason: at 13.3x AFFO, the stock already prices a benign rate path.

Top 3 Risks

1
A renewed rise in long-term interest rates. This is not one risk among several — it is the dominant variable. The 2022–23 episode produced a 36.0% drawdown with zero operational deterioration, and a repeat would simultaneously cut the share price, raise the cost of debt as the 5.1-year weighted-average maturity book rolls, and make selling new shares so expensive that the $10B investment programme would reduce AFFO per share instead of adding to it. The 94.4% fixed-rate debt profile buys time; it does not confer immunity.
2
A large tenant credit event in discount retail or pharmacy. Dollar General (3.3%), 7-Eleven (3.0%) and Walgreens (2.9%) together represent 9.2% of annualized base rent, and the grocery / convenience / home-improvement industry cluster is 26.2%. A mass store-closure programme or a Chapter 11 filing at any of the top handful of clients would force re-leasing at a time when recapture rates are only ~103%, and trigger further impairments on top of the $256.0M already taken in nine months.
3
Execution risk in the new adjacencies — data centres, gaming and Europe. The Cloud Capital JV, the GIC vehicle, the KKR Europe venture and the European build-out are all recent, all involve partners, and all carry underwriting profiles unlike the freestanding-retail deals that built the track record. Data-centre residual value, hyperscale tenant re-leasing risk and technological obsolescence are genuinely different problems from re-letting a dollar store. Foreign-currency and derivative losses already run at $28.4M for nine months. Diversification that expands the addressable market also expands the ways to be wrong, and a stumble in a headline JV would damage the one thing this company cannot afford to lose — the market's belief that its cash flow is boring.
  Industry Commentary
Interest Rates and the Investment Spread
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Net-lease REIT growth is arithmetic: acquisition cap rate minus the blended cost of its debt and equity, multiplied by volume. Realty Income deployed at a 6.8% weighted-average initial cash yield in Q3 (6.5% US and other, 7.1% Europe; 7.1% for the nine months) against a balance sheet levered 5.4x net debt/EBITDAre with 94.4% fixed-rate debt. When long yields rise, both sides of that equation move against the company at once — the cost of new debt rises immediately, and the share price falls, making new shares more expensive to sell. That is exactly why the stock lost 36.0% between July 2022 and October 2023 while occupancy stayed above 98%. It is also why the company leans on partners: in Q3 it raised $472.5 million of equity at an average $60.49 a share, issued $1.0 billion of 3.750% convertible notes, and signed the KKR Europe venture. Every element of the current strategy — private capital funds, EUR bonds, convertibles — is a response to this single constraint.

Tenant Credit in Discount Retail and Pharmacy
ONGOING HEADWIND

The formats that made freestanding net lease attractive — dollar stores, convenience, pharmacy, quick-service restaurants — are the same formats now facing e-commerce competition, store closures and, in pharmacy, falling drug reimbursement. Realty Income's answer is granularity: 1,842 clients across 96 industries with no single tenant above 3.3% of rent, plus a deliberate shift of new capital toward industrial (57.3% of Q3 new investment) and investment-grade credit (53% of the cash income on Q3 investments; 34.9% of total rent). The evidence so far is mixed — blended rent recapture of 103.5% means space is being re-let at higher rents, not lower, but nine-month impairments totalled $256.0M, $111.6M of them in Q3.

The Search for Yield — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND

Realty Income's shareholders include many individual investors who own it for the 676 consecutive monthly dividends, 116 consecutive quarterly increases and over 32 straight years of growth. That base tends to hold through price swings, which supports the share price. Bull framing: if long-term rates decline, income investors return to 5%-yielding property shares, the share price rises toward its historical valuation range, and cheaper share sales make new purchases pay off again — the two effects reinforce each other, which is why the stock rose 18.9% in the two months to February 2026. Bear framing: the same holders sell first when cash and short-term bonds offer a competitive yield without the interest-rate risk. Then the share price falls, selling new shares stops being practical, investment volume gets cut, AFFO growth decays toward the ~1% same-store rate, and the dividend increases shrink to token amounts — which is precisely the loop that produced the 2022–23 slump. The 17 Hold ratings out of 25 are the Street declining to pick a side.
