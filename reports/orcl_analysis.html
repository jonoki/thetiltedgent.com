01 Company Overview

Oracle is the world's dominant enterprise database vendor and, over the past three years, has re-invented itself as the fourth major cloud infrastructure provider — and the most aggressive pure bet on AI training capacity among the large caps. Fiscal 2026 (ended May 31, 2026) revenue was $67.4B, up 17% YoY. The first quarter of fiscal 2027 (ended August 31, reported September 10) is where the backlog started turning into revenue: revenue $19.3B, up 30%, with Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI, Oracle's rentable compute/storage cloud) up 121% to $7.4B. Cloud now makes up 60% of revenue, up from 48% a year earlier. Contracted backlog (RPO) reached $664B, of which Oracle expects to recognize about 13% over the next twelve months. The growth costs a lot to build: $28.5B of capex in one quarter (vs $8.5B a year earlier), free cash flow of about −$5.4B, and $19.9B of new equity sold through an at-the-market program. The stock has made a violent round trip: a +36% single-day surge to an intraday record $345.72 in September 2025 on the RPO shock, then a ~67% collapse to $114.50 on July 28, 2026, as investors soured on debt-funded AI capex and OpenAI counterparty risk. The September beat did not change the price much. At $148.56, Oracle is roughly the 29th most valuable company in the world — and one of the most polarizing.

Leadership was restructured in September 2025: Clay Magouyrk and Mike Sicilia became co-CEOs, longtime CEO Safra Catz moved up to Executive Vice Chair, and founder Larry Ellison remains Executive Chair and CTO. He is also the largest shareholder, with roughly 40% of the company. On September 12, 2026, Oracle announced that Ellison had cancelled his 10b5-1 plan to sell Oracle stock, stating that “No Oracle stock was sold under that plan, and he has no other plans to sell any of his Oracle stock.” The same month, the company added about $700M to its AI-linked 2026 Restructuring Plan, taking the estimated cost to roughly $2.8B.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Database Lock-In
Mission-Critical Switching Costs
$664B Contracted RPO
Full-Stack Apps + Infra
Multicloud DB Partnerships
OCI Price-Performance Design
Founder Ownership Alignment

Oracle's classic moat is switching costs: Oracle Database underpins the transactional core of most large banks, telecoms, airlines and governments, with support renewal rates above 95% and decades of stored procedures, tuning and compliance built around it. That gravity is now being weaponized for cloud — Oracle Database@Azure, @AWS and @Google Cloud (launched 2023–2025) let customers run Oracle's database inside rival clouds, and grew 404% YoY in Q4 FY26, Oracle's fastest-growing product ever. On top sits a full-stack position no rival matches: apps (Fusion/NetSuite) + database + AI infrastructure. OCI's later, cleaner architecture (flat non-blocking networks, RDMA clustering) delivers credible price-performance advantages for giant GPU clusters, which is why OpenAI, Meta, xAI and Nvidia itself rent from Oracle. Finally, the $664B RPO (up $209B in a year) is itself a moat of sorts — contracted, multi-year demand visibility no software peer possesses — though its concentration is also the key vulnerability. Ellison's ~40% stake keeps management brutally long-term.

Key Vulnerabilities
◆
Customer concentration (OpenAI). Roughly $300B of the $664B RPO — nearly half — reportedly comes from OpenAI, a company with ~$25B of annualized revenue, persistent losses, and similar-scale commitments to Microsoft and others. Oracle's own FY26 10-K added risk language about "non-payment and non-performance" by highly leveraged customers. If OpenAI renegotiates or stumbles, the backlog story unravels.
◆
Balance-sheet leverage, dilution & negative FCF. $169B total debt including leases (StockAnalysis; $125.3B of notes payable on the Aug 31 balance sheet), D/E of ~2.5x (vs ~0.7x for software peers), and trailing free cash flow of −$28.7B after $28.5B of capex in Q1 alone. Leverage fell from ~3.9x only because Oracle sold $19.9B of new stock in the quarter, lifting the share count 5% (2,880M → 3,024M). Management plans ~$40B of debt and equity raising in FY27. Oracle is financing customers' AI ambitions with its own balance sheet, and its shareholders are now funding part of it directly.
◆
Hyperscaler and neocloud competition. AWS, Azure and Google have 10–20x Oracle's cloud scale and their own multi-gigawatt buildouts, while neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe) compete for the same GPU-rental deals at thin margins. AI compute could commoditize before Oracle's capacity fully lands.
◆
Legacy erosion & tech disruption. The software segment ($24.5B in FY26; −3% YoY in Q1 FY27) faces steady leakage to PostgreSQL and cloud-native databases; if support revenue decays faster than cloud replaces it at equivalent margin, the earnings engine funding the buildout weakens.

Notable One-Off Items

The reported numbers carry unusual distortions worth flagging. (1) Of the RPO, roughly $75B reflected customer prepayments or customer-supplied hardware in AI contracts as of FY26 — backlog quality varies. (2) Oracle raised $43B of debt and $5B of equity during FY26, then sold $19.9B of common stock through its at-the-market program in Q1 FY27 alone. It plans ~$40B of new capital in FY27, and the share count is rising, diluting EPS. (3) Q1 operating cash flow of $23.1B (+184%) is unusually large relative to $4.76B of net income. The 10-Q shows why: $11.4B came from an increase in deferred revenues from customer prepayments with a significant financing component (zero a year earlier). Customers are pre-funding capacity, which flatters OCF and FCF for now. (4) Only ~13% of the $664B RPO is expected to convert to revenue in the next twelve months, so the backlog headline dramatically overstates near-term revenue. (5) Restructuring: $165M was expensed in Q1 under the 2026 Restructuring Plan, which management then expanded by ~$700M after quarter-end. None of these are classic write-downs, but each materially reshapes the optics of the reported numbers.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

The conversion has started. Q1 FY27 revenue grew 30%, OCI grew 121%, EPS beat by 10%, and RPO climbed to $664B, with customers prepaying billions to secure capacity. Oracle guides FY27 to at least $90B of revenue and $8.10 of non-GAAP EPS. At 18.3x that guidance and a 0.58 PEG, the market is still pricing substantial failure. If even the non-OpenAI backlog converts on schedule, $148.56 buys an accelerating hyperscaler at a legacy-software multiple. Two more things help the bull: the founder has just declined to sell, and the multicloud database is a second act.

▼ Bear Case

Nearly half the backlog reportedly depends on OpenAI — a loss-making startup with ~$25B revenue and commitments it cannot currently fund — while Oracle funds the capacity with debt ($169B incl. leases), new stock ($19.9B sold in one quarter, +5% share count) and customer prepayments. TTM free cash flow is −$28.7B. If AI training demand pauses or OpenAI renegotiates, Oracle is left with leveraged, depreciating GPU real estate, a growing share count, and a gross margin already bending under GPU mix (64.0% TTM, down from 65.8%). The market's verdict on a clean beat — up 7% after hours, down on the day — shows how little benefit of the doubt is left.

Top 3 Risks
01
OpenAI counterparty concentration. A reported ~$300B of the $664B RPO from one unprofitable customer; Oracle's own 10-K flags non-payment risk from leveraged customers. This is the fulcrum of the entire investment case.
02
Financing, leverage and dilution. D/E ~2.5x (only after $19.9B of new equity), negative FCF (−$28.7B TTM) and dependence on continuous access to debt and equity markets at acceptable terms. Every quarter funded with stock dilutes the per-share payoff; a credit downgrade or spread blowout would force a capex retreat and gut the growth story.
03
Execution and margin risk on the buildout. Delivering multiple gigawatts of powered capacity on schedule — against power, chip and construction constraints — at returns above the cost of capital (ROIC today: 11.3%) is unproven at this scale. The ~$2.8B restructuring plan shows the legacy cost base being cut to help pay for it.
  Industry Commentary: The AI Infrastructure Reckoning
AI Financing Circularity & Counterparty Credit
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

2026's defining market debate is whether AI infrastructure demand is real end-demand or a circular financing loop: Nvidia invests in OpenAI, OpenAI commits ~$300B to Oracle, Oracle borrows to buy Nvidia GPUs. OpenAI's ~$25B annualized revenue stands against reported commitments in the hundreds of billions across Oracle, Microsoft and others; its ~$122B raise buys time, not certainty. Oracle is the most exposed public-market expression of this loop — hence CDS spreads at multi-year wides and prominent bears calling the AI capex cycle "17x the dot-com bubble." Oracle's worst week since 2001 (June 2026) was priced off this exact fear, and the September 2026 print showed it has not gone away: a 10% EPS beat and a $664B backlog could not hold a 7% after-hours gain through the next session.

The Debt-Funded Capex Arms Race
ONGOING HEADWIND

Hyperscaler capex is running past $400B/yr industry-wide, but the funding models diverge sharply: Microsoft, Google and Amazon self-fund from massive FCF, while Oracle (and the neoclouds — CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe) lean on debt. Oracle added $43B of debt in FY26 and plans ~$40B of new capital in FY27. It has already sold $19.9B of equity through its ATM program and received $11.4B of customer prepayments in Q1, a sign that customers and shareholders are now funding the buildout alongside creditors. It carries $169B of total debt including leases against $36.4B of cash. Power availability, not chips, is now the binding constraint on delivery timelines — and interest expense compounds regardless of whether the gigawatts energize on schedule. Cloud infrastructure spend (~$390B TTM, +28%) still supports the demand side; the cost of capital decides the winners.

What It Means: Bull vs Bear Framing
EMERGING TAILWIND (IF DEMAND HOLDS)

If AI inference demand keeps compounding — and Q4's $67B of new contracts from four separate $8B+ customers suggests demand beyond OpenAI — Oracle converts backlog into a $90B FY27 and a doubling of cloud revenue by FY28, and today's 18.3x forward multiple looks like the buying opportunity of the cycle; that is roughly the $400 Street-high case. If demand plateaus or the OpenAI keystone slips, leverage works in reverse: revenue misses meet fixed interest costs and dilution, and the $110 Street-low target (Redburn, Sell) comes into play. Few mega-caps have ever offered this wide a distribution of credible outcomes — position sizing matters more than direction here.
