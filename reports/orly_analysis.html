01 Company Overview
O'Reilly Automotive is a specialty retailer and wholesale distributor of automotive aftermarket parts, tools, supplies, equipment and accessories, operating 6,695 stores across the United States, Puerto Rico, Mexico and Canada as of the end of Q2 2026. It sells through two channels out of the same store base — DIY (do-it-yourself retail customers) and DIFM (do-it-for-me, meaning professional installers, independent repair shops and fleet operators) — a dual-market model that lets one inventory pool serve two demand streams and is the structural reason O'Reilly's store-level economics differ from a pure retailer's.

Fiscal 2025 revenue was $17.78 billion, up 6.0% YoY, on comparable store sales growth of 4.7% (versus 2.9% in 2024), with diluted EPS of $2.97, up 10%. Trailing-twelve-month revenue has since risen to $18.57 billion (+8.5%). At a $73.78 billion market capitalization O'Reilly is the 345th most valuable public company in the world and by far the most valuable pure-play auto parts retailer — roughly 49% larger than AutoZone despite carrying less trailing revenue, a valuation gap that reflects the market's read on comp durability and capital allocation rather than scale.

The moat is availability, not price — and availability is a physical asset. Understand the professional customer's economics and the whole model follows. An independent repair shop has a customer's car on a lift and a bay generating revenue only while work is happening. If the correct part is not in the technician's hands within an hour or two, the bay goes idle, the promised completion time slips, and the shop loses more in labor throughput than it could ever save by shopping a part for a few dollars less. Parts availability, not price, wins the professional customer. That is why O'Reilly competes on fill rate rather than on discount.

Delivering that fill rate requires a layered hub-and-spoke network: large regional distribution centers feed hub stores that carry substantially broader assortments than a normal store, and those hubs feed the surrounding spoke stores multiple times per day. The result is that a spoke store can source an unusual SKU from its hub within hours rather than overnight, giving the local installer effective access to an assortment far larger than what physically sits on that store's shelves. Inventory per store was $892,000 at Q2 2026, up 7% YoY, with total inventory of $5.97B (+10.6%) — O'Reilly deliberately carries more working capital per door than a pure-retail model would justify, because the incremental slow-turning SKU is what converts a professional account. The company's most recent capacity addition, a 690,000-square-foot Atlanta distribution center relocated at the end of 2024, is explicitly framed by management as "unlocking additional expansion in the Southeastern United States."

This is a genuine barrier because it is density-dependent and capital-heavy. A competitor cannot match same-day availability in a given metro without first building comparable store density and a hub layer and the delivery routes to connect them — an investment that is unprofitable until the volume arrives, and the volume does not arrive until the availability exists. It also explains why e-commerce has not disrupted this category the way it disrupted others: a part shipped tomorrow is worthless to a bay that is idle today. The dual-channel model reinforces the whole structure, since DIY foot traffic helps carry the fixed cost of a store footprint that also functions as a commercial distribution node.
Key Vulnerabilities
1. DIY transaction counts are in decline. Q2 2026 DIY comps were low-single-digit with negative transaction counts; the dollar growth came from ticket, which came substantially from 5.5% same-SKU inflation. Half the business is not growing in units. If inflation moderates to the guided 1–2% in the second half without a unit recovery, DIY dollar comps compress toward zero and the blended comp leans entirely on professional.

2. Negative stockholders' equity limits balance sheet flexibility. Decades of buying back stock above book value have left shareholders' equity at -$1.84 billion and book value per share at -$2.25, against $9.59B of total debt and only $262 million of cash. A current ratio of 0.75 and a quick ratio of 0.09 mean the company is structurally reliant on inventory conversion and revolver access. This works beautifully while free cash flow is $2B+ and stable; it offers no cushion if comps go negative for several consecutive quarters.

3. Tariff and input-cost pass-through is not guaranteed. Management noted O'Reilly is "not paying a lot of direct tariffs" because suppliers were typically the importer of record — but that only relocates the exposure, it does not eliminate it. Same-SKU inflation of 5.5% in Q2 2026 was the visible pass-through. If competitive dynamics prevent full pass-through in a future round, gross margin absorbs it; if pass-through is full but demand is elastic, units absorb it.

4. Cost inflation in SG&A the company does not control. Self-insurance reserves jumped to $297.3 million at year-end 2025 from $149.4 million a year earlier, and management explicitly blamed Q4 2025 SG&A overruns on "heightened inflation in team member health care and casualty claim costs." Healthcare and casualty claim inflation, plus legal expense, are the swing factor that has repeatedly turned good comp quarters into merely in-line EPS quarters.

Notable One-Off Items
Negative stockholders' equity is not a distress signal — it is an accounting consequence of the buyback. O'Reilly has repurchased 1.46 billion shares (split-adjusted) for $27.49 billion at an average price of $18.77 since the program began in January 2011. Because treasury stock is carried at cost and that cost has run far above accumulated retained earnings, book equity is -$1.84 billion. The practical effect: ROE, D/E and price-to-book are all undefined or meaningless for this company, and any screen that ranks on them will mis-rank ORLY badly. Judge it on ROIC (36.48%), free cash flow, and debt/EBITDAR instead. The buyback pace remains aggressive: 22.7M shares for $2.10B at $92.26 average in FY2025, and 34M shares for $3.1B year-to-date through late July 2026 — including 16.7M shares for $1.51B at $90.40 in Q2 2026 alone, funded partly by "incremental borrowings as we move towards our leverage target."

Tariffs and same-SKU inflation. Q2 2026 same-SKU inflation ran 5.5%, a direct pass-through of 2025 tariff-driven supplier price increases. Management expects this to decelerate to 1%–2% in the second half of 2026 as the 2025 increases annualize. This is the key modeling item: a large slice of current comp growth is price that mechanically disappears from the comparison. Management also noted O'Reilly pays limited direct tariffs because "other suppliers were the importer of record," and has worked to share tariff-refund benefits with suppliers — an arrangement that is favorable but also outside the company's control.

Self-insurance reserves. Self-insurance reserves rose to $297.3 million at year-end 2025 from $149.4 million — a near-doubling that materially pressured SG&A. Management attributed Q4 2025 SG&A overruns to "heightened inflation in team member health care and casualty claim costs" and flagged continuing "year-over-year SG&A pressures from self-insurance and legal line items that ramped in the second half of 2025," with first-half 2026 experience in line with expectations. This is a real, recurring cost trend rather than a one-time charge, and it is the reason operating margin has drifted from 21.98% in FY2021 to 19.46% in FY2025 despite gross margin holding.

Inventory accounting. Inventory rose 10.6% YoY to $5.97 billion with inventory per store of $892,000 (+7%), targeted to grow ~5% by year-end. No LIFO commentary appeared in the FY2025 release or the Q2 2026 call transcript reached for this analysis — readers modeling inventory-accounting effects should verify the method directly in the 10-K rather than rely on this page.

Stock split. The board approved a 15-for-1 split on March 13, 2025, effective June 9, 2025. Every per-share figure and every price on this page is split-adjusted. Any pre-June-2025 source quoting ORLY near $1,300 is unadjusted and not comparable.

Five-year trajectory. ORLY closed September 2021 at $40.74 split-adjusted and closed at $91.20 on August 19, 2026 — a total return of approximately +124%, or roughly 17.5% annualized over five years, with no dividend contribution since the company pays none. That was achieved with a beta of 0.52, which is the actual point: this is index-beating compounding delivered at roughly half of market volatility, driven by mid-single-digit comps, flat-to-up gross margin, and a share count shrinking ~3% a year.

The path was not linear. The stock roughly tripled from the 2022 lows into the September 2025 peak of $107.81, then spent the following eleven months in a $82–$102 range while earnings kept growing — a textbook multiple de-rating. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The professional/DIFM business is compounding at double-digit comps for a fourth straight quarter on growing ticket counts, which is share capture, not inflation — and the hub-and-spoke distribution network that produces it cannot be replicated quickly by anyone. The structural backdrop is favorable and slow-moving: 293 million US light vehicles at an average age of 13 years and 3.3 trillion miles driven in 2025. Meanwhile ROIC is 36.5%, the share count shrinks ~3% a year, and the multiple has already de-rated 15% from the September 2025 peak while EPS grew 10% and FY2026 guidance was raised. You are paying 26x forward for a low-beta compounder that just beat and raised.

Bear Case

A material portion of the current 6% comp is price, not units: same-SKU inflation was 5.5% in Q2 and is guided to fall to 1–2% in the second half, and DIY transaction counts are already declining. Strip the inflation and half the company is shrinking in units. Operating margin has fallen from 21.98% in FY2021 to 19.46% in FY2025 on SG&A the company does not control — health care and casualty claim inflation doubled self-insurance reserves to $297.3M. The balance sheet has no cushion: -$1.84B equity, 0.75 current ratio, 0.09 quick ratio, $9.59B debt and $262M cash, with buybacks now partly debt-funded toward a 2.5x leverage target. At 29x trailing and a PEG near 2, a single negative comp quarter re-rates this hard.

Top 3 Risks
01
The inflation cliff in second-half comps. Same-SKU inflation drops from 5.5% to a guided 1%–2% in H2 2026. If unit growth does not fill that ~4-point gap, reported comps fall toward the bottom of the raised 4%–6% range or below it — and the multiple, not the earnings, is what takes the damage.
02
Uncontrollable SG&A inflation. Self-insurance reserves nearly doubled to $297.3M, and health care, casualty and legal costs have repeatedly overshot management's own expectations. This has already cost roughly 250 basis points of operating margin since FY2021 and there is no obvious mechanism to reverse it.
03
Balance-sheet fragility if comps turn negative. Negative equity of -$1.84B, a 0.75 current ratio, a 0.09 quick ratio and $9.59B of debt against $262M of cash work only while free cash flow is $2B+ and predictable. Buybacks are now partly debt-funded. A multi-quarter comp downturn would force a rapid choice between the repurchase program and the leverage target.
  Industry Commentary
The Aging US Vehicle Fleet & Deferred Maintenance
EMERGING TAILWIND
Management cited over 293 million light cars and light trucks on US roads with an average age of 13 years, and a record 3.3 trillion miles driven in 2025. This is the single most important structural fact about the industry, and it is slow-moving and highly forecastable: vehicles aged 7–15 years are the sweet spot for aftermarket parts demand, because they are out of warranty, out of the dealership service relationship, and still worth repairing. High new-vehicle prices have kept consumers in older cars longer, deepening the pool. Management noted similar dynamics in Mexico and Canada. The one caveat worth holding: an average age this high partly reflects deferred purchases, and deferred maintenance eventually becomes either a repair or a scrappage decision.
Tariffs on Imported Parts
ONGOING HEADWIND
The aftermarket supply chain is import-heavy, and 2025's tariff round pushed supplier prices up materially. O'Reilly's exposure is indirect: management stated the company is "not paying a lot of direct tariffs" because "other suppliers were the importer of record," and has worked to share tariff-refund benefits with suppliers. The visible result was 5.5% same-SKU inflation in Q2 2026, guided down to 1%–2% in the second half as the 2025 increases annualize. Gross margin held flat at 51.4% through it, which is evidence of pass-through power — but it is a two-edged fact: the same pass-through that protected margin is now the comp headwind, because price-driven comp mechanically disappears from the year-over-year comparison.
Consumer Trade-Down: Repair Over Replacement
BULL VS BEAR FRAMING
The bull framing: when new-vehicle affordability deteriorates, the marginal consumer repairs instead of replacing. That is counter-cyclical demand, and it is why beta is 0.52 and why October 2022 produced a +19% month for this stock. It supports both channels — DIY for the cost-conscious owner, DIFM for the owner who lacks the tools for an increasingly complex vehicle.

The bear framing: the Q2 2026 data does not actually show trade-down producing units. DIY transaction counts declined low-single-digit; only professional ticket counts grew. A genuinely stressed consumer defers everything, including repair, until the failure is unavoidable — which shows up as declining traffic with a rising average ticket, exactly what O'Reilly reported. The resolution of these two framings over the next two quarters, as inflation fades and units are exposed, is the central question for the stock.
