01 Company Overview
O'Reilly Automotive is a specialty retailer and wholesale distributor of automotive aftermarket parts, tools, supplies, equipment and accessories, operating 6,758 stores across 48 US states, Puerto Rico, Mexico and Canada at September 30, 2026. It sells through two channels out of the same store base — DIY (do-it-yourself retail customers) and DIFM (do-it-for-me, meaning professional installers, independent repair shops and fleet operators). One inventory pool serves both kinds of customer, which is why O'Reilly's store economics differ from a pure retailer's.

In the third quarter of 2026 (ended September 30, released October 28 after the close), sales rose 9% to $5.12 billion, comparable store sales grew 7.2% and diluted EPS rose 14% to $0.97, ahead of the $0.92 Nasdaq consensus. The quarter included a $39 million benefit from tariff refunds, which CFO Jeremy Fletcher described on the call as "a unique item, as a 1-off item." Management raised its full-year 2026 outlook: comparable sales of 5.5%–6.5% (from 4%–6%) and EPS of $3.25–$3.35 (from $3.20–$3.30). Fiscal 2025 revenue was $17.78 billion, up 6% YoY, with diluted EPS of $2.97; trailing-twelve-month revenue is now $18.99 billion. At a $70.90 billion market capitalization (804.61 million shares × the $88.12 close, our calculation) O'Reilly is the most valuable pure-play auto parts retailer — about 39% larger than AutoZone ($51.02B) despite less trailing revenue ($18.99B against $20.34B). Investors are paying for steady sales growth and buybacks, not for size.

The traditional storefront business: brake pads, batteries, filters, wipers, fluids, accessories and hard parts sold to consumers doing their own repairs, plus free ancillary services (battery testing, alternator/starter testing, oil recycling, paint mixing, loaner tools) that exist to pull traffic. DIY carries the higher gross margin of the two channels because retail pricing is less negotiated than commercial pricing, so as professional sales grow faster than DIY, the company-wide gross margin percentage is pulled down even while gross profit dollars rise.

In Q3 2026 DIY comparable sales rose by a mid-single-digit percentage, CEO Brad Beckham said on the call, up from low-single-digit in Q2. The gain came from a bigger average ticket, helped by same-SKU inflation of slightly over 4%, while DIY transaction counts fell again: "The pressure of the DIY transaction counts were more than offset by increases in average ticket." That is the honest read: fewer DIY customers are coming through the door, and the dollar growth is price. Fixing an older car is cheaper than replacing it, which supports the channel, but on this evidence it does not bring in more DIY customers.

The moat is availability, not price — and availability is a physical asset. Understand the professional customer's economics and the whole model follows. An independent repair shop has a customer's car on a lift and a bay generating revenue only while work is happening. If the correct part is not in the technician's hands within an hour or two, the bay goes idle, the promised completion time slips, and the shop loses more in labor throughput than it could ever save by shopping a part for a few dollars less. Parts availability, not price, wins the professional customer. That is why O'Reilly competes on fill rate (how often the part asked for is in stock) rather than on discount.

Delivering that fill rate requires a layered hub-and-spoke network: large regional distribution centers feed hub stores that carry substantially broader assortments than a normal store, and those hubs feed the surrounding spoke stores multiple times per day. The result is that a spoke store can source an unusual SKU from its hub within hours rather than overnight, giving the local installer effective access to an assortment far larger than what physically sits on that store's shelves. Inventory per store was $912,000 at September 30, 2026, up from $858,000 a year earlier (+6.3%), with total inventory of $6.17B against $5.61B — O'Reilly deliberately keeps more stock per store than a pure retailer would, because the extra slow-selling part is what wins a professional account. A recent capacity addition, a 690,000-square-foot Atlanta distribution center relocated at the end of 2024, was framed by management as room to expand further in the Southeast.

This is a genuine barrier because it is density-dependent and capital-heavy. A competitor cannot match same-day availability in a given metro without first building comparable store density and a hub layer and the delivery routes to connect them — an investment that is unprofitable until the volume arrives, and the volume does not arrive until the availability exists. It also explains why e-commerce has not disrupted this category the way it disrupted others: a part shipped tomorrow is worthless to a bay that is idle today. The dual-channel model reinforces the whole structure, since DIY foot traffic helps carry the fixed cost of a store footprint that also functions as a commercial distribution node.
Key Vulnerabilities
1. DIY transaction counts are still falling. Q3 2026 DIY comps rose mid-single digits, but on fewer transactions; the dollar growth came from a bigger average ticket, helped by same-SKU inflation of slightly over 4%. Half the business is not growing in units. CFO Jeremy Fletcher expects inflation of "maybe 2.5, 3%" in the fourth quarter. If price increases keep fading without a recovery in transactions, DIY dollar growth shrinks and the blended comp leans on professional.

2. Negative shareholders' equity limits balance-sheet flexibility. Years of buying back stock above book value have left shareholders' equity at -$2.09 billion at September 30, 2026 (book value per share -$2.60), against $7.71 billion of long-term debt and only $194 million of cash. A current ratio of 0.77 and a quick ratio of 0.09 mean the company relies on selling its inventory quickly and on access to borrowing. That works while free cash flow is about $2 billion a year and steady; it leaves no cushion if comps go negative for several quarters in a row.

3. Passing on tariffs and supplier costs is not guaranteed. O'Reilly pays few tariffs directly because its suppliers are usually the importer, but that moves the exposure rather than removing it. Same-SKU inflation of slightly over 4% in Q3 2026 was the visible pass-through, and the $39 million of tariff refunds in the quarter is, in the CFO's words, a one-off. If competition blocks a future round of price increases, gross margin takes the hit; if the increases go through but customers buy less, units do.

4. Cost inflation in SG&A the company does not control. Self-insurance reserves jumped to $297.3 million at year-end 2025 from $149.4 million a year earlier, and management blamed the Q4 2025 overrun in SG&A (selling, general and administrative costs) on health care and casualty claim inflation. In Q3 2026 President Brent Kirby said "SG&A dollar growth was higher than expected with our average per store growth of 4.8 percent", and CEO Brad Beckham pointed to medical, self-insurance and fuel costs. These costs are what has repeatedly turned good comp quarters into merely in-line EPS quarters.

Notable One-Off Items
Negative stockholders' equity is not a distress signal — it is an accounting consequence of the buyback. Since the program began in January 2011 and through the date of the Q3 2026 release, O'Reilly has repurchased 1.50 billion shares (split-adjusted) for $31.02 billion at an average price of $20.63. Because bought-back stock is carried at cost and that cost has run far above accumulated retained earnings, book equity is -$2.09 billion. The practical effect: ROE, D/E and price-to-book are all undefined or meaningless for this company, and any screen that ranks on them will mis-rank ORLY badly. Judge it on ROIC (36.20%), free cash flow, and debt/EBITDAR instead. The buyback pace remains heavy: 22.7M shares for $2.10B at $92.26 average in FY2025, and 38.5M shares for $3.47B at $89.99 in the first nine months of 2026 — including 11.8M shares for $1.03B at $87.34 in Q3 — plus 2.3M shares for $204M after the quarter ended. About $727M of the current authorization remained. Weighted diluted shares were 812.2M in Q3, down 4.7% from 852.7M a year earlier. Part of the money is borrowed: in August O'Reilly sold $1.6B of senior notes ($700M at 4.800% due 2029, $500M at 5.050% due 2031, $400M at 5.550% due 2037; closed Aug 14, 2026, 8-K), and long-term debt rose to $7.71B from $5.92B a year earlier.

Tariffs and same-SKU inflation. Same-SKU inflation ran slightly over 4% in Q3 2026, down from 5.5% in Q2, as supplier price increases tied to the 2025 tariffs pass their one-year mark; CFO Jeremy Fletcher expects about 2.5%–3% in the fourth quarter. This is the key item for forecasting: a large slice of recent comp growth is price, and it drops out of the year-over-year comparison once the increases are a year old. Q3 also included a $39 million benefit from tariff refunds, which Fletcher called "a unique item, as a 1-off item." O'Reilly pays few tariffs directly, since suppliers are usually the importer.

Self-insurance reserves. Self-insurance reserves, a current liability on the balance sheet, rose to $297.3 million at year-end 2025 from $149.4 million, and management said SG&A ran above its expectations in Q4 2025 on inflation in team member health care and casualty claim costs. In Q3 2026 SG&A per store grew 4.8%, more than management expected, though SG&A fell to 30.9% of sales from 31.1% and operating margin rose to 21.4% from 20.7%. The cost trend is recurring rather than a one-time charge, and it is why operating margin drifted from 21.89% in FY2021 to 19.46% in FY2025 even as gross margin held. FY2026 operating margin is guided to 19.5%–20.0%.

Inventory. Inventory rose to $6.17 billion at September 30, 2026 from $5.61 billion a year earlier, with inventory per store of $912,000 (+6.3%). Accounts payable were 121.8% of inventory, down from 125.9%: suppliers still finance more than the full value of the stock on the shelves.

Stock split. The board approved a 15-for-1 split on March 13, 2025, effective June 9, 2025. Every per-share figure and every price on this page is split-adjusted. Any pre-June-2025 source quoting ORLY near $1,300 is unadjusted and not comparable.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The professional/DIFM business keeps posting double-digit comps — slightly over 10% in Q3 — on growing ticket counts, which means winning customers from rivals, not just raising prices; the hub-and-spoke network behind it cannot be copied quickly. The backdrop moves slowly in O'Reilly's favor: management cites about 293 million US light vehicles averaging 13 years old. Return on invested capital is 36.2%, diluted shares fell 4.7% in a year, and Q3 beat with a 7.2% comp while full-year guidance went up again. Yet the stock is 18% below its September 2025 month-end high, at 24 times FY2027 consensus EPS: you are paying less than a year ago for a business earning more.

Bear Case

Price is still doing a lot of the work: same-SKU inflation was slightly over 4% in Q3 and DIY transaction counts are still falling. The CFO sees inflation easing to about 2.5%–3% in Q4; strip it out and half the company is shrinking in units. Costs the company does not control keep rising — SG&A per store grew 4.8% in Q3, more than management expected — and operating margin fell from 21.89% in FY2021 to 19.46% in FY2025. Q3's beat included a $39M tariff refund that management calls a one-off. The balance sheet has no cushion: -$2.09B equity, a 0.77 current ratio, a 0.09 quick ratio, $7.71B of long-term debt and $194M of cash, with buybacks partly paid for by new borrowing toward a 2.5x leverage target. At 27 times trailing earnings and a PEG of 1.7, a single negative comp quarter could cut the price hard.

Top 3 Risks
01
Fading price increases in comps. Same-SKU inflation slowed from 5.5% in Q2 to slightly over 4% in Q3 and is expected at about 2.5%–3% in Q4. If unit growth does not fill the gap, comps slow into 2027 — and the price investors pay for the earnings, not the earnings themselves, is what takes the damage.
02
SG&A inflation the company can't control. Self-insurance reserves nearly doubled to $297.3M in 2025, and SG&A per store grew 4.8% in Q3 2026, more than management expected, on medical, self-insurance and fuel costs. Operating margin fell about 2.4 percentage points from FY2021 to FY2025 (21.89% to 19.46%), and there is no obvious way to reverse the trend.
03
Balance-sheet fragility if comps turn negative. Negative equity of -$2.09B, a 0.77 current ratio, a 0.09 quick ratio and $7.71B of long-term debt against $194M of cash work only while free cash flow is about $2B a year and predictable. Buybacks are partly paid for with borrowing. Several quarters of falling comps would force a quick choice between the buyback program and the leverage target.
  Industry Commentary
The Aging US Vehicle Fleet & Deferred Maintenance
EMERGING TAILWIND
On the Q2 2026 call management cited over 293 million light cars and light trucks on US roads with an average age of 13 years, and a record 3.3 trillion miles driven in 2025. This is the single most important fact about the industry, and it moves slowly and predictably: vehicles aged 7–15 years are the sweet spot for aftermarket parts demand, because they are out of warranty, out of the dealership service relationship, and still worth repairing. High new-vehicle prices have kept consumers in older cars longer, deepening the pool. Management noted similar dynamics in Mexico and Canada. The one caveat worth holding: an average age this high partly reflects deferred purchases, and deferred maintenance eventually becomes either a repair or a scrappage decision.
Tariffs on Imported Parts
ONGOING HEADWIND
The aftermarket supply chain is import-heavy, and 2025's tariff round pushed supplier prices up materially. O'Reilly's exposure is mostly indirect: its suppliers are usually the importer, so the tariffs reach it as higher supplier prices. The visible result was 5.5% same-SKU inflation in Q2 2026 and slightly over 4% in Q3, with about 2.5%–3% expected in Q4 as the 2025 increases pass their one-year mark. Q3 also brought $39 million of tariff refunds, which management treats as a one-off. Gross margin rose to 52.4% in Q3, which shows the company can pass costs on — but that cuts both ways: the price increases that protected margin now become a drag on comps, because growth that came from price drops out of the year-over-year comparison.
Consumer Trade-Down: Repair Over Replacement
BULL VS BEAR FRAMING
The bull framing: when new cars get less affordable, more owners repair instead of replacing. That is demand that holds up in bad times, and it helps explain a beta of 0.54 and the +19% month this stock had in October 2022. It supports both channels — DIY for the cost-conscious owner, DIFM for the owner who lacks the tools for an increasingly complex vehicle.

The bear framing: the 2026 data does not actually show trade-down producing units. DIY transaction counts fell in both Q2 and Q3; only professional ticket counts grew. A genuinely stressed consumer defers everything, including repair, until the failure is unavoidable — which shows up as declining traffic with a rising average ticket, exactly what O'Reilly reported. Which framing wins over the next few quarters, as inflation fades and unit trends show through, is the central question for the stock.
