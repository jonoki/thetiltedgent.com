01 Company Overview

Occidental Petroleum is a large-cap independent oil and gas producer built around a Permian Basin core, with additional US positions in the Rockies and the Gulf of America and international operations in Oman, the UAE and Algeria. Following the January 2026 divestiture of its chemicals arm, the company now reports through two operating segments — Oil and Gas and Midstream and Marketing — plus a development-stage Low Carbon Ventures effort centred on direct air capture. Continuing-operations revenue for fiscal 2025 was $21.59B (-1.9% YoY), restated to exclude the chemicals business; on a trailing basis revenue has since inflected to $23.93B (+18.5% YoY) as the 2026 oil-price shock flowed through. At a $61.50B market capitalisation, Occidental ranks roughly #411 globally and #194 in the United States by market value.

The equity story right now is not really a growth story — it is a balance-sheet repair story levered to crude. Principal debt has fallen from $20.8B at the end of Q3 2025 to $11.8B after Q2 2026, the lowest level since Q2 2019, funded by $9.7B of OxyChem sale proceeds and a windfall from war-driven oil prices. Sitting on top of the common stock is Berkshire Hathaway's ~$8.5B, 8% preferred position — roughly $700M a year of cash that leaves before common holders see anything — plus warrants on 83.86M shares. How and when that preferred is retired is the single most important structural variable in the story.

THE BERKSHIRE STRUCTURE — WHY IT DOMINATES THE COMMON
Berkshire's August 2019 $10B preferred investment financed the Anadarko takeover. As of 2026 roughly $8.5B of face value remains outstanding at an 8% coupon — about $700M of annual cash dividends that rank ahead of the common. The original terms require Occidental to redeem $0.10 of preferred (at a 10% premium) for every $1.00 of common distributions above $4.00 per share on a trailing-twelve-month basis, which is precisely why buybacks and dividend growth have been kept deliberately modest. The preferred first becomes optionally redeemable in August 2029 at a 5% premium; management has ranked that redemption fourth in its capital priority stack (behind the dividend, the balance sheet and the $10B principal-debt target) and explicitly ahead of opportunistic buybacks. Barclays expects Occidental to hit the $10B debt milestone and prefund the preferred by the second half of 2027. Separately, warrants on 83.86M shares struck at $59.62 (~$5B of exercise value, expiring one year after the preferred is redeemed) sit just in the money at the current $61.52 price — a live dilution overhang of roughly 8% of the current share count.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
PERMIAN CORE SCALE CO₂ EOR EXPERTISE LOW-COST OPS $7.80/BOE PERMIAN EGRESS CAPACITY OMAN & UAE CONCESSIONS BERKSHIRE ANCHOR DAC FIRST-MOVER

Occidental's moat is a cost-and-inventory moat rather than a pricing moat — no oil producer sets the price of its product. What differentiates OXY is the combination of a deep, contiguous Permian position (deepened by CrownRock in August 2024), domestic lease operating expense of $7.80/boe that is running 6% inside its own guidance, and forty years of accumulated CO2 enhanced-oil-recovery know-how that few peers can replicate. That EOR franchise matters twice over: it lowers the decline rate on legacy barrels, and it gives the Low Carbon Ventures business an internal customer for captured CO2 at a time when nobody else has one. Management's claim of a 45% recovery uplift in Permian CO2 pilots — potentially moving unconventional recovery factors from ~10% to 15–20% — is, if it scales, worth more than any carbon-credit narrative. Sustaining capital is guided to fall from $5.0–5.1B in 2027 to $4.5B by 2030 on lower decline rates and better well costs, and management has laid out a path to more than $4B of incremental annual sustainable cash flow by 2030, roughly 85% of it achievable at lower oil prices. The Midstream and Marketing arm adds a real if lumpy hedge: when Permian gas goes negative at the wellhead, the firm-transport position monetises the basis. Finally, Berkshire's 26.6% stake and clearance to go to 50% functions as a partial floor on the equity — though as discussed below, the same relationship is also the largest claim ahead of common holders.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Commodity price beta with a levered balance sheet. Q2 2026 Oil and Gas pre-tax income of $2.8B versus $1.0B in Q1 came off roughly a 38% move in realised crude. That works spectacularly on the way up and is brutal on the way down. Even after cutting principal debt to $11.8B, Occidental still carries $14.63B of total debt on the balance sheet against $42.38B of equity, and the entire 2026 deleveraging story was underwritten by a war premium in crude that has already begun unwinding — Brent averaged $126.41 in April and is near $97 now.

2. The preferred and warrant overhang. ~$8.5B of 8% preferred consumes about $700M of cash annually before common holders participate, the mandatory-redemption formula caps how fast common distributions can grow, and 83.86M warrants at $59.62 are now in the money — roughly 8% potential dilution. Management has explicitly deprioritised buybacks until the 2029 preferred redemption is addressed. Any equity holder is, in effect, subordinated to Berkshire.

3. Loss of the OxyChem counter-cyclical buffer. Selling chemicals removed the one segment whose earnings did not move with WTI, while retaining its legacy environmental and tort liabilities. Reported EPS is now almost entirely a function of one input price, and the residual liability tail has no offsetting revenue.

4. Low Carbon Ventures is capital-hungry and unproven. Stratos has consumed roughly $400M a year of capital and will not begin operations until 2027. The economics depend on the 45Q credit regime — a policy variable, not a commercial one — and on voluntary carbon-removal offtake at prices no market has yet validated. Management is openly seeking partners, which is a reasonable de-risking move and also an admission that the capital intensity is uncomfortable on its own balance sheet.

5. Permian gas takeaway and basis risk. Domestic natural gas realised negative $1.48/Mcf in Q2 2026. Occidental currently converts that into a midstream profit, but the underlying condition — associated gas with insufficient egress — is a structural drag on Permian returns and can force curtailments if pipelines go down.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS — READ BEFORE TRUSTING ANY TTM MULTIPLE

OxyChem disposal gain (Q1 2026). The $9.7B all-cash sale closed January 2, 2026 and is the dominant reason TTM net income is $6.54B against FY2025 net income of $1.61B, and why TTM EPS of $6.51 sits against FY2025 EPS of $1.61. Every trailing multiple on this page — P/E, net margin, ROE, earnings yield — is distorted by it. The forward P/E of 13.77 and the 2026 consensus EPS of $5.77 are the more honest valuation anchors.

Discontinued-operations restatement. Chemicals has been reclassified out of continuing operations, restating FY2025 revenue to $21.59B and FY2024 to $22.02B. Any comparison to pre-2026 published figures for Occidental will not tie.

Reported versus adjusted Q2 2026 EPS. Reported $2.75 against adjusted $2.40 — a $0.35 gap attributable to items management excluded from adjusted results (typically derivative mark-to-market, asset sale effects and disposal-related charges). Consensus was $1.84, so the beat is real on either basis, but the $2.75 figure should not be annualised.

Preferred redemption premia. Mandatory redemptions of the Berkshire preferred carry a 10% premium and the optional August 2029 redemption a 5% premium. These premia are charges against income available to common shareholders when they occur and will recur as the preferred is retired.

Interest expense step-down. Annualised interest has been reduced by approximately $630M versus 2025. This is a genuine, recurring improvement — the opposite of a one-off — and it flatters 2026 comparisons against 2025 in a way that will not repeat at the same magnitude.

Midstream basis windfall. The record ~$960M midstream quarter came substantially from an abnormally wide Waha-to-Gulf-Coast gas spread; management guided Q3 income lower as it narrows. Do not extrapolate.

⚠ STATIC — AUG 20, 2026

Five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 close of $27.64 to $61.52 on August 20, 2026, OXY returned +122.6% in price terms — roughly 17.3% annualised — but that headline flatters a genuinely miserable middle. The stock trebled from late 2021 into the October 2022 high of $68.30 on the post-invasion energy shock and Berkshire's very public accumulation, then did nothing for two years, drifting sideways in the mid-$50s through 2023 before grinding lower all through 2024 as crude softened and the CrownRock debt landed. It bottomed at $38.40 in April 2025 — a 44% drawdown from the 2022 high — and spent the rest of 2025 in the $40s. The entire 2026 recovery is a war trade: the late-February Iran–Israel–US conflict drove Brent from $69.82 in January to $126.41 in April, and OXY from $44.97 to a March close of $64.70. That premium then unwound hard — back to $48.57 by June — before the Q2 beat and continued deleveraging drove the stock to $61.52. Anyone who bought the 2022 top is still down 10% five years later. That is the honest characterisation of this equity: it is a leveraged, mean-reverting call option on crude, not a compounder.

Relative performance. Over the ten years of Vicki Hollub's tenure, OXY's share price fell 26% while ConocoPhillips rose 153% and Chevron rose 88% — a devastating comparison that explains both the CEO change and the current strategic urgency. On a one-year view the gap has narrowed: OXY is +39.95% over 52 weeks, against roughly +38% for ConocoPhillips, +50% for Chevron, +50% for ExxonMobil, +47% for Diamondback and +25% for EOG (all measured as market-capitalisation change). So OXY has been an average performer in an exceptional year for the sector, after a decade of severe underperformance. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The balance sheet is being repaired faster than the market priced. Principal debt has gone from $20.8B to $11.8B in three quarters — the lowest since Q2 2019 — with $4.2B of cash against it, and annualised interest is down ~$630M. Barclays expects the $10B milestone and prefunding of the Berkshire preferred by 2H 2027, which would retire ~$700M of annual preferred dividends and free capital for buybacks that management has deliberately deferred. Underneath, the operating business is genuinely low-cost ($7.80/boe domestic LOE, 6% inside guidance), sustaining capital is guided down to $4.5B by 2030, and management has laid out a path to more than $4B of additional annual sustainable cash flow by 2030 with ~85% of it achievable at lower oil prices. At 13.77x forward earnings with a 7.36% FCF yield, you are paid to wait — and Berkshire, at a low-$50s cost basis with clearance to go to 50%, is a large and patient buyer standing underneath the stock.

Bear Case

Strip out the war and there is no story. Brent averaged $69.82 in January and $126.41 in April; every dollar of the 2026 deleveraging was funded by a geopolitical premium that has already fallen back to ~$97 and can keep falling. FY2025 — a normal price year — produced EPS of $1.61 and a ~7.5% net margin on continuing operations, and the current TTM EPS of $6.51 is largely an OxyChem disposal gain that will never repeat. The company sold its only counter-cyclical earnings stream while keeping its environmental liabilities, still generates a 10.63% ROE and 8.60% ROIC in a windfall year, and sits behind $8.5B of 8% preferred plus 83.86M in-the-money warrants (~8% dilution). Under the prior decade of management the stock fell 26% while ConocoPhillips rose 153%. At a forward multiple above every major E&P peer, you are paying a premium for a fix that is not finished.

01
Crude price reversion. Oil and Gas pre-tax income swung from $1.0B to $2.8B on a ~38% move in realised crude. A retreat from ~$97 Brent toward the $70 level that prevailed in January 2026 would remove most of the free cash flow now servicing the deleveraging plan and stall the $10B debt target — the sole pillar of the current bull thesis.
02
Structural subordination to Berkshire. ~$8.5B of 8% preferred takes ~$700M of cash annually ahead of common; the mandatory-redemption formula (10% premium on every $0.10 redeemed per $1.00 of common distributions above $4.00/share) caps distribution growth; and 83.86M warrants at $59.62 are in the money at today's $61.52. Buybacks are explicitly deprioritised until 2029. Common holders are last in line by design.
03
Capital-allocation track record and the 2027 spend step-up. Anadarko (2019) and CrownRock (2024) were both bought with expensive capital near cycle highs, and the result is an 8.60% ROIC in a windfall year. 2027 capex steps up to ~$5.9B, and roughly $400M a year has gone into a direct air capture business with no revenue whose economics depend on the 45Q credit regime. There is no track record here that justifies assuming the next allocation decision will be a good one.
  Industry Commentary
Oil Prices, OPEC+ Policy and the Hormuz Premium
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every material number on this page traces back to one variable. Brent averaged $69.82 in January 2026; the Iran–Israel–US conflict that erupted in late February disrupted Middle East supply and Strait of Hormuz shipping, driving Brent to an average of $126.41 in April before the premium eased to roughly $97 today. UBS has publicly modelled a $150 worst case. Occidental realised $96.78/bbl for crude in Q2 against WTI of $92.79, producing $2.8B of Oil and Gas pre-tax income versus $1.0B in Q1. The company's own international assets in Oman and the UAE sit inside the disrupted region — Q2 international volumes were reduced by the conflict, so the geopolitics cut both ways. The asymmetry to watch: a ceasefire or an OPEC+ supply response would compress prices toward the pre-war $70 level and take the deleveraging story with it, while any further escalation around Hormuz would do the opposite. Separately, domestic natural gas realised negative $1.48/Mcf — Permian associated gas remains structurally long relative to takeaway capacity.
Balance-Sheet Repair Is the Equity Story
EMERGING TAILWIND
Occidental has been a deleveraging story since the $55B Anadarko acquisition in 2019 took peak debt to $38.5B. Debt stood at $15.2B at the end of Hollub's tenure and $11.8B principal after Q2 2026 — the lowest since Q2 2019 — with net principal debt of $7.6B against $4.2B of unrestricted cash. Total balance-sheet debt fell from $23.35B at FY2025 year-end to $14.63B, equity rose from $36.60B to $42.38B, debt-to-equity halved from 0.64 to 0.35, and the current ratio moved from 0.94 to 1.41. Two things funded it: $9.7B of OxyChem proceeds and a war-inflated crude price. Richard Jackson's stated near-term target is $10B of principal debt, after which the preferred redemption and then buybacks come into view. This is a real, measurable, management-controllable improvement — and it is the reason the stock trades at a forward multiple above cheaper, less-levered peers like EOG (10.04x) and Devon (9.39x). Consolidation across the sector (Devon's $58B Coterra merger, Occidental's own CrownRock deal) continues to concentrate Permian inventory in fewer hands.
Carbon Capture Economics and 45Q — Bull vs Bear
ONGOING HEADWIND
Occidental is the most exposed US producer to engineered carbon removal, and the market has never been sure whether that is an asset or a distraction. The bull framing: CO2 and power are ~30% of the operating cost of an EOR barrel, so Occidental is the one DAC developer with a captive internal customer for the product; Stratos moves from construction to operations in 2027; roughly $400M of annual Low Carbon Ventures capital rolls off from next year, mechanically lifting free cash flow; and management is bringing in partners to share the burden. Layered on top, Permian CO2-EOR pilots are reportedly delivering a 45% recovery uplift, potentially taking unconventional recovery factors from ~10% to 15–20% — a far larger prize than carbon credits. The bear framing: DAC has no demonstrated commercial market. Its returns depend on the 45Q tax credit, a policy variable subject to political reversal, and on voluntary carbon-removal offtake at prices that have never been validated at scale. The plant is not yet commissioned, Trains 3 and 4 required non-technology repairs, and Occidental has spent hundreds of millions annually on it while carrying $8.5B of 8% preferred. The resolution: the capex roll-off is close to certain and near-term positive regardless of outcome; the commercial proof is a 2027–2030 question that should be valued as an option, not underwritten as an earnings stream.
