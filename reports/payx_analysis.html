01 Company Overview

Paychex sells payroll, HR, benefits and insurance services to small and mid-sized employers — about 840,000 customers, some 800,000 of them payroll clients, mostly in the United States (fiscal 2026 10-K); it says it pays 1 in 11 U.S. private-sector workers. Among the listed payroll companies on this page only ADP is larger, and since buying Paycor in April 2025 Paychex also competes for mid-market clients against Paycom, Paylocity and the venture-funded newcomers. Fiscal 2026 (ended May 31, 2026) revenue was $6.51B, up 17%, most of it bought. The first quarter of fiscal 2027, reported September 23, was the first with Paycor in both periods: revenue +6% to $1.63B, adjusted EPS +10% to $1.34. At $35.35B of market value it ranks #704 globally (companiesmarketcap.com, September 29 close).

The quarter moved the debate rather than settling it. Paychex paid about $4.1B for Paycor with borrowed money and carries $4.56B of long-term borrowings against about $1.0B of cash and corporate investments. With the deal lapped, reported growth is now organic growth, and the engine ran at two speeds: Management Solutions, the payroll and HR software core, grew 4%, while PEO & Insurance grew 12% as clients moved up from HR outsourcing (ASO, administrative services only) into the co-employment PEO. Management calls that shift "left pocket, right pocket" — revenue moving between segments, and growing as it moves. The stock fell 8.8% on the day and closed September 29 at $99.31, 18.4% below this report's previous edition. At that price it trades at 19.7× trailing GAAP earnings and 16.4× forward — level with Paycom and Paylocity — yields 4.79%, and the average analyst target is 15% above it. Whether a 4% core with a 12% PEO is a slowing roll-up or a better mix at a lower price is the question now.

This is the recurring-fee business: payroll processing and tax filing, the Paychex Flex and Paycor HCM (human capital management — the software layer covering hiring, time, benefits and HR compliance) platforms, retirement-plan administration, and HR outsourcing (ASO) sold per client, per employee and per payroll. Revenue rises with client count, checks per client (how many people each client pays — a direct read on small-business hiring) and revenue per check (price plus extra modules). Paycor — over 49,000 clients and about 2.7M employees supported at announcement — was folded in here. In Q1 FY2027 the segment grew 4% to $1,213.1M, "driven by product penetration and price realization," and came in "slightly below our expectations," in Schrader's words. Part of the gap is where the revenue lands: when an ASO client upgrades to the PEO, its revenue leaves this segment for PEO & Insurance, and Gibson put the uplift at "about 3.5x." ASO-to-PEO conversions ran at double management's plan. The FY2027 guide stays at 5–6%, which Schrader said was built "roughly split between pricing and ancillary attachment" — not client growth — and he warned the segment could trend "towards the low end" if the PEO upgrades continue. He put Q4 FY2026 organic growth in the segment at "probably around 5%." Margin profile: this is where most of the ~42–44% adjusted operating margin lives; one more processed check costs close to nothing.

A PEO (professional employer organization) becomes the co-employer of a client's staff: Paychex puts the workers on its own tax IDs, buys health insurance and workers' compensation at large-group rates, and charges the small business a bundled fee. The accounting point matters more after this quarter. Gibson said that, apart from a Florida health program, the insurance Paychex provides across the country does not run through its PEO revenue — Paychex earns an administrative fee for each worksite employee — whereas some competitors book the premiums as revenue. The Florida program (MPP) is pass-through, and its enrollment was up by double digits, per Schrader. Q1 segment revenue rose 12% to $367.6M, "primarily due to growth in the number of average PEO worksite employees and increased PEO insurance volumes" (release); Schrader put worksite-employee growth at "high single digits, just shy of double digits," and Gibson said PEO retention was at a record. Referrals into the PEO from the HCM sales teams were up almost 50% year on year. The FY2027 guide was raised to 7–8% from 6–7%, with two caveats from the CFO: comparisons get harder as the year goes on, and the October and January benefit enrollments are still ahead. Paychex supports about 2.6 million worksite employees across its HR outsourcing products (10-K).

When a client funds payroll, Paychex holds the cash — wages, withholding, payroll taxes — for a few days before it reaches employees and tax authorities. Across hundreds of thousands of clients that is $4,348.4M of funds held for clients at August 31, 2026 ($4,832.2M at May 31; point-in-time figures — the FY2026 average balance was $5,768.2M, earning 3.5%, per the 10-K). The float is invested in short and intermediate fixed income, and the yield is nearly pure profit. FY2026 interest on client funds was $210.9M, up 30%, on higher average balances from Paycor and realized gains from repositioning the portfolio in the second quarter (10-K). In June management guided FY2027 down to $195–205M; in September it raised the range to $200–210M, which Schrader said "includes the most recent 25 basis point increase to the Fed funds rate." Q1 float income rose 5% to $49.8M on "stronger reinvestment yields on our long-term portfolio." The new range is flat to 5% below FY2026. Rate sensitivity: Paychex estimates that a 25 basis point change in short-term rates moves earnings by about $4.5–5.0M after tax over twelve months (FY2026 10-K) — about a penny a share (our arithmetic, on 356M shares). Its target mix is about 35% short-term securities and 65% longer securities with an average duration of two to three and a quarter years, which reprice only as they roll over. Small per notch, but it lands on earnings in both directions.

The uncomfortable pairing: over the last four quarters interest on client funds was $213.1M and interest expense on Paychex's own debt was $266.4M (our sum of the two releases). In Q1 alone: $49.8M earned, $65.1M paid. The company still pays more interest than its float earns.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
PAYROLL-TAX SWITCHING COSTS 800K PAYROLL CLIENTS CPA, BANK & BROKER REFERRALS CLIENT-FUNDS FLOAT PEO SCALE IN INSURANCE BUYING RECORD PEO RETENTION 42–44% ADJ. MARGIN → PRICING ROOM

The moat is inertia with a legal edge. Payroll is the one back-office function a small business cannot get wrong — a missed tax deposit brings penalties, and some of that liability can land on the owner personally — so once a provider is filing correctly, the reward for switching is small and the risk is real. Paychex adds a distribution machine most software companies would envy: a territory sales force, referral relationships with the accountants, banks and insurance brokers who advise small-business owners (Gibson said those channels "drive a significant portion of our leads"), and now Paycor's mid-market reach. The PEO deepens the lock-in: Schrader called it "very sticky for clients when they have all their insurances through us," and Gibson reported record PEO retention. The financial expression of the moat is a 42.0% adjusted operating margin in Q1 FY2027 (43.2% for FY2026) and a 47% return on equity — numbers that survive because incumbents have not had to compete hard on price; Gibson said he has "not seen any real shifts at all in the competition." The float adds a subtler layer: Paychex earns interest on money that belongs to its clients. What the moat is not is technological. Paychex Flex is competent; it is not why clients stay.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The core grew 4% in the first clean quarter. Management Solutions was below management's own plan, its full-year guide of 5–6% rests on price and module attach rather than more clients, and the CFO has already pointed it toward the low end. Some of the shortfall is revenue moving into the PEO, but a payroll core growing at 4% is what the market is now pricing.
▸
Software-native competitors are cheaper and faster at the small end. Gusto and Rippling (private; financials not disclosed) sell self-serve payroll that undercuts a salesperson-plus-service model, while Paycom (+9.2% revenue TTM) and Paylocity (+11.0%) out-grow Paychex's core in the mid-market Paycor was bought to defend. Paychex's answer is AI: more than 2,000 agents and features deployed across the business, AI spending up fivefold on last year, and WISE Hire, an agentic recruiting product launched with the results. Whether that holds price is the open question.
▸
Growth now leans on the PEO — and on health-insurance season. PEO & Insurance grew 12% while the core grew 4%. Gibson called employee health benefits "a major issue that's facing our markets" and said health inflation "causes people to look around" — good for PEO sales, but the October and January enrollments decide whether clients stay, and in the Florida program the plans employees pick move reported revenue.
▸
Leverage and the payout. Paycor cost about $4.1B, funded with incremental debt. Long-term borrowings are $4.56B, debt/EBITDA 1.49×, and interest expense ($266.4M over four quarters) exceeds float income ($213.1M). The dividend takes 94% of GAAP earnings; in Q1 dividends ($424.1M) exceeded operating cash flow ($413.5M, hit by tax-payment timing) and no shares were bought back.

Notable One-Off Items

The GAAP numbers are still dragged down by the deal, the adjusted numbers still leave part of its cost out, and the next quarter has a hard comparison built in.

Paycor acquisition-related costs: $65.5M in Q1 FY2027, down from $84.8M a year earlier: $56.9M of intangible amortisation, $8.5M of compensation costs (replacement awards, severance, retention) and $0.1M other. They separate GAAP operating income of $619.2M (38.0% margin) from adjusted operating income of $684.7M (42.0%), and GAAP EPS of $1.21 from adjusted $1.34. The compensation piece is fading; the amortisation is not — $56.9M a quarter is an annual rate near $230M (our arithmetic), after $242.0M in FY2026 within total FY2026 acquisition-related costs of $304.2M. Adjusted EPS will keep excluding it.

Q2 has a hard comparison. Schrader said Q2 FY2026 included two one-time items — a revenue-synergy benefit from the acquisition and realized gains from repositioning the investment portfolio — so Q2 FY2027 revenue growth is guided to about 4% with an adjusted operating margin of about 40%. Excluding those items, he said, Q2 growth would match Q1's.

Cash timing. Q1 operating cash flow of $413.5M, against $718.4M a year earlier, reflected the timing of client and corporate tax payments, per the CFO. Q1 dividends were $424.1M; share repurchases were zero, against $160.1M in Q1 FY2026. Other income, net fell 54% to $10.9M.

Interest expense was $65.1M in Q1 (−5%). It did not exist at this scale before the Paycor financing, and it is why FY2026 net income grew only 6% on 17% revenue growth and why FY2025 EPS fell 1.9%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The Paycor arithmetic, a year and a half in. Paychex paid about $4.1B for a business with over 49,000 clients and about 2.7M employees supported, funded with incremental debt. What it got: FY2026 Management Solutions growth of 20%, float income that grew 30% as Paycor's client funds came along (with help from portfolio-repositioning gains), and a sales force that now refers mid-market clients into the PEO — referrals up almost 50% in Q1. What it costs: $266.4M of interest expense over the last four quarters, more than the float earned; amortisation of $56.9M a quarter that adjusted EPS leaves out; and a GAAP operating margin of 38.0% against an adjusted 42.0%. Schrader now expects the enterprise business to grow in the high single digits, in line with its peers; analysts on the call recalled hopes for double digits at the purchase.

What the first lapped quarter showed. Total revenue +6%, adjusted operating margin +130bp to 42.0%, adjusted EPS +10%, total expenses +1% — a clean quarter on the income statement. Underneath, Management Solutions +4% and PEO & Insurance +12%, with management attributing much of the gap to ASO clients upgrading into the PEO at "about 3.5x" the revenue. Guidance for the year did not change in total; it moved between segments. The market's verdict was the September 23 close: −8.77%. The stock now prices a mature payroll processor growing its core at 4–5%, with a PEO doing the accelerating and a Q2 guided to about 4%.

The two structural earners, sized. Float: $49.8M in Q1 and $200–210M guided for the year on $4.35B of client funds at August 31 — 3% of revenue but, at near-100% margin, roughly 7–8% of operating income (est.: $213.1M of TTM float income against $2.87B of TTM adjusted and $2.59B of GAAP operating income). A 25bp move in short-term rates is worth about $4.5–5.0M after tax, about a penny a share (10-K; per-share figure our arithmetic). PEO & Insurance: $367.6M in Q1, 22–23% of the mix. Management's account is that outside the Florida program, insurance premiums do not flow through Paychex's PEO revenue, so the segment's growth tracks worksite employees (high single digits) more than medical inflation; the Florida program's enrollment was up double digits. Paychex does not publish the split, which is itself worth knowing.

Macro sensitivity — the small-business labour market. Revenue is a function of how many small businesses Paychex serves, how many people each employs and what it charges per check. The guide leans on price and attach, so checks per client and worksite employees are the swing factors. Gibson described the hiring backdrop as "kind of low fire, a little hire," said he was "still not seeing any signs of a recession," and reported that out-of-business and financial-stress losses improved in the quarter. If small-business hiring softens, checks per client, PEO worksite employees and the float balance all shrink together, and the ~44% target margin has operating leverage in reverse. The 0.81 beta understates how correlated those three lines are.

Valuation, three ways. (i) 19.7× trailing GAAP EPS of $5.04 — the number that charges for the acquisition amortisation and the interest. (ii) 17.6× trailing adjusted EPS of $5.63, or 16.4× forward (16.7× the FY2027 consensus of $5.96) — what the Street quotes. (iii) 17.5× free cash flow ($2.02B TTM; EV/FCF 19.4×) with a 4.79% dividend yield — the income lens, and the most flattering, because free cash flow ignores the amortisation entirely. At the September 4 edition the stock was 24.9× trailing and 20.4× forward with the average target below the price; now it offers roughly a 6% forward earnings yield plus a 4.8% dividend yield, trades level with Paycom and Paylocity on forward earnings, and sits below the average target. Consensus FY2027 adjusted EPS barely moved ($5.98 at the previous edition, $5.96 now); the 18% fall came almost entirely from the multiple.

Bull Case

Paychex is a 42–44% adjusted-margin business with about 840,000 customers, record PEO retention and a PEO growing worksite employees at high single digits — its highest-value, stickiest product. The Q1 "miss" was a mix shift the CFO called "left pocket, right pocket": total revenue grew 6%, the adjusted margin rose 130bp, adjusted EPS grew 10% and the float guide went up. At 16.4× forward earnings with a 4.79% yield, the stock is cheaper than ADP and level with faster-growing Paycom and Paylocity. If PEO upgrades keep running at double management's plan, lifetime value per client rises and the market will have de-rated the stock for a quarter that met its own guide.

Bear Case

Take away the relabelling and the payroll core grew 4%, below its 5–6% guide, with price doing about half the work and client count not a driver. Bears will read part of the PEO jump as insurance volume — Florida program enrollment was up double digits — and two enrollment seasons lie ahead that management itself cited as a reason for caution. Q2 is guided to about 4%. The dividend takes 94% of GAAP earnings, Q1 dividends exceeded Q1 operating cash flow, buybacks paused, and the debt costs more in interest ($266.4M over four quarters) than the float earns ($213.1M). Most post-print target changes were cuts, and 15 of 19 analysts sit at Hold.

01
Core deceleration below the guide. Management Solutions grew 4% against a 5–6% full-year guide that the CFO has already steered toward the low end. With clients not a growth driver, a 3–4% core in Q2 — when total growth is guided to about 4% — would test the market's patience with the "left pocket, right pocket" explanation.
02
Enrollment season and health costs. The PEO is now the growth engine, and its October and January enrollments are still ahead. Health-cost inflation drives employers to shop, which helps new sales but tests retention, and in the Florida program the plans employees choose move reported revenue.
03
Rates, employment and the payout. The float ($200–210M guided, near-100% margin) moves with rates in both directions; a small-business hiring slowdown would hit checks per client, PEO worksite employees and float balances at once. Against that sit $4.56B of borrowings and a $4.76 dividend that is 94% of GAAP EPS — covered by free cash flow (about $1.69B a year at the current rate against $2.02B TTM, our arithmetic), but with little room left for the ~10% annual raises of recent years if EPS grows 7–9%.
  Industry Commentary
Payroll Float Is a Rate Trade Wearing a Software Multiple
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every payroll processor earns interest on client money in transit — ADP, Paychex, Paycom and Paylocity all do — and from 2022 to 2025 rising rates made that near-100%-margin line grow quickly. Paychex's rose 30% in FY2026 to $210.9M, partly on Paycor's added balances. In June it guided FY2027 below that ($195–205M); in September it lifted the range to $200–210M, a revision the CFO tied to the most recent 25bp increase in the Fed funds rate. The lesson for the group is that this line swings with rate decisions rather than with anything management does: reported margins in 2023–2025 were helped by it, and the next move in rates, up or down, lands on earnings as the portfolio rolls over. Sensitivity for PAYX, by its own estimate: about $4.5–5.0M of after-tax earnings per 25bp move in short-term rates over a year (FY2026 10-K).
Incumbents Are Buying the Mid-Market They Failed to Build
ONGOING HEADWIND
Paychex–Paycor ($4.1B, April 2025) is the sector's template: a small-business incumbent buys a mid-market software vendor for growth and channel, funds it with debt, and reports adjusted EPS that excludes the amortisation of what it bought. A year and a half later, Schrader expects the enterprise business to grow in the high single digits, in line with its peers, and said the post-COVID employment growth that helped the mid-market when Paycor was bought has not returned. Paycom (+9.2%) and Paylocity (+11.0%) keep growing organically in the same segment, and after September all three trade at about 16–17× forward earnings (PAYX 16.4×, Paycom 16.6×, Paylocity 16.3×) while ADP holds 21.4×. Gusto and Rippling attack the bottom of the market with self-serve pricing. The question for the whole group is whether "HCM platform" is a moat or a commodity — and Paychex's answer, for now, is to sell advice and co-employment on top of the software.
Small-Business Hiring Is the Real Denominator
STABLE — FOR NOW
Checks per client is the payroll industry's version of same-store sales: it captures whether existing small businesses are adding heads. Paychex publishes its own monthly Small Business Employment Watch because its revenue, its PEO worksite employees and its float balance all move with it. On the September 23 call Gibson described a "low fire, a little hire" market, said clients are "having trouble finding people," and reported improving out-of-business losses. Bull framing: with the guide built on price and attach, any pickup in small-business hiring flows almost entirely to revenue at a high incremental margin. Bear framing: the same lever cuts in reverse — a hiring slowdown lowers checks, worksite employees and float in the same quarter, and the 0.81 beta gives no warning because most of the stock's history was written when Paychex carried little debt and rates were a tailwind.
