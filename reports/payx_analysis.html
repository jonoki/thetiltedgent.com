01 Company Overview

Paychex sells payroll, HR, benefits and insurance services to small and mid-sized employers — roughly 800,000 clients, mostly in the United States. It is the number-two name in U.S. small-business payroll behind ADP, and after buying Paycor in April 2025 it now also competes for mid-market clients against Paycom, Paylocity and the venture-funded newcomers. Fiscal 2026 (ended May 31, 2026) revenue was $6.51B, up 17% — the fastest growth in decades and almost entirely bought rather than earned. At $43.29B of market value it ranks roughly #576 globally (companiesmarketcap.com; stockanalysis.com ranks it #367 on a narrower universe).

The debate on this page is simple to state. Paychex spent about $4.1B and took on roughly $4.6B of net debt to buy growth it was no longer generating organically. Strip Paycor out and the underlying business is growing mid-single digits, client count is guided "relatively flat," and the two quiet profit engines — interest earned on client payroll float and PEO insurance pass-through — are now, respectively, shrinking with rates and structurally low-margin. Management is guiding FY2027 revenue growth of just 5–6%. The stock pays a 3.91% dividend that consumes 97% of GAAP earnings, trades at 24.9× trailing GAAP earnings, and consensus says Hold with a price target below the current price. Whether that is a fair price for a bond-like compounder or a rich one for a slowing roll-up is the whole question.

A PEO (professional employer organization) becomes the co-employer of a client's staff: Paychex puts the workers on its own tax IDs, buys health insurance and workers' compensation at large-group rates, and charges the small business a bundled fee. The important accounting point is that a chunk of this revenue is not really Paychex's — it is insurance premium collected from the client and handed to the carrier. Paychex reports PEO revenue net of most payroll pass-through but includes certain insurance premiums and the workers' comp float, so segment revenue overstates the economic value added and segment margin understates it relative to Management Solutions. Growth here follows worksite employees (the number of client staff on the PEO) and medical-plan attach; both move with small-business hiring and with health-insurance inflation, which is why the segment can grow 6–7% (FY2027 guide) while real volume grows less. The insurance agency side (health, workers' comp, business insurance brokered to non-PEO clients) is commission income. FY2026 segment growth of 7% and the FY2027 guide of 6–7% are the steadiest numbers in the company — and the lowest-quality dollars.

When a client funds payroll, Paychex holds the cash — wages, withholding, payroll taxes — for a few days before it reaches employees and the IRS. Multiply that by 800,000 clients and the balance sheet shows $4,832.2M of funds held for clients at May 31, 2026 (a point-in-time figure; the average balance across the year, which is what actually earns the interest, was not disclosed in the sources fetched). That float is invested in short and intermediate fixed income, and the yield is nearly pure profit: no salespeople, no servers, no churn. FY2026 interest on client funds was $210.9M, up 30%, because Paycor brought its own float with it. Now the tide turns: management guides FY2027 to $195–205M, a decline of 3–8%, as reinvestment yields roll lower. Rate sensitivity (est.): on a ~$5B average balance, every 25 basis points of yield is roughly $12–13M of revenue, or about $0.03 of after-tax EPS — small per notch, but it drops straight to the bottom line in both directions. A full percentage point is about $50M, or roughly 2% of operating income.

The uncomfortable pairing: FY2026 interest income on client funds was $210.9M. FY2026 interest expense on Paychex's own (mostly Paycor) debt was $269.5M. The company now pays more interest than its float earns.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
PAYROLL-TAX SWITCHING COSTS 800K-CLIENT DISTRIBUTION CPA REFERRAL CHANNEL CLIENT-FUNDS FLOAT PEO SCALE IN INSURANCE BUYING COMPLIANCE DATA MOAT 44% ADJ. MARGIN → PRICING ROOM

The moat is inertia with a legal edge. Payroll is the one back-office function a small business cannot get wrong — a missed tax deposit brings penalties and a personal liability for the owner — so once a provider is filing your 941s correctly, the reward for switching is small and the risk is real. Paychex compounds that with a distribution machine most software companies would envy: a direct sales force organised by territory, a decades-old referral relationship with the accountants who actually advise small-business owners, and now Paycor's mid-market channel and partnership network. The financial expression of the moat is a 43.2% adjusted operating margin on FY2026 revenue and a 44.77% return on equity — numbers that survive because incumbents have not had to compete on price. The float adds a second, subtler layer: Paychex earns interest on money that belongs to its clients, which no software-only entrant without a payroll-tax license can replicate. What the moat is not is technological. Paychex Flex is competent; it is not why clients stay.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Organic growth is mid-single digits and client count is flat. Q4 FY2026 Management Solutions grew 14% with ~8 points from Paycor; FY2027 is guided at 5–6% with the deal fully lapped. Management described client count growth as "relatively flat." A 24.9× P/E on a business whose unit count is not growing is a price paid for pricing power and buybacks, not for expansion.
▸
Software-native competitors are cheaper and faster at the small end. Gusto and Rippling (private; financials not disclosed) sell self-serve payroll that undercuts a salesperson-plus-service model, while Paycom (+9.2% revenue TTM) and Paylocity (+11.0%) out-grow Paychex organically in the mid-market Paycor was bought to defend. Paychex's answer is to bolt AI ("WISE", 600+ features and agents) onto a legacy stack; whether that holds price is the open question.
▸
Two macro exposures the company does not control. Interest on client funds ($210.9M) moves with short-term rates and is guided down for FY2027. Checks-per-client and PEO worksite employees move with small-business hiring — the segment of the labour market most sensitive to credit conditions and consumer demand. Both have been tailwinds since 2022; both can reverse together.
▸
Integration and leverage. Paycor cost about $4.1B, funded entirely with debt. Net debt stands at $4.56B, interest expense ran $269.5M in FY2026, and the call flagged "sales territory alignment challenges." The company has exceeded its $80M+ cost-synergy target, but revenue synergies remain a promise "over the next several years." Debt/EBITDA of 1.52× is manageable; it is also the highest in Paychex's history.

Notable One-Off Items

The FY2026 GAAP numbers are distorted in one direction — down — and the adjusted numbers are distorted in the other. Both matter.

Paycor acquisition-related costs: $304.2M for FY2026 ($71.1M in Q4 alone), comprising $242.0M of intangible amortisation ($60.5M in Q4), $52.1M of compensation costs (severance, retention, replacements; $10.4M in Q4) and integration costs. All of it is added back to reach adjusted operating income of $2,814.7M (43.2% margin) from GAAP operating income of $2,510.5M (38.6%). The compensation and integration pieces are genuinely non-recurring. The amortisation is not: $4.5B of goodwill and a stack of identified intangibles will keep producing roughly $240M a year of amortisation for years, and adjusted EPS will keep excluding it. On a per-share basis the add-back was $0.84 net of a $0.20 tax benefit — the entire gap between $4.89 GAAP and $5.51 adjusted.

The Q4 comparison is flattered by last year's mess. Q4 FY2026 operating income rose 40% and diluted EPS rose 43% — but only because Q4 FY2025 (the quarter Paycor closed, April 14, 2025) absorbed the initial deal costs and a 30.2% operating margin. Adjusted EPS growth in Q4 was a more honest +11%.

Interest expense of $269.5M is not a one-off but it is new: it did not exist at this scale before FY2025. It is why net income grew only 6% on 17% revenue growth and why FY2025 EPS actually fell 1.9%.

Interest on client funds grew 30% to $210.9M on Paycor's added float. Guidance for FY2027 is $195–205M, so this line flips from tailwind to headwind of roughly $6–16M — about 0.5% of operating income. Small, but every dollar of it is margin.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The Paycor arithmetic, laid out. Paychex paid about $4.1B for a business with ~49,000 clients and ~2.7M employees served, and financed all of it with debt. What it got: FY2026 Management Solutions growth of 20% (of which Q4's ~8 points from Paycor is the cleanest read), $210.9M of float income that grew 30% because Paycor's client funds came along, and cost synergies that beat the $80M+ target inside the first fiscal year. What it paid beyond the price: $269.5M of interest expense, $242M of amortisation and $62M of one-time compensation and integration in FY2026 alone, a GAAP operating margin that fell 100bp to 38.6% while the adjusted margin rose 70bp to 43.2%, and net income growth of just 6% on 17% revenue growth. The deal was "neutral to slightly accretive" to adjusted EPS in year one, exactly as promised — and adjusted EPS excludes the amortisation of the thing it bought.

What is left when the deal is lapped. FY2027 guidance is the first honest look: total revenue +5–6%, Management Solutions +5–6%, PEO & Insurance +6–7%, interest on funds down to $195–205M, adjusted EPS +7–9%. That is a mature payroll processor whose unit count is flat, growing revenue through price and attach, growing earnings through margin (~44% adjusted) and buybacks, and paying out essentially everything. It is a perfectly good business. It is also being asked to carry a 24.9× GAAP / 20.4× forward multiple while Paycom and Paylocity — growing 9–11% organically — trade at 17.5× forward.

The two structural earners, sized. Float: $210.9M of revenue on a $4.83B year-end balance (average balance not disclosed) is 3.2% of revenue but, at near-100% margin, something like 8% of operating income. Every 25bp of yield is ~$12–13M or ~$0.03 of EPS (est.). PEO & Insurance: $1,433M of revenue, 22% of the mix, but a meaningful share of that is insurance premium passing through to carriers — the segment adds far less value than its revenue line implies, and its growth partly reflects medical-cost inflation rather than volume. The company's disclosure does not let an outside analyst separate the two, which is itself worth knowing.

Macro sensitivity — the small-business labour market. Paychex's revenue is a function of how many small businesses exist (client count), how many people each employs (checks per client) and how much Paychex charges per check. Management has told investors client count will be "relatively flat," which means FY2027 growth depends on the other two. Checks per client is the purest read on small-business hiring in the U.S. economy — Paychex publishes its own Small Business Employment Watch for that reason (the latest release was not fetched and is not quoted here). If small-business hiring softens, checks per client, PEO worksite employees and the float balance all shrink at once, and the ~44% margin has operating leverage in reverse. The 0.81 beta understates how correlated those three lines are.

Valuation, three ways. (i) 24.9× trailing GAAP EPS of $4.89 — the number that charges for the acquisition amortisation and the interest. (ii) 22.1× adjusted FY2026 EPS of $5.51, or 20.4× forward on ~$5.98 — what the Street quotes. (iii) 18.8× free cash flow ($2.32B; EV/FCF 20.35×) with a 3.91% dividend yield — the income investor's lens, and the most flattering, because FCF ignores the amortisation entirely and the debt only shows up in EV. The defensible base is between (i) and (ii): the amortisation is a real cost of the growth Paychex bought, but it is not a cash cost, and the interest is already in both. At ~22× a business guided to grow earnings 7–9%, the stock offers a roughly 4.5% earnings yield plus 3.9% dividend yield for a total shareholder yield that is fine — not the bargain it was at $92, and not the compounder the 2024 multiple assumed.

Bull Case

Paychex is a 43–44% adjusted-margin annuity with 800,000 clients who almost never leave, a 27% ROIC even after $4.5B of goodwill, and $2.32B of free cash flow that funds a 3.9% dividend and buybacks with room to spare. Paycor's cost synergies are already exceeded; revenue synergies — PEO, retirement and insurance sold into 49,000 mid-market clients — are pure upside not in the 5–6% guide. If rates stay higher for longer the float guide proves conservative, and a 20× forward multiple on a defensive compounder with a growing dividend is where quality has historically been bought, not sold.

Bear Case

Strip out the acquisition and this is a mid-single-digit grower with flat client count, a dividend that consumes 97% of GAAP earnings, $4.6B of net debt where there used to be almost none, and a float-income line that is now shrinking. It trades at a premium forward multiple to faster-growing Paycom and Paylocity, five of eighteen analysts rate it Sell, the average target is 10% below the price, and short interest is 6% of float. The June rally priced in a guide that merely wasn't worse; the September 29 print has to deliver on it.

Top 3 Risks

01
Organic deceleration below the 5–6% guide. With client count flat, growth rests on price and module attach against AI-native competitors whose pitch is cheaper and simpler. A 3–4% organic print at a 20× forward multiple with a Hold consensus and a $111 average target would remove the June rally in a quarter.
02
Rates and small-business employment turning together. The float ($210.9M, near-100% margin) is already guided down; a faster easing cycle deepens that. A small-business hiring slowdown hits checks per client, PEO worksite employees and float balances simultaneously — three revenue lines with one macro driver.
03
Dividend growth versus GAAP payout. $4.76 annualised against $4.89 GAAP EPS is a 97% payout. The dividend is safe on cash flow, but a company that has raised it ~10% a year now has 7–9% EPS growth to fund it, and every dollar going to a 1.5×-levered balance sheet's interest bill is a dollar not going to the payout or the buyback.
  Industry Commentary
Payroll Float Is a Rate Trade Wearing a Software Multiple
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every payroll processor earns interest on client money in transit — ADP, Paychex, Paycom, Paylocity all do it — and from 2022 to 2025 that line was the fastest-growing, highest-margin revenue in the sector. Paychex's rose 30% in FY2026 to $210.9M, partly on Paycor's added balances; its FY2027 guide of $195–205M is the first decline of the cycle. The industry-wide implication is that reported margin expansion in 2023–2025 was partly a rates windfall, and that the reversal will show up as "margin pressure" in FY2027 press releases across the group. Investors who paid a software multiple for a rate-driven earnings bump own the unwind. Sensitivity for PAYX: ~$12–13M of revenue and ~$0.03 of EPS per 25bp on a ~$5B balance (est.).
Incumbents Are Buying the Mid-Market They Failed to Build
ONGOING HEADWIND
Paychex–Paycor ($4.1B, April 2025) is the sector's template: a small-business incumbent with a flattening client count buys a mid-market software vendor for growth and channel, funds it with debt, and reports adjusted EPS that excludes the amortisation of what it bought. Meanwhile Paycom (+9.2%) and Paylocity (+11.0%) keep growing organically in the same segment at lower forward multiples (17.6× and 17.5×), and Gusto and Rippling attack the bottom of the market with self-serve pricing that a territory sales force cannot match. The question for the whole group is whether "HCM platform" is a moat or a commodity — and whether the ~44% margins the incumbents earn are the reason new entrants keep showing up.
Small-Business Hiring Is the Real Denominator
EMERGING TAILWIND — IF IT HOLDS
Checks per client is the payroll industry's version of same-store sales: it captures whether existing small businesses are adding heads. Paychex publishes its own monthly Small Business Employment Watch precisely because its revenue, its PEO worksite-employee count and its float balance all move with it. Bull framing: with client count flat by management's own admission, any pickup in small-business hiring flows almost entirely to revenue at a 44% incremental margin, and the FY2027 guide of 5–6% assumes little of it. Bear framing: the same lever cuts in reverse — a small-business hiring slowdown lowers checks, worksite employees and float in the same quarter, and the 0.81 beta gives no warning because the stock's history was written in an era when Paychex carried no debt and rates were a tailwind.
