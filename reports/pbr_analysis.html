01 Company Overview

Petrobras is Brazil's national oil company: a listed, state-controlled producer that pumps oil from deep water beneath a thick layer of salt off Brazil's coast (the pre-salt), refines crude into diesel and gasoline for the Brazilian market, and sells natural gas. In the third quarter of 2026 it produced a record 3.51 million barrels of oil equivalent a day of its own, 11.7% more than a year earlier and 5.3% more than in 2Q26 (3Q26 operating report, October 28), ran its refineries at 99% of rated capacity and set a quarterly record for diesel output, 769 thousand barrels a day. Sales revenue was $33.0B, up 40.4% on 3Q25, and net income attributable to shareholders was $7.74B, up 28.4% on a year earlier but 25.8% below 2Q26's $10.43B.

Two forces explain the quarter. The first is price: Brent crude averaged $97.00 a barrel in 3Q26, against $104.52 in 2Q26 and $69.07 a year earlier, after what Petrobras's 1Q26 release calls "the start of the conflict in the Middle East". The second is volume: new floating production ships in the Búzios field, including P-79, which started on May 1; the next two, P-80 and P-82, are expected to start production in 2027. The stock has moved with politics as much as with oil. PBR closed 2025 at $11.85, closed at $21.65 on October 2 (the previous edition's date) and reached a daily close of $26.16 on October 23. After President Lula won Brazil's October 25 runoff, a result Oil & Gas 360 reports surprised markets, PBR fell 14.0% on October 26 (close to close, our calculation from the daily rows) and closed at $18.34 on November 13, up 40.8% in a year (from $13.03 on November 13, 2025). At that price the market values the whole company at $112.0B, about 4.3 times trailing earnings.

That low multiple is the price of the shareholder that owns the majority of the votes. The Brazilian Federal Government holds 50.26% of the voting shares and appoints the chair of the board. The same government has spent 2026 paying Petrobras subsidies to hold down diesel, gasoline and cooking-gas prices (about R$16B received by October 13), charging it an export tax on crude oil and diesel ($965M in 2Q26 and $867M in 3Q26), and collecting R$251.3B from it in taxes and government take in the first nine months, a record according to the 3Q26 results presentation. Lula, whose government is that controlling shareholder, won re-election on October 25. Chief Executive Magda Chambriard told Reuters the share-price fall was speculative and that the company's business and investment prospects remained strong, as reported by Oil & Gas 360. For a company whose pricing, dividends and leadership all run through Brasília, politics matters as much as Brent.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Pre-salt deepwater reserves Pre-salt 2.96M boed FPSO delivery pipeline Refining at 99% utilisation Integrated crude-to-pump chain Sovereign backing Net debt/EBITDA 1.00x

The moat is geology plus execution. Pre-salt reservoirs are large and productive, and Petrobras develops them by adding FPSOs one after another, with eight new production systems in the 2026–30 plan. P-79 started three months early, and own output rose 11.7% in a year to a record 3.51M boed. The refining system protects the other end: Petrobras's plants ran at 99% of capacity in 3Q26 and set a quarterly diesel record. The weakness is that the moat belongs to the company, while the profits are shared with the state. Brazil collects through taxes, royalties, the export tax and, through its majority vote, dividend and pricing policy.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The controlling shareholder sets the terms. The Federal Government holds 50.26% of the votes and appoints the chair of the board that approves dividends and joins programmes such as the diesel subsidy. President Lula's re-election on October 25, 2026 keeps the same government as that shareholder, and the ADS fell 14.0% the next session.
02
Windfall-style taxes are already here. The export tax on crude oil and diesel cost $122M in 1Q26, $965M in 2Q26 and $867M in 3Q26. A levy that grows with exports and prices transfers part of any oil-price windfall from shareholders to the Treasury.
03
A Brent-driven quarter is not a run-rate. Brent averaged $97.00 in 3Q26 and $104.52 in 2Q26, against $63.69 in 4Q25. Net income of $7.74B in 3Q26 and $10.4B in 2Q26 compares with $2.9B in 4Q25. If the conflict premium fades, earnings fall back toward 2025 levels: $19.6B of net income for the whole of 2025, against $27.3B over the trailing four quarters.
04
Two currencies. PBR is priced in dollars, but Petrobras's domestic sales, taxes and dividends are set in reais. Dividends declared in reais shrink in dollars if the real weakens: the 3Q26 average rate was R$5.14 per dollar, and the PTAX rate moved from R$5.2238 per dollar on October 2 to R$5.6518 on November 13, so a dollar cost 8.2% more in reais.

Notable One-Off Items

Petrobras reports net income both with and without what it calls one-off events. In 3Q26, net income was $7.74B as reported and $9.32B excluding one-offs, down 16% from 2Q26's $11.07B on that basis; the release points to lower gross profit, higher expenses for asset disposals and write-offs, legal contingencies and smaller foreign-exchange gains as the real strengthened. Government measures run through the release line by line: the export tax on crude oil and diesel cost $867M and the road-diesel subsidy programme brought in $2,699M. In 2Q26 the figures were $10.43B reported against $11.07B excluding one-offs, and in 1Q26 $6.20B against $4.54B. For FY2025, reported net income of $19.63B compares with $18.12B excluding one-offs. On the cash side, Petrobras redeemed its 5.999% notes due 2028 on September 25, 2026, paying $1.09B including a make-whole premium of $11.5M (an early-repayment penalty), funded from cash on hand.

It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 30, 2021 close of $10.34 to the November 13, 2026 close of $18.34, PBR's price rose 77.4%. That figure leaves out the dividends, which for Petrobras are most of the story. Yahoo's dividend records list more than $15 per ADS paid over the window ($15.19), nearly twice the $8.00 price gain, and on its dividend-adjusted close, which reinvests the payouts, the five-year gain is more than 450%, driven by the very large distributions of 2022–23. For four years the price went sideways: every month-end between January 2022 and December 2025 closed between $10.43 and $17.07, while the company paid large dividends. Then 2026 broke the range, with a 75% rise from December to March, month-end closes of $22.03 in April and $22.05 in October, and a daily close of $26.16 on October 23, before the 14.0% fall of October 26.

The 6-period monthly RSI reached 86.2 in April 2026, the second-highest reading in the series after 90.5 in March 2022, the run-up to Petrobras's $36.6B profit year of 2022. A 14.8% fall in May and a 13.9% fall in June cooled it to 48.7; it rebuilt to 66.3 in September and 70.2 in October, just over the overbought line. With the November fall to $18.34 it now reads 48.9, mid-range. The price sits below its 10-month moving average, at about $19.4. The lowest reading in the window, 32.2, came in April 2025, when the stock fell 21.3% in a single month.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 4.3 times trailing earnings and 2.9 times EBITDA, you buy record production (3.51M boed, +11.7%), refineries running at 99%, a 19% free-cash-flow yield and leverage down to 1.00x net debt/EBITDA, with 2026 guidance for output and operating cash flow just raised. Even at 2025's $69 average Brent the company earned $19.6B. Dividends of 7.0% gross over the past year come on top, and R$15.3B more was declared on November 10. The Equatorial Margin discoveries, the Côte d'Ivoire blocks and 21 new ANP blocks give a credible answer to "what comes after Búzios". If the re-elected government leaves the governance and dividend framework intact, a price of 4–5 times earnings for a producer that is growing and paying down debt is low.

Bear Case

The trailing numbers rest on two exceptional quarters at $97–105 Brent. The controlling shareholder has already shown, in 2026 alone, that it will tax exports, subsidise pump prices through the company and take R$251B in nine months. Lula's re-election, which Oil & Gas 360 reports surprised markets, took 14.0% off the ADS in one session, and it has since fallen to 30% below its October 23 close. Raising 2026 capex guidance from US$19.4B to US$24–25.5B means more cash going out. If Brent normalises and Brasília leans on pricing or dividends, the low price-to-earnings ratio will turn out to have been fair.

Top 3 Risks

01
Political control of pricing, dividends and leadership. The Federal Government holds 50.26% of the votes. Lula's October 25, 2026 re-election, the subsidy programmes (PMs 1,340, 1,358, 1,359, 1,363, 1,391) and the export tax are all reminders that minority holders own the cash flows only after the state has set its terms.
02
Oil-price normalisation. 3Q26 Brent of $97.00 and 2Q26's $104.52 compare with $63.69 in 4Q25. Quarterly net income moved from $2.9B to $10.4B and $7.7B across those quarters. A return to 2025 prices takes most of that away, and the TTM P/E of 4.3 would rise accordingly.
03
Execution and frontier risk. Growth depends on new FPSOs arriving on time and on exploration replacing Búzios over the next decade. Frontier exploration in deep water off Amapá and West Africa is expensive and uncertain, and 2026 production guidance of 3.3–3.4M boed is already at the 3.4M peak the plan sets for 2028–29. 2026 capex guidance was raised to US$24–25.5B.
Industry Commentary
The 2026 oil shock — windfall for producers, problem for governments
CRITICAL VARIABLE

Brent averaged $63.69 in 4Q25, $80.61 in 1Q26, $104.52 in 2Q26 and $97.00 in 3Q26, according to Petrobras's releases; the 1Q26 release ties the rise to "the start of the conflict in the Middle East". For an exporter with low-cost barrels, that is a straight transfer to the bottom line: Petrobras's exports were $12.67B of 2Q26 revenue against $5.68B a year earlier, and $11.0B in 3Q26. For a government that owns the producer and also faces voters at the pump, it is a dilemma. Brazil's answer, visible line by line in the filings, has been to tax the export side and subsidise the domestic side, with Petrobras as the channel for both.

State-controlled oil companies and the minority shareholder
STRUCTURAL DISCOUNT

Petrobras's owner has goals beyond profit, and 2026's filings show them: pump prices held down through subsidy programmes, an export tax, and R$251.3B of taxes and government take in nine months. The company's history also includes the "Car Wash" (Lava Jato) corruption investigation into bribes allegedly paid to executives over inflated construction contracts, which ended in settlements worth billions. The current framework is a written Shareholder Remuneration Policy (45% of free cash flow when gross debt is at or below the plan's ceiling), a Business Plan with a US$75B gross-debt limit and a US$6B minimum cash balance, and subsidy receipts reported filing by filing. The question each election poses is whether that framework holds; the October 25 result returned the same president to office.

Deepwater as the low-cost frontier
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Oil production was 2,832 thousand barrels a day in 3Q26, up 12.4% in a year, as new FPSOs came on in the pre-salt. The 2026–30 plan names its exploration highlights: the Southern and Southeastern Basins, the Equatorial Margin, and assets in Colombia, São Tomé and Príncipe and South Africa. In 2026 Petrobras has reported three oil discoveries in the Equatorial Margin block FZA-M-59, a gas discovery in the Sandia-1 well off Colombia (announced August 3; Petrobras operates with 44.44%, Ecopetrol holds 55.56%), approval to negotiate for four blocks in Ghana's offshore Keta Basin (August 21), signed for eight blocks in Côte d'Ivoire, and won 21 blocks in the ANP's October 8 rounds. The plan's US$7.1B exploration budget is small next to the US$109B total, but it is the part that decides whether production stays near 3.4M boed after the 2028–29 peak.
