01 Company Overview

PACCAR designs, builds and finances heavy- and medium-duty commercial trucks under three nameplates — Kenworth and Peterbilt in North America and DAF in Europe — and runs a parts distribution business and a captive finance arm alongside them. Kenworth and Peterbilt together held a 30% retail share of the U.S./Canada Class 8 market in 2025 (Class 8 = the heaviest highway trucks, over 33,000 lb gross vehicle weight — the tractors that pull 53-foot trailers). FY2025 revenue was $28.44B, down 15.5% from $33.66B, and net income was $2.38B ($4.51 per share), down 43% — the company's 87th consecutive year of profit, but a trough year by any measure. At the October 28, 2026 close the company was worth $53.9B (526.5M shares × $102.42).

This is a cycle stock, and the whole page turns on one question: where in the cycle is 2026? The third quarter, reported on October 27, kept the recovery going: revenue $7.94B, up 19%, net income $847.5M ($1.61 a share), up 44%, and deliveries of 40,100 trucks, up from 32,900 in Q4 2025, though short of the roughly 42,000 guided because some suppliers missed parts. Truck, Parts & Other gross margin has recovered from 12.0% to 14.5%, and PACCAR's first forecast for 2027 (240,000–280,000 U.S./Canada Class 8 trucks) is at or above 2026's. The worry is the 2027 emissions rule: the EPA has not yet set the penalty that would let fleets keep buying today's engines, and on the call management said some customers are waiting for it before ordering 2027 trucks. The stock has fallen 23% from its July month-end close to $102.42, and at 19.5× trailing and 14.7× next year's consensus earnings it now prices in more doubt than it did in the summer.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

PREMIUM BRANDS & RESALE VALUES DEALER NETWORK DENSITY PARTS ANNUITY ON INSTALLED BASE CAPTIVE FINANCE (A+/A1) LOCAL-FOR-LOCAL FOOTPRINT IN-HOUSE MX ENGINES 87 YEARS OF UNBROKEN PROFIT

The moat is a premium-brand truck wrapped in two service businesses that make money regardless of whether the truck sells. Kenworth and Peterbilt hold a 30% share of a market where the buyer is a professional fleet manager who tracks total cost of ownership to the penny: fuel economy, uptime, driver retention (a comfortable cab is a recruiting tool) and, critically, resale value. High resale values feed PFS's residuals and lower the effective lease cost, which supports pricing on the new truck, which supports the brand. Around that sits the dealer network and Parts — a growing installed base of trucks that will need components for a decade — and the finance arm that keeps the customer in the family. The result is a truck OEM that has stayed profitable through every downturn since 1938, including a 2025 trough in which revenue fell 15.5% and the company still earned a 9.0% net margin and paid $2.72 per share in dividends. What the moat does not do is insulate volume: PACCAR builds to order, and orders are a function of freight rates, interest rates and regulation.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The cycle is the business. FY2023 revenue $35.13B and EPS $8.76; FY2025 revenue $28.44B and EPS $4.51. That is a 49% earnings decline from peak to trough in a business with 30% share and no competitive loss. Truck & Other gross margin was roughly 9% (derived) in Q2 2026 even as the recovery began. Trailing EPS has since climbed to $5.24, still 40% below the 2023 peak; anyone paying 19.5× trailing earnings is underwriting a recovery to somewhere between the trough and that peak.
▸
EPA 2027 NOx rule (NOx = nitrogen oxides; the 2027 standard cuts the limit to 35 milligrams per horsepower-hour). The EPA kept the 35 mg limit but proposed letting makers keep selling current engines by paying a nonconformance penalty (NCP), which management put at $6,000–7,000 per truck on its July call; it said fully compliant engines would likely cost more, and analysts on that call put the extra cost at $8,000–10,000 or more. The proposal was published in July 2026 and its comment period closed on August 29; it was still not final when PACCAR reported on October 27, and management said some customers were waiting for the final penalty levels before ordering 2027 trucks. The bull says the rule smooths the cycle once it lands; the bear points to August orders falling 35% month-over-month (ACT) and September at 18,700, down 9.5% year-over-year (ACT preliminary), as the pre-buy ending in real time.
▸
Section 232 truck tariffs and the Mexico footprint. A 25% Section 232 tariff on imported medium- and heavy-duty trucks and parts took effect November 1, 2025. PACCAR builds Peterbilts in Mexicali, Mexico, as well as in Texas, Ohio and Canada. On the Q2 call (July 2026) management said "local-for-local production provides some tariff benefits to us" and "That gives us a stable tariff operating environment," with tariff surcharges removed for 2026 and a small benefit in Q2 that "will carry forward in Q3." On the Q3 call management described the net tariff effect as not material, with price increases still covering cost increases. The vulnerability is the opposite side of that sentence: the benefit depends on competitors (Daimler and Traton are the largest Mexico-built Class 8 suppliers) being hurt more than PACCAR, and on the USMCA treatment of Mexicali output staying as it is. Neither is in PACCAR's control.
▸
European litigation overhang. PACCAR booked a $264.5M after-tax non-recurring charge in Q1 2025 for civil litigation in Europe, with management saying it was "making good progress on substantially resolving the EU civil litigation." Whether further charges follow is unknown.

Notable One-Off Items

European civil litigation charge, $264.5M after-tax, Q1 2025. This is the single biggest distortion in the trailing-five-quarter numbers. It took Q1 2025 net income to $505.1M ($0.96) from an adjusted $769.6M ($1.46), and FY2025 GAAP net income of $2.38B ($4.51) compares with adjusted $2.64B ($5.01). It is out of the TTM window (which now runs Q4 2025 – Q3 2026), so the $5.24 TTM EPS above is clean. It matters for the year-on-year comparisons, though: nine-month 2026 EPS of $4.18 is up 21% on the reported $3.45 but only 6% on the adjusted $3.95 that the Q3 release gives for the first nine months of 2025.

Tariff surcharge and price-cost. PACCAR applied tariff surcharges in 2025 and removed them for 2026; management described a positive price-cost effect in Q1 and a tariff benefit in Q2 that "will carry forward in Q3." On the Q3 call it called the net tariff effect not material and said there was no one-off benefit in the quarter. The regime itself (the Nov 1, 2025 Section 232 tariff) could change with a signature.

Supplier constraints. Missed parts from a few suppliers held Q3 deliveries to 40,100 against roughly 42,000 guided, and management said building trucks that then wait for a part is one reason the Q4 margin guide is flat. The shortfall is timing, not lost orders, if suppliers catch up as management expects.

Extra dividend. FY2025 dividends declared were $2.72 per share ($1.43B), of which roughly $1.40 (derived) was the December extra dividend. The TTM yield of 2.69% therefore depends on the extra being repeated; the regular quarterly rate alone would yield 1.37% (derived). PACCAR has paid an extra dividend in most years but it is discretionary and sized to the cycle.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From a split-adjusted October 2021 month-end close of $59.75 to $102.42 on October 28, 2026, PCAR is up 71.4% in price — a compound annual rate of roughly 11.4% before dividends. Over the same dates SPY rose 68.2% and QQQ 87.9% (price only, Yahoo closes). PACCAR has paid a regular dividend plus a year-end extra throughout, so its total return is higher than the price figure. The shape is unusual for a cyclical: the stock rose through the 2022 rate shock (it never closed a month below $54.89), then nearly doubled from $68.78 in May 2023 to $123.89 in March 2024 as the 2023 earnings peak was reported. It then spent almost two years — April 2024 to December 2025 — going nowhere, oscillating between $90 and $117 while FY2025 earnings fell 43%. That is the market looking through the trough. The breakout came in January 2026 ($122.91) on the order surge, the stock made its highest monthly close of the window at $132.68 in July 2026 after the Q2 print, and then fell in each of the next three months — $124.13 in August, $109.81 in September (−11.5%) and $102.42 at October 28, 22.8% below the July close — through the August order slump, a soft September order month and an in-line Q3. The monthly RSI (6-period) dropped from 71.7 in July to 36.9. A 71% five-year price gain with a 0.99 beta means PACCAR roughly matched the S&P 500 (SPY) and trailed the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) over the window. Over one year the stock is up only +3.3%, against +12.4% for SPY: the January breakout has been given back.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the units, because everything else follows from them. PACCAR delivered 32,900 trucks in Q4 2025, 33,000 in Q1 2026, 38,700 in Q2 and 40,100 in Q3, and guides 41,000–43,000 for Q4. Company-wide Truck, Parts & Other gross margin moved 12.0% → 13.1% → 14.4% → 14.5% over the same four quarters and is guided to 14–15% in Q4. Net income moved $557M → $605M → $752M → $847.5M. This is what an operating-leverage recovery looks like at a build-to-order manufacturer: every truck over the break-even level earns a gross margin far above the average, so a 17% sequential volume gain (Q1→Q2) produced a 24% profit gain, and a 4% gain (Q2→Q3) a 13% one. The margin line is flattening, though: management guided Q4 roughly level with Q3, partly because trucks built short of a supplier's part cost more to finish.

Then separate what is cyclical from what is not. Parts did $1.67B of pretax income in FY2025 and PFS $485M; together that is roughly $2.16B of the company's ~$3.07B of operating income — about 70% of trough-year profit from the two segments that are not the truck cycle (derived). Parts' gross margin of 29.8% versus a Truck & Other gross margin of about 9% (derived) is a three-to-one spread, and Parts revenue grew 3% in a year when truck revenue fell by mid-teens. This is why PACCAR earned a 9% net margin at the bottom and why the stock never closed a month below $90 during the downturn. Valued on its own, the Parts and PFS stream could plausibly support a substantial share of today's $53.9B market value; after the autumn sell-off the market is paying much less for the truck recovery on top than it was in July.

The EPA 2027 question, stated precisely. Historically a tightening emissions standard produces a pre-buy (fleets rush to buy cheaper current-generation trucks) followed by a post-buy hangover. Analysts on PACCAR's July call put the extra cost of a fully compliant 2027 truck at $8,000–10,000 or more. The EPA's proposal to permit continued sales of current engines under nonconformance penalties of roughly $6,000–7,000 per truck narrows that gap and, in PACCAR's view on its July call, converts a spike-and-crash into "a continued improved cycle through the balance of the year," with "a stronger 2027 and not much drop-off." Three months on, the proposal is still not final, and that delay is now the story: on the Q3 call management said some customers are waiting for the final penalty levels before ordering 2027 trucks. The evidence against: August Class 8 orders fell 35% month-over-month (ACT), FTR called it the end of the pre-buy, and September came in at 18,700 (ACT, −9.5% year-over-year) with ACT expecting the backlog to shrink. The evidence for: the 2026 order season closed at 350,677 units against a retail forecast now narrowed to 240,000–260,000, PACCAR guides Q4 deliveries higher and forecasts 240,000–280,000 for 2027, and management said order intake is rising. The tiebreaker is the EPA's final rule and the order data that follow it; PACCAR's Q4 report (late January 2027, est.) gives the first 2027 delivery read.

Tariffs, honestly. The Section 232 tariff of 25% on imported medium- and heavy-duty trucks has, so far, helped PACCAR more than it has hurt: surcharges came off for 2026, and on the Q3 call management said price was still running ahead of cost, though it called the net tariff effect on results not material. This rests on PACCAR's Texas, Ohio and Canadian plants versus competitors' heavier Mexican footprints — Daimler Truck and Traton's International are the largest Mexico-built Class 8 suppliers. PACCAR's own Mexicali plant means the exposure is not zero. A "benefit" that depends on trade policy staying exactly where it is should be discounted accordingly.

Valuation. At $102.42 the stock trades at 19.5× trailing EPS of $5.24, 14.7× consensus FY2027 EPS of $6.99 (17.4× FY2026's $5.88; both adjusted, stockanalysis.com), 24.9× trailing free cash flow, and yields 2.69% including the extra dividend. The trailing multiple is meaningless on its own for a cyclical — the same price is 11.7× the FY2023 peak EPS of $8.76. The honest framing is mid-cycle: if FY2027 earnings land midway between the trough ($4.51) and the peak ($8.76), roughly $6.60, the stock is at about 15.5×, a modest multiple for a business with a 30% share, a 30%-margin parts annuity and 87 consecutive years of profit; consensus at $6.99 already sits above that midpoint. If 2027 is a post-pre-buy hangover and earnings stall near $6, the multiple is about 17× on flat growth. The Street still rates 10 of 19 Hold but now puts its average target 27.5% above the price: in the summer the stock looked like a good business at a fair price; now it looks like the same business at a lower price, waiting on the EPA.

Bull Case

The trough is behind: quarterly EPS has risen from $1.06 to $1.61 in three quarters, net income is up 44% year-over-year, gross margin is up 250 bps (basis points; 100 bps = 1 percentage point) since Q4 2025, and Q4 deliveries are guided higher again. PACCAR's first 2027 forecast (240,000–280,000 Class 8 trucks) is at or above 2026's, and once the EPA sets its penalty the orders customers are holding back should arrive. Parts (record $1.78B quarter) and PFS ($138.1M Q3 pretax) earn through the cycle. After a 23% fall from the July close, the stock is 14.7× next year's consensus earnings, with a PEG under 1, a 0.99 beta and every analyst target above the price.

Bear Case

2026's order surge was the pre-buy, and it has ended: ACT's August orders fell 35% month-over-month and September was down 9.5% year-over-year, with the backlog expected to shrink. 2027-model trucks carry extra emissions cost (about $6,000–7,000 per truck under the proposed penalty, more for fully compliant engines), and fleets are waiting on a penalty rule with no date. Supplier misses cut Q3 deliveries below guidance, the Q4 margin guide is flat, and Parts pretax income fell year-over-year in Q3 ($404.4M vs $410.0M). TTM EPS is still 40% below the 2023 peak and 19.5× trailing is not cheap for a cyclical; analysts cut targets after the print, and the extra dividend that gets the yield to 2.69% is discretionary.

Top 3 Risks

01
A 2027 post-pre-buy air pocket. If fleets front-loaded 2026 purchases and balk at MY2027 pricing, deliveries could fall from the Q3/Q4 2026 run-rate even as the industry forecast says otherwise. The Q4 guide passed the first test (41,000–43,000, above Q3's 40,100), but the orders that decide 2027 are on hold until the EPA sets its penalty. A long delay, or a penalty high enough to push fleets to the costlier compliant engines, would be the tell.
02
Tariff regime change. PACCAR's current price-cost advantage is a function of a 25% Section 232 tariff that penalises competitors' Mexican output more than its own Mexicali plant. A renegotiation, exemption, or USMCA carve-out would remove the tailwind — and a broadening of tariffs to inputs PACCAR imports would turn it into a headwind.
03
Freight recession relapse hitting PFS and Parts together. The two "non-cyclical" segments are less cyclical, not acyclical. A renewed downturn would lower truck utilisation (fewer Parts sales), push used-truck values down (PFS residuals and credit losses on a $22.5B book), and compress the ~$2.2B of pretax income that currently makes the trough tolerable. Parts pretax income was already slightly down in the first nine months of 2026 ($1.22B vs $1.25B), and management cited some fleets running fewer miles.
  Industry Commentary
EPA 2027: Pre-Buy or Smoothed Cycle?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The 2027 NOx standard (35 mg/hp-hr) is the single largest variable in the North American Class 8 market for the next 18 months. The EPA's proposed nonconformance-penalty path lets current engines keep selling at roughly $6,000–7,000 per truck (PACCAR's estimate); fully compliant engines will likely cost more, $8,000–10,000 or more by the figures analysts gave on PACCAR's July call. The 2026 order season closed up 39% at 350,677 units (FTR), far above the 230,000–270,000 retail forecast PACCAR gave at the time — a backlog oversubscribed by ~35,000 units. August orders then fell 35% month-over-month (ACT) as surcharge-free slots filled, and September came in at 18,700 (ACT) to 21,300 (FTR), with some makers not fully opening their 2027 order books. FTR read August as the pre-buy ending; ACT read it as a capacity constraint. The EPA's penalty rule, proposed in July and not final by late October, is now what fleets are waiting for, and every truck maker's 2027 depends on where it lands.
Section 232 Truck Tariffs Redraw the North American Map
ONGOING HEADWIND
Since November 1, 2025, imported medium- and heavy-duty trucks and parts face a 25% tariff. Mexico is the largest source: Daimler Truck (Saltillo, Santiago Tianguistenco), Traton's International (Escobedo) and PACCAR's Peterbilt (Mexicali) all build there, and Transport Topics reported in February 2026 that OEMs were largely maintaining Mexican output regardless. The net effect so far has been higher industry pricing and a relative advantage for the OEM with the most U.S. capacity — PACCAR — but the tariff is a headwind for the industry's cost base and for the fleets that buy from it, and it is a policy lever that can be pulled in either direction without warning.
Freight Rates Have Turned — and Europe Is Quietly Recovering
EMERGING TAILWIND
On its January 2026 call management tied the order recovery to rising freight rates after a multi-year trucking recession, with fewer drivers letting carriers charge more. It reported used-truck values up 4% year-over-year at that call, and the Q3 2026 release credited "an improving used truck market" for PFS's results; higher used-truck values lower the effective cost of a new truck and protect the resale values PFS lends against. In Europe, PACCAR forecasts >16-tonne registrations of 300,000–320,000 in 2026 and a similar range in 2027 (Oct 27, 2026), and DAF's XD Electric and XF Electric were named International Truck of the Year 2026. Bull framing: a synchronised North American and European upcycle with a parts annuity underneath, at 14.7× next year's consensus earnings. Bear framing: freight rate improvement that is driven by capacity exiting, not demand growing, is fragile — and every recovery in this industry has ended with an emissions rule change or a tariff. This one has both scheduled for 2027.
