01 Company Overview

PG&E Corporation owns Pacific Gas and Electric Company, the regulated utility that delivers electricity and natural gas to roughly 16 million Californians across the north and centre of the state — about 5.5 million electric and 4.5 million gas customer accounts. FY2025 revenue was $24.94B, GAAP EPS $1.18 and non-GAAP core EPS $1.50, up 10% on 2024. On paper this is the most boring business model in the market: spend capital on poles, wires and pipes, have the regulator add it to the rate base, earn an allowed return on it.

The catch is fire. PG&E filed for Chapter 11 in January 2019 after catastrophic wildfires linked to its equipment, was removed from the S&P 500 on January 18, 2019, emerged from bankruptcy in July 2020, and was re-admitted to the index on October 3, 2022. Everything since has been an argument about whether the state's liability rules let a utility here earn its allowed return after fire risk. On August 31, 2026 that argument went against the shareholders: California's wildfire bill, SB 492, was amended without the liability protection Governor Newsom had sought, and PCG fell 20.06% in one session to $13.27. Two days later management deferred $2B of 2027 capital spending, stopped giving growth guidance beyond 2027 and launched a strategic review aimed at getting back to investment grade. At $12.85 the stock trades at 9.5x trailing earnings and 0.86x book. That is a regulated utility priced as if its allowed return is not really its return. The Q3 results on October 22 kept 2026 and 2027 guidance intact, and management said it will announce the strategic review's outcome no later than summer 2027.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territory ~16M Californians served $69B regulated rate base 9.98% CPUC allowed ROE 12.7 GW data-center pipeline 1,278 miles undergrounded

The moat is the license. Nobody builds a second grid in Northern California, so demand for PG&E's wires is as durable as demand for electricity. The weakness is that the same license makes PG&E answerable for what its wires do. Under California's current framework a utility-caused fire can generate claims larger than years of earnings, and insurers can pursue the utility to recover what they paid out (subrogation). The regulator sets the return on capital, but the courts and the legislature decide the liability. On August 31, 2026 the legislature declined to change that.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Liability framework unchanged. Newsom proposed ending subrogation, then phasing it out, then capping it at 50%, and legislators rejected each version. Per 24/7 Wall St., the amended SB 492 also left out a $6 billion per-incident liability cap and a Wildfire Fund replenishment mechanism, and KTVU reports that Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas did not bring the bill to a vote on the session's last night. The company's own verdict (8-K, August 31, 2026): SB 492 "falls short of creating long-term durability needed to attract affordable investment."
02
The shared Wildfire Fund is being drawn down by someone else's fire. California's first wildfire fund has roughly $21–22B of claim-paying capacity (sources differ). A second continuation account of about $18B covers fires after September 2025. The state expects claims from the 2025 Eaton Fire, in Southern California Edison's territory, to exhaust the first fund. PG&E already booked $78M of accelerated Wildfire Fund amortization in Q2 2026 because of other utilities' claims activity.
03
Sub-investment-grade and debt-funded. $65.48B of debt, D/E of 1.88, negative free cash flow and an S&P rating one notch below investment grade. When risks go up, Poppe said, "lenders charge more." That cost reaches customers' bills, which in turn narrows the political room for rate increases.
04
Affordability is the binding constraint. Management's own bridge has capex adding 9–10% a year to rates, offset by O&M cuts, load growth and efficiencies to reach a 0–3% net bill increase. Every assumption in that bridge has to hold. The $2B deferral now delays new-housing hookups, renewable interconnections and large-load expansion beyond 1.6 GW.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $11.60 to the October 26, 2026 settled close of $12.85, PCG gained 10.8% on price, with a dividend that was only reinstated in 2023. The endpoints hide the path. The stock rebuilt steadily from bankruptcy-era lows to a $21.63 peak in November 2024 and has since given back 40.6% of that level (month-end to settled close) in three separate air pockets: January 2025 (−22.4%), June 2025 (−17.4%) and August 2026 (−23.6%). Each drop was a one-month event, and the recoveries in between were slow grinds. That pattern is typical of event risk.

The 6-period monthly RSI is at 31.8, just above the oversold line of 30; it was 25.7 at the September month-end. It was at a similar level after the June 2025 drop (27.5 in June, 28.3 in July), and that episode was followed by a partial recovery. The 10-month moving average of roughly $15.75 sits 22.6% above the current price, so the long-term trend is broken, not bending. Oversold readings on a stock whose problem is a legal framework say more about how fast it fell than about value.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 9.5x trailing GAAP earnings, about 7.8x 2026 core guidance and 0.86x book for a monopoly utility with a $69B rate base, a 9.98% allowed ROE and ~10% core EPS growth guided for both 2026 and 2027. Management reaffirmed 2026 and 2027 guidance on September 2, the day it cut capex, and again with Q3 results on October 22. A 12.7 GW data-center pipeline offers load growth that can lower rates per customer. Ignition rates are down by two-thirds since 2017, with three straight years without a major fire caused by its system. Every analyst target, including the lowest at $14, sits above the price. If the strategic review produces a credible route to investment grade, or the legislature revisits liability, the discount to book should close.

Bear Case

The discount is the point. A regulated return is only worth its face value if the owner gets to keep it, and California has just declined, again, to limit what one fire can cost. BMO now puts the liability drag at $10 a share, most of today's price. The shared Wildfire Fund is expected to be exhausted by another utility's fire. The balance sheet carries $65.5B of debt, rated below investment grade, with negative free cash flow. Growth guidance beyond 2027 has been withdrawn, and cutting capex to protect credit also slows the rate-base growth the bull case depends on. Analyst targets set before August 31 are stale, and three downgrades in one day suggest where they are heading.

Top 3 Risks

01
A major utility-caused wildfire without a liability backstop. SB 492 did not deliver the subrogation limits or per-incident cap the company sought. One large ignition is a balance-sheet event, not an earnings event.
02
Credit and growth trade off against each other. Reaching investment grade argues for less debt-funded capex. Earnings growth argues for more. The review has to choose, and the rate-base path to $106B by 2030 is already withdrawn pending that choice.
03
Wildfire Fund depletion and allowed-return pressure. Eaton Fire claims are expected to drain the first fund, PG&E is already accelerating its fund amortization, and Q2 2026 earnings cited a lower CPUC return. The fund is shrinking at the same time as the allowed return.

CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The 2026 fight was over who pays when utility equipment starts a fire. Governor Newsom proposed ending subrogation, which lets insurers recoup wildfire payouts from utilities, and then offered a phase-out and a 50% cap. Legislators rejected every version after insurance executives warned it would push up premiums and destabilise the state's insurance market. The amended SB 492 kept a victim fast-pay program, curbs on hedge-fund purchases of wildfire claims, capped attorney fees and a ban on executive bonuses after fires that destroy 500 or more structures. Consumer advocates were not satisfied either. TURN's Mark Toney said "the legislature adopted nothing" and called for a plan "that makes the $40 billion last". For investors the result is the status quo: a regulator sets a ~10% return, and liability still has no per-incident cap and no subrogation limit.

Affordability and the capex squeeze
ONGOING HEADWIND

PG&E has lowered electric prices four times in two years, according to its FY2025 release, and targets 0–3% annual bill growth even though capex alone pushes rates up 9–10% a year. That gap is closed with O&M cuts (2.5% in 2025, 2–4% targeted) and load growth. The September 2 deferral removes about $2B of 2027 debt financing, but it also delays connections for new housing, renewables and large loads. That is the kind of growth that spreads fixed costs across more customers and helps affordability.

Load growth — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: a 12.7 GW data-center pipeline, with about 1.8 GW expected in service by 2030, is exactly what a high-fixed-cost grid needs. Every gigawatt spreads the network's cost over more kilowatt-hours, and management says each one can cut rates about 1% for everyone. The bear framing: most of that pipeline (8.2 GW) is still at the application stage, and serving it requires the capital that is now being rationed. Large-load expansion beyond 1.6 GW is among the deferred projects. Load growth only helps if the utility can finance the wires to reach it, and financing is what the liability framework is making more expensive.
