01 Company Overview

Public Service Enterprise Group is a two-piece company that most investors treat as one. The larger piece, PSE&G, is New Jersey's dominant regulated electric and gas utility — poles, wires, pipes, meters, and a state-approved rate of return on the capital sunk into them. The smaller piece, PSEG Power & Other, owns a 3,758 MW carbon-free nuclear fleet that sells into the PJM wholesale market (the regional grid operator's market for electricity). FY2025 revenue was $12.17B, up 18.3% YoY, with GAAP net income of $2.11B ($4.22 per share) and non-GAAP operating earnings of $2.03B ($4.05 per share). PSE&G contributed $1,745M of the $2,029M in operating earnings — 86%. At a $37.4B market capitalisation PEG is a mid-sized member of the S&P 500's utility group, not a mega-cap.

The central debate of this report is whether PEG is a regulated utility that happens to own reactors, or a nuclear merchant story wearing a utility's clothes. The market spent 2024 pricing it as the latter — the stock ran from $57.99 in January 2024 to $94.30 in November 2024 on the thesis that data-centre demand would let PSEG contract its nuclear output at premium prices. Two years later the stock closed at $75.04, 20.4% below that high, and PSEG's Q3 2026 release and presentation announced no contracted nuclear offtake (a long-term deal to sell the plants' output to a buyer). The regulated business kept growing the whole time. What has shrunk is the hope of a nuclear deal, not the utility. Everything below is an attempt to size each half honestly.

This is the steady-growth engine, and it works the way every regulated utility works: PSE&G spends capital on transmission, distribution, gas main replacement and clean-energy programmes; the New Jersey Board of Public Utilities (BPU) approves the spend into rate base — the pool of invested capital on which the utility is permitted to earn a return — and customers pay that return through rates. Year-end 2025 rate base was approximately $36 billion, up 7% from 2024, and management guides to a 6–7.5% rate-base CAGR through 2030. The capital plan behind it is $24–28B in total for 2026–2030, of which $22.5–25.5B is regulated; 2026 regulated investment alone is roughly $4.2B, a 13.5% step-up on 2025. Q3 2026 segment operating earnings were $506M against $515M a year earlier (nine months: $1,425M against $1,393M). The October 2024 New Jersey distribution base-rate case settlement is the current revenue foundation. The affordability counterweight is real: New Jersey's politics have turned on the cost of bills, and PSE&G cut residential gas heating bills by 5% effective October 1, 2026 (Q3 2026 presentation).

Fourteen percent of earnings, and close to one hundred percent of the argument. The fleet produced 30.9 TWh in FY2025 — carbon-free, always-on, sitting inside the PJM market at the eastern end of the biggest data-centre build-out in the country. Two things drive the segment. First, the nuclear production tax credit (PTC) created by the Inflation Reduction Act, which works as a floor rather than a subsidy: if realised power prices fall below a threshold, the credit tops the revenue back up; if prices exceed it, the credit pays nothing. PSEG's FY2025 release says that output "realized prices in excess of the nuclear production tax credit" — meaning the floor was not needed, the fleet was earning market prices, and the downside protection sits unused underneath. That is the best possible configuration: participate on the way up, protected on the way down. Second, the contracting option. Q3 2026 operating earnings of $68M versus $50M (nine months: $352M versus $274M) show the segment improving, but the stock was priced for something much larger: multi-year deals selling nuclear output directly to hyperscalers (the giant cloud-computing companies) at a premium to market. Management's language remains conditional — the Q2 2026 release said PSEG would "continue to pursue opportunities incremental to our long-term forecast, including the potential to contract our nuclear output under multi-year agreements," at the Q2 2026 call CEO LaRossa said "there's more opportunities for long-term [power purchase agreement] type or utility-like agreements" and "we see an opportunity that might present itself," and after the Q3 results he said "PSEG Power is continuing to develop terms with large load entities for contracting arrangements" (Utility Dive, Aug 5 and Nov 3, 2026). Note also that the segment's GAAP result swings violently against the operating figure — Q3 2026 GAAP was a $(130)M loss against $68M of operating earnings, the hedge-accounting and decommissioning-fund swings that operating earnings exclude (see Section 04). Operationally, the Q3 nuclear capacity factor (the share of possible output the plants actually produced) was 95.5% against 92.4% a year earlier; PSEG forecasts 30–32 TWh of 2026 nuclear output, over 95% hedged; a Salem uprate (more output from the existing units) of about 200 MW in total, about 112 MW of it PSEG's share, is in development, with co-owner Constellation's share supporting a power deal with Google (Utility Dive, Nov 3, 2026); and PSEG has notified the Nuclear Regulatory Commission that it intends to seek 20-year licence extensions. PSEG's quarterly filing adds that an inspection during the fall 2026 Salem 1 refuelling outage found the thermal shield inside the reactor vessel is degraded, and testing is expected to extend the outage (Utility Dive, Nov 3, 2026).

Industry size: PJM is the largest U.S. wholesale power market by load; PSEG's 3,758 MW is a low-single-digit share of PJM capacity (est.)

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Exclusive NJ franchise $36B regulated rate base 3,758 MW carbon-free nuclear IRA PTC price floor PJM location premium 15 yrs dividend growth Self-funded capital plan

The core advantage is structural and boring: PSE&G holds a legally exclusive franchise to deliver electricity and gas across the densest, wealthiest part of New Jersey. Nobody can build a competing distribution network, and the return on invested capital is set by regulators rather than by competition. That converts $4.2B a year of 2026 capital spending into a contractually visible earnings stream and underwrites the 6–8% operating-earnings growth target through 2030. Layered on top is something most regulated peers do not have — a carbon-free nuclear fleet with a federal price floor beneath it and a scarcity premium above it. The moat is not that PSEG can out-compete anyone. It is that the regulated half cannot be competed with at all, and the unregulated half owns an asset class nobody is building more of quickly.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Affordability politics in New Jersey. Rising bills driven partly by data-centre load growth are a live state-level issue: PSE&G cut residential gas heating bills 5% from October 1, 2026, and LaRossa says its planned BPU filing "is all about staying aligned with the governor's goals to keep rates flat for as long as possible" (Utility Dive, Nov 3, 2026). Regulated ROEs and rate-case outcomes are political as much as financial, and New Jersey is not a forgiving jurisdiction when customers are angry.
02
The nuclear contract that keeps not arriving. The large-load pipeline is enormous on paper — about 11 GW of data-centre inquiries in May 2026, up from 9.4 GW at the end of June 2025 — but management itself expects only 10–20% of it to materialise, and Utility Dive, citing CFO Daniel Cregg, reported in May 2026 that data-centre developers appear to be cooling on New Jersey, partly because the state offers fewer financial incentives than others. Talks with customers have run for well over a year with no announced megawatts, counterparty or price. Every quarter without a deal takes away part of what the 2024 share price was paying for.
03
Rate-sensitivity and leverage. A beta of 0.49 makes PEG a bond proxy (a stock bought for steady income, so it trades like a bond), and bond proxies fall when long yields rise. Debt-to-equity is 1.48 with $25.63B of total debt against $837M of cash, and the funding plan for $24–28B of capital spending assumes no new equity. If rates or construction costs move against that plan, the no-dilution promise is the first casualty.
04
PJM market design risk. LaRossa has publicly criticised PJM's capacity-auction governance and said that absent change "we will be living with the outcome of that [capacity auction] process." Capacity prices, load-forecast methodology and the reliability backstop auction are all in flux — and the nuclear segment's economics sit directly downstream of decisions PSEG does not control.

Notable one-off items — read operating earnings, not GAAP. PSEG's GAAP net income is structurally noisy because the company marks its energy hedges to market each quarter. A hedge that locks in a future power price is an economic stabiliser, but accounting forces its unrealised value through the income statement, so GAAP earnings move inversely to the very price swings the hedges are meant to neutralise. Non-GAAP operating earnings strip out those mark-to-market (MTM) gains and losses, nuclear decommissioning trust (NDT) fund movements, and other material infrequent items. The gap is not cosmetic: in the third quarter of 2026, GAAP net income fell to $376M ($0.76) from $622M ($1.24), a 40% decline — while operating earnings rose to $574M ($1.15) from $565M ($1.13). At the segment level the divergence is starker still: PSEG Power & Other posted a GAAP loss of $(130)M against $68M of operating earnings. For FY2025 the relationship reversed — GAAP $4.22 above operating $4.05 — which is exactly the point: the difference is direction-less noise. Guidance, the dividend and the 6–8% growth target are all set on the operating measure, so that is the measure this report leads with. It is also, unavoidably, a company-defined number: taking it at face value means accepting management's definition of what is infrequent.

RSI compares the size of recent gains with the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 a stock has risen hard and fast and is called "overbought"; below 30 it has fallen hard and fast and is "oversold". It measures momentum, not value — an oversold reading says the selling has been persistent, not that the shares are cheap.

Nearly five years, and little to show for it in price. PEG closed December 2021 at $66.73 and November 9, 2026 at $75.04 — a price-only gain of 12.5% over 4.9 years, roughly 2.4% a year, before dividends. Add a dividend yield of 3.57% on today's price and the capital appreciation looks modest: the stock has largely made a round trip. The path is far more interesting than the endpoints.

Three distinct regimes. From the start of this window through the end of 2023 the stock traded between roughly $56 and $70 while the Fed raised interest rates and bond-like stocks fell; the October 2022 close of $56.07 was the low of that stretch. Then came the AI-utility trade. From $57.99 in January 2024 the stock ran 63% in ten months to $94.30 in November 2024, as the market decided that any owner of always-on carbon-free generation inside PJM was about to be paid a scarcity premium by hyperscalers. Since that peak it has been a slow deflation: $84.49 by December 2024, a failed rally to $89.79 in July 2025, a second failed rally to $86.07 in February 2026, and then a slide to a $67.34 close on September 30, 2026 — 28.6% below the November 2024 high and the lowest month-end close since March 2024. The stock has since rebounded to $74.29 at the October month-end and $75.04 on November 9, 20.4% below the November 2024 high and 13.4% above the 52-week low of $66.15 (the intraday low, reached on September 25 and again on September 29).

The 6-period monthly RSI tells the same story without the narrative. It peaked near 90 (89.9) in November 2024 — the highest reading in this window — fell to 22.4 at the September 2026 month-end, below the 30 oversold line and the weakest reading of the window (the previous low was 24.4 in October 2022), and has recovered to 45.5 on the November 9 close, a partial month. The 3-month average ($72.22) still sits below the 10-month average ($77.52). That is a two-month rebound inside a longer downtrend, not yet a new uptrend.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 16.6× forward earnings and collecting 3.6% for a business whose regulated half — 86% of operating earnings — grows rate base 6–7.5% annually on a $24–28B capital plan that management says needs no new equity and no asset sales. That alone supports the 6–8% operating-earnings growth target through 2030 and a sixteenth consecutive dividend increase. The 3,758 MW nuclear fleet is thrown in nearly free at these levels, with an IRA production tax credit underneath it as a price floor and a roughly 11 GW large-load interconnection pipeline above it. One signed multi-year offtake contract lifts the value of the Power segment and re-opens the 2024 narrative.

Bear Case

The nuclear contract has been under discussion for over a year with no megawatts, counterparty or price disclosed, and management's own conversion assumption for interconnection inquiries is 10–20%. Strip the option out and this is a 0.49-beta bond proxy at 21× trailing earnings with a 1.48 debt-to-equity ratio, negative free cash flow (−$173M over twelve months) against a $1.3B annual dividend bill, and 4.10% ROIC — a rate-sensitive utility funding a large capital programme with debt. New Jersey affordability politics are tightening at exactly the moment data-centre load is pushing bills higher, and the target changes since the Q3 print have mostly been cuts.

Top 3 Risks

01
The nuclear contract never lands on commercial terms worth having. PSEG is negotiating with hyperscalers who have other options, and PJM's rules — capacity auctions, load forecasts, the reliability backstop — keep shifting underneath the deal. A contract signed at market rates rather than a premium would confirm the bear case as surely as no contract at all.
02
Regulatory and affordability pressure in New Jersey. Rate base only earns what the BPU allows it to earn. With residential bills rising and data centres widely blamed, the political path of least resistance is a tougher next rate case, a lower authorised return, or cost allocations that shift large-load expense onto the utility.
03
Rates, leverage and the no-equity promise. $25.63B of debt, $837M of cash, negative free cash flow and a $24–28B capital programme to fund. The plan assumes no new equity issuance; if long rates rise or construction costs run, that assumption breaks and the fix is dilution, a slower capital plan, or a strained dividend — and a 0.49-beta stock tends to fall on higher long yields regardless.

Industry Commentary

CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
New Jersey has a generation problem it cannot build its way out of quickly
LaRossa's framing is blunt: "The facts are that there has not been any new baseload generation built in New Jersey for quite some time." Meanwhile PSE&G's large-load queue stood at about 11 GW in May 2026, against 9.4 GW at the end of June 2025. PSEG's entire nuclear fleet is 3,758 MW. At management's own 10–20% conversion assumption, that queue means 1.1–2.2 GW of new demand, equal to roughly 30–60% of the fleet's capacity, in a state that has built little new supply. That is why higher prices for scarce power are plausible — and why bills, and therefore politics, are the real limit rather than physics.
ONGOING HEADWIND
PJM market design is unsettled, and PSEG does not control it
The nuclear segment's economics run through PJM's capacity auction, its load forecast and its new bilateral contracting and reliability backstop processes — all of which are being actively rewritten. LaRossa has publicly questioned the governance ("How crazy does it sound that the governors have to get a Members Committee vote to allow a vote to take place to have a seat at the table?") and warned that absent reform "we will be living with the outcome of that [capacity auction] process." He declined to give specifics on PSEG's own supply proposals in August 2026, saying PJM's plans were in flux, including its load forecast (Utility Dive, Aug 5, 2026). FERC suspended PJM's reliability backstop auction on Sep 29, 2026, and PJM filed a revised plan on Oct 29. Unquantifiable timing risk sits on top of a quantifiable asset.
EMERGING TAILWIND
The PTC floor makes the nuclear fleet a one-way bet — and frames the whole debate
The IRA nuclear production tax credit tops up revenue when realised power prices fall below a threshold and pays nothing when they exceed it. PSEG's FY2025 release says that output — 30.9 TWh — "realized prices in excess of the nuclear production tax credit," so the fleet captured market prices with the floor sitting unused beneath it. Bull read: you own a call option on data-centre power pricing with the downside insured by federal statute, and the market is currently paying you little for it — the stock has given back more than half of its 2024 AI gain. Bear read: an option with no expiry date and no announced counterparty is just a story, and 14% of operating earnings cannot carry a valuation on its own. The regulated 86% has to do the work, and at 16.6× forward earnings with a 3.6% yield it is priced to do exactly that — no more.
