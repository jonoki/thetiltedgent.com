01 Company Overview

PulteGroup is the third-largest homebuilder in the United States by closings, operating a deliberately un-focused brand portfolio — Centex for entry-level, Pulte Homes for move-up, Del Webb for active adults, plus DiVosta and John Wieland regionally — alongside a captive mortgage, title and insurance arm. FY2025 revenue was $17.31B, down 3.5% year over year, and the trailing twelve months through June 30, 2026 have deteriorated further to $16.41B, down 7.3%. The company is a component of the S&P 500 and ranks roughly #1,080 globally by market capitalisation.

The central debate is simple and it is not about PulteGroup's execution. The company is still earning a ROE near 15% and a 25% home sale gross margin in what is unambiguously a bad housing tape. The question is whether that 25% is a durable trough or a way-station on the road down. Home sale gross margin has fallen from 27.0% in Q2 2025 to 25.0% in Q2 2026, and the mechanism is explicit: incentives ran 10.4% of sales price in Q2 2026 versus 8.7% a year earlier. That 170-basis-point incentive step-up is roughly the entire margin decline. If the 30-year mortgage rate falls, that spread is handed straight back to the income statement. If it does not, incentives are simply the new cost of selling a house and the stock is a value trap wearing an 11.9× multiple.

Del Webb is the single most defensible asset PulteGroup owns and the reason its cycle beta is lower than a pure entry-level builder's. Active-adult buyers are disproportionately cash or high-down-payment purchasers selling a paid-off or low-balance home in a high-cost market and relocating to a lower-cost one. They are the cohort least dependent on the 30-year mortgage rate, which is exactly what you want owning 22% of your closings when rates are the binding constraint on everyone else. The brand also carries amenity-driven pricing power — golf, clubhouses, pickleball, programming — that competitors cannot replicate without decades of community-scale land assembly. Demographically the 65+ cohort is the fastest-growing age band in the US through the 2030s, so this is a secular tailwind sitting inside a cyclical business. The catch: these are very large, very long-duration master-planned communities, which is precisely why Pulte owns more of its land than peers (see Section 03).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
DEL WEBB BRAND 3-LEG BUYER MIX 25.0% HOME SALE GM 12.3% DEBT/CAPITAL 228K LOTS CONTROLLED CAPTIVE MORTGAGE RETURN-FOCUSED MODEL

The core advantage is margin, not scale. PulteGroup closes roughly 29,000 homes a year to D.R. Horton's ~90,000, yet earns a home sale gross margin of 25.0% against Horton's 20.7% — a 430-basis-point spread that is worth roughly $1.2B of annual gross profit on Pulte's revenue base. That gap is bought with the buyer mix (an active-adult cash buyer does not need a rate buydown), the brand architecture (three distinct nameplates let Pulte discount Centex without cheapening Del Webb), and a refusal to chase volume. Marshall's "return-focused operating model" is the label; the observable evidence is a ROIC of 14.5% and a net debt-to-capital ratio of 3.3% at a point in the cycle when the industry is discounting hard.

A correction to a common framing: Pulte's optioned land share is frequently cited as a differentiator. The Q2 2026 investor materials put total lots controlled at 228,000, of which 55% are under option and 45% are owned. That is a lower optioned share than D.R. Horton, which controls 441,900 of 568,500 lots (≈78%) off balance sheet, largely through Forestar. So the honest read is the reverse of the usual story: PulteGroup carries meaningfully more dirt on its own balance sheet than the largest peer does. There is a defensible reason — Del Webb master-planned communities are multi-decade, amenity-heavy land assemblies that simply cannot be optioned in the way a 150-lot entry-level subdivision can — but it is a real difference in downside flexibility, and it is the thing to watch if the cycle gets worse rather than better. At roughly 228,000 lots against ~29,000 annual closings, Pulte controls about 7.9 years of supply, with roughly 3.5 years of it owned outright.

Key Vulnerabilities

→
The incentive load is the whole margin story, and it is not under management's control. Incentives ran 10.4% of sales price in Q2 2026 against 8.7% a year earlier. That 170bp step-up almost exactly accounts for the 200bp decline in home sale gross margin (27.0% → 25.0%). Incentives did tick down sequentially from 10.9% in Q1, which is the bull's evidence that this has peaked — but the level is set by the 30-year mortgage rate and the competitive behaviour of builders with worse land positions, not by anything Pulte decides.
→
Backlog value is shrinking while units grow. Backlog is 10,966 homes (up 2%) worth $6.8B (down 1%). Units up, dollars down means the average backlog home is getting cheaper — mix, price, or incentive, and probably all three. At ~29,000 annual closings, 10,966 units is under five months of production. Thin backlogs give a builder very little visibility and very little pricing leverage into the next two quarters.
→
Land ownership cuts both ways. With 45% of 228,000 lots owned, PulteGroup has roughly 3.5 years of supply capitalised on the balance sheet and is guiding to $5.4B of land spend in 2026. In an improving market that is operating leverage. In 2007–2009, land on the balance sheet is what killed builders. Net debt-to-capital of 3.3% means the company can absorb a great deal before this becomes existential — but it is the structural difference between Pulte and the option-heavy model, and it should be priced as such.
→
SG&A deleverage is compounding the revenue decline. SG&A rose to 10.1% of home sale revenues in Q2 2026 from 9.1% a year earlier. Community count is up 8% year over year to 1,074 while closings fell 8% — the company is carrying the fixed cost of more sales offices across fewer sales. Full-year guidance of 9.5%–9.7% implies management expects this to improve in the back half, but it is a bet on volume, not on cost action.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70, the stock has been bought hard enough that buyers may be exhausted ("overbought"); below 30, sold hard enough that sellers may be ("oversold"). It describes momentum, not value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a year. This is a 6-month RSI on monthly closes, so it reads the medium-term swing rather than day-to-day noise. PHM's current reading of 42.4 is squarely neutral-to-soft: the stock is not washed out, which is itself informative for anyone waiting to buy capitulation.

Over the five years from the October 2021 close of $48.08 to the September 10, 2026 close of $116.41, PHM returned +142.1% on price alone, before the ~1% dividend and before the compounding effect of a buyback that has retired a meaningful share count. That is a very good five years — and it is almost entirely a 2022–2024 story. The stock peaked at a monthly close of $143.53 in September 2024 and has not made a new high in two years; it is 18.9% below that peak today. A 142% five-year return that stopped dead twenty-four months ago is the signature of a cyclical that has already discounted its recovery.

The shape is worth reading carefully. The 2022 collapse to $37.50 in September 2022 — a 34% drawdown from the December 2021 close — was the market pricing a rate shock. The recovery from there to $143.53 was a 283% move in exactly two years, driven by the realisation that builders could buy the rate down and keep selling houses. Since then the stock has traced three lower highs: $143.53 (Sep 2024), $137.20 (Feb 2026) and $137.21 (Jun 2026). Each rally has failed at roughly the same level and each has been sold as incentives climbed again. The 10-month moving average sits near $124 and price is below it, as it has been for most of the last four months.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the best-margin large homebuilder in America at 11.9× trailing and ~10.8× forward earnings, with a net debt-to-capital ratio of 3.3% and $1.8B of buyback authorisation against a $21.8B market cap. The margin decline has a single, identifiable, reversible cause — a 170-basis-point incentive step-up — and incentives already fell sequentially from 10.9% to 10.4% in Q2. Orders are growing 6%, community count 8%, and Del Webb gives 22% of closings to a buyer who does not need a mortgage rate to cooperate. If the 30-year rate falls even 75 basis points, incentives compress, gross margin steps back toward 27%, and the earnings base moves from ~$10 to something with a 13 in front of it. The Street's $141.86 average target implies 21.9% upside and the high is $166.

Bear Case

Revenue is down 7.3% and falling faster each quarter (−12.4% in Q1, −9.6% in Q2). EPS has already fallen 33% from the FY2024 peak of $14.69 and consensus has it down another 10.9% in 2026. The company is responding to a demand problem by building more communities — count up 8% while closings fell 8% — which is why SG&A deleveraged 100 basis points. Backlog dollars are shrinking even as units grow. And 45% of 228,000 lots sit on the balance sheet against a $5.4B land spend guide, meaning the company is capitalising more dirt into a market where it is discounting 10.4% to move product. A low P/E on a cyclical two years past its peak, with earnings still falling, is the textbook value trap — RBC's $116 target on a $116 stock is not a bearish call, it is a "this is already fair" call.

Top 3 Risks

01
The incentive load does not come down. At 10.4% of sales price on a $544,000 average home, PulteGroup is giving back roughly $57,000 per closing. If the 30-year mortgage rate stays where it is or rises, that number goes up, not down, and the 24.5%–25.0% gross margin guide becomes a ceiling rather than a floor.
02
Owned land in a deteriorating market. Roughly 3.5 years of owned lot supply plus $5.4B of 2026 land spend is the bet that this is a trough. If it is a mid-cycle pause rather than a bottom, impairments become a live question — the mechanism that destroyed homebuilder equity in 2007–2009. The 3.3% net debt-to-capital ratio buys a great deal of time, but it does not change the direction of the exposure.
03
Community count growth into falling absorption. Q2 2026 absorption was roughly 2.34 net orders per community per month (7,536 ÷ 1,074 ÷ 3). Guiding community count up another 3%–5% in the back half only works if absorption holds. If it does not, the company carries the fixed cost of more sales offices across the same or fewer sales, and SG&A deleverage compounds the gross margin problem instead of offsetting it.
Industry Commentary
Affordability is the binding constraint, and it is a rate problem
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

PulteGroup's own language in the Q2 release is unusually blunt: "market conditions remain highly competitive as macroeconomic uncertainty, volatile interest rates and strained affordability weigh on housing demand." The arithmetic behind that sentence is the incentive line. Incentives went from 8.7% of sales price in Q2 2025 to 10.4% in Q2 2026 — on a $544,000 house, from roughly $47,000 to roughly $57,000 of builder-funded concession per closing. That is not a marketing choice; it is the price of bridging the gap between what a household can pay monthly and what the house costs at prevailing mortgage rates. Average selling price fell 3% year over year to $544,000 even as the mix shifted toward the higher-priced move-up and active-adult brands, which means realised pricing is weaker than the headline suggests. Nothing about this improves without the 30-year mortgage rate moving.

The industry is competing on land structure, and Pulte has chosen the harder road
ONGOING HEADWIND

The defining structural shift in US homebuilding over the last decade has been the migration of land off the balance sheet. D.R. Horton now controls 78% of its 568,500 lots under option, largely through its Forestar affiliate, which lets it shrink production fast without eating an impairment. PulteGroup controls 228,000 lots with 55% optioned and 45% owned — a materially heavier balance sheet. This is a deliberate consequence of the business mix rather than an oversight: a Del Webb community is a multi-decade, amenity-anchored land assembly that no land banker will option on normal terms. The trade is real margin today (25.0% vs 20.7% home sale gross margin) against real flexibility in a downturn. Investors should understand they are buying that trade, not avoiding it — and should treat the common claim that Pulte's optioned share is a structural advantage over peers as simply incorrect on the current disclosure.

What the bull and the bear are actually arguing about
EMERGING TAILWIND

Strip away the noise and the disagreement reduces to one variable with two readings of the same fact. The fact: incentives fell from 10.9% of sales price in Q1 2026 to 10.4% in Q2, and home sale gross margin rose 60 basis points sequentially to 25.0%. The bull reads that as the inflection — the worst is priced, orders are already growing 6%, community count is growing 8%, and a buyer paying 11.9× trailing earnings for a business still earning 14.5% ROIC at the bottom of its cycle is being paid to wait, with a 5% buyback yield doing the waiting. The bear reads it as one quarter of noise inside a two-year downtrend in which revenue growth has gone −1.4%, −4.3%, −1.6%, −6.3%, −12.4%, −9.6% and gross margin has gone 30.2% → 29.5% → 29.2% → 26.9% → 25.6%. Both are looking at the same income statement. The tiebreaker is the 30-year mortgage rate, and neither analyst controls it — which is the honest reason the Street's targets run from $100 to $166 on a $116 stock.
