01 Company Overview

Every box that arrives on a doorstep started as a roll of containerboard. Packaging Corporation of America makes that board in its own mills and then turns most of it into corrugated boxes, displays and protective packaging in its own plants. The mill's output is largely sold to itself. A much smaller Paper segment makes uncoated freesheet office and printing papers. TTM revenue is $9.82B, with Q3 2026 sales up 12.5% on a year earlier; FY2025 revenue was $8.99B, up 7.23% from $8.38B.

Over the last twelve months PCA got bigger and leaner at once. On September 2, 2025 it closed a $1.8B cash purchase of Greif's containerboard business: two mills (Riverville and Massillon) with about 800,000 tons of annual capacity, plus eight sheet-feeder and corrugated plants. Three months later, on December 3, 2025, it announced it would shut the No. 2 paper machine and kraft pulping at its Wallula, Washington mill, removing 250,000 tons of high-cost capacity. Then, in 2026, the industry started pushing price. After two rounds earlier in the year, PCA announced an unusually large $140-per-ton increase effective September 1; Fastmarkets RISI, the industry's price index, recognised $70 a ton of the round for linerboard in September and nothing more in October. On October 21 PCA reported third-quarter earnings of $3.04 per share excluding special items, ahead of its own $2.91 guide, and guided the fourth quarter to $2.81. The stock trades at $253.74, near the top of its $191.50–$259.98 52-week range. A trailing P/E of 31 looks rich, but restructuring charges have dented the earnings it is measured on; the forward multiple is about 19. The market is pricing one question that is easy to ask and hard to answer: how much of that $140 actually sticks?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Mill-to-box vertical integration Consolidated North American supply Lower-cost mill system after Wallula Local box-plant network Price leadership in 2026

The moat is steel and permits. Boxes are heavy, bulky and cheap per unit, so they have to be made close to the customer. That is why the industry consolidates instead of being disrupted, and why paying $1.8B for Greif's two running mills can beat building one. It is also why PCA could open with a $140 increase: in a market that lost nearly 10% of its capacity in 2025, someone has to go first. The weakness is that the moat protects the industry, not the price. Containerboard is a commodity, and when demand softens, the price takes the hit.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
PCA does not control whether its price sticks. Contract box prices are commonly tied to the prices Fastmarkets RISI publishes. RISI recognised $70 of the third round for linerboard in September, in line with the $50–$70 Truist's contacts had expected in July, and nothing more in October. If that is most of what sticks, then both the Q4 guide and the $14.23 consensus EPS for 2027 depend on price that may never arrive.
02
Demand is uneven. PCA's legacy corrugated shipments fell 0.5% per day in Q3 2026, and management cited "some weakness in agricultural end markets in our corrugated business." A price increase pushed into a soft market is the classic setup for having to give it back.
03
The costs PCA can't control are rising. Freight cost $0.26 per share against Q3 2025, and labor and operating expense $0.28. For Q4 management expects freight to increase with diesel prices and higher wood fiber costs, and it cites continued headwinds from freight, recycled fiber and chemicals. The Greif mills also raised PCA's exposure to recycled fiber, which gets more expensive with the same box demand that supports board prices.
04
Debt paid for Greif. PCA said it would fund the $1.8B with $1.5B of new debt and cash on hand. Total debt was $4.40B at June 30 (the September 30 balance sheet comes with the 10-Q), against $761.8M of cash and marketable securities at September 30 per the release; gross debt is about 2.2x TTM EBITDA of $1.98B. That is manageable, but the dividend takes 74% of GAAP earnings over the last twelve months, which leaves less room if the cycle turns.

Notable One-Off Items

Two things distort the reported numbers. The first is Wallula. The December 2025 decision to shut the No. 2 machine and kraft pulping came with about $205M of estimated pre-tax restructuring charges, mostly non-cash, and Q4 2025 GAAP net income fell to $101.8M. Special items continued into 2026: Q3 2026 carried $0.11 per share of charges ($6.2M of facility closure costs, $3.0M of acquisition and integration costs, $3.0M for fire damage at a corrugated products facility and $0.3M for Wallula), the gap between $2.93 reported and $3.04 excluding special items. The second is Greif. Since September 2, 2025 the results include a business with ~$1.2B of annual sales, so headline growth overstates organic growth; the gap closes from Q4 2026, because Q4 2025 already held a full quarter of Greif. In Q3 2026 legacy corrugated shipments were down 0.5% per day and total shipments up 10.6%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The industry cut nearly 10% of North American capacity in 2025. PCA took out 250,000 tons of its own highest-cost production and bought 800,000 tons of running capacity at about 6.6x EBITDA after synergies. Producers then pushed through two rounds of price increases before trying a third. If even part of the $140 sticks, the earnings math is steep. Consensus has EPS rising from $8.11 GAAP TTM to $10.61 in 2026 and $14.23 in 2027, which puts the stock at 18x 2027 earnings. Q3 EPS excluding special items came in at $3.04, above the company's $2.91 guide, and price and mix added $0.79 per share against Q3 2025. A 2.4% dividend pays you to wait.

Bear Case

Containerboard is a commodity, and the Fastmarkets RISI index that sets its price recognised only $70 of the $140 for linerboard in September and showed no change in October. Legacy corrugated shipments fell 0.5% per day in Q3, and management cited weakness in agricultural end markets. A $140 increase announced into that kind of market tends to be partly recognised and then given back. The costs, meanwhile, are here now: freight, labor, fiber and chemicals all rose against Q3 2025, the Q4 guide of $2.81 is below Q3's $3.04, and the Greif deal added $1.5B of borrowing. At 31x trailing and 19x forward earnings, with a 74% payout ratio and a 3.3% FCF yield, the price already reflects much of the recovery.

Top 3 Risks

01
Price increases fall short. The Q4 guide assumes earlier increases keep rolling in; the release says prices "are expected to improve as we continue to implement earlier price increases." In July Truist's industry contacts suggested $50–$70 of the latest $140 could be recognised; Fastmarkets RISI recognised $70 for linerboard in September and nothing more in October. Any round that is only partly recognised comes straight out of 2026–2027 estimates.
02
Box demand. PCA's legacy shipments slipped 0.5% per day in Q3 and it cited weak agricultural end markets. If volumes weaken, the capacity cuts that support pricing may not be enough.
03
Rising costs during integration. Freight cost $0.26 per share against Q3 2025, and for Q4 PCA expects recycled fiber costs "to continue to increase." The $60M synergy target now has more cost inflation to absorb than when the deal was announced.
Industry Commentary
Capacity discipline in North American containerboard
EMERGING TAILWIND

Packaging Dive reports that 2025 facility closures removed "nearly 10%" of North American containerboard production capacity. PCA's own Wallula move was about cost. As Truist's Michael Roxland wrote at the time, "these capacity adjustments are being driven by cost and not due to any incremental demand deterioration." Less supply against steady demand gave producers the confidence to announce three rounds of increases in 2026.

The price-realisation gap
KEY UNCERTAINTY

Fastmarkets RISI recognised a net $100/ton for the first two rounds of 2026 increases, the last $50 of it in June. In September it recognised $70 of the third round for linerboard and $100 for medium, then showed prices flat in October: a net $170/ton for linerboard so far in 2026. The third wave was uneven, with PCA at $140 and others at $80–$110 for linerboard. How much of it RISI recognises will decide whether PKG at $254 is early or late in the cycle. In its Q3 release PCA said prices "are expected to improve as we continue to implement earlier price increases."

Input costs
ONGOING HEADWIND

PCA's Q4 outlook expects freight to increase "with diesel fuel prices increasing above third quarter levels" and higher wood fiber costs "due to winter yields and mill mix," and its Q3 release cites continued cost headwinds from freight, recycled fiber and chemicals. Recycled fiber is the awkward one. Its price rises with the same box demand that supports board prices, so part of any price gain goes straight back out as cost.
