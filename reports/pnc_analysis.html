01 Company Overview

PNC is a Pittsburgh-based diversified financial services company and one of the largest banks in the United States, with $627.8 billion of total assets, $378.8 billion of loans and $466.4 billion of deposits as of September 30, 2026. Full-year 2025 revenue was a record $23.10 billion, up 7.2% year over year, with diluted EPS of $16.59 (+20.7%). Its market capitalisation of roughly $86.4 billion ranks it #141 in the S&P 500.

The latest quarter. Third-quarter 2026 net income was $2.097 billion, or $4.97 a diluted share ($5.09 adjusted, which adds back $36 million of FirstBank integration costs and $25 million of net Visa-related items). Revenue of $6.562 billion was 11% above a year earlier but 5% below Q2, because Q2 had included a $448 million gain on Visa shares in Retail Banking and capital-markets revenue was lower in Q3. Average loans rose 2% from Q2 to $372.2 billion, and management guided to another 2.5% in Q4.

The critical framing for everything below: PNC is a commercial bank first. At September 30, 2026, $277.0 billion — 73.1% — of loans were commercial and only $101.8 billion (26.9%) were consumer. That mix is the opposite of a card- or auto-lender's, and it changes the shape of credit risk. A consumer book deteriorates early, broadly and predictably; a commercial book deteriorates late, in lumps, and in a small number of large names. PNC's annualised net charge-off rate was 0.28% in Q3 (about 0.24% without $35 million of charge-offs tied to the student-loan transfer — our calculation), which looks pristine, but a commercial book's charge-offs can go from there to multiples of that in two or three quarters when a handful of large credits break. That asymmetry — quiet, then loud — is the single most important thing to understand about owning this stock.

SCALE REGULATORY COMPLIANCE

PNC's moat is a funding moat, not a product moat. Banking products are commodities; funding is not. At September 30, 2026 PNC held $466.4 billion of deposits against $378.8 billion of loans — a loan-to-deposit ratio of about 81% (our calculation), meaning the bank is a net funder rather than a net borrower and does not need wholesale markets to grow. Within that base, average noninterest-bearing deposits were $101.2 billion (22% of average deposits of $461.2 billion), which pay no interest at all. The corporate treasury-management franchise reinforces this: operating accounts sit at the bank because payments, payroll and cash concentration run through it, which makes those balances far less rate-sensitive than a rate-shopping retail CD. On top of that sits a middle-market commercial relationship business that is genuinely hard to replicate — credit, treasury, capital markets and advisory sold together into companies that will not casually switch banks — and a $499 billion client-asset pool that generates fee income without consuming capital. The result is visible in returns: 18.28% ROTCE in Q3 2026 against a 57% efficiency ratio.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Commercial real estate — and specifically office. This is the most-watched number on any commercial bank, so it deserves precision rather than reassurance. Total CRE loans were $37.0 billion at September 30, 2026 — 9.8% of the $378.8 billion loan book. The latest office detail we have is from June 30, 2026: a portfolio of $5.1 billion, or 1.39% of total loans and roughly 14% of CRE, of which 29.4% was criticised and 8.1% nonperforming, with a reserve of 8.8% — roughly $450 million, or about 6 times the reserve rate the bank holds against its loans as a whole (1.48% at that date). Read those three numbers together: nearly a third of the office book was already flagged as stressed, and management had pre-funded losses at a rate that assumes a large share of the criticised balances will not pay in full. On the Q3 call CFO Reilly said nonperforming loans rose $167 million, or 8%, to $2.2 billion, and that “The increase was primarily driven by the pull-through of previously reserved CRE office loans as well as one large commercial credit.” The total allowance for credit losses was 1.42% of loans at September 30 (1.48% at June 30), and nonperforming loans were 0.58% of loans. The honest read is that office is a contained problem, not a solved one — $5.1 billion is small enough that even a catastrophic outcome is an earnings event rather than a capital event, but a 29.4% criticised rate says the deterioration is real, and a falling reserve ratio is management's judgement, not a fact.

2. AOCI and the securities book. PNC held $146.4 billion of investment securities at quarter-end — about 23% of total assets — split between available-for-sale (about $72.0B average in Q3) and held-to-maturity (about $79.3B average). AOCI stood at −$6,472 million, down from −$4,120 million at June 30: the paper loss widened by $2.35 billion in one quarter. The mechanism matters: unrealised losses on AFS securities reduce equity but never pass through the income statement unless the bank is forced to sell — which is precisely what destroyed Silicon Valley Bank in 2023, when a deposit run forced liquidation of underwater bonds and crystallised losses that had been invisible in reported earnings. Sizing it for PNC: −$6.47 billion of AOCI across ~397 million shares is roughly $16.29 per share, meaning tangible book value would be about $124 rather than the reported $107.64 absent AOCI — the accumulated marks are suppressing TBVPS by roughly 13% (our calculation). Two points cut the other way: those marks accrete back as bonds pull to par, mechanically growing tangible book, and held-to-maturity marks do not run through AOCI at all, so the reported figure understates the total economic gap between book and market on the securities portfolio.

3. Deposit mix and the funding gap. Deposit beta is the share of a policy-rate move that a bank has to hand to its depositors. It is the difference between a rate cycle being a windfall and a squeeze. In Q3 2026 average noninterest-bearing balances were $101.2 billion, 22% of average deposits, down from $103.5 billion and 23% in Q2. Period-end deposits grew 4% from the prior quarter to $466.4 billion while period-end loans grew 3% to $378.8 billion, and borrowed funds fell to $79.8 billion from $85.7 billion at June 30. A commercial bank funds itself differently from a consumer-deposit bank: corporate operating balances are stickier than retail savings but larger, more concentrated, and more professionally managed — a single treasurer can move hundreds of millions in an afternoon in a way a million retail depositors cannot. The noninterest-bearing share is the buffer against that; watch it, because it eroding is the first sign the funding moat is thinning.

4. Capital, costs and integration. The CET1 ratio was an estimated 9.8% at September 30, 2026, against 9.9% at June 30, 10.1% at March 31 and 10.6% at year-end 2025. That is the lowest of the four readings and it constrains buybacks: repurchases were $0.6 billion in Q3, and Reilly said on the call that Q4 repurchases should be about the same, a pace of roughly 2.8% of the $86.4 billion market cap a year (our calculation). Costs are the second pressure: the 2026 outlook for noninterest expense growth rose to about 9.5% from about 8.5% in the previous edition, with the Q4 adjusted-expense outlook up about 5% from Q3. The FirstBank integration cost $36 million in Q3, and about $15 billion of the $47 billion of loan growth in the first nine months of 2026 came with that acquisition; management said the remaining growth, more than $30 billion, was organic.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

Four items distort the Q3 2026 and trailing figures and should be normalised out of any run-rate. The release adds back the first two to reach adjusted EPS of $5.09: $36 million of integration costs and a net $25 million of Visa-related items, $48 million after tax, or $0.12 a share.

1. The FirstBank acquisition. PNC completed the system conversion on June 22, 2026, migrating approximately 780,000 customers, 1,620 employees and 95 branches across Colorado and Arizona. Integration costs were $36 million in Q3. About $15 billion of the $47 billion of average-loan growth in the first nine months of 2026 came with the acquisition, so growth rates on both sides of the balance sheet are acquisition-inflated in 2026 and will not repeat. It is also a main reason CET1 fell from 10.6% at year-end 2025 to 9.8%, alongside the loan growth itself.

2. Visa. Q2 2026 included a $448 million gain on the Visa share exchange in Retail Banking, which is why Retail net income fell 18% from Q2 to Q3 even though it rose 5% from a year earlier. Q3 itself carried an $11 million gain from the Visa share exchange and a $36 million charge from fair-value adjustments on a Visa-related derivative, a net $25 million negative.

3. Student-loan exit. PNC moved a $1.4 billion student-loan portfolio to held-for-sale in Q3, which put $35 million into net charge-offs and cut average consumer loans by $1.4 billion from Q2. Reilly said “the transfer represents the final step in exiting this nonstrategic business. We expect the sale to have a de minimis financial impact.”

4. Reserve level. The allowance for credit losses fell to 1.42% of loans from 1.48% at June 30, while the provision for credit losses was $189 million against $167 million a year earlier. A lower reserve ratio adds to profit without any cash being collected, so part of the earnings quality rests on management's credit judgement.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It describes momentum, not value — a stock can stay overbought for months.

Five-year trajectory. PNC closed at $197.00 at the end of November 2021 and at $217.39 on October 16, 2026 — a price return of +10.4%, or roughly 2.0% a year compounded over 4.9 years (price only, dividends excluded). But the path is what matters, and it was violent. The stock lost 44.4% from the January 2022 close of $205.99 to the October 2023 low of $114.47, then rose 90% off that low to October 16, 2026, with a month-end peak of $249.87 in July 2026 (+118% from the low). An investor who bought at the January 2022 close did not see a higher month-end close until November 2024. This is the practical illustration of why a 0.93 beta understates a bank's real risk: beta is calibrated on ordinary market noise, and bank equity does not behave ordinarily in a funding crisis.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

PNC is earning an 18.28% ROTCE at a 57% efficiency ratio and trades at 11.57× earnings and 2.02× tangible book — a return profile well above cost of equity at an unremarkable multiple. The asset-repricing tailwind is mechanical rather than speculative: management calculates the margin reaches 3% or above in Q4, net interest income is guided up about 15.5% for 2026, and Q4 net interest income is guided to grow faster (about 4%) than loans (about 2.5%). More than $30 billion of the $47 billion of loan growth this year was organic, per the Q3 call. Meanwhile −$6.47bn of AOCI accretes back into tangible book as bonds pull to par, adding roughly $16 per share of book value over time with no operational effort. The 18% dividend increase to a 3.68% yield says management believes it.

Bear Case

The credit cycle has not been tested. A 0.28% charge-off rate on a book that is 73.1% commercial is not evidence of underwriting quality — it is evidence that nothing has broken yet, and commercial losses arrive late and in lumps. At June 30 the office portfolio was 29.4% criticised and 8.1% nonperforming, and the total reserve ratio has fallen to 1.42%. CET1 is down 80bp since year-end 2025 to 9.8%, capping buybacks at about $0.6bn a quarter. Costs are running hotter than the previous edition's outlook (expense growth about 9.5% versus about 8.5%), Q3 revenue came in below the roughly $6.61bn expected, the margin slipped to 2.94%, and AOCI widened by $2.35bn in a quarter. The easy re-rating has been collected; from here the shareholder is paid only for earnings that must survive a credit downturn that hasn't happened yet.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
Commercial credit normalisation, led by CRE. $37.0bn of CRE (9.8% of loans), including office that stood at $5.1bn, 29.4% criticised and 8.1% nonperforming against an 8.8% reserve at June 30. A commercial book's losses do not creep — they step. Charge-offs moving from 0.28% to 0.75% on a $378.8bn book would be roughly $1.8bn a year more in losses (our calculation), a meaningful share of earnings.
02
Funding mix and margin delivery. Noninterest-bearing deposits slipped to 22% of the average base from 23%. If corporate treasurers reallocate operating balances — which they can do in size, quickly, in a way retail depositors cannot — the move to a 3% margin does not happen and the funding moat thins.
03
AOCI, securities and forced-sale risk. $146.4bn of securities (23% of assets) with −$6.47bn of AOCI suppressing tangible book by roughly $16.29 per share, and held-to-maturity marks that never appear in AOCI at all. These losses are harmless right up until they are not: the SVB mechanism is that a deposit outflow forces sales, crystallising paper losses into real capital destruction. PNC's 81% loan-to-deposit ratio and 22% noninterest-bearing base make this unlikely — but it is the tail that actually kills banks.
  Industry Commentary
Office CRE — The Sector's Open Wound
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every commercial bank in America is being valued partly on what the market believes about office. PNC's numbers let you judge rather than guess: $5.1bn office (1.39% of loans), 29.4% criticised, 8.1% nonperforming, 8.8% reserved at June 30, 2026 — a reserve rate about 6× the 1.48% held against the total book at that date. The sizing is the good news: even a 25% loss on the entire office portfolio would be ~$1.3bn pre-tax, against $2.10bn of quarterly net income. It is an earnings problem, not a solvency problem. Total CRE was $37.0bn at September 30. On the Q3 call Reilly said the quarter's rise in nonperforming loans was “primarily driven by the pull-through of previously reserved CRE office loans as well as one large commercial credit.”
The Margin Race
ONGOING
PNC's net interest margin was 2.94% in Q3 2026, down 2bp from Q2 and up 15bp from a year earlier. Management expects 3% or above in Q4, and guided net interest income up about 4% on average loans up about 2.5%. The funding side is the check: noninterest-bearing deposits slipped to 22% of the average base from 23% in Q2, while borrowed funds fell to $79.8bn from $85.7bn. Whether the margin gets to 3% is the whole net interest income story for 2027.
Growth: Buy It or Build It — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND
Bull framing: PNC closed and converted FirstBank inside a single cycle, adding 95 branches and 780,000 customers in Colorado and Arizona, and about $30 billion of this year's loan growth came organically; Demchak says that is a reason not to do a large deal. Bear framing: acquisitions are how banks import credit problems they did not underwrite, and they consume the exact capital that would otherwise absorb a downturn — CET1 is 9.8% against 10.6% at year-end 2025. If the credit cycle turns while PNC is at that lower capital level, the bank will be defending capital rather than deploying it, and the buyback stops first.
