01 Company Overview

PNC is a Pittsburgh-based diversified financial services company and one of the largest banks in the United States, with $616.0 billion of total assets, $368.0 billion of loans and $449.8 billion of deposits as of June 30, 2026. Full-year 2025 revenue was $22.32 billion, up 7.48% year over year, on diluted EPS of $16.59 (+20.7%); trailing-twelve-month revenue has since risen to $24.32 billion. Its market capitalisation of roughly $96.6 billion makes it the 254th most valuable listed company in the world and the 127th most valuable in the United States.

The critical framing for everything below: PNC is a commercial bank first. At June 30, 2026, $263.9 billion — 71.7% — of loans were commercial and only $104.1 billion (28.3%) were consumer. That mix is the opposite of a card- or auto-lender's, and it changes the shape of credit risk. A consumer book deteriorates early, broadly and predictably; a commercial book deteriorates late, in lumps, and in a small number of large names. PNC's current annualised net charge-off rate of 0.25% looks pristine, but a commercial book's charge-offs can go from 0.25% to multiples of that in two or three quarters when a handful of large credits break. That asymmetry — quiet, then loud — is the single most important thing to understand about owning this stock.

SCALE REGULATORY COMPLIANCE

PNC's moat is a funding moat, not a product moat. Banking products are commodities; funding is not. At June 30, 2026 PNC held $449.8 billion of deposits against $368.0 billion of loans — a loan-to-deposit ratio of about 82%, meaning the bank is a net funder rather than a net borrower and does not need wholesale markets to grow. Within that base, $103.5 billion (23% of average deposits) pays no interest at all, and the rate paid on the remaining interest-bearing deposits was just 1.91% in Q2 2026, down 5 basis points sequentially. The corporate treasury-management franchise reinforces this: operating accounts sit at the bank because payments, payroll and cash concentration run through it, which makes those balances far less rate-sensitive than a rate-shopping retail CD. On top of that sits a middle-market commercial relationship business that is genuinely hard to replicate — credit, treasury, capital markets and advisory sold together into companies that will not casually switch banks — and a $503 billion client-asset pool that generates fee income without consuming capital. The result is visible in returns: 17.88% ROTCE in Q2 2026 against a 60% efficiency ratio.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Commercial real estate — and specifically office. This is the most-watched number on any commercial bank, so it deserves precision rather than reassurance. Total CRE loans were $36.0 billion at June 30, 2026 — 9.8% of the $368.0 billion loan book, having grown $690 million in the quarter on retail and industrial exposures. Inside that sits the office portfolio: $5.1 billion, or 1.39% of total loans and roughly 14% of CRE. The condition of that $5.1 billion is poor and PNC does not pretend otherwise. 29.4% of it is criticised, 8.1% is nonperforming, and PNC carries a reserve of 8.8% against the portfolio — roughly $450 million, or about 35 times the reserve rate it holds against the book as a whole (1.48%). Read those three numbers together: nearly a third of the office book is already flagged as stressed, and management has pre-funded losses at a rate that assumes a large share of the criticised balances will not pay in full. CFO Reilly's framing on the Q2 call was that "we have worked through the commercial real estate office portfolio, still some work to do there, but we did release some reserves." The honest read is that office is a contained problem, not a solved one — $5.1 billion is small enough that even a catastrophic outcome is an earnings event rather than a capital event, but the 29.4% criticised rate says the deterioration is real and the reserve release is management's judgement, not a fact.

2. AOCI and the securities book. PNC held $149.5 billion of investment securities at quarter-end — about 24% of total assets — split roughly evenly between available-for-sale (~$73.7B average) and held-to-maturity (~$73.4B average). AOCI stood at −$4,120 million, improved from −$3.8 billion at March 31 in nominal terms but still a material drag. The mechanism matters: unrealised losses on AFS securities reduce equity but never pass through the income statement unless the bank is forced to sell — which is precisely what destroyed Silicon Valley Bank in 2023, when a deposit run forced liquidation of underwater bonds and crystallised losses that had been invisible in reported earnings. Sizing it for PNC: −$4.12 billion of AOCI across ~399 million shares is roughly $10.33 per share, meaning tangible book value would be about $121 rather than the reported $111.09 absent AOCI — the accumulated marks are suppressing TBVPS by roughly 8.5%. (Note that AFS net unrealised losses alone were $2.1 billion, so about half of AOCI is hedges and other components rather than the bond book.) Two mitigants and one caveat. Mitigant one: those marks accrete back as bonds pull to par, mechanically growing tangible book. Mitigant two: PNC is actively repositioning — it sold ~$4 billion of AFS securities at a $139 million realised loss and reinvested at yields 120 basis points higher, converting an unrealised loss into a permanent earnings uplift. The caveat: held-to-maturity marks do not run through AOCI at all, so the reported figure understates the total economic gap between book and market on the securities portfolio. The HTM unrealised loss was not disclosed in the materials retrieved for this build and is not estimated here.

3. Deposit beta and the funding mix. Deposit beta is the share of a policy-rate move that a bank has to hand to its depositors. It is the difference between a rate cycle being a windfall and a squeeze. PNC did not disclose a cumulative deposit beta figure in the Q2 2026 materials retrieved for this build, and none is invented here. What is disclosed: the rate paid on interest-bearing deposits was 1.91%, down 5bp sequentially; non-interest-bearing balances grew 4% sequentially to $103.5 billion, 23% of average deposits; and CFO Reilly flagged that "we do have rate paid drifting back up to first quarter levels," i.e. the cost tailwind is ending. A commercial bank funds itself differently from a consumer-deposit bank: corporate operating balances are stickier than retail savings but larger, more concentrated, and more professionally managed — a single treasurer can move hundreds of millions in an afternoon in a way a million retail depositors cannot. The 23% non-interest-bearing share is the buffer against that; watch it, because it eroding is the first sign the funding moat is thinning.

4. Capital rebuild and integration execution. The CET1 ratio fell to 9.9% at June 30, 2026 from 10.1% at March 31 and 10.6% at year-end 2025, driven by the FirstBank acquisition and higher borrowings. That is still at management's 10% operating target, but it is the low end of the recent range and it constrains buybacks: repurchases ran $610 million in Q2 with a similar amount guided for Q3, modest against a $96.6 billion market cap. Meanwhile the FirstBank integration is not friction-free — Demchak conceded the bank "underestimated the lack of digital awareness" among acquired customers, producing unexpected branch traffic. Borrowed funds jumped 25% sequentially to $85.7 billion, a reminder that rapid balance-sheet growth is being part-funded wholesale rather than entirely by deposits.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

Three items distort the Q2 2026 and trailing figures and should be normalised out of any run-rate.

1. The FirstBank acquisition. PNC completed the system conversion on June 22, 2026, migrating approximately 780,000 customers, 1,620 employees and 95 branches across Colorado and Arizona. This is the dominant reason CET1 fell from 10.6% at year-end 2025 to 9.9%, why average loans and deposits grew 12–13% year over year in Retail Banking, and why consensus FY2026 revenue growth of 13.90% far exceeds the FY2025 organic rate of 7.48%. Growth rates on both sides of the balance sheet are acquisition-inflated in 2026 and will not repeat.

2. Securities repositioning loss. Management sold roughly $4 billion of available-for-sale securities at a $139 million realised loss, reinvesting at yields 120 basis points higher. This is the gap between reported EPS of $4.81 and adjusted EPS of $4.85 in Q2 2026. Economically it is a positive — a one-time charge exchanged for a permanent yield uplift — but it flatters forward NIM relative to the trailing period.

3. Office CRE reserve release. The allowance for credit losses fell to 1.48% of loans from 1.52% at March 31, and Reilly confirmed "we did release some reserves" against office commercial real estate. Reserve releases flow straight to pre-tax income without any cash being collected. With 29.4% of the office book still criticised, this is a judgement call that could reverse, and Q2 earnings quality is modestly overstated to the extent of it.

Beyond these, no material litigation charges, goodwill impairments or discrete tax items were disclosed in the Q2 2026 release.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It describes momentum, not value — a stock can stay overbought for months.

Five-year trajectory. PNC closed at $164.11 at the end of September 2021 and at $242.22 on August 28, 2026 — a price return of +47.6%, or roughly 8.1% a year compounded. Including dividends, which have averaged in the 3%–4% range across the window, total return is approximately +75% (est.). But the path is what matters, and it was violent. The stock lost 37% from the December 2021 close of $169.25 to the May 2023 trough of $103.22, then roughly doubled off that low over the following three years. An investor who bought at the top of 2021 spent nearly two and a half years underwater. This is the practical illustration of why a 0.90 beta understates a bank's real risk: beta is calibrated on ordinary market noise, and bank equity does not behave ordinarily in a funding crisis.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

PNC is earning a 17.88% ROTCE at a 60% efficiency ratio and trades at 13.33× earnings and 2.18× tangible book — a return profile well above cost of equity at an unremarkable multiple. The asset-repricing tailwind is mechanical rather than speculative: NIM is guided above 3% by year-end, full-year net interest income up 15%–15.5%, and the $4bn securities swap at +120bp converts dead AOCI weight into permanent earnings. FirstBank adds 780,000 customers in genuinely faster-growing markets, and management says new markets are outgrowing legacy ones. Meanwhile −$4.12bn of AOCI accretes back into tangible book as bonds pull to par, adding roughly $10 per share of book value over time with no operational effort. The 18% dividend increase to a 3.30% yield says management believes it.

Bear Case

The credit cycle has not been tested. A 0.25% charge-off rate on a book that is 71.7% commercial is not evidence of underwriting quality — it is evidence that nothing has broken yet, and commercial losses arrive late and in lumps. The office portfolio is 29.4% criticised and 8.1% nonperforming, yet PNC is releasing reserves, so Q2 earnings quality is partly a judgement call. CET1 fell 70bp in six months to 9.9%, capping buybacks at ~$610m a quarter against a $96.6bn cap. Borrowed funds rose 25% sequentially, meaning growth is being part-funded wholesale. Deposit costs are already "drifting back up" by the CFO's own account. And at 2.18× tangible book the crisis discount is gone — the easy re-rating has been collected, so from here the shareholder is paid only for earnings that must survive a credit downturn that hasn't happened yet.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
Commercial credit normalisation, led by CRE. $36.0bn of CRE (9.8% of loans) including $5.1bn of office that is already 29.4% criticised and 8.1% nonperforming against an 8.8% reserve. A commercial book's losses do not creep — they step. Charge-offs moving from 0.25% to even 0.75% on a $368bn book is roughly $1.8bn of annual pre-tax provision, a meaningful share of earnings.
02
Funding cost and deposit-mix erosion. Non-interest-bearing deposits are 23% of the average base and the rate paid on interest-bearing deposits is already drifting higher by management's own admission. If corporate treasurers reallocate operating balances — which they can do in size, quickly, in a way retail depositors cannot — the NIM path above 3% does not happen and the funding moat thins.
03
AOCI, securities and forced-sale risk. $149.5bn of securities (24% of assets) with −$4.12bn of AOCI suppressing tangible book by roughly $10.33 per share, and held-to-maturity marks that never appear in AOCI at all and were not disclosed in the materials retrieved here. These losses are harmless right up until they are not: the SVB mechanism is that a deposit outflow forces sales, crystallising paper losses into real capital destruction. PNC's 82% loan-to-deposit ratio and 23% non-interest-bearing base make this unlikely — but it is the tail that actually kills banks.
  Industry Commentary
Office CRE — The Sector's Open Wound
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Every commercial bank in America is being valued partly on what the market believes about office. PNC's numbers let you judge rather than guess: $5.1bn office (1.39% of loans), 29.4% criticised, 8.1% nonperforming, 8.8% reserved — that reserve rate is ~6× the 1.48% held against the total book. The sizing is the good news: even a 25% loss on the entire office portfolio would be ~$1.3bn pre-tax, against $2.06bn of quarterly net income. It is an earnings problem, not a solvency problem. The bad news is the trajectory question — Reilly says "still some work to do there" while simultaneously releasing reserves, and PNC does not disclose office criticised trends quarter-to-quarter in the summary materials, so outside investors cannot easily verify whether 29.4% is improving or worsening.
The Deposit-Cost Turn
ONGOING HEADWIND
The industry spent 2024–2025 enjoying falling deposit costs as rates came down. That is ending. PNC's rate paid on interest-bearing deposits fell 5bp to 1.91% in Q2 2026, but Reilly explicitly flagged rate paid "drifting back up to first quarter levels." Simultaneously, borrowed funds rose 25% sequentially to $85.7bn — wholesale funding is filling a gap that deposits are not. The offsetting force is asset repricing: fixed-rate loans and securities booked at cycle-low yields are rolling into materially higher ones, which is why NIM can rise to 3%+ even as funding costs stabilise. The race between those two forces is the whole net interest income story for 2027.
Consolidation and the Scale Premium — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND
Bull framing: regulatory tolerance for regional-bank M&A has clearly improved — PNC closed and converted FirstBank inside a single cycle, adding 95 branches and 780,000 customers in Colorado and Arizona, and management reports new markets outgrowing legacy ones. Scale is the defence against both compliance cost and deposit competition, and PNC has the currency (2.18× tangible book) and the operating record to keep buying. Bear framing: acquisitions are how banks import credit problems they did not underwrite, and they consume the exact capital that would otherwise absorb a downturn — CET1 fell 70bp to 9.9% doing this one. The integration is already producing unmodelled costs from FirstBank customers' lower digital adoption. If the credit cycle turns while PNC is mid-integration and at its 10% CET1 floor, the bank will be defending capital rather than deploying it, and the buyback stops first.
