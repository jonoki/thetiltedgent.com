01 Company Overview

Pentair makes the equipment that moves and cleans water: pumps and filtration for homes, farms and industry, water softeners and commercial water treatment, and the pumps, heaters, filters and automation that run a backyard swimming pool. It was founded in 1966 in Minnesota by five former Litton Industries engineers — the name is "penta" for the five founders — merged with Tyco's flow-control business in 2012, spun its electrical division off as nVent in May 2018, and is today an Irish-incorporated company run from London with its US base in Golden Valley, Minnesota. Trailing-twelve-month revenue to June 30, 2026 was $4.01B, with a 41.4% gross margin, a 22.8% operating margin and GAAP EPS of $3.98.

For five years this was a quiet compounder: it closed September 2025 at $110.76, up from $40.63 at the September 2022 low, and hit an intraday high of $113.95 on October 3, 2025 (Yahoo Finance daily history) — the top of its 52-week range. Then the pool business broke. On the evening of July 14, 2026 Pentair pre-announced second-quarter sales of about $930 million, down 17%, blamed a destock in the pool distribution channel worth roughly $170 million of sales and $105 million of segment income in the quarter, cut its full-year outlook from 2–4% sales growth to a 4–7% decline, and disclosed that its recently appointed CFO had left on July 10. The stock fell 15.0% the next day, to $64.33. It has kept falling since and closed September 21 at $55.78 — 51% below the October high. The central debate: is this a one-season inventory flush in a business whose two other segments just grew segment income by double digits, or evidence — as a securities lawsuit now alleges — that Pentair's pool franchise was losing share and borrowing sales from the future?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Installed base & replacement demand Distributor & dealer relationships Brand in pool equipment Water-treatment product breadth High segment margins (23–30% ROS) Moderate leverage (D/E 0.47)

The moat is the installed base. A pool pump or heater, once plumbed in, tends to be replaced with the same brand by the same service technician, and water-treatment systems carry recurring filter and service demand. That is why Pentair's segment margins run between 23% and 30% even in a bad quarter. The vulnerability is that the moat protects end-demand, not channel orders: distributors decide how much inventory to hold, and when they decide to hold less, the manufacturer's reported sales fall far harder than anything the homeowner did. The open question the lawsuit raises is whether the moat itself — share at the dealer — eroded as well.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Channel concentration in Pool. The company itself attributed the Q2 miss to inventory realignment "with major channel partners". When a few large distributors reset stocking levels at once, a segment that was 38% of Q2 2025 sales can lose 42% of its revenue in a quarter.
02
Credibility and litigation. Securities class actions (filed in the District of Minnesota and the Southern District of New York) cover purchases from March 11, 2025 to July 14, 2026 and allege that the 80/20 programme damaged Pool customer relationships, cost market share and was masked by above-normal rebates. These are allegations, not findings — but the guidance cut arrived eleven weeks after the April 28 Q1 report, with a CFO exit in between.
03
Rate-sensitive end market. New pools, remodels and big-ticket upgrades are discretionary and financed; the company named higher interest rates and inflation as part of the worsening pool conditions. Replacement demand is stickier, but it can be deferred with repairs.
04
Deal risk at a low point. A ~$1.4B acquisition — about 16% of the $8.90B market value — signed in the same month as a guidance cut, under an interim CFO, with cash of $91.8M and $1.76B of existing debt. Integration of a new HVAC-adjacent platform adds execution load when management attention is already stretched.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the picture. First, the destock itself: ~$170M of Q2 sales and ~$105M of Pool segment income that management attributes to channel inventory rather than end-demand. If that reading is right, FY2026 earnings understate normalised power; if the lawsuit's reading is right, prior years overstated it. Second, GAAP vs adjusted: the FY2026 GAAP EPS guide of $3.86–$4.06 sits about $0.74 below the adjusted $4.60–$4.80 — the adjustments are doing real work this year, and the forward P/E above uses the adjusted consensus. Third, Taco: closing in Q4 will bring deal costs, purchase accounting and new debt into the 2026–2027 numbers. Separately, a small discrepancy to be aware of: the TTM EPS of $3.98 is on StockAnalysis's net-income basis, which includes discontinued operations. For Q1 2026 that is $1.05; the company's own Q1 release shows $0.98 from continuing operations and $1.05 in total. On a continuing-operations basis TTM EPS would be lower and the P/E slightly higher.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 close of $72.63 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $55.78, PNR lost 23.2% of its price. Dividends ($0.80 a share in 2021 rising to $1.08 on a trailing basis) soften that but do not change the sign. The endpoints hide two completely different stocks. From the September 2022 low of $40.63 the shares rose 173% to $110.76 by September 2025 as margins expanded — operating margin went from 15.4% in FY2022 to 23.3% in FY2025 on almost unchanged revenue. Then, in the last twelve months, the entire gain went away: −49.6% from the September 2025 month-end to the September 21 close, and −46.4% since December 31, 2025.

The 6-period monthly RSI reads 15.9 — deep in oversold territory, the seventh consecutive month-end reading below 30 (every close since March 2026), with a low of 14.8 at the May 2026 close. The stock has closed lower in seven of the eight months since January 2026, the only exception a June bounce of 8.2% that July's pre-announcement erased. The 10-month moving average sits near $80.57, 44% above the price: this is a trend still pointed down, not a bottom that has been confirmed. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 14.0x trailing GAAP earnings and about 12x the 2026 adjusted consensus for a company whose two non-pool segments just grew segment income 27% and 17%, whose gross margin rose to 42.35% in the worst quarter of the destock, and which yields 7.6% in trailing free cash flow and 1.94% in dividends. Distributors cannot run inventory below what the season requires for long; if end-demand for replacement pool equipment is intact, the ~$250M destock reverses into 2027 as a restock, on top of Taco's ~$540M of revenue. Every analyst target on the board — the lowest is $60 — sits above the September 21 close, and Wolfe upgraded into the weakness.

Bear Case

A 42% sales collapse in one segment, a full-year outlook cut from +2–4% to −4–7%, and a CFO leaving months into the job are not the fingerprints of a routine channel adjustment. The class action alleges the 80/20 programme cost Pool share and that rebates pulled revenue forward — if even part of that is true, the pre-2026 margins were flattered and the "normal" earnings base is lower than the bulls assume. Meanwhile management is committing ~$1.4B, about 16% of the market cap, to an acquisition under an interim CFO. The stock has fallen seven of the last eight months and printed a new 52-week low on the banner date; the trend has not turned.

Top 3 Risks

01
The destock is really share loss. If Pool sell-through does not recover when channel inventory normalises, the ~$250M is not a timing effect but a permanent revenue reset in the segment that was 38% of Q2 2025 sales.
02
Litigation and credibility. The class period runs from March 11, 2025 to July 14, 2026. Discovery, settlement cost and management distraction are real even if the claims fail, and a second guidance miss would be read against this backdrop.
03
Acquisition execution with a stretched balance sheet. ~$1.4B for Taco against $91.8M of cash and $1.76B of existing debt, closing in Q4 2026 with a permanent CFO not yet in place at the data-as-of date.
Industry Commentary
Pool channel inventory reset
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Pool equipment is sold through distributors who build inventory in the first half for the summer season. When they decide to carry less — because of higher financing costs, slower new-pool construction or simply a reset in how many weeks of stock they want on hand — the manufacturer sees the cut in full and at once, even if homeowners are buying the same number of replacement pumps. Pentair's own language pointed at exactly that: a more pronounced inventory realignment with major channel partners than it had estimated, and worsening conditions including higher interest rates and inflation. The Q3 guide for Pool of −23% to −25% says the reset is not finished. The 2027 season's stocking orders, placed in late 2026 and early 2027, are the first real test of how much of the fall was inventory and how much was demand or share.

80/20 simplification — margin engine or franchise risk
ONGOING HEADWIND

Pentair's operating margin rose from 15.4% in FY2022 to 23.3% in FY2025 on revenue that barely moved ($4,122M to $4,176M). That is the output of a deliberate programme to concentrate on the most profitable products and customers — the 80/20 approach the lawsuit targets. Pruning low-margin customers and SKUs can be exactly right. It can also hand shelf space and dealer loyalty to a competitor, which in pool equipment means Hayward and others. The plaintiffs allege the latter happened and was obscured by rebates. Q2 2026 itself cuts both ways: segment income in Flow and Water Solutions rose 27% and 17% while Pool's fell 62%.

Water, cooling and the Taco bet
EMERGING TAILWIND

Management's answer to a stalled pool franchise is diversification into hydronic heating and cooling: Taco adds roughly $540M of projected 2026 revenue at about 10.5x 2026E EBITDA (including tax benefits), with the interim CFO describing it as a new growth engine into energy efficiency, comfort cooling, HVAC and data-centre build-outs (CFO Dive, July 28, 2026). The bull framing: a business with 23–30% segment margins in water treatment and flow buys into faster-growing building and data-centre water loops at a reasonable multiple, lowering pool from 38% of sales toward a smaller share. The bear framing: a management team that has just cut its own full-year sales outlook by six to eleven points is committing ~$1.4B at the low point of its own share price. The way to adjudicate is two reported numbers: Pool core sales growth in the first half of 2027, and Taco's contribution at the margin management promised.
