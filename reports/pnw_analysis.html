01 Company Overview

Pinnacle West is a holding company with one job that matters: it owns Arizona Public Service (APS), the largest utility in Arizona, which generates, transmits and sells electricity across the state and serves about two-thirds of the Phoenix metro area. APS operates and partly owns the Palo Verde nuclear station. The holding company was set up in 1985 as AZP Group and took the Pinnacle West name and the PNW ticker in 1987; the utility's roots go back to the Phoenix Light and Fuel Company of 1886. Revenue over the twelve months to June 30, 2026 was $5.55B, net income $640M, diluted EPS $5.19.

A regulated utility doesn't win customers; it's handed a territory and told what it may earn. The equation is rate base × allowed return: APS spends capital on plants, wires and substations, the Arizona Corporation Commission (ACC) adds that spending to the rate base, and customer rates are set to give shareholders an allowed ROE on the equity-financed slice. APS's current ACC allowed ROE is 9.55% on a 51.93% equity layer, set in February 2024. In June 2025 APS filed for a 10.70% ROE and a net increase of $579.5M (13.99%) in customer revenue. Hearings ended July 7, 2026; ACC staff and the state's consumer advocate have argued for an ROE "closer to 9%", and the Attorney General's expert for 6%. A decision is expected in December. That vote is the biggest single input to this stock's next five years, more than the weather and more than data centers.

The other half of the story is load growth. Phoenix is adding homes, chip fabs and data centers at once. In Q2 2026 APS's weather-normalized retail sales rose 9.6% year over year, commercial and industrial 12.7%, and on August 2 the system set a record peak of 9,164 MW. More demand needs more plant, which grows the rate base, which only turns into earnings if the Commission lets it into rates at a fair return. So growth is the engine and regulation is the gearbox.

The two regulatory numbers that actually matter

For a utility, the income statement comes after two numbers the regulator controls: how big the rate base is and what return it may earn.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territory Regulated return on rate base Palo Verde nuclear stake Phoenix population & fab growth Transmission network (FERC 10.75%) Essential-service demand

APS has no competitor for most of the customers it serves, so the moat is legal, not commercial. The price of that moat is that someone else sets your price. Each dollar of plant earns only what the ACC allows and only once the ACC recognises it. Arizona's recent record shows how wide the outcomes are: the 2021 order gave APS 8.7% against 10.15% requested and disallowed $215.5M of Four Corners pollution-control spending and APS took the order to court. The dispute was settled in June 2023 at an 8.9% ROE with the $215.5M recovered over four years (Utility Dive). The 2024 order came in at 9.55% against 10.25% requested. The 2025 filing also proposes moving a "Court Resolution Surcharge" into base rates. So the moat is solid, but the regulator decides how much it pays.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The December vote is binary-ish. The parties are far apart: 10.70% requested, "closer to 9%" from staff and RUCO, 6% from the Attorney General's expert. Illustration (our round-number assumption, not a company figure; the company's August 2026 investor deck shows an approved ACC rate base of about $10.36B): on a $10B ACC rate base with a 52% equity layer, every 10 basis points of ROE is about $5.2M of annual net income, or roughly $0.04 a share on 121.2M shares. The ~170bp gap between the request and staff is then worth about $0.7 a share a year, against a 2026 EPS guide of $4.55–4.75.
02
Growth consumes cash. Operating cash flow over the twelve months to June 2026 was $1.77B, capex $2.70B, and free cash flow −$926M before a $431M dividend. The gap is funded with debt (total debt $16.51B, debt/equity 2.33) and new shares ($1.0–1.2B planned for 2026–2028). Every dollar of rate base growth has to be financed before it earns, and new equity dilutes per-share growth.
03
Affordability politics. A 14–16% requested increase draws a crowd. At the May hearing kickoff, one activist told commissioners a yes vote would be "a death sentence to many of our most vulnerable citizens." Pressure like that is how a well-argued request becomes a 9% outcome.
04
Earnings step down before they step up. 2026 guidance of $4.55–4.75 is below both FY2025's $5.05 and the TTM $5.19. The Q2 release shows why: higher interest charges, higher depreciation and amortization and lower transmission revenue outran weather, customer growth and slightly lower O&M. Consensus FY2027 EPS of $5.57 (+17.9%) assumes new rates arrive on time and at a reasonable return.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported figures. First, weather. APS's earnings depend heavily on how hot the summer is, which is why guidance is "weather-normalized". The Q2 2026 release credits favorable weather as a partial offset to higher costs, and a cool summer can work the other way. Second, timing within the year. H1 2026 EPS of $1.70 was up on H1 2025, but Q2 alone fell to $1.43 from $1.58 as higher interest, depreciation and lower transmission revenue outran weather and sales growth. Third, equity dilution. Forward equity sales priced but not yet settled will raise the share count as they settle, so per-share figures lag absolute profit growth. The FY2021 profit margin of 16.27% came from a different regulatory period and does not show the current run-rate.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You get one of the fastest-growing service territories in US utilities: weather-normalized sales +9.6% in Q2, C&I +12.7%, customers +2.1%, a record 9,164 MW peak, and chip-fab commitments management puts at $265B. That demand justifies a $10.35B capex plan and rate base growth of about 6.4% a year to ~$15.7B by 2028. If the ACC grants anything near a 10% ROE and adopts the formula rate mechanism, the regulatory lag that has kept the fair-value return at 2.54% starts to close. Consensus FY2027 EPS of $5.57 (+17.9%) is then achievable, and $93.31 is about 16.8× that, with a 3.9% yield while you wait. The stock has already fallen 12.8% from its June month-end, so some rate-case risk is priced in.

Bear Case

Arizona's regulators granted APS less than it asked in both of the last two rate cases (8.7% vs 10.15% in 2021, later settled at 8.9%; 9.55% vs 10.25% in 2024), and staff is at "closer to 9%" this time. A sub-9.55% ROE, no formula rate and a smaller-than-hoped increase would leave 2027 consensus too high. Meanwhile the build-out needs new equity ($1.0–1.2B) and more debt on a balance sheet already at 2.33× debt/equity with −$926M of TTM free cash flow. At 19.8× 2026 consensus EPS the stock is not cheap on near-term earnings. An 8% short float and a fresh $89 target from Morgan Stanley say part of the market expects the December vote to disappoint.

Top 3 Risks

01
Rate-case outcome. The spread between 10.70% requested and ~9% recommended is worth roughly $0.7 a share a year on our illustrative $10B / 52% equity assumption. The formula-rate decision matters as much as the headline ROE because it decides how quickly future capex earns.
02
Financing and dilution. Capex of $2.6–2.7B a year against ~$1.8B of operating cash flow means continuous debt and equity issuance. Higher interest rates raise both costs, and interest expense was already a named drag on Q2 EPS.
03
Large-load concentration and cost allocation. Growth depends on data centers and fabs. If those projects slip, or if the ACC shifts less of the cost onto them than the 47% XHLF request implies, APS either strands capacity or pushes more of the bill onto households, which feeds straight back into risk 01.
Industry Commentary
Load growth meets affordability
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

US utilities are being asked to add generation and transmission for data centers and reshored manufacturing at a pace not seen in decades, and APS is at the sharp end of it. Its long-term sales growth guide is 5–7% a year through 2030. The bill for that build-out lands in rate cases, and rate cases are political. APS's filing is explicit about the tension: it proposes rate structures meant to "mitigate cross-subsidization by customer classes that are not directly contributing to the costs caused by significant and concentrated system load growth among large high-load-factor customers such as data centers." How the ACC allocates that cost will set how much growth APS can take on without a residential backlash.

Regulatory lag and the formula-rate experiment
ONGOING HEADWIND

Traditional rate cases use a historical test year. APS's pending case uses calendar 2024 and asks for $306.7M of post-test-year plant, so a fast-growing utility is always earning on last year's balance sheet. The ACC approved formula rates as a policy in 2024, and APS is asking to use one. If adopted, rates would reset each year on the prior 12 months of data. That is good for earned returns, and exactly what RUCO objects to on behalf of customers facing repeated increases. Whichever way it goes, the result will tell investors how Arizona plans to pay for growth for the rest of the decade.

Firm generation is scarce again
EMERGING TAILWIND

APS owns roughly 6,200 MW of capacity, operates Palo Verde (which management calls "the only nuclear plant in the world not on a body of water," running on "100% recycled wastewater"), and plans up to 2 GW of new gas generation plus the Cholla conversion. In a system whose peak demand just beat its prior record by more than 500 MW, owning dispatchable capacity is valuable. For a regulated utility, that value shows up as rate base, and only if the Commission agrees to put it there.
