01 Company Overview

Pinnacle West is a holding company with one job that matters: it owns Arizona Public Service (APS), Arizona's largest electric company, which generates, transmits and sells electricity to about 1.5 million homes and businesses across the state, including part of the Phoenix metro area. APS operates and partly owns the Palo Verde nuclear station. The holding company was set up in 1985 as AZP Group and took the Pinnacle West name and the PNW ticker in 1987. Revenue over the twelve months to September 30, 2026 was $5.71B, net income $661M, diluted EPS $5.32.

A regulated utility doesn't win customers; it's handed a territory and told what it may earn. The equation is rate base × allowed return: APS spends capital on plants, wires and substations, the Arizona Corporation Commission (ACC) adds that spending to the rate base, and customer rates are set to give shareholders an allowed ROE on the equity-financed slice. APS's current ACC allowed ROE is 9.55% on a 51.93% equity layer (the share of the rate base treated as funded by shareholders), set in February 2024. In June 2025 APS filed for a 10.70% ROE and a net increase of $579.5M (13.99%) in customer revenue; by its May 2026 rejoinder (its last written reply) the request had grown to a $692M total revenue requirement, $609M after $83M of transfers from existing bill surcharges, or 14.69% on customer bills. The ACC's own staff recommends a smaller increase ($506M in its final written testimony, a 10.22% bill impact) and an ROE of 9.55%–9.80%, with the lower end if the formula rate described below is adopted (APS's November 2026 investor deck). In May the Arizona Capitol Times reported staff and the Residential Utility Consumer Office (RUCO, the state's consumer advocate) at an ROE "closer to 9%", and testimony from the Attorney General's expert suggesting 6%. Hearings ended July 7, 2026. The ACC says the judge's recommended order is due by late November and the Commission must vote by December 31, with new rates expected in early 2027; the November deck estimates a final decision in December. That vote is the biggest single input to this stock's next five years, more than the weather and more than data centers.

The other half of the story is load growth. Phoenix is adding homes, chip fabs and data centers at once. In Q3 2026 APS's weather-normalized retail sales rose 9.5% year over year (9.6% in Q2), commercial and industrial sales were up 14.5%, and the system set a record peak of 9,166 MW on August 2, up from 8,648 MW the year before (Q3 release). On November 3 management raised its 2026 weather-normalized sales-growth guidance to 8%–10% and set 8%–10% a year through 2030. The committed large-customer pipeline is 5.8 GW, of which about 0.9 GW is being served and about 4.9 GW is still to come, with roughly 20 GW more as an uncommitted opportunity (November 2026 investor deck). More demand needs more plant, which grows the rate base, which only turns into earnings if the Commission lets it into rates at a fair return. So growth is the engine and regulation is the gearbox.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Exclusive service territory Regulated return on rate base Palo Verde nuclear stake Phoenix population & fab growth Transmission network (FERC 10.75%) Essential-service demand

APS has no competitor for most of the customers it serves, so the moat is legal, not commercial. The price of that moat is that someone else sets your price. Each dollar of plant earns only what the ACC allows and only once the ACC recognises it. Arizona's recent record shows how wide the outcomes are: the 2021 order gave APS 8.7% against 10.15% requested and disallowed $215.5M of Four Corners pollution-control spending and APS took the order to court. The dispute was settled in June 2023 at an 8.9% ROE: APS could recover the $215.5M, and the revenue lost over the prior 19 months was spread over four years (Utility Dive). The 2024 order came in at 9.55% against 10.25% requested. So the moat is solid, but the regulator decides how much it pays.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The December vote could swing either way. The parties are far apart: APS asks for 10.70%, staff recommends 9.55%–9.80%, with the lower end if the formula rate is adopted (in May staff and RUCO were reported at "closer to 9%"), and testimony from the Attorney General's expert suggested 6%. Illustration (our round-number assumption, not a company figure; the company's November 2026 investor deck shows an approved ACC rate base of about $10.36B): on a $10B ACC rate base with a 52% equity layer, every 10 basis points of ROE (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point) is about $5.2M of annual net income, or roughly $0.04 a share on 121.2M shares. The 90–115 basis-point gap between the request and staff's range is then worth about $0.4–0.5 a share a year, against a raised 2026 EPS guide of $5.05–5.25.
02
Growth consumes cash. Operating cash flow over the twelve months to September 2026 was $1.83B, capex (capital spending) $2.81B, and free cash flow −$979M before dividends (StockAnalysis, TTM). The gap is funded with debt (total debt including leases $16.97B, debt/equity 2.26; borrowings alone $11.43B) and new shares ($1.0–1.2B planned for 2026–2028, about $920M of it priced under forward sales). Every dollar of rate base growth has to be financed before it earns, and new equity dilutes per-share growth.
03
Affordability politics. A requested increase of 14–16% on customer bills draws a crowd. At the May hearing kickoff, one activist told commissioners a yes vote would be "a death sentence to many of our most vulnerable citizens." Pressure like that is how a well-argued request becomes a 9% outcome.
04
Earnings are up this year, but the rate case sets the next step. 2026 guidance is now $5.05–$5.25 (midpoint $5.15, up from $4.65 before the Q3 print) against FY2025's $5.05. Q3 EPS rose to $3.49 from $3.39 as higher sales, weather and customer growth outran higher depreciation and interest. StockAnalysis's FY2026 consensus average of $5.00 (range $4.80–$5.24) sits below the new guidance midpoint, and its FY2027 average of $5.58 (+11.6% on the FY2026 average) assumes new rates arrive on time and at a reasonable return.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported figures. First, weather. APS's earnings depend heavily on how hot the summer is, which is why guidance is "weather-normalized". The Q3 2026 release describes the quarter as substantially warmer than normal, with residential cooling degree days up 3% on 2025, and lists favorable weather among the drivers of the $21M rise in net income; a cool summer can work the other way. Second, timing within the year. Q3 supplied $3.49 of the $5.20 earned in the first nine months of 2026; Q1 earned $0.27 and Q2 $1.43 (StockAnalysis quarterly figures), and Q4 2025 earned $0.12, so the trailing figure swings with summer. Third, equity dilution. Forward equity sales priced but not yet settled will raise the share count as they settle, so per-share figures lag absolute profit growth. The FY2021 profit margin of 16.27% came from a different regulatory period and is not a guide to today's margin.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $65.05 to the November 9, 2026 settled close of $105.29, PNW gained 61.9% on price (closing prices, dividends not included), with a yield of about 3.5% on top. Most of it came in two stretches, after a flat start. From November 2021 to February 2024 month-ends ranged from $64.51 (September 2022, the low of the window) to $82.82 and finished at $68.33. The stock then climbed to $93.70 by November 2024. Month-ends stayed between $86.96 and $95.25 through 2025, and the second stretch was a 20.6% gain in the first half of 2026 (December 2025 $88.70 to the June 2026 month-end of $107.00, the high of the window). Three weaker months followed, to $93.04 at the end of September (13.0% below June), and then October gained 14.2% to $106.28.

The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 81.0 in April 2026, its most overbought reading in the window, fell to 39.6 in September, and now stands at 61.2 (November's reading uses the partial month). The 10-month moving average is about $101.5, below the current price. The stock has recovered to within 1.6% of its June month-end high and is sitting just ahead of the rate order.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You get a fast-growing service territory: weather-normalized sales +9.5% in Q3, commercial and industrial sales +14.5%, customers +1.8%, a record 9,166 MW peak, a committed large-customer pipeline of 5.8 GW of which only about 0.9 GW is being served, and a guided 8%–10% sales growth a year through 2030. That demand justifies a $10.35B capex plan and rate base growth of about 6.4% a year to ~$15.7B by 2028. If the ACC grants anything near a 10% ROE and adopts the formula rate mechanism, the regulatory lag that has kept the fair-value return at 2.54% starts to close. Consensus FY2027 EPS of $5.58 (+11.6% on the FY2026 average of $5.00) is then achievable, and $105.29 is about 18.9× that, with a 3.5% yield while you wait. Management raised 2026 EPS guidance by $0.50 at both ends of the range in November.

Bear Case

Arizona's regulators granted APS less than it asked in both of the last two rate cases (8.7% vs 10.15% in 2021, later settled at 8.9%; 9.55% vs 10.25% in 2024), and staff's recommended range this time tops out at 9.80%, with the lower end if the formula rate is adopted. A sub-9.55% ROE, no formula rate and a smaller-than-hoped increase would leave 2027 consensus too high. Meanwhile the build-out needs new equity ($1.0–1.2B) and more debt on a balance sheet already at 2.26× debt/equity including leases, with −$979M of TTM free cash flow. At 21.1× FY2026 consensus EPS (20.4× the new guidance midpoint) the stock is not cheap on near-term earnings, and it gained 14.2% in October ahead of the vote. Short interest (shares sold in a bet that the price will fall) stands at 10.3% of shares outstanding.

Top 3 Risks

01
Rate-case outcome. The spread between 10.70% requested and staff's 9.55%–9.80% is worth roughly $0.4–0.5 a share a year on our illustrative $10B / 52% equity assumption. The formula-rate decision matters as much as the headline ROE because it decides how quickly future capex earns.
02
Financing and dilution. Capex of $2.6–2.7B a year against ~$1.8B of operating cash flow means continuous debt and equity issuance. Higher interest rates raise both costs, and higher interest charges were a named offset in both the Q2 and Q3 releases.
03
Large-load concentration and cost allocation. Growth depends on data centers and fabs. If those projects slip, or if the ACC shifts less of the cost onto them than the 47% XHLF request implies, APS either strands capacity or pushes more of the bill onto households, which feeds straight back into risk 01.
Industry Commentary
Load growth meets affordability
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

US utilities are being asked to add generation and transmission for data centers and reshored manufacturing at a pace not seen in decades, and APS is at the sharp end of it. Its long-term sales growth guide is 8–10% a year through 2030 (raised on November 3, 2026). The bill for that build-out lands in rate cases, and rate cases are political. APS's filing addresses the tension: it proposes rate structures meant to keep other customer classes from paying for the costs caused by concentrated load growth from large customers such as data centers. How the ACC allocates that cost will set how much growth APS can take on without a residential backlash.

Regulatory lag and the formula-rate experiment
ONGOING HEADWIND

Traditional rate cases use a historical test year. APS's pending case uses calendar 2024 and asks to include $306.7M of plant finished after that year, so a fast-growing utility is always earning on last year's balance sheet. The ACC approved formula rates as a policy in 2024, and APS is asking to use one. If adopted, rates would reset each year on the prior 12 months of data. That is good for earned returns, and exactly what RUCO objects to on behalf of customers facing repeated increases. Whichever way it goes, the result will tell investors how Arizona plans to pay for growth for the rest of the decade.

Firm generation is scarce again
EMERGING TAILWIND

APS owns or leases about 6.3 GW of generating capacity (November 2026 investor deck), operates Palo Verde, and plans about 2 GW of new gas generation plus the Cholla conversion. In a system whose peak demand just beat its prior record by more than 500 MW, owning power plants that can run when needed is valuable. For a regulated utility, that value shows up as rate base, and only if the Commission agrees to put it there.
