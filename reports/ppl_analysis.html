01 Company Overview

In a card room the house never plays a hand. It takes a rake — a slice of every pot, fixed in advance, whoever wins. A regulated utility is the closest thing the stock market has to that seat, with one twist: the rake is set by a state commission, not by the house. PPL delivers electricity and gas to roughly 3.6 million customers through three regulated franchises — LG&E and KU in Kentucky, PPL Electric Utilities in Pennsylvania and Rhode Island Energy. It builds poles, wires, pipes and power plants; the regulator adds that spend to the rate base; and the utility is allowed to earn a set allowed return on equity on it. Rate base was $29.0B at year-end 2025 and management's plan, updated on November 6, 2026, grows it about 10% a year on average from 2026 through 2030 on a $25B capital plan for 2027–2030.

The company in its current shape is only five years old. PPL sold its U.K. network business, Western Power Distribution, to National Grid for £7.8B on June 14, 2021 (about $10.4B net proceeds), and bought Narragansett Electric — renamed Rhode Island Energy — in May 2022. FY2021 — the year of the sale — shows a $1.48B net loss, and FY2022, the first year with Rhode Island Energy, shows revenue up 36.6%: old per-share history describes a different company. Since then the story has been simple arithmetic — ongoing EPS of $1.60, $1.69, $1.81 for 2023–2025, guided to $1.92–$1.96 for 2026 after the November 6 narrowing — plus one new variable: data centers. PPL Electric counts 35.3 GW of data-center projects in advanced stages, 11.4 GW of it under signed service agreements. The tension in 2026 is that three commissions each handed PPL less than it asked for: the stock fell to a $31.97 close on September 24, then recovered to $35.67, still 8.5% below its February month-end close of $38.98. With the third-quarter results, management raised its long-term earnings growth target to 7%–9% a year and its capital plan to $25B.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Franchised service monopolies Regulated return on $29.0B rate base ~60% near real-time capex recovery Future test years in all 3 states Large-load tariffs with minimum bills A- rating at S&P

Nobody competes with PPL to string a wire down a street in Allentown or Louisville. The moat is legal, not commercial: a franchise territory plus a regulator who is obliged to let the utility recover prudent costs and earn a fair return. Rates are set on future test years in all three jurisdictions, and the company says about 60% of the capital plan is recovered close to real time. The new data-center tariffs add a second layer — ten- to fifteen-year minimum terms, minimum-demand billing and collateral — designed so that if a hyperscaler walks, the stranded cost lands on the hyperscaler rather than on households. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: the regulator that guarantees the rake also sets it, and 2026 showed all three are willing to cut it.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Affordability politics. PUC Chairman Steve DeFrank on the Pennsylvania decision: "This proceeding reflects one of the central challenges facing utility regulation today – balancing the investments necessary to maintain a safe and reliable electric system with the affordability concerns facing households and businesses." Rhode Island kept the 2018-era ROE and paired the increase with $163M of bill credits; Kentucky refused the earnings-sharing mechanism. A 10.3% rate-base CAGR means customer bills must keep rising, and every rising bill is a political event.
02
Negative free cash flow, by design. TTM operating cash flow was $2.60B against $4.69B of capex — free cash flow of −$2.09B (StockAnalysis), before the $0.63B of dividends paid in the first nine months of 2026. The updated plan needs about $3.0B of new equity on top of more than $1.5B already executed through forward sales in 2025 and 2026, plus new debt. Equity issuance dilutes per-share growth; the stock price at issuance decides by how much.
03
Earned below allowed. Consolidated GAAP ROE is 8.88% (TTM) against allowed ROEs of 9.275%–10.05%. The two are not directly comparable — allowed ROEs apply to rate-base equity in each jurisdiction, consolidated ROE to the whole balance sheet — but the gap is the regulatory-lag cost investors are paying while new rates catch up.
04
Data-center conversion risk. 35.3 GW in "advanced stages" is not 35.3 GW of load. About a third (11.4 GW) is under signed service agreements, and two data centers that began service in Q2 2026 ramp to about 2 GW only by 2031 (Q2 2026 update). The QTS agreement for Invitium is a nonbinding term sheet, and its combined-cycle units are not expected until 2032–2033.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The rate base is contracted growth: $29.0B at year-end 2025 growing about 10% a year on average from 2026 through 2030 under the November 2026 plan, with about 60% of spend recovered close to real time and every jurisdiction on future test years. The company says it has consistently achieved at least the midpoint of its 6%–8% EPS growth target — ongoing EPS of $1.60, $1.69 and $1.81 for 2023–2025, reported as 8.1%, 7.0% and 7.1% growth — and on November 6 raised its target to 7%–9% a year through at least 2030, with growth expected in the top half of that range, plus a 3.20% yield growing about 4%–6% (at the low end). The 2026 rate cases were trimmed, not rejected: all three delivered increases. On top sits optionality the plan doesn't count — $12B–$16B of Kentucky generation and Invitium JV capex, and a 35.3 GW Pennsylvania data-center pipeline. At about 18x forward earnings and 8.5% below its February month-end high, the market is paying for the rake and getting the pipeline for free.

Bear Case

A 10.3% rate-base CAGR is only worth something if regulators let you earn on it, and in 2026 all three said "less." Kentucky trimmed the settlement's rates and refused the sharing mechanism, Rhode Island held a 2018-era 9.275% and piled on $163M of bill credits, Pennsylvania trimmed the ask by $81M. Consolidated ROE is 8.88%. Free cash flow is −$2.09B and the plan needs about $3.0B of new equity on top of more than $1.5B already executed, so every dollar of rate-base growth is partly paid for by dilution. Customer bills are the product's price, and affordability is now the political story in every state PPL operates in. At 20.5x trailing GAAP earnings, a utility whose allowed returns are drifting down is not obviously cheap — and the average analyst target of $40.22 sits only 12.8% above the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Allowed-return compression. The allowed ROE is the rake. Kentucky at 9.775%, Rhode Island at 9.275% and an undisclosed Pennsylvania distribution ROE set the base for the next rate cycle; a lower rake on a bigger base still grows earnings, but slower than the plan assumes if the trend continues.
02
Financing and dilution. The updated plan puts $25B of capex into 2027–2030 and needs about $3.0B of new equity on top of more than $1.5B already executed through forward sales, along with new debt. Higher interest rates raise the debt cost between rate cases; a lower share price raises the dilution cost of the equity.
03
Data-center demand that doesn't show up. 35.3 GW in advanced stages, 11.4 GW signed. The large-load tariffs protect against stranded network costs, but they do not protect the growth story that supports the share price’s premium to earnings if hyperscaler plans slip.
Industry Commentary
Affordability is the new regulatory constraint
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Utility investors have long treated the allowed ROE as a slow-moving constant. 2026 made it a live variable. PPL faced three commissions in one year and got three outcomes below its request: Kentucky trimmed the settlement's rates and removed the earnings-sharing mechanism; Pennsylvania cut the revenue ask by more than $80M; Rhode Island declined any ROE increase and paired the rate rise with bill credits large enough to fully offset the electric increase over twelve months, per local reporting. The PUC chairman in Pennsylvania put the tension in one line — "This proceeding reflects one of the central challenges facing utility regulation today – balancing the investments necessary to maintain a safe and reliable electric system with the affordability concerns facing households and businesses." When the rake-setter starts worrying about the players' bankrolls, the rake stops going up.

Data centers turn utilities into growth stocks
EMERGING TAILWIND

PPL's growth pipeline is now measured in gigawatts: 35.3 GW of data-center projects in advanced stages in Pennsylvania (up 3.5 GW, about 11%, in a quarter and up for eleven straight quarters, per the company), 14.7 GW of potential load in Kentucky. The industry's answer to "who pays if they leave" is the large-load tariff — PPL now has one in each of its two big states, with ten- or fifteen-year minimum terms and minimum-demand billing. The strategic twist is Invitium: a joint venture with Blackstone Infrastructure (PPL 51%) to build generation for Pennsylvania data centers, outside PPL Electric's regulated wires business. It holds reservations for 5 GW of gas combined-cycle turbines and on November 6 signed a nonbinding term sheet with QTS for about 2 GW over a 20-year initial term; its combined-cycle units are not expected until 2032–2033.

The capex super-cycle has to be financed
ONGOING HEADWIND

PPL's capex rose from $1.97B in FY2021 to $4.03B in FY2025 and $4.69B on a trailing basis; operating cash flow went from $2.27B to $2.60B over the same span. PPL closes the gap with debt, equity forwards and hybrid "equity units." For shareholders the question is not whether the capex earns a return — regulation broadly guarantees that it can — but whether the return on the new equity exceeds its cost. With allowed ROEs of roughly 9.3%–10% and a stock trading at 1.75x book, the arithmetic still works; at a much lower price relative to book value it would work much less well.
