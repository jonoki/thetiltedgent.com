01 Company Overview

Public Storage is the largest owner and operator of self-storage in the United States. After the July 22, 2026 close of the National Storage Affiliates (NSA) merger and the September 1 close of PS Canada, it owned or operated 4,764 self-storage facilities with about 339 million rentable square feet at September 30, 2026, across 42 U.S. states, Puerto Rico and Canada. Revenue for the twelve months to September 2026 was $5.04B, up 5.2% (our sum of the last four quarters against the four before); fiscal 2025 revenue was $4.83B, up 2.5% from $4.72B in FY2024. It ranks #197 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation at $63.07B. It also holds a 35% common equity interest in Shurgard, a self-storage operator in Western Europe.

Why EPS is the wrong lens here. A REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust — a company that must distribute most of its taxable income to shareholders and pays little corporate tax in return) charges depreciation against buildings that in practice hold or gain value, so GAAP earnings systematically understate cash generation; that is exactly why the industry reports FFO instead. In Q3 2026 Public Storage earned $1.69 of GAAP EPS and $4.34 of Core FFO per share. GAAP earnings were under 40% of Core FFO because the quarter carried $412.6M of depreciation and amortisation (up from $290.4M a year earlier as the NSA and PS Canada buildings came on) and $114.6M of merger and integration costs, which Core FFO leaves out.

The central debate on this page is narrower than "is storage a good business." Storage leases are month-to-month, which means pricing resets in weeks rather than years — in both directions. From 2022 through 2025 that mechanism ran against the company: housing turnover collapsed, new-customer street rates fell, and same-store revenue rolled over. Q3 2026 is the second quarter in a row in which the mechanism points the right way: management said average move-in rents rose 9% year over year, the strongest since the second quarter of 2022, and same-store revenue (from the facilities it has owned and run steadily in both years) grew 0.5% after falling 0.6% in Q2. The caution is in the same call: management said October move-in rent growth was not quite 9%, move-in volumes were modestly lower and occupancy roughly flat. Whether that is an inflection or a bounce is still the question, and it is decided mostly by housing transactions, not by anything management controls.

A 35% common equity interest in Shurgard Self Storage Limited — 338 facilities across seven Western European countries (Q3 2026 release). Because the stake is non-controlling it is accounted for on the equity method: none of Shurgard's revenue appears in Public Storage's revenue, only its share of Shurgard's earnings. Two honest readings. The bull reading is that the stake is a cheap way to own the growth of European storage — a market with far less storage space per person than the U.S. — and that its value in the accounts says nothing about what it is worth on the market. The bear reading is simpler: it is a small contributor next to a $5B-a-year revenue base, and it does not move the thesis in either direction today.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Largest U.S. Owner 4,764 Facilities 339M Sq Ft Brand Search Traffic Net Debt/EBITDA 3.4× $4.1B Liquidity 3.6% Cost of Debt Irreplaceable Infill Sites

The moat is the cost of capital, not the buildings. Self-storage is a commodity product — a concrete box with a roll-up door — and no operator has a durable product advantage. What Public Storage has is the ability to buy those boxes more cheaply than anyone else can. At September 30, 2026 it carried net debt to EBITDA of 3.4× (3.0× a year earlier; 4.7× counting its preferred shares, which pay a fixed dividend and rank behind the debt), a weighted average interest rate of 3.6% (3.0% a year earlier), about $4.1B of liquidity, and credit ratings of A2 (Moody's) and A (S&P), unchanged. In a year when same-store results were flat to negative it raised $6.2B of debt and $6.0B of common share and unit issuance or committed issuance and bought a public competitor outright, so leverage is higher than a year ago, though still moderate. A company that can pay for acquisitions without straining its balance sheet can out-buy its peers in a slow market. The second advantage is the brand, which captures a disproportionate share of unbranded "storage near me" search, which matters enormously for a product bought under time pressure by someone in the middle of moving house.

Key Vulnerabilities

→
Month-to-month leases cut both ways. The feature that let storage reprice upward almost instantly in 2021 is the same feature that let it reprice downward almost instantly from 2023. There is no contractual lease term to hide behind and no rent escalator. Realised rent per occupied square foot is $22.29, up only 0.2%, and the portfolio has no mechanism to protect it other than demand holding up. Management said October move-in rent growth was running below Q3's 9%.
→
Demand is downstream of housing turnover, which is depressed. Existing-home sales ran at a 3.99 million seasonally adjusted annual rate in September 2026, up 1.5% from August but down 2.2% from a year earlier (National Association of Realtors, October 13, 2026). People rent storage when they move: Fisher said about 45% of move-in activity comes from moves. Fewer moves, fewer new customers, and the ones already in place are the only pricing lever left. Public Storage does not control this variable at all.
→
Costs keep rising whether or not rents do. Same-store direct cost of operations rose 2.0% in Q3 against revenue up 0.5%; only a fall in indirect costs (down 11.2%) kept NOI in step with revenue. Fisher said property taxes have been rising about 3–4% a year over the last several years. A flat-revenue year is a flat-to-down-NOI year, because wages, insurance and property taxes do not fall when street rates do.
→
Low barriers to new supply. Storage is among the cheapest and fastest commercial property types to build, so any period of strong rents invites competing development in the same submarkets. On the call, Fisher said new supply is still pressuring some Sunbelt markets, and Boyle said strong demand should absorb it over the next several years, even on the west coast of Florida. The company's own answer is to be the developer — a 4.6M sq ft, $788.9M pipeline — but that is a defence against someone else's supply, not an elimination of it.
→
Integration and issuance risk. The NSA merger added thousands of stores at once, about 14,000 inherited units need clean-up (call), and roughly $6.0B of equity and units were issued or committed this year. Q3 GAAP earnings per share fell 35.5% to $1.69 on integration costs and depreciation, and Core FFO per share rose only 0.7% even though Core FFO in dollars rose 8.2%: the share count grew nearly as fast. Synergies of $110–130M are guided over three to four years.

Notable one-off items. Three distortions matter. (1) FFO versus Core FFO. Q3 FFO fell 12.0% to $3.81 while Core FFO rose 0.7% to $4.34; the gap is mostly $114.6M of transaction and integration costs that Core strips out. Anyone quoting the 12% decline is quoting a merger bill. (2) The NSA merger closed on July 22 and PS Canada on September 1, 2026 — so Q3 is the first blended quarter (about two months of NSA, one month of PS Canada), and year-over-year comparisons of total revenue (+11.6%) are not like-for-like; the same-store pool excludes all of it. The 126.2% GAAP payout ratio is a fourth artefact: on Core FFO the payout is 70.6%.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 close of $327.38 to the October 30, 2026 close of $324.77, PSA lost 0.8% in price over the window. Add dividends — $68.15 per share of ordinary and special dividends went ex over the window, including a one-off $13.15 in July 2022 (Yahoo Finance dividend history) — and the total return with dividends reinvested is +24.0%, or about 4.5% a year (Yahoo Finance adjusted close, Nov 30, 2021 → Oct 30, 2026; computed). That is the whole five-year outcome for the largest owner of self-storage in America: you were paid your dividends and the price went nowhere. The path inside that flat line matters more than the line. The stock peaked at $390.28 in March 2022, at the top of the post-pandemic storage boom and just as the rate-hiking cycle began, then fell 38.8% to $238.71 in October 2023 as long rates peaked and street rates rolled over simultaneously — the double hit that defines a rate-sensitive business with month-to-month pricing. It rallied 52.4% to $363.87 by September 2024 on the first rate-cut expectations, then ground 28.7% lower to $259.50 in December 2025 as the operating trough deepened, and has recovered to $324.77, the highest month-end close since November 2024.

Relative performance. PSA's 52-week price change is +15.63% (closes, Oct 30, 2025 → Oct 30, 2026), its beta is 0.93, and a total return of about 4.5% a year over five years is modest for the largest company in its industry. Against its own sector, the read is clearer and less flattering — in Q3 2026 Extra Space grew same-store revenue 2.7% and same-store NOI 4.6% and CubeSmart 1.9% and 2.1%, while Public Storage grew both 0.5%; Extra Space grew Core FFO 4.3%, CubeSmart adjusted FFO 1.5% and Public Storage 0.7%. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The mechanism, stated plainly. Self-storage leases are month-to-month. There is no lease term, no escalator, no expiry schedule — a customer can leave at thirty days' notice and a new customer pays whatever the market clears at today. That single structural fact explains almost everything about this business. On the way up it is the best pricing model in real estate: in 2021 storage repriced upward within weeks while office and industrial landlords waited years for leases to roll. On the way down it is the worst: from 2023 onward there was nothing between falling street rates and reported revenue. Public Storage's realised annual rent per occupied square foot is $22.29, up 0.2%; average occupancy is 92.2%, up 0.3 points. The portfolio is fuller and no longer getting cheaper, which is what produces same-store revenue of +0.5% and same-store NOI of +0.5% even though direct costs rose 2.0%.

The gap that matters: street rates versus in-place rents. Two prices operate in a storage portfolio at once. The street rate is what a new customer pays today — highly visible, highly elastic, and the number that collapsed after 2022. The in-place rent is what the existing customer is paying, which drifts upward through periodic increases and is anchored by inertia: the cost of renting a truck and moving your possessions again generally exceeds a rent increase, so customers stay. For most of 2023–2025 in-place rents sat meaningfully above street rates, meaning the portfolio was defended by customers who would not pay today's asking price if they were shopping today. That is a fragile equilibrium — it holds until move-outs accelerate. The Q3 2026 data says the street rate has turned: management said average move-in rents were up 9% year over year, the strongest since the second quarter of 2022, and Boyle said new-customer pricing improved year over year for a second straight quarter. Fisher also said that with new-customer pricing stronger, replacing a tenant who leaves costs less, which gives more room to raise rents on the customers it already has. The convergence of street rate and in-place rent is what has to happen before same-store revenue growth becomes more than a rounding error, and October's move-in rent growth, said to be below 9%, is the first test.

Where the housing cycle actually sits. The demand driver is not the economy in general, it is transactions. People rent storage when they move — downsizing, divorcing, relocating, renovating, waiting between houses. Fisher said about 45% of move-in activity comes from moves. Existing-home sales ran at a 3.99 million seasonally adjusted annual rate in September 2026, up 1.5% from August and down 2.2% from a year earlier, with 4.8 months of supply and a median price of $417,500 (National Association of Realtors, October 13, 2026). Management said it does not expect a meaningful impact from higher mortgage rates or less affordability. Note what this means for the bull case: Public Storage's operating improvement in 2026 has come without help from housing turnover. Move-in rents and same-store revenue turned while transactions were flat to lower, which suggests supply absorption and pricing discipline rather than a demand recovery. If housing turnover ever normalises toward its pre-2022 pace, that is upside not currently in the numbers. If it does not, the recovery has to come from the supply side.

The balance sheet is the strategy, and it is being used. Quantify it: net debt to EBITDA of 3.4× (3.0× a year earlier), total indebtedness of $12.2B against a $63.07B market capitalisation and roughly $74.7B enterprise value (our calculation: market cap plus $11.63B of net debt per stockanalysis.com), a weighted average interest rate of 3.6% (3.0% a year earlier), only $650M — 5.3% of debt — maturing in the fourth quarter of 2026, and about $4.1B of liquidity. Add the $4.72B of preferred equity on the September 30 balance sheet (up from $4.35B at year-end) — perpetual, never repaid in instalments, and paid ahead of common shareholders but behind lenders — and net debt plus preferred is 4.7× EBITDA per the release. The cost of money has moved: the $500M of ten-year notes priced on October 29 carry a 5.600% coupon against the 3.6% average on existing debt, so each refinancing raises interest cost. What the balance sheet bought in this cycle is the point: Fisher said the company has deployed or committed over $12.5 billion this year across acquisitions, development and lending, at a weighted average going-in yield (first-year income as a share of the price paid) in the low 5s and a yield in the high 6s once the properties are fully leased, funded without raising leverage.

The dividend has been held flat, and that is a choice. $3.00 per quarter, $12.00 per year, unchanged since the step-up from $2.00 in the first quarter of 2023 — fifteen straight quarters at $3.00 through September 2026 (Yahoo Finance dividend history), three and a half years without an increase. On Core FFO the payout is 70.6%, which is comfortable; the headline 126.2% GAAP payout ratio is an artefact of measuring a cash distribution against a depreciation-laden earnings figure and should be ignored. The yield is 3.69%. So the flat dividend is not a stress signal — it is a capital-allocation statement. Retained cash flow funded the development pipeline and part of the acquisition programme. The test is arithmetic: if $110–130M of NSA synergies, $355–363M of non-same-store NOI and a $788.9M development and expansion book compound faster than the dividend growth a shareholder gave up, holding it flat was correct. If they do not, shareholders financed empire-building out of their own distribution.

Valuation. Be explicit about the denominator, because it changes the answer. On trailing GAAP EPS of $9.51 the stock trades at 34.2× — a number that charges the company for depreciating buildings that are not depreciating, and which no REIT investor uses. On the midpoint of full-year Core FFO guidance ($17.00), it trades at 19.1× P/FFO. That is the number that matters. Against peers on their own guidance midpoints — treat as estimates — Extra Space sits near 17.9× and CubeSmart near 16.5×. Public Storage therefore trades at roughly a 7–16% premium to both, while posting the weakest same-store revenue growth, the weakest same-store NOI growth and the slowest Core FFO growth of the three. The premium is defensible only on balance sheet and scale: moderate leverage, A-grade credit ratings, 4,764 facilities, and a demonstrated ability to buy competitors at the bottom of a cycle. The bear's version is that you are paying a premium multiple for the slowest-growing asset in the sector and calling the balance sheet a moat. Both descriptions are of the same set of facts, and the analyst ratings reflect that stalemate exactly: 16 Holds out of 22, average target $331.83, nobody selling and almost nobody buying.

Bull Case

The operating trough is behind it and the leading indicator turned: move-in rents were up about 9% year over year in Q3, the strongest since the second quarter of 2022, same-store revenue grew 0.5% and same-store NOI grew 0.5%, with occupancy up. Management raised full-year Core FFO guidance a second time, to $16.90–17.10, and raised the same-store revenue and NOI guides. Meanwhile the balance sheet did what a moderately levered balance sheet is supposed to do in a downturn: it bought National Storage Affiliates, added a US$1.2B Canadian platform, and funded both — the CFO said — in full. Ancillary revenue grew 31.5% in the quarter. If housing turnover ever normalises from a 3.99M-unit annual pace, none of that upside is in today's numbers.

Bear Case

Same-store revenue and NOI are up only 0.5%, Core FFO per share rose just 0.7% while Extra Space grew 4.3%, and GAAP EPS fell 35.5% — yet PSA trades at 19.1× Core FFO versus roughly 16.5–17.9× for peers. The guidance raise leans on acquisitions and lease-up, was funded with roughly $6.0B of newly issued shares and units, and new ten-year debt now costs 5.6% against a 3.6% average. Management said October move-in rent growth was below Q3's 9% with volumes lower and occupancy flat. The dividend has been flat for three and a half years at a 3.69% yield. The demand driver is housing turnover, which is running below last year, and the pricing model is month-to-month, so there is no contractual protection if street rates roll over again. Five years of ownership has delivered about 4.5% a year in total return.

  Industry Commentary
Housing Turnover Is the Sector's Demand Curve
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Self-storage does not sell space, it sells a solution to a transition — moving, downsizing, renovating, splitting a household. That makes existing-home sales the sector's demand curve, and it is running at a 3.99 million seasonally adjusted annual rate as of September 2026, up 1.5% month over month and down 2.2% year over year, with 4.8 months of supply. Every operator in the industry — Public Storage at +0.5% same-store revenue in Q3, CubeSmart at +1.9%, Extra Space at +2.7% — is operating inside the same constraint, and the spread between them is execution and portfolio geography rather than demand. When transaction volumes normalise, storage revenue improves everywhere at once and no management team gets credit for it. Until then, the sector's growth has to be bought.
Consolidation Has Replaced Organic Growth
ONGOING HEADWIND
With same-store growth near zero at the largest operator, scale is being bought rather than earned. Public Storage acquired National Storage Affiliates outright in an all-stock deal that closed July 22, 2026, taking the listed U.S. self-storage universe from four major names to three, and added a US$1.2 billion Canadian platform on September 1. Extra Space is expanding through third-party management — 1,963 third-party-managed stores at September 30 — which buys share without buying assets, and announced that Noah Springer will succeed Joe Margolis as CEO on January 1, 2027. The strategic logic is sound: a fragmented industry with low barriers to construction rewards whoever has the cheapest capital during the downturn. The shareholder consequence is less comfortable — growth delivered through share issuance means the per-share numbers improve only if the synergies land, and Public Storage's are guided over three to four years.
Month-to-Month Pricing Turns First — In Both Directions
EMERGING TAILWIND
The absence of lease terms makes storage the fastest-reporting property type in real estate: the cycle shows up in street rates months before it shows up in reported revenue. That is why the most important number in Public Storage's Q3 2026 call is not the +0.5% same-store revenue but the 9% year-over-year rise in average move-in rents, the strongest since the second quarter of 2022. Bull framing: new-customer pricing is the leading indicator, it has now improved for two straight quarters, and reported revenue follows with a lag measured in quarters. At 19.1× Core FFO with moderate leverage, that is a reasonable entry into an inflection. Bear framing: the same mechanism means there is nothing underneath you if it reverses. Management said October's move-in rent growth was below 9% with lower volumes, and housing transactions are still falling year over year; you are paying a premium to faster-growing peers to find out which it is.
