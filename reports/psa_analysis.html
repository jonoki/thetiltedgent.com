01 Company Overview

Public Storage is the largest owner and operator of self-storage in the United States and, after the July 2026 close of the National Storage Affiliates merger, operates a combined platform of over 4,500 locations and more than 327 million rentable square feet. FY2025 revenue was $4.83B, up 2.5% from $4.72B in FY2024; the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $4.91B, up 3.2%. It ranks roughly #455 globally by market capitalisation at $58.64B. It also holds a 35% common equity interest in Shurgard, the largest self-storage platform in Western Europe.

Why EPS is the wrong lens here. A REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust — a company that must distribute most of its taxable income to shareholders and pays little corporate tax in return) charges depreciation against buildings that in practice hold or gain value, so GAAP earnings systematically understate cash generation; that is exactly why the industry reports FFO instead. In Q2 2026 Public Storage earned $2.55 of GAAP EPS and $4.17 of Core FFO per share — a 63% gap that is almost entirely depreciation on concrete boxes that have not depreciated.

The central debate on this page is narrower than "is storage a good business." Storage leases are month-to-month, which means pricing resets in weeks rather than years — in both directions. From 2022 through 2025 that mechanism ran against the company: housing turnover collapsed, new-customer street rates fell, and same-store revenue rolled over. Q2 2026 is the first quarter in which the mechanism appears to be turning: move-in rents were up 1.6% year over year — the first time since 2021 that both new move-in rates and occupancy were up together. Whether that is an inflection or a bounce is the whole question, and it is decided by housing transactions, not by anything management controls.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Largest U.S. Owner 4,500+ Locations 327M+ Sq Ft Brand Search Traffic Net Debt/EBITDA 2.9× $3.8B Liquidity 3.3% Cost of Debt Irreplaceable Infill Sites

The moat is the cost of capital, not the buildings. Self-storage is a commodity product — a concrete box with a roll-up door — and no operator has a durable product advantage. What Public Storage has is the ability to buy those boxes more cheaply than anyone else can. It carries net debt to EBITDA of 2.9×, down from 3.1× a year earlier, against a REIT sector where 5–6× is unremarkable; its weighted average interest rate is 3.3%; and it has $3.8B of liquidity available. In a year when the industry's operating fundamentals were negative, it executed $5.9B of debt capital markets activity and $6.0B of share and unit issuance and bought a public competitor outright. That is what the balance sheet is for: a low-levered REIT does not out-earn its peers in good times, it out-buys them in bad ones. The second-order moat is demand aggregation — the brand captures a disproportionate share of unbranded "storage near me" search, which matters enormously for a product bought under time pressure by someone in the middle of moving house.

Key Vulnerabilities

→
Month-to-month leases cut both ways. The feature that let storage reprice upward almost instantly in 2021 is the same feature that let it reprice downward almost instantly from 2023. There is no contractual lease term to hide behind, no rent escalator, no weighted average lease expiry. Realised rent per occupied square foot fell to $21.89 from $22.06 and the portfolio has no mechanism to stop that other than demand returning.
→
Demand is downstream of housing turnover, which is depressed. Existing-home sales ran at a 4.06 million seasonally adjusted annual rate in July 2026, down 1.7% month over month and up only 0.7% year over year. People rent storage when they move. Fewer moves, fewer new customers, and the ones already in place are the only pricing lever left. Public Storage does not control this variable at all.
→
Negative operating leverage in a fixed-cost box. Direct cost of operations rose 4.3% against revenue down 0.6%, turning a small revenue decline into a 2.2% NOI decline. Property taxes, insurance and payroll do not fall when street rates do. A flat-revenue year is a down-NOI year by construction.
→
Low barriers to new supply. Storage is among the cheapest and fastest commercial property types to build, so any period of strong rents invites competing development in the same submarkets. The company's own answer is to be the developer — a 4.0M sq ft, $691.7M pipeline — but that is a defence against someone else's supply, not an elimination of it.
→
Integration and issuance risk. The NSA merger added 1,100 stores and 575,000 units at once, and roughly $6.0B of equity and units were issued in the period. Synergies of $110–130M are guided over three to four years — a slow payback against immediate dilution of the per-share numbers that FFO is measured in.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $332.18 to the September 4, 2026 close of $302.01, PSA lost 9.1% in price over five years. Add dividends — roughly $55 per share across the window, computed from reported annual DPS of $8.00 (2021, part), $8.00 (2022), $12.00 (2023, 2024, 2025) and $9.00 year-to-date 2026 — and the total return is approximately +7.5%, or about 1.5% a year (est., computed, not sourced). That is the entire five-year outcome for the largest owner of self-storage in America: you were paid your dividend and gave back most of it in price. The path inside that flat line matters more than the line. The stock peaked at $390.28 in March 2022, at the top of the post-pandemic storage boom and just as the rate-hiking cycle began, then fell 38.8% to $238.71 in October 2023 as long rates peaked and street rates rolled over simultaneously — the double hit that defines a rate-sensitive business with month-to-month pricing. It rallied 52.4% to $363.87 by September 2024 on the first rate-cut expectations, then ground 28.7% lower to $259.50 in December 2025 as the operating trough deepened, and has recovered to $302.01 today.

Relative performance. A verified S&P 500 and real-estate-sector total return over the identical five-year window could not be sourced from the pages fetched and is therefore not stated. What can be said from data on this page: PSA's 52-week price change is +3.51%, its beta is 0.94, and a roughly 1.5% annualised five-year total return is a poor absolute outcome for a period in which the broad U.S. equity market rose substantially. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The mechanism, stated plainly. Self-storage leases are month-to-month. There is no lease term, no escalator, no expiry schedule — a customer can leave at thirty days' notice and a new customer pays whatever the market clears at today. That single structural fact explains almost everything about this business. On the way up it is the best pricing model in real estate: in 2021 storage repriced upward within weeks while office and industrial landlords waited years for leases to roll. On the way down it is the worst: from 2023 onward there was nothing between falling street rates and reported revenue. Public Storage's realised annual rent per occupied square foot is $21.89, down 0.8%; occupancy is 92.5%, up 0.2 points. The portfolio is fuller and cheaper, which is precisely what an operator does when it chooses volume over rate — and precisely what produces same-store revenue of −0.6% and same-store NOI of −2.2% when direct costs are simultaneously up 4.3%.

The gap that matters: street rates versus in-place rents. Two prices operate in a storage portfolio at once. The street rate is what a new customer pays today — highly visible, highly elastic, and the number that collapsed after 2022. The in-place rent is what the existing customer is paying, which drifts upward through periodic increases and is anchored by inertia: the cost of renting a truck and moving your possessions again generally exceeds a rent increase, so customers stay. For most of 2023–2025 in-place rents sat meaningfully above street rates, meaning the portfolio was defended by customers who would not pay today's asking price if they were shopping today. That is a fragile equilibrium — it holds until move-outs accelerate. The Q2 2026 data says it held and is now improving from both sides: move-in rents rose 1.6% year over year after falling 2.4% in Q1, June alone ran +4%, and move-out activity fell 8%. Fisher's framing was that this was "the first time since 2021 that both new move-in rates and occupancy were up on a year-over-year basis." The convergence of street rate and in-place rent is what has to happen before same-store revenue turns positive, and the company has put a date on it: Q4 2026.

Where the housing cycle actually sits. The demand driver is not the economy in general, it is transactions. People rent storage when they move — downsizing, divorcing, relocating, renovating, waiting between houses. Existing-home sales ran at a 4.06 million seasonally adjusted annual rate in July 2026, down 1.7% from June and up only 0.7% year over year, with 4.6 months of inventory and a median price of $434,100. NAR's chief economist described sales as "remarkably stable, even amid the rising mortgage rate environment of the past few months" — stability at a depressed level is exactly the environment that produces flat storage revenue. Note what this means for the bull case: Public Storage's operating improvement in Q2 2026 came without help from housing turnover. The move-in rate inflection happened while transactions were roughly flat, which suggests supply absorption and pricing discipline rather than a demand recovery. If housing turnover ever normalises toward its pre-2022 pace, that is upside not currently in the numbers. If it does not, the recovery has to come entirely from the supply side.

The balance sheet is the strategy. Quantify it: net debt to EBITDA of 2.9× (down from 3.1×), total debt of $10.3B against a $58.64B market capitalisation and $68.56B enterprise value, a weighted average interest rate of 3.3%, only $650M — 6.3% of total debt — maturing in 2026, and $3.8B of available liquidity. Most equity REITs run 5–6× net debt to EBITDA; Public Storage runs roughly half that. Add the $4.35 billion of preferred equity outstanding — perpetual, non-amortising, senior to the common in the capital stack but junior to the debt — and total leverage including preferred is approximately 4.2× EBITDA (computed), still conservative. The preferred is the underappreciated piece: it is permanent capital that costs a fixed coupon and never matures, which is an excellent instrument to own when rates fall and an expensive one when they do not. What the balance sheet bought in this cycle is the point: while the operating numbers were negative, the company completed $5.9B of debt-market activity, issued $6.0B of shares and units, acquired a listed competitor for $10.5B, agreed a $1.2B Canadian platform, and closed $454.9M of individual assets. That is only possible from a position of underleverage.

The dividend has been held flat, and that is a choice. $3.00 per quarter, $12.00 per year, unchanged since the step-up from $8.00 in 2023 — three and a half years without an increase, with consensus forecasting FY2026 at $12.02 (+0.2%). On Core FFO the payout is 71%, which is comfortable; the headline 114.5% GAAP payout ratio is an artefact of measuring a cash distribution against a depreciation-laden earnings figure and should be ignored. The yield is 3.97%, below CubeSmart's 5.37% and Extra Space's 4.65%. So the flat dividend is not a stress signal — it is a capital-allocation statement. Retained cash flow funded the development pipeline and part of the acquisition programme. The test is arithmetic: if $110–130M of NSA synergies, $343–357M of non-same-store NOI and a $691.7M development book compound faster than the ~4% a dividend investor gave up, holding it flat was correct. If they do not, shareholders financed empire-building out of their own distribution.

Valuation. Be explicit about the denominator, because it changes the answer. On trailing GAAP EPS of $10.48 the stock trades at 28.8× — a number that charges the company for depreciating buildings that are not depreciating, and which no REIT investor uses. On the midpoint of full-year Core FFO guidance ($16.90), it trades at 17.9× P/FFO. That is the number that matters. Against peers — annualising a single quarter, so treat as estimates — Extra Space sits near 16.2× and CubeSmart near 15.7×. Public Storage therefore trades at roughly a 10–14% premium to both, while posting the weakest same-store revenue, the weakest same-store NOI and the only declining Core FFO of the three. The premium is defensible only on balance sheet and scale: 2.9× leverage, 3.3% cost of debt, 4,500+ locations, and demonstrated ability to buy competitors at the bottom of a cycle. The bear's version is that you are paying a premium multiple for the slowest-growing asset in the sector and calling the balance sheet a moat. Both descriptions are of the same set of facts, and the analyst tape reflects that stalemate exactly: 16 Holds out of 22, average target $336.28, nobody selling and almost nobody buying.

Bull Case

The operating trough is behind it and the leading indicator already turned: move-in rents +1.6% year over year in Q2 (June +4%) after −2.4% in Q1, move-outs down 8%, and occupancy up — the first time since 2021 that rate and occupancy rose together. Management has guided same-store revenue to turn positive in Q4 2026 and raised full-year Core FFO guidance to $16.75–17.05. Meanwhile the balance sheet did what a 2.9×-levered balance sheet is supposed to do in a downturn: it bought National Storage Affiliates for $10.5B, added a $1.2B Canadian platform, and closed $454.9M of assets — all funded at a 3.3% weighted average cost of debt. Ancillary revenue is compounding at 12% on a customer base that just grew by 575,000 units. If housing turnover ever normalises from a 4.06M-unit annual pace, none of that upside is in today's numbers.

Bear Case

Same-store revenue is −0.6%, same-store NOI is −2.2%, realised rent per occupied square foot is falling, and Core FFO per share declined 2.6% while Extra Space grew 4.9% — yet PSA trades at 17.9× Core FFO versus roughly 16× for peers. The guidance raise came from acquisitions and lease-up, not from the core portfolio, and it was funded with $6.0B of newly issued shares and units, some struck at $321.98 against a $302.01 share price. The dividend has been flat for three and a half years at a 3.97% yield below both peers. The demand driver is housing turnover, which is stuck near a 4.06M annual rate, and the pricing model is month-to-month, so there is no contractual protection if street rates roll over again. Five years of ownership has delivered roughly 1.5% a year in total return.

Top 3 Risks

01
The Q4 2026 revenue inflection does not happen. Management staked a dated, specific claim: same-store revenue growth improving in Q3 and turning positive in Q4. Everything in the current price assumes it. A miss would say the +1.6% move-in print was seasonal, would leave a 17.9× multiple attached to a shrinking core portfolio, and would arrive at the same moment NSA integration costs are running.
02
Housing turnover stays frozen. Storage demand is a derivative of moving, and existing-home sales at a 4.06M annual rate are running well below the pre-2022 norm with only +0.7% year-over-year growth. Public Storage has no lever on this variable. If mortgage rates stay elevated, the new-customer funnel stays narrow, and the month-to-month lease structure that makes the recovery fast also makes the stagnation immediate.
03
Issuance and integration dilute the per-share numbers faster than synergies arrive. Roughly $6.0B of common shares and OP units were issued, PS Canada units are struck at $321.98 with an earn-out at $375, and NSA synergies of $110–130M are guided over three to four years. FFO is a per-share measure. The share count moved immediately; the synergies have not.
  Industry Commentary
Housing Turnover Is the Sector's Demand Curve
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Self-storage does not sell space, it sells a solution to a transition — moving, downsizing, renovating, splitting a household. That makes existing-home sales the sector's demand curve, and it is running at a 4.06 million seasonally adjusted annual rate as of July 2026, down 1.7% month over month and up 0.7% year over year, with 4.6 months of inventory. Every operator in the industry — Public Storage at −0.6% same-store revenue, CubeSmart at −0.8%, Extra Space at +2.4% — is operating inside the same constraint, and the spread between them is execution and portfolio geography rather than demand. When transaction volumes normalise, storage revenue improves everywhere at once and no management team gets credit for it. Until then, the sector's growth has to be bought.
Consolidation Has Replaced Organic Growth
ONGOING HEADWIND
With same-store growth negative across most of the sector, scale is now being bought rather than earned. Public Storage acquired National Storage Affiliates outright for $10.5 billion in stock on July 22, 2026, taking the listed U.S. self-storage universe from four major names to three, and added a $1.2 billion Canadian platform on top. Extra Space is expanding through third-party management — 67 sites added in a single quarter — which buys share without buying assets. The strategic logic is sound: a fragmented industry with low barriers to construction rewards whoever has the cheapest capital during the downturn. The shareholder consequence is less comfortable — growth delivered through share issuance means the per-share numbers improve only if the synergies land, and Public Storage's are guided over three to four years.
Month-to-Month Pricing Turns First — In Both Directions
EMERGING TAILWIND
The absence of lease terms makes storage the fastest-reporting property type in real estate: the cycle shows up in street rates months before it shows up in reported revenue. That is why the most important number in Public Storage's Q2 2026 release is not the −0.6% same-store revenue but the +1.6% move-in rate — with June alone at +4%, after Q1 at −2.4% — and the 8% decline in move-outs. Bull framing: new-customer pricing is the leading indicator, it has inflected, existing customers are staying, and reported revenue follows with a lag measured in quarters — management says Q4 2026. At 17.9× Core FFO with 2.9× leverage, that is a reasonable entry into an inflection. Bear framing: the same mechanism means there is nothing underneath you if it reverses. One quarter of positive move-in rates in a market where housing transactions are still flat is a data point, not a trend, and you are paying a premium to slower-growing peers to find out which one it is.
