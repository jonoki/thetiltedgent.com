01 Company Overview

PayPal Holdings operates a two-sided digital payments network connecting consumers and merchants, spanning branded checkout (the orange-and-blue PayPal button a shopper actively chooses at checkout), unbranded card processing through Braintree (white-label acquiring that runs a large merchant's card payments invisibly, with no PayPal branding on the page), peer-to-peer transfer through Venmo, cross-border remittance through Xoom, and a set of adjacent products including PayPal Credit, Honey, Hyperwallet and Paidy. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $33.17B, up 4.32% year over year — the fourth consecutive year of decelerating growth from the +18.26% posted in 2021. The company carries a market capitalisation of $45.05B, ranking it roughly the 568th most valuable listed company in the world — down from a peak market value of $274B at the end of 2020.

That last pair of numbers is the entire story. PayPal is not being analysed here as a fintech leader; it is being analysed as a broken-growth business. The stock de-rated roughly 80% from its 2021 level while revenue kept compounding, which means the market did not stop believing PayPal has a business — it stopped believing the business grows. The job of this report is to establish numerically whether the survivor is cheap or correctly priced.

02 Core Businesses

Before the segments: three definitions carry this entire report, and retail commentary routinely conflates them.

Total Payment Volume (TPV) is the gross dollar value of payments processed across all of PayPal's rails — the top-line "how much money moved" number. Transaction take rate is transaction revenue divided by TPV: the fraction of each processed dollar PayPal keeps as revenue. Transaction margin dollars is transaction revenue minus transaction expense and transaction credit losses — the actual gross profit generated by moving money, and the single most important number management guides on. TPV can rise while take rate falls, and the product of the two can rise while transaction margin dollars stall. Branded checkout means the shopper consciously selects PayPal and PayPal owns the customer relationship — high take rate. Unbranded processing means Braintree quietly acquires card payments for a large merchant, competing directly with Adyen, Stripe and Fiserv on price — very thin take rate. Active accounts counts accounts that transacted at least once in the trailing twelve months.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
2-SIDED NETWORK STORED CREDENTIAL MERCHANT ACCEPTANCE RISK & FRAUD DATA BUYER PROTECTION TRUST CROSS-BORDER RAILS REGULATORY LICENSING

PayPal's moat is real but it is narrower than its scale suggests, and it is concentrated almost entirely in the branded checkout business. The durable assets are a stored payment credential held by hundreds of millions of consumers, near-universal merchant acceptance in online retail, two decades of proprietary fraud and risk data that lets PayPal underwrite transactions competitors would decline, and — the underrated one — buyer protection, which is the actual reason many consumers choose the PayPal button on an unfamiliar merchant's site. That trust asset is genuinely hard to replicate. Against that, the unbranded Braintree business has essentially no moat at all: it is a commodity acquiring service sold on price and integration quality against extremely well-capitalised competitors, and it is the part of the business growing fastest. A company whose moated segment is losing relative share while its unmoated segment grows is, structurally, a company whose blended economics deteriorate even when total volume rises. That is precisely what the gross-margin series shows.

Checkout disintermediation by platform owners. Apple Pay is the default on the device the consumer already holds; Shop Pay is native to the merchant's own storefront; Click to Pay is owned by the card networks whose rails PayPal rides. None of these needs to win outright to damage PayPal — each simply needs to take incremental share of the click. Every branded checkout click lost is replaced, at best, by a much thinner unbranded processed dollar.

Structural take-rate compression. The revenue-versus-gross-profit divergence documented above (6.9% vs 3.7% four-year CAGR) is not a one-off; it is what happens when the mix shifts toward large-merchant processing. Absent a genuine reversal in branded volume growth, this is a permanent drag on transaction margin dollars regardless of how large TPV becomes.

Flat account base. Growth in a two-sided network normally comes from adding accounts. PayPal abandoned its long-term account-target framework in 2022 and has since managed the base rather than grown it, leaning on transactions per account instead. Engagement per user is the better metric for revenue, but a flat or shrinking account base removes the compounding optionality that justified the old multiple. Specific active-account and transactions-per-account figures could not be sourced live and are therefore not stated.

Regulatory and credit exposure. PayPal holds customer balances, extends consumer and merchant credit, and operates across dozens of licensing regimes. Interest earned on balances is levered to the policy rate; credit losses are levered to the consumer cycle. Neither is under management's control, and both flow directly into transaction margin dollars.

08 Summary & Key Risks

THE VALUATION QUESTION, STATED DIRECTLY

PYPL trades at 9.93× trailing earnings ($52.67 ÷ $5.30 TTM EPS = 9.94 on rounded inputs; 9.93 as reported), 9.49× forward earnings, 7.44× EV/EBITDA, 1.32× sales, and generates a 14.6% free cash flow yield ($7.70 FCF per share on a $52.67 price). On every one of those measures it is cheap in absolute terms and roughly a third of the NASDAQ-100's multiple. So: is a low multiple on decelerating transaction margin dollars cheap, or is it a value trap?

The honest answer is that the multiple is correct for the growth rate, and the growth rate is the thing at risk. Consider the arithmetic. Consensus has 2026 revenue at $34.72B (+4.67%) and EPS at $5.39 (+1.48%). A business growing earnings 1.5% deserves a single-digit multiple; 9.9× is not a mispricing, it is a fair price for stagnation. The PEG ratio of 1.34 makes the point without ambiguity — adjusted for growth, PYPL is not cheap relative to the market despite a headline P/E a third of it.

The deeper problem is what sits underneath even that modest EPS number. TTM net income of $4,902M is 6.3% below FY2025's $5,233M. TTM EPS of $5.30 is only 2.0% below FY2025's $5.41. The entire 4.3-point difference is the buyback. Strip out the repurchase and the operating business is shrinking in absolute profit, not merely growing slowly. On a four-year view the same wedge appears in the gross-profit line: FY2021→FY2025 revenue compounded at 6.9% while gross profit compounded at 3.7% — the take-rate compression from the unbranded mix, showing up exactly where theory says it should.

That is the value-trap mechanism in full. A buyback funded by a 14.6% FCF yield can hold EPS flat for several years while the underlying profit pool erodes. The multiple stays optically low the whole time, the "cheap" screen keeps firing, and the share price still goes nowhere because earnings per share are being manufactured rather than earned. The five-year chart is what that looks like: down 79.8% while EPS rose from $3.52 to $5.30.

What would settle it. One number: transaction margin dollars growing faster than TPV. If branded checkout volume reaccelerates, or if value-added services and advertising scale enough to offset the Braintree mix, then gross profit growth converges back toward revenue growth, the buyback becomes genuinely accretive rather than compensatory, and a 9.9× multiple on a business compounding high single digits is materially too cheap. If it does not, the buyback shrinks the company one share at a time and the multiple never re-rates. This report could not source the transaction margin dollar series live — which is precisely why the reader must go and read it in PayPal's Q2 2026 release before taking a position either way.

Bull Case

A 14.6% free cash flow yield, a 22.27% ROIC, only $2.96B of net debt and an 8.74% total shareholder yield mean PayPal can return nearly a tenth of its market capitalisation to shareholders every year without straining the balance sheet — and at 9.9× earnings every dollar of that is highly accretive. Cost discipline is real (headcount down 23% from the 2021 peak; operating margin up from 14.68% in FY2022 to 18.70% in FY2025). If branded checkout stabilises and value-added services and advertising scale, gross-profit growth converges back to revenue growth and the multiple has enormous room to expand from a third of the index.

Bear Case

Revenue growth has decelerated for four straight years (18.3% → 8.5% → 8.2% → 6.8% → 4.3%), gross profit compounded at only 3.7% against revenue's 6.9%, and TTM net income is already 6.3% below FY2025. The mix shift toward unbranded Braintree volume is structural, not cyclical, and it converts headline volume growth into no profit growth. The buyback is masking absolute earnings decline, not amplifying growth. Apple Pay, Shop Pay and network wallets attack the only high-margin part of the business, and Venmo monetisation has been promised since 2018 without ever showing in the consolidated numbers. 33 of 43 analysts are on Hold and two firms just cut targets to below the current price.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
Structural take-rate compression from the branded/unbranded mix. Braintree volume grows faster than branded checkout at a fraction of the take rate, so TPV and revenue can keep rising while transaction margin dollars stall — exactly the pattern visible in the 6.9% revenue versus 3.7% gross-profit four-year CAGRs. This is the single risk that determines whether 9.9× is cheap or correct, and it is not cyclical.
02
Buyback-masked earnings decline. Shares fell 7.83% year over year, converting a 6.3% net income decline into a 2.0% EPS decline. Screens and headlines will read the EPS line. If operating profit keeps eroding, the repurchase merely delays recognition while consuming the free cash flow that constitutes the entire bull case — and the accretion math stops working the moment the multiple falls further.
03
Checkout disintermediation by parties that own the distribution. Apple owns the device, Shopify owns the storefront, and Visa and Mastercard own the rail. None has to beat PayPal outright; each only needs to capture incremental clicks. Losses here hit the highest-margin dollars in the business and show up with a lag in the gross-margin line rather than in any single announcement — which is why the deterioration was visible in the FY2021–FY2023 margin series long before it was visible in revenue.
  Industry Commentary
The Commoditisation of Payment Acceptance
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Merchant acquiring has become a scale-and-price business. Stripe, Adyen, Fiserv and a long tail of processors compete for large-merchant volume on basis points, and the marginal economics approach the cost of the underlying interchange plus a thin spread. PayPal's participation in that market through Braintree is strategically defensible — it keeps PayPal present at large merchants and preserves the option to convert unbranded volume into branded checkout — but it is financially dilutive at the margin. The evidence is in the audited series: reported gross margin fell from 46.99% (FY2021) to 39.59% (FY2023), a 740 basis-point compression, during a period when revenue grew every single year. Growing revenue while losing 740bp of gross margin is the numerical fingerprint of commoditisation, and it recovered only partially (to 41.48% in FY2025, 40.48% trailing).
Distribution Now Beats Product in Consumer Payments
ONGOING HEADWIND
The decisive change since 2021 is that the parties controlling the surfaces where payment happens — operating systems, e-commerce platforms and card networks — have all launched their own checkout products. PayPal's historical advantage was that it was the only trusted, universally accepted alternative to typing a card number. That advantage was a product advantage. It is now competing against distribution advantages it cannot replicate: Apple Pay is pre-installed, Shop Pay is embedded in the merchant's own cart, Click to Pay is written into the network rules. PayPal's counter-assets — buyer protection, two decades of fraud data, and a stored credential base measured in the hundreds of millions — are genuine, but they defend a position rather than expand one. This is why the active-account base has been managed rather than grown since the 2022 abandonment of the account-target framework, and why transactions per account became the metric management chose to emphasise instead. Both series were unobtainable from a live source in this session; of the two, transactions per account is the one that drives revenue, while the account count is the one that drives the long-term optionality the old multiple was built on.
Bull vs Bear: What the Next Four Quarters Decide
EMERGING TAILWIND (CONDITIONAL)
Bull framing: the margin trough is behind it. Gross margin bottomed at 39.59% in FY2023 and has recovered to 41.48% (FY2025); operating margin rose from 14.68% (FY2022) to 18.70% (FY2025); ROIC is 22.27%; free cash flow is $6.59B against a $45.05B market capitalisation. Value-added services and the advertising initiative monetise assets PayPal already owns at near-100% incremental margin. On that path, 9.9× trailing earnings and 7.44× EV/EBITDA are absurdly low for a business earning 22% on capital.

Bear framing: the recovery in margin was cost-cutting, and cost-cutting ends. Headcount is down 23% from the 2021 peak and cannot fall indefinitely; TTM operating income of $6,010M is already 3.1% below FY2025's $6,203M and TTM net income 6.3% below. Consensus has 2026 EPS growing 1.48%. On that path the low multiple is not an opportunity, it is an accurate quotation for a business whose profit pool is being competed away one basis point of take rate at a time.

The resolution is a single disclosure line. If transaction margin dollars grow faster than TPV over the next four quarters, the bull framing is right and the stock is materially mispriced. If they do not, the bear framing is right and no amount of buyback fixes it. Everything else in the PayPal debate — Venmo, crypto, agentic commerce, the checkout redesign — is commentary on that one number.
