01 Company Overview

Royal Caribbean Group sails three brands of its own — Royal Caribbean International, Celebrity Cruises and Silversea — and owns half of TUI Cruises, a German joint venture that also runs Hapag-Lloyd Cruises; counting the joint venture's ships, the combined fleet was 71 ships at September 30, 2026. Around the ships sits a growing network of company-owned private destinations, and in September 2026 it agreed to buy half of Sandals and Beaches Resorts, the Caribbean all-inclusive resort chain. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $17.94B, up 8.8% year over year, with $4.27B of net income and diluted EPS of $15.61; on a TTM basis revenue is $19.14B and EPS $16.90. At roughly $79.8B of market value it is the most valuable listed cruise operator — about 49% of the four largest listed cruise companies' combined value ($163.5B, our calculation at the October 28 closes) — and ranks #160 in the S&P 500.

The reason this business is worth studying is operating leverage. A cruise ship is an enormous fixed cost that sails on a published schedule whether it is full or empty: crew, fuel, port fees, food, insurance, depreciation and interest are all incurred before the first guest boards. That means the last few percentage points of occupancy and the last few dollars of ticket price fall almost entirely to profit — and the first few points of shortfall fall almost entirely out of it. In the peak summer quarter, Q3 2026, Royal Caribbean turned a 9% revenue increase into a 45.4% adjusted EBITDA margin and a 14% rise in adjusted EPS, and in 2020–21 the same mechanism ran violently in reverse: revenue fell to $1.53B in FY2021 and the company lost $5.26B.

The cruise fare itself — cabin, meals in the main dining rooms, entertainment, and in most cases port charges and taxes. It is sold months in advance, which is why the two operating metrics management guides on are both stated per unit of capacity. APCD (Available Passenger Cruise Days) is the industry's capacity unit: one berth available for one day, calculated at double occupancy — so a 5,000-berth ship on a 7-day sailing generates 35,000 APCDs whether or not it fills. Full-year 2026 guidance is 56.8 million APCDs, up 6.6%, slowing to about 4.5% capacity growth in 2027. Net Yield is net revenue divided by APCD — revenue per available berth-day, the cruise industry's equivalent of a hotel's revenue per available room. Q3 2026 net yields rose +0.9% as reported and +0.6% in constant currency, ahead of guidance for roughly flat yields; full-year guidance moved to about +2.1% in constant currency (from +1.75% to +2.25% in July), with Q4 guided to +4.2% to +4.7%.

Occupancy above 100% is not a typo. The denominator assumes two passengers per cabin; when families and groups book third and fourth berths, actual passengers exceed double-occupancy capacity. Q3 2026 occupancy was 112.4% — 2.68 million passengers on 14.8 million APCDs. Because those incremental berths ride on a ship that was sailing anyway, they are among the most profitable revenue in the business.

Onboard revenue is everything a guest spends after the fare is paid: beverage and dining packages, casino, shore excursions, spa, retail, internet and photography. It is the second, higher-margin layer on top of a cost base that has already been committed. The incremental cost of pouring one more cocktail or selling one more excursion seat is small relative to price, so onboard spend converts to profit at a materially better rate than ticket revenue — which is why management sells pre-cruise packages aggressively and why the shift toward package pre-purchase is a genuine margin lever, not just a marketing tactic. Onboard has held right around 31% of revenue in 2026, and in Q3 it grew 13.6% against 6.8% for ticket revenue. On the cost side, the metric that pairs with net yield is Net Cruise Cost excluding Fuel per APCD — the controllable operating cost of making one berth available for one day, with fuel stripped out because it is a commodity management does not set. Q3 2026 NCC ex-fuel fell 1.9% as reported / 1.8% in constant currency, a little better than the July guidance of down 1.1–1.6%, though the company says some costs moved into Q4; full-year guidance stays approximately flat in constant currency and Q4 is guided down 0.9–1.4%. Net yield growing while cost per APCD is flat is precisely how operating leverage shows up in the P&L.

Revenue from private islands and beach clubs is reported inside onboard and ticket revenue, but strategically it is a separate business: Royal Caribbean is buying the destination as well as the transport, capturing spend that used to leak to third-party ports and excursion operators. The network expands from three destinations to eight by 2028. Alongside it sits the single most useful forward indicator in the industry. Customer deposits are cash collected today for sailings that have not happened — a balance-sheet liability (the company owes a cruise, not cash) and simultaneously the cleanest read on demand, because guests vote with deposits long before they board. At September 30, 2026 the balance stood at $6.034 billion — up from $5.739 billion at the end of 2025, down from $6.736 billion at June 30, after the peak summer sailings — against $851 million of cash on hand. In other words, customers are pre-funding a large share of working capital; a deteriorating deposit balance would be the earliest visible crack in the story. In the Q3 release the company said booking volumes had risen in the mid-teens across all major channels since its July earnings call, and Jason Liberty said "2027 bookings are pacing ahead of historical levels at higher rates…" Newbuild commitments for its own brands total about $19.4 billion for 12 ships and roughly 40,630 berths, with $1.5 billion already deposited (10-Q, September 30, 2026); Legend of the Seas, the third Icon-class ship, was delivered in June 2026.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
FLEET SCALE OWNED DESTINATIONS SHIPYARD SLOT SCARCITY BRAND LADDER LOYALTY / REPEAT RATE TAX STRUCTURE DIRECT BOOKING DATA

The moat is best understood as the flip side of the operating leverage. Because ships are lumpy, capital-intensive and take years from order to delivery — Royal Caribbean's own order book runs to 2032 (10-Q) — supply cannot respond quickly to demand. That slow supply lets an operator with the newest, largest ships out-earn the field for years at a time; the Icon and Oasis classes are Royal Caribbean's largest. Owning the destinations at the other end of the voyage captures a second margin pool that a competitor calling at third-party ports cannot. The brand ladder (Royal Caribbean International for contemporary, Celebrity for premium, Silversea for ultra-luxury) lets the group monetize a guest across their lifetime rather than losing them upmarket. The Sandals stake, once it closes, extends the same idea onto land: resorts in the region where most of the ships already sail.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Balance-sheet fragility in a demand shock. Total debt is $22.4 billion (current portion plus long-term debt, September 30, 2026) against $851 million of cash and $11.9 billion of book equity — a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.88×. The current ratio is 0.22, which looks alarming until you realize customer deposits sit in current liabilities; still, it leaves no cushion. The industry only survived 2020–21 by borrowing at punitive rates, and the same mechanism would apply again.

2. Capacity is being added into a cyclical market. About $19.4 billion of ships on order will be funded largely with debt, and the $3 billion Sandals stake adds a term loan on top. If the incremental berths arrive into a soft demand year, net yields compress at exactly the moment interest and depreciation step up — the fixed-cost trap running in reverse.

3. Fuel and currency. Fuel is excluded from the headline cost metric precisely because it is volatile and largely uncontrollable. Q3 2026 net yields were +0.9% as reported versus +0.6% constant currency — a 0.3-percentage-point currency tailwind that can reverse — and the company said higher fuel prices and currency moves partly offset its October guidance raise. Both flow straight through the operating leverage.

4. Geopolitical and regulatory exposure to itineraries. In July management flagged a modest near-term hit to bookings on select itineraries from prolonged geopolitical activity. Ships can be redeployed, but redeployment is expensive and dilutes yield. Separately, the offshore tax structure that delivers a ~1.7% effective rate has been a recurring target of US legislative proposals; any change would be a step-function hit to EPS.

Notable One-Off Items. The single largest distortion in RCL's reported numbers is not a one-off at all but a permanent structural feature: the effective tax rate of roughly 1.7%. Royal Caribbean is incorporated in the Republic of Liberia and its shipping income is largely exempt from US federal income tax; the nine-month 2026 provision was $66 million on $3,887 million of pre-tax income. Comparing RCL's net margin or P/E to a normal US corporate taxed at 21% is therefore apples-to-oranges — on a fully-taxed basis, TTM EPS of $16.90 would be closer to $13.60 (our calculation at a 21% rate). Second, free cash flow swings with ship deliveries: it was negative ($416M) on a trailing basis at the June quarter and is +$1.51B at September, because the twelve months now include fewer delivery payments; a ship is paid for largely on delivery, so the figure will dip again as the $19.4B order book arrives. Third, the year-over-year comparisons in the 2021–2023 columns are meaningless as growth rates — FY2021 revenue of $1.53B reflects a near-total operating shutdown, and the +477% FY2022 and +57% FY2023 growth figures are recovery arithmetic, not underlying demand. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operating leverage is intact and now points the right way: FY2026 guidance has net yields up about 2.1% constant currency against net cruise costs ex-fuel per APCD approximately flat, which converts modest revenue growth into ~14.5% adjusted EPS growth, and the guide was raised twice in three months. Customer deposits of $6.03B and 2027 bookings pacing ahead of history at higher rates give unusual forward visibility, while capacity growth slows to about 4.5% in 2027. Two credit upgrades into investment grade (Moody's Baa2, S&P BBB−) cut funding costs on $22B of debt, and at 17.7× trailing and 16.7× forward earnings with a $353.46 consensus target, the stock is 18% below an August 2025 month-end high it reached on materially lower earnings.

Bear Case

This is a $22.4B-debt, 0.22-current-ratio business with a beta of 1.82, adding $19.4B of debt-funded ships and a $3B loan-funded resort stake into a consumer cycle nobody can forecast. The same fixed-cost structure that produces 45% peak-quarter EBITDA margins produced a $5.26B loss in 2021. Q3 net yields grew just +0.6% in constant currency, fuel and currency are already eating into the guidance raise, and management flagged geopolitical softness on select itineraries in July. Strip out the ~1.7% offshore tax rate and reported earnings look far less exceptional; a flat twelve months for the shares (+1.8%) while targets rose suggests the market is pricing the cycle rather than the quarter.

01
Demand shock into a fixed cost base. Occupancy of 112.4% and net yields are the entire earnings algorithm. A recession, a health scare or a regional conflict that forces itinerary changes hits revenue immediately while crew, fuel, port and depreciation costs continue — the 2020–21 experience, when revenue fell to $1.53B and the loss was $5.26B, is the template.
02
Leverage and the newbuild commitment. Net debt of $21.5B (September 30, 2026) against TTM EBITDA of $7.16B (stockanalysis) is roughly 3.0× (Moody's put it at 3.0× at year-end 2025), with $19.4B of ships on order to be funded largely with debt and a $3B term loan lined up for the Sandals stake. If yields soften while deliveries land, leverage rises just as cash flow falls, and the investment-grade rating that lowered funding costs is the first thing at risk.
03
The tax structure is a policy variable, not a moat. Liberian incorporation and the US shipping-income exemption deliver an effective tax rate near 1.7% — a $66M provision on $3,887M of nine-month pre-tax income. Legislative proposals to tax cruise operators as US corporates resurface periodically; a move to anything approaching a normal rate would cut EPS by roughly a fifth overnight with no operational change whatsoever.
  Industry Commentary
The Shutdown Debt Overhang
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Cruising all but stopped in 2020–21: Royal Caribbean's FY2021 revenue was $1.53B against $17.94B in FY2025, and it survived by borrowing. Total debt is still $22.4B against $851M of cash and $11.9B of equity, and the $19.4B newbuild book and the $3B Sandals stake will be funded largely with more of it. The deleveraging path has been real — Moody's moved the credit from Baa3 to Baa2 in February 2026, S&P from BB+ to BBB− in February 2025, with debt/EBITDA at 3.0× and expected to stay in a 2.5–3.0× band — but every dollar of EBITDA still services a capital structure built in an emergency.
Yield Growth: Slow Q3, Faster Q4
WATCH ITEM
The post-pandemic pricing surge is over. Q3 2026 net yields grew just +0.6% in constant currency while capacity rose 8%, so most of the growth came from more berths rather than better prices — a lower-quality source of earnings, because berths carry fixed cost and price does not. Q4 is guided to +4.2% to +4.7%, and capacity growth slows to about 4.5% in 2027; if that guide holds, price rather than berths drives growth again. Management's other lever is cost: NCC ex-fuel per APCD guided flat for the year and down 0.9–1.4% in Q4. That spread, not the top line, is what has to hold.
Owning the Destination
EMERGING TAILWIND
Expanding company-owned destinations from three to eight by 2028 moves spend that used to leave the ecosystem — shore excursions, food, beverage, retail at port — onto Royal Caribbean's own P&L at high incremental margin, while making the itinerary itself a differentiated product competitors cannot copy without building their own. It is the clearest structural margin lever in the story and it does not depend on ticket pricing. The Sandals stake takes the same idea onshore: half of a resort chain in the same islands, for about $3 billion, expected to close in early 2027.
Bull vs Bear, Framed
THE DEBATE
Both sides agree on the mechanism and disagree only on where in the cycle we are. Bulls see net yields still rising against flat unit costs, $6.03B of customer deposits and 2027 bookings ahead of history at higher rates, investment-grade credit lowering the cost of $22B of debt, and 17.7× earnings for ~14.5% adjusted EPS growth. Bears see a slow Q3 yield, a new $3B debt-funded bet on land resorts, a 0.22 current ratio and a 1.82 beta heading into an unknowable consumer cycle. The operating leverage that took this stock more than ten times off its June 2022 low is the same leverage that cost it 58% between the March and June 2022 month-end closes. Position size, not price target, is the real decision here.
