01 Company Overview

Royal Caribbean Group operates 69 ships (as of Dec 31, 2025) across three brands — Royal Caribbean International, Celebrity Cruises and Silversea — plus a growing network of company-owned private destinations. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $17.94B, up 8.8% year over year, with $4.27B of net income and EPS of $15.64; on a TTM basis revenue is $18.68B and EPS $16.21. At roughly $74.7B of market value it is the world's most valuable cruise operator — about 48% of the entire listed cruise sector's market capitalization ($151.0B) and roughly the 353rd most valuable public company globally.

The reason this business is worth studying is operating leverage. A cruise ship is an enormous fixed cost that sails on a published schedule whether it is full or empty: crew, fuel, port fees, food, insurance, depreciation and interest are all incurred before the first guest boards. That means the last few percentage points of occupancy and the last few dollars of ticket price fall almost entirely to profit — and the first few points of shortfall fall almost entirely out of it. Royal Caribbean converted a 6% revenue increase in Q2 2026 into a 37.9% EBITDA margin, and in 2020–21 the same mechanism ran violently in reverse: revenue fell to $1.53B in FY2021 and the company lost $5.26B.

The cruise fare itself — cabin, meals in the main dining rooms, entertainment, and in most cases port charges and taxes. It is sold months in advance, which is why the two operating metrics management guides on are both stated per unit of capacity. APCD (Available Passenger Cruise Days) is the industry's capacity unit: one berth available for one day, calculated at double occupancy — so a 5,000-berth ship on a 7-day sailing generates 35,000 APCDs whether or not it fills. Full-year 2026 guidance is 56.9 million APCDs, up 6.6%. Net Yield is net revenue divided by APCD — revenue per available berth-day, the cruise industry's equivalent of RevPAR. Q2 2026 net yields rose +1.9% as reported and +1.2% in constant currency, beating guidance by about 100 basis points; full-year guidance is +1.75% to +2.25% constant currency.

Occupancy above 100% is not a typo. The denominator assumes two passengers per cabin; when families and groups book third and fourth berths, actual passengers exceed double-occupancy capacity. Q2 2026 occupancy was 110.2% — 2.4 million passengers across roughly 15 million passenger cruise days. Because those incremental berths ride on a ship that was sailing anyway, they are among the most profitable revenue in the business.

Onboard revenue is everything a guest spends after the fare is paid: beverage and dining packages, casino, shore excursions, spa, retail, internet and photography. It is the second, higher-margin layer on top of a cost base that has already been committed. The incremental cost of pouring one more cocktail or selling one more excursion seat is small relative to price, so onboard spend converts to profit at a materially better rate than ticket revenue — which is why management sells pre-cruise packages aggressively and why the shift toward package pre-purchase is a genuine margin lever, not just a marketing tactic. Onboard has held right around 31% of revenue in 2026. On the cost side, the metric that pairs with net yield is Net Cruise Cost excluding Fuel per APCD — the controllable operating cost of making one berth available for one day, with fuel stripped out because it is a commodity management does not set. Q2 2026 NCC ex-fuel rose +4.4% as reported / +3.9% constant currency, but full-year guidance is approximately flat in constant currency and Q3 is guided down 1.1–1.6%. Net yield growing while cost per APCD is flat is precisely how operating leverage shows up in the P&L.

Revenue from private islands and beach clubs is reported inside onboard and ticket revenue, but strategically it is a separate business: Royal Caribbean is buying the destination as well as the transport, capturing spend that used to leak to third-party ports and excursion operators. The network expands from three destinations to eight by 2028. Alongside it sits the single most useful forward indicator in the industry. Customer deposits are cash collected today for sailings that have not happened — a balance-sheet liability (the company owes a cruise, not cash) and simultaneously the cleanest read on demand, because guests vote with deposits long before they board. At June 30, 2026 the balance stood at $6.736 billion, against $875 million of cash on hand. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
FLEET SCALE OWNED DESTINATIONS SHIPYARD SLOT SCARCITY BRAND LADDER LOYALTY / REPEAT RATE TAX STRUCTURE DIRECT BOOKING DATA

The moat is best understood as the flip side of the operating leverage. Because ships are lumpy, capital-intensive and take four to five years from order to delivery — and because the world's large-cruise-ship yards are a handful of firms with multi-year backlogs — supply cannot respond quickly to demand. That structural supply discipline lets an operator with the newest, largest, highest-yielding ships out-earn the field for years at a time. Royal Caribbean's Icon and Oasis classes carry the highest net yields in the industry, and owning the destinations at the other end of the voyage captures a second margin pool that a competitor chartering third-party ports cannot. The brand ladder (Royal Caribbean International for contemporary, Celebrity for premium, Silversea for ultra-luxury) lets the group monetize a guest across their lifetime rather than losing them upmarket. Finally, cruising still prices at a discount to comparable land-based vacations, which gives the industry a value proposition that holds up in a soft consumer environment better than most discretionary categories.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Balance-sheet fragility in a demand shock. Total debt is $23.4–23.5 billion against $875 million of cash and $10.5 billion of book equity — a debt-to-equity ratio of 2.25×. The current ratio is 0.21, which looks alarming until you realize customer deposits sit in current liabilities; still, it leaves no cushion. The industry only survived 2020–21 by borrowing at punitive rates, and the same mechanism would apply again.

2. Capacity is being added into a cyclical market. $16.5 billion of ships on order will be funded largely with debt. If the incremental berths arrive into a soft demand year, net yields compress at exactly the moment interest and depreciation step up — the fixed-cost trap running in reverse.

3. Fuel and currency. Fuel is excluded from the headline cost metric precisely because it is volatile and largely uncontrollable. Q2 2026 net yields were +1.9% as reported versus +1.2% constant currency — a 70bp currency tailwind that can reverse. Both flow straight through the operating leverage.

4. Geopolitical and regulatory exposure to itineraries. Management explicitly flagged softness on "select itineraries" from prolonged geopolitical activity. Ships can be redeployed, but redeployment is expensive and dilutes yield. Separately, the offshore tax structure that delivers a ~2% effective rate has been a recurring target of US legislative proposals; any change would be a step-function hit to EPS.

Notable One-Off Items. The single largest distortion in RCL's reported numbers is not a one-off at all but a permanent structural feature: the effective tax rate of roughly 1.8–2.1%. Royal Caribbean is incorporated in the Republic of Liberia and its shipping income is largely exempt from US federal income tax; the six-month 2026 provision was $39 million on $2,126 million of pre-tax income. Comparing RCL's net margin or P/E to a normal US corporate taxed at 21% is therefore apples-to-oranges — on a fully-taxed basis, TTM EPS of $16.21 would be closer to $13. Second, free cash flow is negative ($416M TTM) because capex of $7.20B exceeded $6.79B of operating cash flow; this is a deliberate newbuild investment cycle, not an earnings-quality problem, but it means the equity story depends on debt-funded growth converting to yield. Third, the year-over-year comparisons in the 2021–2023 columns are meaningless as growth rates — FY2021 revenue of $1.53B reflects a near-total operating shutdown, and the +477% FY2022 and +57% FY2023 growth figures are recovery arithmetic, not underlying demand. Q2 2026 adjusted EPS of $4.21 was down 4% year over year despite the revenue increase, on drydock and cost timing.

The five-year trajectory. From $88.95 at the end of September 2021 to $279.41 on August 28, 2026, RCL returned +214.1% on price alone — roughly 3.1× your money in five years, and comfortably ahead of the S&P 500 over the same window (approximately +90%, est.). But that headline hides one of the more violent round trips in the large-cap market. The stock lost more than 60% between April and June 2022, bottoming at a $34.91 monthly close as the market priced a recession, a fuel spike and a balance sheet that had just absorbed two years of zero revenue. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The operating leverage is intact and now points the right way: FY2026 guidance has net yields up 1.75–2.25% constant currency against net cruise costs ex-fuel per APCD approximately flat, which converts modest revenue growth into ~14% EPS growth. Customer deposits of $6.74B and a record booked position at record pricing for 2027 give unusual forward visibility. Two credit upgrades to solid investment grade (Moody's Baa2, S&P BBB−) cut funding costs on $23B of debt, and at 17.2× trailing earnings with a PEG of 0.91 and a $346.92 consensus target, the stock is 23% below an August 2025 high it reached on materially lower earnings.

Bear Case

This is a $23.5B-debt, 0.21-current-ratio, negative-free-cash-flow business with a beta of 1.78, adding $16.5B of debt-funded capacity into a consumer cycle nobody can forecast. The same fixed-cost structure that produces 37.9% EBITDA margins produced a $5.26B loss in 2021. Q2 adjusted EPS actually fell 4% year over year, net yield growth has decelerated to +1.2% constant currency, and management has already flagged geopolitical softness on select itineraries. Strip out the ~2% offshore tax rate and reported earnings look far less exceptional; the market's 25% de-rating over twelve months, against rising analyst targets, suggests it is discounting the cycle rather than the quarter.

01
Demand shock into a fixed cost base. Occupancy of 110.2% and net yields are the entire earnings algorithm. A recession, a health scare or a regional conflict that forces itinerary changes hits revenue immediately while crew, fuel, port and depreciation costs continue — the 2020–21 experience, when revenue fell to $1.53B and the loss was $5.26B, is the template.
02
Leverage and the newbuild commitment. Net debt of $22.6B against TTM EBITDA of $6.93B is roughly 3.3× (Moody's calculated 3.0× on its own adjustments at year-end 2025), with $16.5B of ships on order to be funded largely with debt. If yields soften while deliveries land, leverage rises just as cash flow falls, and the investment-grade rating that lowered funding costs is the first thing at risk.
03
The tax structure is a policy variable, not a moat. Liberian incorporation and the US shipping-income exemption deliver an effective tax rate near 2% — a $39M provision on $2,126M of six-month pre-tax income. Legislative proposals to tax cruise operators as US corporates resurface periodically; a move to anything approaching a normal rate would cut EPS by roughly a fifth overnight with no operational change whatsoever.
  Industry Commentary
The Shutdown Debt Overhang
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Cruise is the only major industry whose revenue went to literally zero for over a year. Royal Caribbean's FY2021 revenue was $1.53B against $17.94B in FY2025 — and it survived by borrowing. Total debt is still $23.4B against $875M of cash and $10.5B of equity, and the $16.5B newbuild book will be funded largely with more of it. The deleveraging path has been real — Moody's moved the credit from Baa3 to Baa2 in February 2026, S&P from BB+ to BBB− in February 2025, with debt/EBITDA at 3.0× and expected to stay in a 2.5–3.0× band — but every dollar of EBITDA still services a capital structure built in an emergency.
Yield Growth Is Decelerating
ONGOING HEADWIND
The post-pandemic pricing surge is over. Q2 2026 net yields grew just +1.2% in constant currency, and Q3 is guided to approximately flat. With capacity up 6.6% for the year and 8.5% in Q3, the growth is now coming from more berths rather than better prices — a lower-quality source of earnings, because berths carry fixed cost and price does not. Management's answer is the cost side: NCC ex-fuel per APCD guided flat for the year and down 1.1–1.6% in Q3. That spread, not the top line, is what has to hold.
Owning the Destination
EMERGING TAILWIND
Expanding company-owned destinations from three to eight by 2028 moves spend that used to leave the ecosystem — shore excursions, food, beverage, retail at port — onto Royal Caribbean's own P&L at high incremental margin, while making the itinerary itself a differentiated product competitors cannot copy without building their own. It is the clearest structural margin lever in the story and it does not depend on ticket pricing.
Bull vs Bear, Framed
THE DEBATE
Both sides agree on the mechanism and disagree only on where in the cycle we are. Bulls see net yields still rising against flat unit costs, $6.74B of customer deposits and record 2027 bookings confirming demand, investment-grade credit lowering the cost of $23B of debt, and 17.2× earnings for ~14% EPS growth. Bears see decelerating yields, accelerating capacity, negative free cash flow, a 0.21 current ratio and a 1.78 beta heading into an unknowable consumer cycle. The operating leverage that makes this stock a triple off a trough is the same leverage that makes it a two-thirds drawdown when demand blinks. Position size, not price target, is the real decision here.
