01 Company Overview

Regions is a Birmingham, Alabama bank that has spent fifty-five years becoming the default checking account of the American South. It began in 1971 as First Alabama Bancshares — a merger of three Alabama banks, the oldest dating to 1856 — took the Regions name in 1994, then doubled itself twice: Union Planters in 2004 ($5.9B) and AmSouth in 2006 ($10B). It needed $3.5B of TARP money in the crisis and repaid it in April 2012. Today it earns a trailing $7.24B of revenue and $2.15B of net income, is worth $24.32B — roughly the #1006 company in the world by market value — and has been an S&P 500 constituent since August 28, 1998.

The hand Regions holds is its deposit base. In Q2 2026 it paid 1.69% on interest-bearing deposits, a figure the company calls peer-leading, and that cheap funding is why a plain-vanilla lender earns a 19.0% return on average tangible common equity — above management's own 16–18% long-term target. The question the market is asking in September 2026 is whether that edge holds. At the Barclays conference on September 15, CEO John Turner said plainly that competition is rising — new banks entering Regions' markets, larger banks expanding into the Southeast and Texas — and management had already trimmed its margin outlook as investment-grade loans and tight credit spreads eat into yields. The stock, which closed at an all-time high of $32.40 on July 16, settled at $28.55 on September 21, down 12.1% from its 52-week high. This report is about whether the South's cheapest deposits are still cheap.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Low-cost Southern deposit base Dense branch footprint, 15 states Primary-account relationships ~$69B available liquidity Uninsured deposits ~181% covered Growing Sun Belt markets

Regions' edge is not a product; it is habit. Millions of households and small businesses across the South keep their operating cash where they always have, and that inertia lets Regions pay 1.69% on interest-bearing deposits and earn a 3.66% margin. That is why ROATCE runs at 19%. The weakness is that habit can be priced away. Turner said at the Barclays conference that larger institutions are expanding into the Southeast and Texas and named Wells Fargo's expansion after its asset cap was lifted as an example. Deposit franchises are hard to build and slow to lose, but they are not free to defend. Regions is now spending on branches, bankers and a new core deposit system to hold its position.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Competition for Southern deposits. "If it weren't for competition, it'd be a great business," Turner told the Barclays conference. Management assumes a mid-30s deposit beta if rates rise. The low-cost deposit base is the whole moat, and national banks opening branches in Regions' markets are going after exactly that.
02
Margin squeeze from the loan mix. Loan growth is coming mostly from investment-grade corporate borrowers at tight spreads, and management trimmed its margin outlook as a result. Growth that earns a lower margin can leave net interest income growing more slowly than the loan book. The full-year NII guide is 2.5–4%.
03
Rates and the bond book. The 1.2-point gap between CET1 of 10.7% and 9.5% including AOCI is unrealised losses on securities. Management warned of AOCI pressure in the current quarter because of rates. Q2 also carried $41M of securities losses from repositioning the portfolio.
04
A seven-year core-system replacement. The deposit-system conversion is in testing, with the customer pilot planned for Q1. Management chose an incremental rollout over a single cut-over, which lowers the risk. But core conversions are where banks lose customers, and this one also blocks depository M&A until it is done.

Notable One-Off Items

The main one-off in Q2 2026 is $41M of net securities losses, which is why reported non-interest income fell 2.5% YoY to $630M while adjusted non-interest income rose to $670M. It also opens the gap between GAAP EPS of $0.64 and adjusted EPS of $0.68, and between reported ROATCE of 19.0% and adjusted 20.2%. Banks take losses like these on purpose: they sell low-yielding bonds and reinvest at higher yields, a small hit now for more interest income later. Expense adjustments were small ($1,121M reported vs $1,116M adjusted). The second distortion is buybacks: TTM EPS rose 15.0% against net income growth of 7.5%, with the share count down 3.95% in a year. Per-share growth is real, but about half of it comes from a shrinking denominator.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The bull case pays 11.6x trailing earnings and 2.07x tangible book for a bank earning a 19.0% ROATCE, above its own 16–18% target. It funds itself at 1.69% on interest-bearing deposits, runs a 56.9% adjusted efficiency ratio and saw credit improve on every measure in Q2 (net charge-offs down to 0.42%, NPLs to 0.67%). The 4.20% dividend yield is twice the industry's, on a 49% payout that was just raised 13%, and the share count is shrinking by about 4% a year. Management has guided the margin toward 3.70% by year-end with fixed-rate asset repricing still to come, and it has ruled out the dilutive bank M&A that has hurt peers' per-share numbers. After a 12% pullback the stock is back at its 10-month average, and the average target is 15.6% above the price.

Bear Case

The bear case says the moat is being contested in plain sight. The CEO says competition is rising, larger banks are expanding into the Southeast and Texas, and management has already trimmed its margin outlook because new loans are investment-grade credits at tight spreads. Fee income is tracking the low end of its 3–5% guide, mortgage is weak, and a Q2 beat still produced a down day. Street sentiment is poor: 4 of 22 analysts rate it Sell, Barclays reiterated Sell after hosting the company, the lowest target equals the price, and 6.58% of the float is short. A 19% ROATCE is flattered by charge-offs near cyclical lows. If credit normalises toward the top of the 40–50bps guide or above it, returns fall back toward the 16–18% target and the case for a premium to tangible book weakens.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deposit competition erodes the funding edge. Two-thirds of revenue is net interest income, and the 1.69% interest-bearing deposit cost is the reason ROATCE is 19%. Management assumes a mid-30s deposit beta if rates rise. A faster pass-through, forced by new entrants and larger banks building in Regions' markets, hits margin directly.
02
Margin guidance keeps slipping. The year-end ~3.70% NIM target has already been trimmed once because of loan mix and spreads. Another cut at the October 16 print would put the 2.5–4% NII growth guide under pressure, since net interest income is 65.6% of adjusted revenue.
03
Credit normalisation from a low base. Net charge-offs of 0.42% and a 1.63% allowance reflect a benign quarter, and management says the reserve ratio could fall further. A turn in office, transportation or consumer credit would raise provisions just as the reserve cushion has been drawn down to CECL day-one levels.
Industry Commentary
The fight for Sun Belt deposits
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Southern and Texas banking markets are growing, and growth draws competitors. At the Barclays conference Turner described new banks entering Regions' markets and larger institutions expanding across the Southeast and Texas, and cited Wells Fargo's post-asset-cap expansion by name. Regions is answering with 130–150 new branches over three to four years, 75–80 added bankers across commercial, wealth and treasury management, and a new deposit platform. That is the cost of keeping a cheap funding base. Expense growth is guided at 1.5–3.5% for 2026, and management expects operating leverage of less than 100 basis points at the midpoint.

Rates, spreads and the investment-grade loan mix
ONGOING HEADWIND

Commercial loan demand is real: pipelines are up 15% YoY, with energy, power and utilities, healthcare, defence and data-center support named as drivers. But the borrowers are increasingly investment-grade and credit spreads are tight, so each new dollar of loans earns less. That is why the margin outlook was trimmed even as loan growth improved. The other half of the rate story is the bond book: CET1 including AOCI of 9.5% versus 10.7% excluding it, and management's warning of more AOCI pressure this quarter, show that unrealised securities losses still tie up capital that could otherwise go to buybacks.

Returns above target — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

Regions earned a 19.0% ROATCE in Q2 (20.2% adjusted) against a long-term target of 16–18%, and management said the beat came from better credit and strong execution. The bull framing: a bank that clears its own return target with a 56.9% adjusted efficiency ratio, falling charge-offs and a 4.20% yield is being priced at 11.6x earnings, below the industry's 12.81x, because the market is extrapolating competition that has not yet shown up in deposit costs (flat guidance for Q3). The bear framing: above-target returns at low charge-offs are the definition of a cyclical peak. The 2.07x tangible book multiple already pays for them, and the three things that would pull returns back to target — credit, deposit costs, margin mix — are all pointing the wrong way at once. The October 16 print will show which framing the data supports: watch NIM against 3.70%, interest-bearing deposit costs against 1.69%, and net charge-offs against 0.42%.
