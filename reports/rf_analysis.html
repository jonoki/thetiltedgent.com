01 Company Overview

Regions is a Birmingham, Alabama bank that has spent fifty-five years becoming the default checking account of the American South. It began in 1971 as First Alabama Bancshares — a merger of three Alabama banks, the oldest dating to 1856 — took the Regions name in 1994, then doubled itself twice: Union Planters in 2004 ($5.9B) and AmSouth in 2006 ($10B). It needed $3.5B of TARP money in the crisis and repaid it in April 2012. Today it earns a trailing $7.33B of revenue and $2.21B of net income, is worth $23.84B (850.00M shares × the October 21 close) and has been an S&P 500 constituent since August 28, 1998.

The hand Regions holds is its deposit base. In Q3 2026 it paid 1.70% on interest-bearing deposits, a figure the company calls peer-leading, and that cheap funding is why a plain-vanilla lender earns a 21% return on average tangible common equity (22% adjusted) — above the 16–18% target management restated in March 2026. The question the market is asking is whether that edge holds. At a March 2026 conference management described competition in its markets as intensifying, with big banks such as JPMorgan building branches in its footprint, and about half of Q2's loan growth went to investment-grade borrowers, whose loans carry tighter spreads. The October 16 results beat the earnings estimate and kept the guidance ranges, though average deposits slipped 1.2% on the quarter. The stock, which closed at $32.40 on July 16, its highest close since 2007, settled at $28.05 on October 21, down 13.4% from that close. This report is about whether the South's cheapest deposits are still cheap.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Low-cost Southern deposit base Dense branch footprint, 15 states Primary-account relationships ~$64B available liquidity Uninsured deposits ~170% covered Growing Sun Belt markets

Regions' edge is not a product; it is habit. Millions of households and small businesses across the South keep their operating cash where they always have, and that inertia lets Regions pay 1.70% on interest-bearing deposits and earn a 3.68% margin. That is why ROATCE runs at 21%. The weakness is that habit can be priced away. At a March 2026 conference management described competition in its markets as intensifying and named JPMorgan among the big banks building branches in its footprint. Deposit franchises are hard to build and slow to lose, but they are not free to defend. Regions is now spending on branches, bankers and a new core deposit system to hold its position.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Competition for Southern deposits. Management expects an interest-bearing deposit beta in the mid-to-high 30s through the rising-rate cycle (Q3 call). The low-cost deposit base is the whole moat, and national banks opening branches in Regions' markets are going after exactly that.
02
Margin squeeze from the loan mix. About half of Q2's loan growth was investment-grade credit, which carries tighter spreads (Q2 call). Growth that earns a lower margin can leave net interest income growing more slowly than the loan book. At the July Q2 call the full-year NII guide was 2.5–4%.
03
Rates and the bond book. Tangible book value per share fell to $12.65 at September 30 from $13.78 at June 30, even as the regulatory CET1 ratio rose to 10.8%: the regulatory ratio leaves out unrealised bond losses and book value counts them. Q3 also carried $15M of securities losses from repositioning the portfolio, down from $40M in Q2.
04
A core deposit-system replacement. The new cloud deposit platform starts an associate pilot in late October 2026 and a larger pilot in the first quarter of 2027; customers convert over 2027, finishing around the end of the second quarter or into the third (Q3 call). Core conversions are where banks lose customers, and management says it must finish this one before it considers buying another bank.

Notable One-Off Items

The main one-off in Q3 2026 is $15M of net securities losses (against $40M in Q2), which is why reported non-interest income of $675M sits below adjusted non-interest income of $690M. It also opens the gap between GAAP EPS of $0.71 and adjusted EPS of $0.73. Banks take losses like these on purpose: they sell low-yielding bonds and reinvest at higher yields, a small hit now for more interest income later. Expense adjustments were nil this quarter ($1,118M reported and adjusted). The second distortion is buybacks: TTM EPS rose 12.3% against net income growth of 7.7%. Per-share growth is real, but a good part of it comes from a shrinking share count.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The bull case pays 11.0x trailing earnings and 2.22x tangible book for a bank earning a 21% ROATCE (22% adjusted), above its own 16–18% target. It funds itself at 1.70% on interest-bearing deposits, runs a 55.8% adjusted efficiency ratio and saw credit improve again in Q3 (net charge-offs down to 0.29%, NPLs to 0.59%). The 4.28% dividend yield is twice the industry's, on a 47% payout after a 13% dividend increase in July, and the buyback yield is 4.50%. Management has guided the Q4 margin to about 3.70% (3.68% in Q3) with fixed-rate asset repricing still to come, and it has said it is not interested in buying another bank while the deposit-system conversion is under way. After a 13% pullback from the July high, the average target is 13.0% above the price.

Bear Case

The bear case says the moat is being contested in plain sight. Management calls competition in its markets intensifying, with big banks such as JPMorgan building branches there, and about half of Q2's loan growth was investment-grade credit at tighter spreads. Average deposits fell 1.2% in Q3, fee income is tracking the low end of its 3–5% guide, mortgage is weak, and a Q2 beat still produced a down day. Street sentiment is lukewarm: 14 of 23 analysts rate it Hold, 3 rate it Sell or Strong Sell, the lowest target ($27) is below the price, and Citi and Truist cut their targets after the print. A 21% ROATCE is flattered by charge-offs at 0.29% against a full-year guide of 40–50bps. If credit normalises toward the top of that guide or above it, returns fall back toward the 16–18% target and the case for a premium to tangible book weakens.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deposit competition erodes the funding edge. Two-thirds of revenue is net interest income, and the 1.70% interest-bearing deposit cost is the reason ROATCE is 21%. Management expects a deposit beta in the mid-to-high 30s through the rising-rate cycle. A faster pass-through, forced by new entrants and larger banks building in Regions' markets, hits margin directly.
02
Margin guidance can slip. Management has guided the margin to about 3.70% at year-end since the July Q2 call, and the October 16 call kept it (Q3 NIM 3.68%), but the guide assumes the next Fed rate hike comes in December. A miss would put the NII growth guide under pressure, since net interest income is 65.3% of adjusted revenue.
03
Credit normalisation from a low base. Net charge-offs of 0.29% and a 1.61% allowance reflect a benign quarter. The allowance is already at about the 1.62% management gave in July as its CECL day-one equivalent (the reserve level when the current loss-accounting rule took effect), and on the Q3 call Turner said he expects the ratio to be stable from here. A turn in office, transportation or consumer credit would raise provisions with little cushion left to release.
Industry Commentary
The fight for Sun Belt deposits
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Southern and Texas banking markets are growing, and growth draws competitors. At a March 2026 conference management described competition in its markets as intensifying, with big banks such as JPMorgan building branches in its footprint. Regions is answering with about 135–150 new branches over four to five years, more bankers and a new deposit platform. That is the cost of keeping a cheap funding base. Expense growth is guided at 1.5–3.5% for 2026, and management expects positive adjusted operating leverage for the full year (Q3 call).

Rates, spreads and the investment-grade loan mix
ONGOING HEADWIND

Commercial loan demand is real: pipelines were up roughly 15% from a year earlier at Q2, with power and utilities, manufacturing, government and retail trade among the lending categories named, and average loans rose 0.5% in Q3. But about half of Q2's loan growth was investment-grade credit at tighter spreads, so each new dollar of loans earns less. That caps how much loan growth can lift the margin. The other half of the rate story is the bond book: tangible book value per share, which counts unrealised securities losses, fell to $12.65 in Q3 from $13.78 in Q2 while the regulatory CET1 ratio, which leaves them out, rose to 10.8%.

Returns above target — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

Regions earned a 21% ROATCE in Q3 (22% adjusted) against a long-term target of 16–18%, and the Q3 release calls its returns best-in-class in its peer group. The bull framing: a bank that clears its own return target with a 55.8% adjusted efficiency ratio, falling charge-offs and a 4.28% yield is being priced at 11.0x earnings, below the industry's 12.15x, because the market is extrapolating competition that has not yet shown up in deposit costs (1.70% in Q3, up 1 basis point). The bear framing: above-target returns at low charge-offs are the definition of a cyclical peak. The 2.22x tangible book multiple already pays for them, and the three things that would pull returns back to target — credit, deposit costs, margin mix — have little room left to improve.
