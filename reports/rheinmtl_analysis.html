01 Company Overview

Rheinmetall started life in 1889 as the Rheinische Metallwaaren- und Maschinenfabrik, set up to supply ammunition to the German Empire. Today it is a defence company and nothing else: the car-parts business it ran for decades was reclassified as a discontinued operation in December 2025 and agreed in June 2026 to be sold to a subsidiary of the Munich investor AEQUITA, with closing expected in the fourth quarter. What remains builds armoured vehicles (Boxer, Puma, Lynx), military trucks, tank and artillery guns, ammunition and the powder that goes into it, air-defence systems (Skynex, Skyranger), battlefield digital networks, satellites and, since buying the Bremen shipbuilder NVL in February 2026, warships. FY2025 sales were €9,935M, up 29%, with an operating result of €1,841M (18.5% margin). The shares have sat in Germany's DAX since 2023 and the EURO STOXX 50 since 2025.

The poker frame is simple: the order book is a monster hand, and the market has spent a year arguing about the price of the chips. Rheinmetall's backlog was €78.0B at September 30, 2026, about 6.4 years of trailing sales, and management has guided to roughly €50B of sales by 2030 at a margin above 20%. Yet the stock closed at €845.40 on November 9, down 57.4% from its €1,984.50 month-end peak of September 2025, after a year that brought a slow first quarter, the government's cancellation of the F126 frigate programme, €1.7B of negative operating free cash flow in nine months and, on November 4, a second cut to 2026 guidance. The latest quarter itself was not the problem: the third-quarter operating result of €553M rose 62% and beat the consensus Investing.com reported. What keeps slipping is timing. Germany's Arminius order for Boxer vehicles, the largest in the pipeline, will not be placed this year, and some ammunition sales wait on customer acceptance tests. The question is whether the backlog turns into cash at the margins promised, or whether 2025 priced in a decade of perfect execution.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

€78.0B backlog Ammunition & powder capacity Boxer, Puma & Leopard 2 A8 work German home customer (41% of sales) Plants across Europe incl. the east Skynex / Skyranger air defence Multi-year framework contracts

The real moat is capacity that already exists. Armies rearming in a hurry buy from suppliers whose factories are already running, and Rheinmetall has them, from Unterlüß to Spain, Hungary and South Africa, with more being built: a new artillery plant in Lithuania, a medium-calibre factory in Albacete, Spain, that started production in October, and the UK Gun Hall in Telford, opened on October 22. Framework agreements then lock in call-offs for years: the frame backlog alone was €22.9B at end-2025. Germany's own budget adds visibility: the July 2 investor recap cited a 2027 draft defence budget of close to €145B, about 21% above 2026. The weakness is the flip side of the same fact: a handful of governments decide the revenue, and they can delay, re-scope or cancel, as the F126 cancellation and now the Arminius delay showed.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Governments write the cheques and can tear them up. Germany took 41.3% of sales in the first nine months of 2026 and, by the CEO's account on the August 6 call, about 60% of the backlog. The F126 cancellation cost up to €300M of 2026 sales; the slipping Arminius Boxer order, now expected to be placed in 2027, is the first reason the company gave for the second cut. German procurement decisions figured in both cuts to this year's guidance.
02
Cash is lagging profit. Operating free cash flow over nine months was −€1,698M while the operating result was +€1,339M. In November the company dropped its 2026 target of a cash conversion rate (free cash flow ÷ operating result) above 40% and now expects only a positive figure for the year; the September investor presentation still aimed for above 50% on a three-year rolling basis by 2030. The balance sheet went from net cash of €369M at end-2025 to net debt of €2.90B at September 30, part of it funding NVL.
03
Ukraine is a customer, not just a reason. The FY2025 release named Ukraine the most important customer country for Weapon and Ammunition, the highest-margin segment. Any change in the war, or in who pays for Ukraine's ammunition, reaches the profit line directly.
04
Execution at a scale never attempted. Going from €9.9B of sales in 2025 to about €50B by 2030 means new plants, a workforce the company plans to take from 34,000 to roughly 60,000–70,000 (headcount, continuing operations; September 2026 presentation) and a shipbuilder it has owned since February. Construction of several energetics plants has already slipped into 2027, and customer acceptance tests on artillery ammunition lots are now running late too.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the car-parts exit: the civilian business lost €341M in FY2025 (mostly an impairment when it was classified for sale) and €160M in H1 2026, which is why total EPS (€15.62 TTM to June 30) sat far below continuing EPS (€26.47 on the same basis). The sale to AEQUITA is priced provisionally at €350M and is expected to close in the fourth quarter. Second, NVL: the shipbuilder added €679M of sales and €71M of operating result in the seven months from March, and raised goodwill from €1,354M to €2,198M by June 30, so 2026 growth is not all organic. Third, convertible bonds: holders converted €239M of bonds by the end of June and the last €4M in early July, raising the share count by 787,033 to 46,789,567 (about 1.7%) and moving that debt into equity.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 9, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of €78.62 to the November 9, 2026 close of €845.40, Rheinmetall's share price rose 975.3%, a multiple of 10.8. Dividends of €32.90 per share were paid along the way (€3.30, €4.30, €5.70, €8.10 and €11.50, each May from 2022 to 2026). The DAX rose 74.0% over the same window (15,100.13 → 26,267.19). The shape is three acts: a step change in February–March 2022, when the shares gained 45.1% and then 43.8% in consecutive months around Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine; a steady climb through 2023 and 2024; and a vertical 2025, when the stock went from €614.60 at end-2024 to €1,984.50 at end-September 2025, a 223% gain in nine months. Since then it has given back 57.4%.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how stretched 2025 was: it reached 97.4 in May 2025, the highest reading in the series. The unwind took it to 26.4 in June 2026, the lowest, and after a brief October bounce it stands at 28.6 now, back in oversold territory. The price is 28.6% below its 10-month moving average of about €1,184, so the long-term trend is still down. No month-end close since January 2026 (€1,781.50) has been higher.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a backlog of €78.0B, more than six years of sales, at 0.51 times its face value, with management guiding to sales roughly five times 2025's level by 2030 at margins above 20%. Profit is growing faster than sales: operating result +68% in nine months on sales +38%, a 17.5% Q3 margin (14.8% a year earlier), and the highest-margin segment (ammunition, 23.4% over nine months, 29% in FY2025) is the one adding capacity fastest. The November cut moved sales and cash into 2027 rather than cancelling them: Arminius is in its final negotiations, and a down payment the CEO put at more than €3B in August would reverse much of the 2026 cash drain in a single quarter. Germany's draft 2027 defence budget is up about 21%, NATO's core target is rising to 3.5% of GDP, and even the lowest analyst target MarketScreener shows, €900, is above the banner-date close.

Bear Case

The 2025 price assumed flawless execution, and 2026 has delivered the opposite: an 8% first quarter, a cancelled frigate programme, two guidance cuts (sales from €14.0–14.5B in March to about €13.2B in November), a dropped cash-conversion target, and €1.7B of cash out the door in nine months while net debt rose to €2.90B. A backlog is a promise from governments, and governments re-scope or take their time: the backlog fell from €80.5B in June to €78.0B in September. About 60% of it is German, the profit engine's most important customer country in 2025 was Ukraine, and the €50B 2030 target requires a workforce roughly double today's and plants that are already running late. Even after a 57% fall from the peak the shares trade at 28.8 times trailing continuing earnings and a free-cash-flow yield of 0.8%. If the war in Ukraine winds down or budgets slip, the price investors pay for each euro of earnings could fall further.

Top 3 Risks

01
Procurement decisions. The F126 showed that a signed programme can disappear; Arminius, the single largest order in the pipeline, has already slipped from 2026 into 2027 and still needs a parliamentary vote. A delayed German programme headed the reasons for November's cut to 2026 guidance; another delay would move the 2027 numbers.
02
Cash conversion. Nine-month operating free cash flow of −€1,698M; the target for 2026 is now only a positive full-year figure, which needs more than €1.7B in Q4 without any Arminius down payment. If customer payments do not arrive as management expects, net debt, €2.90B at September 30, keeps absorbing the growth.
03
Ukraine exposure and the peace question. Ukraine was the most important customer country of the highest-margin segment in 2025. A ceasefire would not end European rearmament, but it would change the urgency, and the stock is priced on urgency.
Industry Commentary
European budgets: the tailwind is written into law
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Rheinmetall's September 2026 investor presentation notes that NATO is lifting its core spending target to 3.5% of GDP by 2035, and that Germany is ordering now to reach 3.5% as early as 2029. The July 2 recap cited a German 2027 draft defence budget of close to €145B, about 21% above 2026. The EU's SAFE loan programme is already producing orders: Romania alone placed Lynx vehicles (298), four naval vessels (€920M) and medium-calibre ammunition under it in H1 2026. Demand is not the question for this industry. Turning it into deliveries, on time and at the promised margin, is.

Procurement reality: budgets are not orders
ONGOING HEADWIND

The 2026 lesson for European defence stocks is that a bigger budget does not guarantee any one contractor's programme. The F126 frigate was cancelled outright over delays and expected cost overruns; Rheinmetall's recap said the ministry also cited possible recourse issues with the shipbuilder Damen. On the August 6 call a Morgan Stanley analyst asked about press reports of a German draft showing lower cash outlays for ammunition and land systems; Papperger answered that most tank-ammunition contracts were already signed, that nobody had called a halt to the talks on further rounds, and that with the ammunition backlog as high as it is he saw no impact over the next years. In November the German pace hit Rheinmetall again: the Arminius Boxer order, expected for December, is now expected in early 2027 (Defence Industry Europe reported that a detailed price audit is likely to push approval past year-end), and customer acceptance tests on artillery ammunition lots are running late. Across the listed peer group, one-year price returns run from +24.1% (Saab) to −44.5% (Renk), averaging −4.9%, while the DAX rose 11.4% and Rheinmetall fell 51.7%. One way to read the gap: investors are still paying for European defence, and have marked down the one company whose own timetable keeps slipping.

Valuation after the unwind: bull and bear framing
DEBATE

At €845.40 Rheinmetall trades at 28.8 times trailing continuing EPS of €29.34, with an average analyst target 66% higher. The bull framing: earnings are growing faster than the share price is falling. Even the cut guidance implies about 33% sales growth in 2026 at an 18.5% margin, so the multiple on 2026 earnings will be lower than the trailing one, and the 2030 plan does not need to be met in full for today's price to look low. The bear framing: the 2030 plan is a five-fold scale-up dependent on a few governments, guidance has been cut twice in three months, nine months burned €1.7B of cash, and a P/E near 29 still assumes most of it goes right. The test is not the P/E. It is whether the Capital Markets Day on November 25–27 holds the 2030 targets, whether 2026 operating free cash flow ends positive, and whether Arminius is signed in early 2027 as the company now expects.
