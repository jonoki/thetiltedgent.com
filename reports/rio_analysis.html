01 Company Overview

Rio Tinto digs up the raw materials of steel, wiring and cans: iron ore from the Pilbara in Western Australia and Canada, copper (Oyu Tolgoi in Mongolia, Kennecott in Utah and a 30% share of Escondida in Chile), and aluminium from bauxite mines, alumina refineries and smelters in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and Iceland, now joined by a lithium business bought with Arcadium Lithium in March 2025. It is also building Simandou in Guinea, a high-grade iron ore project that shipped its first ore in December 2025.

One company, two parents. Rio Tinto is a dual-listed companies (DLC) structure. Rio Tinto plc trades in London (it is one of the 10 largest companies in the FTSE 100, per Rio's own DLC page) and Rio Tinto Limited trades on the ASX in Sydney. The two were merged by contract in 1995 (then RTZ and CRA) and took the Rio Tinto name in 1997. A sharing agreement fixes an equalisation ratio of 1:1: the two boards are the same people, shareholders of both companies vote as one body on shared matters, and dividends are equalised. The NYSE ticker RIO is an ADS over one plc share, so it tracks the London line in US dollars. The two lines do not trade at the same price. At the October 2 closes a Limited share cost US$114.19 (A$164.81 × 0.6929) against US$93.14 for a plc share (7,056p × 1.3200), a 22.6% premium for the Sydney line (our calculation). Rio's DLC page points to one difference between the two: Rio Tinto Limited pays fully franked dividends, whose Australian tax credits only Australian tax residents can use. The Board considered a shareholder resolution to unify the structure in February 2025 and unanimously concluded it was not in the Group's best interests (2025 Annual Report).

The latest reported period is the first half of 2026 (January 1 – June 30), published July 29, 2026; Rio reports full results twice a year and production every quarter. Consolidated revenue was US$31.03B, up 15.5%; underlying EBITDA was US$14.83B (+28%); net earnings were US$6.66B (+47%) and underlying earnings US$6.85B (+43%). Copper, Aluminium & Lithium together made 57% of reportable-segment EBITDA (US$9.02B of US$15.79B). Iron ore is still the largest single business, and the biggest swing factor: Rio's own sensitivity table puts a 10% move in the iron ore price at US$2.34B of annual underlying EBITDA, against US$1.13B for copper and US$1.26B for aluminium. Customers in Greater China bought 57.9% of H1 2026 revenue.

Against expectations. Rio publishes its own analyst consensus, compiled by Visible Alpha from 13 brokers, as at July 24, 2026. Underlying earnings of US$6,851M beat the US$6,626M consensus by 3.4%, and underlying EPS of 421.4¢ beat 408¢. Underlying EBITDA of US$14,826M came in 2.0% below the US$15,131M forecast. The 211¢ interim dividend beat the 204¢ expected, operating cash flow of US$9,173M beat US$8,740M, and net debt of US$14.06B was below the US$15.21B forecast. The Pilbara unit cost of US$25.0/t was lower than the US$26.2/t analysts had pencilled in. Chief Executive Simon Trott, in the results release: "We achieved a step-change in performance in the first half, which, alongside favourable commodity prices, delivered a 28 per cent increase in underlying EBITDA and a 75 per cent rise in free cash flow." On costs: "We have already banked $870 million of productivity benefits and are on track to reach an annualised run-rate of $1.8 billion by year-end, with significantly more to come as our multi-year program continues to scale."

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Pilbara integrated mine-rail-port system Negative-cost copper (by-product credits) Oyu Tolgoi underground ramp-up Hydro-powered Kitimat smelter Simandou high-grade ore Net gearing 16% Access to London and Sydney capital

In commodities the durable advantage is where you sit on the cost curve. Rio's Pilbara system turned a realised US$85.2 per wet tonne into US$7.0B of half-year EBITDA at a US$25.0/t cash cost; when prices fall, higher-cost mines close first. Copper is even better placed in this half, with by-products paying for more than the cash cost of the copper itself. The balance sheet is conservative: net debt US$14.1B, a net gearing ratio of 16% and cash and liquid investments of US$9.1B at June 30, 2026. The weakness is concentration: iron ore is still the largest EBITDA line, Greater China takes most of its sales, and Simandou adds a large block of new supply into that same market.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Iron ore still drives the earnings. A 10% iron ore move is worth US$2.34B of annual EBITDA, more than copper (US$1.13B) and aluminium (US$1.26B). Iron Ore segment EBITDA fell 1% in H1 while copper rose 84%, so the diversification story depends on copper prices staying high.
02
China bought 57.9% of H1 2026 revenue (US$17.97B of US$31.03B), up from 55.2% a year earlier. Rio's own Simandou adds up to 60 Mtpa of high-grade ore by H2 2028 into that market.
03
Costs move with currencies and diesel. Pilbara unit costs rose despite productivity gains because the Australian dollar strengthened and diesel nearly doubled. Kennecott's furnace breach and the Oyu Tolgoi tax dispute show how much single-site risk sits in the copper growth.
04
Capital calls are heavy. Rio's share of capital investment was US$5.0B in H1 2026 (guide for the year: up to US$11B). Lithium burned US$551M of free cash flow in the half, and the closure provision stands at US$18.9B.

Notable One-Off Items

The gap between statutory and underlying earnings is small this year. In H1 2026 net earnings of US$6.66B compare with underlying earnings of US$6.85B; the main excluded item was a US$0.2B foreign exchange and derivative loss. In FY2025 net earnings fell 14% to US$9.97B while underlying earnings were flat at US$10.87B, with US$0.2B of net impairment charges (after tax) and the absence of 2024's gains on disposals among the excluded items. Three items do matter for cash: the US$7.6B Arcadium Lithium acquisition (March 2025), funded largely by a US$9B bond issue, which is why net debt rose from US$5.5B to US$14.4B during 2025; the US$443M Oyu Tolgoi tax payment in H1 2026, made under protest; and a lower 2026 effective tax rate (~25%, from ~30% previously guided) that Rio expects to return to ~30% from 2027.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

What the line shows. Month-end closes of the NYSE ADS, which represents one Rio Tinto plc share throughout the window, from Yahoo Finance. There were no splits or spin-offs in the window. Dividends are not added back, and Rio pays a lot of them (402¢ per share for 2025 alone), so price return understates what holders earned.

From the October 2021 close of $63.27 to the October 2, 2026 settled close of $94.21, the ADR gained 48.9% on price. Over the same window the FTSE 100 rose 44.5%, the ASX 200 18.5% and SPY 67.6%. In local currency the London shares rose 54.7% (4,560.5p → 7,056p) and the Sydney shares 82.5% (A$90.29 → A$164.81). The chart has two phases. For almost four years the stock moved sideways between $53 and $81, following iron ore and China's property slump; the low was $53.35 in October 2022. Then, from the June 2025 close of $58.33 to the May 2026 high of $106.39, it rose 82.4%, while copper and aluminium prices rose and copper output from Oyu Tolgoi grew. It has since given back 11.4%. The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 94.8 in February 2026, the most overbought reading in the window, and is now 53.5, neutral. The 10-month moving average, $97.32, is above the price; September was the first month-end below it since July 2025, so the long uptrend has stalled.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Rio is turning from an iron ore company into a metals company without giving up the cash machine. Copper EBITDA rose 84% in H1 2026 at a negative C1 cost, Oyu Tolgoi is ramping toward 500 kt a year, aluminium EBITDA rose 31% on higher prices, and lithium adds a third growth line by 2028. Simandou adds a high-grade iron ore stream from 2026. Productivity savings (US$1.8B run rate targeted by year-end) and US$5–10B of planned cash release support the balance sheet, net gearing is 16%, and the policy of returning 40–60% of underlying earnings gives a ~4.9% trailing yield. The ADR trades at 11.9x underlying TTM earnings and 11.2x the FY2026 consensus. The half beat Rio's own published consensus on underlying earnings (+3.4%), the dividend and the Pilbara unit cost, and since then Tomago and Bell Bay have secured power to 2038 and 2031.

Bear Case

Iron ore still sets the earnings: a 10% move is worth US$2.34B of EBITDA, and the segment's EBITDA already slipped 1% in H1 2026, when group underlying EBITDA came in 2.0% below Rio's published consensus while costs rose with the Australian dollar and diesel. Greater China takes 58% of revenue, and Simandou is about to add up to 60 Mtpa of high-grade supply into that market. The copper surge is a price story: production rose only 1% and Kennecott has a damaged smelter. Consensus sees FY2027 EPS 2.6% lower, the year Rio guides its tax rate back to ~30%. The stock has fallen 11.4% from its May high, sits below its 10-month average, and the analyst panel is split, with two named targets (Bernstein $89.50, Morgan Stanley $90) below the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
An iron ore price fall. At US$234M of annual EBITDA per 1% (one tenth of Rio's 10% sensitivity), a 20% drop from H1's US$92.6/dmt realised price would remove about US$4.7B, roughly 16% of TTM underlying EBITDA.
02
Execution on four fronts at once. Simandou commissioning, the Oyu Tolgoi ramp-up (and its Mongolian tax dispute), the lithium build-out and three Pilbara replacement mines all land in 2026–2028, with up to US$11B of 2026 capital investment guided.
03
Copper and gold prices reverse. Copper's 84% EBITDA jump came mostly from price (Rio cites a US$2B price benefit in the segment). A 10% copper fall costs US$1.13B a year, and lower gold raises the C1 cost, which Rio's full-year guide already puts at 30–50¢/lb against H1's −24.7¢.
Industry Commentary
Iron ore: steady prices, more supply coming
WATCH SIMANDOU

Rio's own market table shows the Platts iron ore benchmark (CFR China; 61% Fe from 2026, 62% Fe before) averaged US$105/t in H1 2026, against US$101 a year earlier, and fell from US$106 to US$99 across the half. The new factor is supply: Simandou's rail and port are built for up to 120 Mtpa of exports across both concessions, high-grade ore. Rio is on both sides of that trade, as the owner of the Pilbara and of 27 Mtpa of Simandou capacity. Low-cost producers keep earning through a price fall; the question for the Pilbara is how far prices fall.

Copper, aluminium and lithium: the diversification trade
PRICE TAILWIND

LME copper averaged 593¢/lb in H1 2026, up 39%; LME aluminium averaged US$3,382/t, up 33%; lithium carbonate averaged US$20,714/t, up 125% (Rio's market data table). These moves lifted Copper and Aluminium & Lithium past half of segment EBITDA. Rio's 2025 Annual Report names new power generation and grids as a significant driver of aluminium, copper and lithium demand, with AI data centres and robotics as new drivers. The Glencore talks, abandoned in February, showed it was also willing to buy scale, and it walked away when it could not agree terms that delivered value to its shareholders.

Costs: currency, diesel and closure
ONGOING HEADWIND

The Australian dollar averaged US$0.70 in H1 2026, against the 0.67 Rio's unit-cost guidance assumes, and Rio says diesel climbed from about US$85 to US$140 a barrel during the half, with energy costs raised by the Middle East conflict. Those two pushed Pilbara costs up even as volumes hit a post-2018 high. Smelters run on electricity, and two Australian smelters whose power contracts were running out now have a path, Tomago through a 10-year deal from 2029 to 2038 and Bell Bay through Hydro Tasmania supply and extra government support to the end of 2031. Longer term, Rio carries an US$18.9B provision for closing and rehabilitating mines, and spends about US$1B a year on it.
