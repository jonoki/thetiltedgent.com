01 Company Overview

A note on the calendar before anything else: Raymond James runs a fiscal year ending September 30. Everything labelled "FY2026" on this page covers October 2025 through September 2026, and the "fiscal third quarter of 2026" reported on July 22, 2026 is the calendar quarter ended June 30, 2026. The full fiscal year closes in roughly three weeks from the data date, and is reported on October 28, 2026.

Raymond James Financial is a diversified financial services firm built around a network of affiliated financial advisors, wrapped in four reported segments — Private Client Group, Capital Markets, Asset Management and Bank. FY2025 net revenues were $14.02B, up 9.5% from $12.80B in FY2024, with net income of $2.13B and diluted EPS of $10.30. The trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $15.24B of net revenues and $2.30B of net income. Total client assets under administration reached a record $1.92 trillion at June 30, 2026, up 17% year over year. At a $33.4B market capitalisation the firm ranks roughly #230 in the US by market value (estimated — a precise rank was not verified from sources fetched).

The central debate on this page is narrower than "is wealth management a good business." It is this: Raymond James is two businesses wearing one ticker. One is an asset-light advisory franchise that earns a fee on $1.92T of client assets and grows by recruiting advisors away from wirehouses. The other is a spread bank that funds a $56.2B loan book with $58.8B of client cash swept out of brokerage accounts at a rate the firm largely sets. The second business is where a disproportionate share of incremental profit comes from, and it is the one exposed to Federal Reserve policy and to a decade-long regulatory and litigation campaign over sweep yields. Section 02 and Section 04 take that apart.

This is the swing factor for the whole business model, and it deserves to be explained plainly. When a Raymond James client holds cash in a brokerage account, that cash is "swept" into deposit accounts — either at Raymond James Bank itself or, through the Raymond James Bank Deposit Program (RJBDP), at third-party banks. The firm pays the client one rate and earns another. The gap is almost pure pre-tax profit, and it flows through with essentially no incremental cost. That is why a few basis points of sweep rate move earnings more than a quarter of good investment-banking revenue does.

The numbers at June 30, 2026: domestic cash sweep and Enhanced Savings Program balances of $58.8 billion, up 7% YoY and up 2% sequentially. Average yield earned on RJBDP third-party bank balances was 2.75%, down from 2.96% a year earlier. Consolidated net interest income was ~$560 million in the quarter (interest income $994M less interest expense $434M), and the combined "net interest income plus RJBDP fees from third-party banks" line was $658 million, versus $656 million a year earlier — flat. Hold that thought: client assets grew 17% and sweep balances grew 7%, yet the economics of the cash book were flat year over year, because the yield came down. Bank net interest margin was 2.71%, down 3bp YoY and down 10bp sequentially. Against that, the loan book is genuinely growing: $56.2 billion net, up 13% YoY, with securities-based loans of $24.8 billion, up 34% — lending against client portfolios, which is low-loss-content collateral and the most natural extension of the PCG relationship.

The mechanism to watch: sweep balances × (yield earned − yield paid). Both terms move with Fed policy; the second is also subject to regulatory and litigation pressure across the industry to pay clients more.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
ADVISOR AFFILIATION CHOICE $1.92T CLIENT ASSETS CAPTIVE DEPOSIT FUNDING RECRUITING BRAND CLIENT SWITCHING COSTS VERTICAL AM INTEGRATION 22.5% TOTAL CAPITAL RATIO

The core advantage is advisor mobility without firm mobility. A Raymond James advisor who wants to go independent, join an RIA, or become an employee again can do all three inside the firm. That single structural feature does two things: it makes Raymond James an unusually attractive destination for wirehouse breakaways (hence $56B of prior-firm assets recruited in nine months), and it makes existing advisors unusually hard to poach, because the usual reason to leave — the affiliation model no longer fits — is solved internally. The advisor then brings the client, and the client brings the cash, and the cash funds the bank. Every layer of the moat is downstream of the first one.

The second-order advantage is funding cost. A commercial bank competes for deposits in an open market. Raymond James Bank is funded largely by cash that arrives automatically from brokerage accounts, at a rate the firm sets within regulatory and competitive limits. That is why a $56.2B loan book can support a 2.71% NIM and a 23.5% adjusted ROTCE without taking exotic credit risk.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Sweep-rate regulatory and litigation risk. The entire brokerage industry has faced regulatory scrutiny and private litigation over whether paying clients below-market rates on swept cash breaches a duty of care. Raymond James's own numbers show the sensitivity: average yield on third-party RJBDP balances fell from 2.96% to 2.75% year over year, and combined NII-plus-RJBDP-fees was flat at $658M despite 7% balance growth. A mandated or competitively forced increase in client payout rates would compress that line further, with no offsetting revenue.
02
Cash sorting and rate direction. Sweep balances are the residual after clients decide how much cash to leave uninvested. When short rates are high, clients "sort" cash into money-market funds and Treasuries; when rates fall, sweep balances stabilise but the yield earned on them falls too. There is no rate environment that is unambiguously good for this line — only trade-offs — and the 10bp sequential NIM decline in fiscal Q3 2026 is the visible edge of that.
03
Reduced operating disclosure. The firm has stopped publishing financial-advisor headcount quarterly; the last reported figure is 8,943 as of October 2025. Recruiting production and prior-firm assets are still disclosed, but headcount is the metric that reveals net attrition. Investors are now being asked to take growth on faith in the one number that would show if the recruiting machine were merely offsetting retirements.
04
Market-level beta in fee revenue. $1.15T of fee-based assets billed on prior-quarter-end balances means a sharp equity drawdown flows into revenue with a one-quarter lag and with no ability to cut the cost base as fast, since advisor payouts are the largest expense and are formulaic. The firm's 0.92 beta understates how equity-market-dependent the earnings actually are.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC — SEPTEMBER 10, 2026

RSI compares the size of recent gains with the size of recent losses and scores the result from 0 to 100. Above 70 means the stock has risen a lot in a short span and is conventionally called "overbought"; below 30 is "oversold." It measures momentum, not value — a stock can stay overbought for months in a genuine uptrend. Here it is computed on monthly closes over six months, so each reading summarises half a year of direction.

Over the 60 months from the October 2021 close of $98.59 to the September 10, 2026 close of $173.53, RJF returned +76.0% on price alone, plus a dividend yielding roughly 1.2–1.5% along the way. That is a compound price return of about 11.9% a year — a good outcome, achieved through a path that was anything but smooth. The stock spent the first two-thirds of the window going essentially nowhere: the October 2021 close of $98.59 was not durably exceeded until October 2024, three full years later. Everything the five-year chart shows in aggregate was earned in the final 23 months.

The reason is visible in the segment data. Between 2022 and mid-2024 the two engines were working against each other — rates rose, which helped the spread book but crushed capital-markets issuance and M&A, and then cash sorting pulled sweep balances down as clients moved idle cash into money funds. From late 2024 both engines turned at once: capital markets began recovering from a near-breakeven base, client assets compounded with the equity market, and the loan book grew 13% into securities-based lending. The +38.4% run from the September 2024 close of $122.46 to the November 2024 close of $169.28 is the sharpest re-rating in the window, and the 6-period RSI hit 87.6 that month — the most overbought reading of the five years, and duly followed by a 12-month consolidation.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The advisory franchise is compounding on its own merits: $1.92T of client assets (+17%), $1.15T of fee-based assets (+22%), $56B of prior-firm assets recruited in nine months, and 6.6% annualised organic net new assets. That is a business growing faster than the market it sits in, at 18.8% ROE and 23.5% adjusted ROTCE, trading on 15.1× trailing and 12.6× forward earnings with a 22.5% total capital ratio funding a buyback that retired 3.8% of the shares in a year. If the sweep book merely holds flat and Capital Markets keeps recovering from a depressed base, the earnings power is understated by the current multiple.

Bear Case

Strip out the spread book and the valuation looks different. Combined net interest income and third-party RJBDP fees were flat year over year at $658M despite 7% sweep balance growth, because the yield earned fell from 2.96% to 2.75%. Bank NIM dropped 10bp sequentially. This is the highest-incremental-margin revenue in the firm, it is structurally exposed to both the rate path and to industry-wide regulatory pressure to pay clients more on idle cash, and it is deteriorating at the margin. Add the withdrawal of quarterly advisor-headcount disclosure and nine of fourteen analysts on Hold, and the 15× multiple is a fair price rather than a cheap one.

Top 3 Risks

01
Sweep economics compress further. The $58.8B balance × spread equation is the single largest swing factor in pre-tax income, and both terms are outside management's full control. A combination of falling short rates and rising client payout rates — whether competitive or regulatory — would hit the highest-margin revenue line with no offsetting cost reduction.
02
An equity-market drawdown flows straight to revenue. $1.15T of fee-based assets billed on prior-quarter-end balances means a 20% market decline removes roughly a fifth of the largest recurring revenue line with a one-quarter lag, while advisor compensation — the biggest expense — is formulaic and does not fall as fast in percentage terms.
03
Recruiting economics inflate. The whole growth algorithm depends on recruiting advisors profitably. Competitors including Ameriprise have been raising recruitment packages, and Raymond James has increased its own recruiting spend. Deals are paid up front and amortised over years; if the price of an advisor rises faster than the revenue that advisor brings, the growth continues to look good in assets and worse in returns.
Industry Commentary
The cash-sweep squeeze
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Across the wealth-management industry, the spread earned on client cash has been the quiet engine of earnings growth since 2022 — and it is now under simultaneous pressure from three directions. Rates are being cut, which lowers the yield earned. Regulators and plaintiffs' firms have spent two years pressing brokerages on whether paying below-market rates on swept client cash is consistent with a duty of care, which raises the yield paid. And clients who learned to sort cash into money-market funds during the high-rate period have not unlearned it. Raymond James's own disclosure quantifies the squeeze precisely: average yield on third-party RJBDP balances fell from 2.96% to 2.75% YoY, bank NIM fell 10bp sequentially to 2.71%, and combined NII plus RJBDP fees was flat at $658M against $656M — despite sweep balances rising 7% to $58.8B. Balance growth is no longer translating into revenue growth on this line. That is the definition of a squeeze.

The advisor-recruiting war
EMERGING TAILWIND

The migration of advisors out of wirehouses and into independent and hybrid models has been running for a decade and shows no sign of exhausting. Raymond James is a structural beneficiary because it offers every affiliation model under one roof — and the FY2026 numbers show it collecting: $393M of recruited annual production on $56B+ of prior-firm client assets in nine months, against a trailing-twelve-month figure of $156M and $23B as of fiscal Q3. CEO Paul Shoukry has publicly described "a clear path to exceed the record results set in fiscal 2025." The tailwind has a cost, though: the industry is bidding up recruitment packages — Ameriprise has raised its own, and Raymond James has increased recruiting spend — and those deals are paid in cash today for revenue that arrives over years. The disclosure gap matters here, because with advisor headcount no longer reported quarterly, investors cannot see whether record gross recruiting is producing record net advisor growth or merely offsetting retirements from an ageing advisor population.

Bull versus bear, framed
ONGOING HEADWIND

The two themes above are the whole argument. The bull says: the advisory franchise is a compounding annuity — $1.92T of assets, 22% growth in fee-based balances, 6.6% organic net new assets, record recruiting — and the spread book is a bonus that is currently being marked at zero by a 12.6× forward multiple. The bear says: the spread book is not a bonus, it is where a disproportionate share of the incremental pre-tax dollar comes from, it is flat year over year and compressing sequentially, and the market is correctly refusing to pay up for the rest. Both readings are consistent with the same set of facts; they differ on whether the flat $658M line is a cyclical trough or a structural reset. The October 28, 2026 full-year print is the next opportunity to find out — watch bank NIM, sweep balances, and whether the firm restores advisor-headcount disclosure.
