01 Company Overview

A note on the calendar before anything else: Raymond James runs a fiscal year ending September 30. Everything labelled "FY2026" on this page covers October 2025 through September 2026, and the "fiscal fourth quarter of 2026" reported on October 28, 2026 is the calendar quarter ended September 30, 2026. That print closed the book on FY2026; the next report, fiscal Q1 2027, covers October through December 2026.

Raymond James Financial is a diversified financial services firm built around a network of affiliated financial advisors, wrapped in four reported segments — Private Client Group, Capital Markets, Asset Management and Bank. FY2026 net revenues were a record $15.79B, up 12% from $14.07B in FY2025, with net income to common shareholders of $2.37B and diluted EPS of $11.91 ($12.41 adjusted). Total client assets under administration reached a record $1.94 trillion at September 30, 2026, up 12% year over year. At a $30.2B market capitalisation the firm ranks #318 in the S&P 500 by market value.

The central debate on this page is narrower than "is wealth management a good business." It is this: Raymond James is two businesses wearing one ticker. One is an asset-light advisory franchise that earns a fee on $1.94T of client assets and grows by recruiting advisors away from wirehouses (the large national brokerages). The other is a spread bank that funds a $57.5B loan book with $58.0B of client cash swept out of brokerage accounts at a rate the firm largely sets. The second business is where a disproportionate share of incremental profit comes from, and it is the one exposed to Federal Reserve policy and to industry-wide regulatory and litigation pressure over sweep yields. Section 02 and Section 04 take that apart.

The advisory franchise, and the reason the other three segments exist. Raymond James runs a multi-affiliation model — employee advisors, independent contractors, RIA and custody channels — which is the structural difference versus a wirehouse: an advisor can change affiliation without changing firms. Total client assets under administration hit a record $1.94 trillion at September 30, 2026, up 12% YoY, and PCG assets in fee-based accounts reached $1.16 trillion, up 15% YoY. That fee-based figure is the one that matters for revenue quality: it converts market level into recurring, billed-in-advance revenue rather than transactional commissions.

Recruiting is the growth algorithm. This is the swing factor for the whole business model, and it deserves to be explained plainly. When a Raymond James client holds cash in a brokerage account, that cash is "swept" into deposit accounts — either at Raymond James Bank itself or, through the Raymond James Bank Deposit Program (RJBDP), at third-party banks. The firm pays the client one rate and earns another. The gap is almost pure pre-tax profit, and it flows through with essentially no incremental cost. That is why a small change in the sweep rate moves earnings more than a quarter of good investment-banking revenue does.

The numbers at September 30, 2026: domestic cash sweep and Enhanced Savings Program balances of $58.0 billion, up 3% YoY but down 1% from the prior quarter. Average yield earned on RJBDP third-party bank balances was 2.81% in the quarter, against 2.91% a year earlier (and 2.75% in the third quarter). Consolidated net interest income was ~$564 million in the quarter (interest income $1,034M less interest expense $470M), and the combined "net interest income plus RJBDP fees from third-party banks" line was $674 million, up 3% year over year. Client assets grew 12% and sweep balances 3%, so the cash book is growing more slowly than the advisory book. Bank net interest margin was 2.60%, down 0.11 percentage points both year over year and from the third quarter's 2.71%. Against that, the loan book keeps growing: $57.5 billion net, up 11% YoY, with securities-based loans of $26.2 billion, up 32% — loans secured by clients' own investment portfolios, which carry little credit risk and are the most natural extension of the PCG relationship. The quarter's credit provision was a benefit of $8 million.

The mechanism to watch: sweep balances × (yield earned − yield paid). Both terms move with Fed policy; the second is also subject to regulatory and litigation pressure across the industry to pay clients more.

Comparable cash-sweep businesses sit at LPL Financial and Charles Schwab; both run the same balance-times-spread model.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
ADVISOR AFFILIATION CHOICE $1.94T CLIENT ASSETS CAPTIVE DEPOSIT FUNDING RECRUITING BRAND CLIENT SWITCHING COSTS VERTICAL AM INTEGRATION 24.2% TOTAL CAPITAL RATIO

The core advantage is advisor mobility without firm mobility. A Raymond James advisor who wants to go independent, join an RIA, or become an employee again can do all three inside the firm. That single structural feature does two things: it makes Raymond James an unusually attractive destination for wirehouse breakaways (domestic net new assets were $98.3B in fiscal 2026, up 88%), and it makes existing advisors unusually hard to poach, because the usual reason to leave — the affiliation model no longer fits — is solved internally. The advisor then brings the client, and the client brings the cash, and the cash funds the bank. Every layer of the moat is downstream of the first one.

The second-order advantage is funding cost. A commercial bank competes for deposits in an open market. Raymond James Bank is funded largely by cash that arrives automatically from brokerage accounts, at a rate the firm sets within regulatory and competitive limits. That is why a $57.5B loan book can support a 2.60% NIM and a 23.0% adjusted ROTCE without taking exotic credit risk.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Sweep-rate regulatory and litigation risk. The entire brokerage industry has faced regulatory scrutiny and private litigation over whether paying clients below-market rates on swept cash breaches a duty of care. Raymond James's own numbers show the sensitivity: average yield on third-party RJBDP balances was 2.81% against 2.91% a year earlier, and the combined net-interest-plus-RJBDP-fees line grew only 3% to $674M while client assets grew 12%. A mandated or competitively forced increase in client payout rates would compress that line further, with no offsetting revenue.
02
Cash sorting and rate direction. Sweep balances are the residual after clients decide how much cash to leave uninvested. When short rates are high, clients "sort" cash into money-market funds and Treasuries; when rates fall, sweep balances stabilise but the yield earned on them falls too. There is no rate environment that is unambiguously good for this line — only trade-offs — and the 0.11-point sequential NIM decline in fiscal Q4 2026 (2.71% to 2.60%) is the visible edge of that.
03
Reduced operating disclosure. The firm now reports financial-advisor headcount once a year rather than quarterly; the latest figure is 8,943 at September 30, 2025. Recruiting production and prior-firm assets are still disclosed, but headcount is the metric that reveals net attrition, and with one reading a year it is slow to show whether recruiting is merely offsetting retirements.
04
Market-level exposure in fee revenue. $1.16T of fee-based assets whose quarter-end level sets the next quarter's fees means a sharp equity drawdown flows into revenue with a one-quarter lag and with no ability to cut the cost base as fast, since advisor payouts are the largest expense and are formulaic. The firm's 0.95 beta understates how equity-market-dependent the earnings actually are.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC — OCTOBER 30, 2026

RSI compares the size of recent gains with the size of recent losses and scores the result from 0 to 100. Above 70 means the stock has risen a lot in a short span and is conventionally called "overbought"; below 30 is "oversold." It measures momentum, not value — a stock can stay overbought for months in a genuine uptrend. Here it is computed on monthly closes over six months, so each reading summarises half a year of direction.

Over the 59 months from the November 2021 close of $98.29 to the October 30, 2026 close of $157.22, RJF returned +60.0% on price alone, plus a dividend (a 1.37% yield today). That is a compound price return of about 10.0% a year — a good outcome, achieved through a path that was anything but smooth. The stock was still closing below its starting level as late as October 2023 ($95.44), two years in; it first closed above $150 in November 2024 and reached a monthly-close high of $178.17 in August 2026. September 2026 then gave back 11.3% in a month, and the October 30 close is 11.8% below that high.

The reason is visible in the segment data. Between 2022 and mid-2024 the two engines were working against each other — rates rose, which helped the spread book but crushed capital-markets issuance and M&A, and then cash sorting pulled sweep balances down as clients moved idle cash into money funds. From late 2024 both engines turned at once: capital markets began recovering, client assets compounded with the equity market, and the loan book grew into securities-based lending. The +38.2% run from the September 2024 close of $122.46 to the November 2024 close of $169.28 was the sharpest rally in the window, and the 6-period RSI hit 87.6 that month — the most overbought reading of the window — followed by a 19.0% pullback to $137.04 by April 2025.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The advisory franchise is compounding on its own merits: $1.94T of client assets (+12%), $1.16T of fee-based assets (+15%), $98.3B of domestic net new assets in fiscal 2026 (+88%) and a record year of advisor recruiting, at 18.7% ROE and 23.0% adjusted ROTCE, trading on 13.2× trailing and 11.0× forward earnings with a 24.2% total capital ratio funding $1.25B of buybacks in the year. If the sweep book merely holds flat and Capital Markets keeps recovering, the earnings power is understated by the current multiple.

Bear Case

Strip out the spread book and the valuation looks different. The combined net interest income and third-party RJBDP fees line grew only 3% to $674M while client assets grew 12%, because the yield earned fell from 2.91% to 2.81%. Bank NIM fell 0.11 percentage points in a quarter, to 2.60%. This is the highest-incremental-margin revenue in the firm, it is structurally exposed to both the rate path and to industry-wide regulatory pressure to pay clients more on idle cash, and it is compressing at the margin. Add advisor headcount now reported only once a year, non-compensation expenses guided up about 8% for fiscal 2027 and nine of fifteen analysts on Hold, and the 13× multiple is a fair price rather than a cheap one.

Top 3 Risks

01
Sweep economics compress further. The $58.0B balance × spread equation is the single largest swing factor in pre-tax income, and both terms are outside management's full control. A combination of falling short rates and rising client payout rates — whether competitive or regulatory — would hit the highest-margin revenue line with no offsetting cost reduction.
02
An equity-market drawdown flows straight to revenue. $1.16T of fee-based assets whose quarter-end level sets the next quarter's fees means a 20% market decline removes roughly a fifth of the largest recurring revenue line with a one-quarter lag, while advisor compensation — the biggest expense — is formulaic and does not fall as fast in percentage terms.
03
Recruiting economics inflate. The whole growth algorithm depends on recruiting advisors profitably. Raymond James's recruiting and retention-related compensation reached $460M in fiscal 2026 ($125M in the fourth quarter). Deals are paid up front and amortised over years; if the price of an advisor rises faster than the revenue that advisor brings, the growth continues to look good in assets and worse in returns.
Industry Commentary
The cash-sweep squeeze
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Across the wealth-management industry, the spread earned on client cash has been the quiet engine of earnings growth since 2022 — and it is under pressure from three directions. Falling short-term rates lower the yield earned. Regulators and plaintiffs' firms have pressed brokerages on whether paying below-market rates on swept client cash is consistent with a duty of care, which would raise the yield paid. And clients who learned to sort cash into money-market funds during the high-rate period have not unlearned it. Raymond James's own disclosure quantifies the squeeze: average yield on third-party RJBDP balances was 2.81% against 2.91% a year earlier, bank NIM fell 0.11 percentage points from the third quarter to 2.60%, and combined NII plus RJBDP fees grew only 3% to $674M — while sweep balances were up 3% YoY to $58.0B and down 1% on the quarter. Balance growth is translating into only modest revenue growth on this line.

The advisor-recruiting war
EMERGING TAILWIND

The migration of advisors out of wirehouses and into independent and hybrid models has been running for a decade and shows no sign of exhausting. Raymond James is a structural beneficiary because it offers every affiliation model under one roof — and the FY2026 numbers show it collecting: $98.3B of domestic net new assets in the year, up 88%, with $22.8B in the fourth quarter. CEO Paul Shoukry credited "solid financial advisor retention and record recruiting results." The tailwind has a cost, though: recruiting and retention-related compensation was $460M for the year ($125M in the fourth quarter), paid in cash today for revenue that arrives over years. The disclosure gap matters here, because with advisor headcount now reported only once a year, investors see only annually whether record gross recruiting is producing record net advisor growth or merely offsetting retirements from an ageing advisor population.

Bull versus bear, framed
ONGOING HEADWIND

The two themes above are the whole argument. The bull says: the advisory franchise is a compounding annuity — $1.94T of assets, 15% growth in fee-based balances, 6.4% organic net new assets for the year, record recruiting — and the spread book is a bonus that is currently being marked at zero by an 11.0× forward multiple. The bear says: the spread book is not a bonus, it is where a disproportionate share of the incremental pre-tax dollar comes from, it grew only 3% last quarter and is compressing sequentially, and the market is correctly refusing to pay up for the rest. Both readings are consistent with the same set of facts; they differ on whether the slow-growing $674M line is a cyclical trough or a structural reset. The next opportunity to find out is the fiscal Q1 2027 print (estimated late January 2027) — watch bank NIM, sweep balances, and net interest income plus RJBDP fees against management's outlook of about 1% growth on the quarter.
