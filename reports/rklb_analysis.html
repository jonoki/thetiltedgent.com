01 Company Overview

Rocket Lab designs and flies the small-lift Electron orbital rocket and its suborbital hypersonic-test variant HASTE, and it builds satellites, satellite subsystems and defence payloads through a business it calls Space Systems. FY2025 revenue was $601.8M, up 38% from $436.2M in FY2024; revenue over the twelve months to September 2026 was $881.9M, up 59%, and the third quarter alone set a record at $267.8M, up 73%. Electron was closing in on its 100th launch at the November 9, 2026 earnings call, and the company carries a record $3.57B backlog (work signed but not yet delivered) at a market value of $38.89B.

That $38.89B is the whole argument. Rocket Lab has never earned a profit, and on trailing revenue the stock trades at 44× sales and 39× enterprise value to sales (enterprise value is the market value plus debt, less cash). Electron and Space Systems together produce under a billion dollars of revenue a year at a 36% gross margin and a $227M trailing net loss. No arrangement of those numbers gets you to $39B. The multiple is Neutron — the medium-lift, reusable rocket built to compete for the payloads SpaceX's Falcon 9 carries today. And as of this close, Neutron has never flown. Its first-launch target has slipped from 2024 to 2025 to 2026, and on October 14, 2026 the company moved it again, to 2027; on the November 9 call Peter Beck tied the move to voids (gaps in the composite) found in a propellant tank's forward dome. The funding picture changed too: Rocket Lab sold $3.91B of new shares in the first nine months of 2026, largely to pay the cash half of its pending Iridium purchase, and ended September with $4.70B of cash and investments. Everything below is an attempt to size the gap between the business that exists and the business the price implies.

This is the part of Rocket Lab that actually generates the revenue and virtually all of the growth. It builds complete spacecraft (the Photon line of satellite buses — the frame, power and control systems a payload rides on — and the newer Flatellite flat-panel platform designed for mass-produced constellations), plus the subsystems that go inside other primes' satellites: solar arrays, reaction wheels, star trackers, radios, separation systems, and — following two acquisitions — electro-optical/infrared sensor payloads and laser optical inter-satellite links. The customer base has shifted decisively toward national security. Programme awards include the Space Development Agency Tranche 2 Transport Layer-Beta at $515M and Tranche 3 Tracking Layer at $816M, plus a $397M U.S. Space Force SB-AMTI award (satellites that track aircraft from space) for Flatellite spacecraft and over $160M across two contracts for geostationary satellites including a Space Domain Awareness mission. Electron is a 300 kg-class orbital launcher — the dedicated ride for a customer who does not want to wait for a rideshare slot and needs a specific orbit on a specific date. Rocket Lab flew 21 Electron/HASTE missions in FY2025, and Peter Beck told the November 9, 2026 call that Electron was closing in on its 100th launch. Pricing: Rocket Lab does not publish an average Electron price. One anchor exists: the Department of War's $190M block buy for 20 HASTE suborbital test flights — $9.5M per flight. Structural ceiling: a 300 kg payload class caps revenue per flight. Growth here requires either more flights per year or a bigger rocket — which is what Neutron is for.

Status as of this close: Neutron has not launched. The schedule history is the story. The vehicle was originally targeted for a 2024 debut. In November 2025 the debut was pushed to 2026. On January 21, 2026 the Stage 1 propellant tank ruptured during hydrostatic pressure testing — attributed to a manufacturing defect in the hand-laid composite process, with future production moving to automated fibre placement. In February 2026 the first-launch target moved to Q4 2026; in May management said the vehicle remained on track for a 2026 debut; on August 10 it guided only to delivery to the launch pad in Q4 2026. Then on October 14, 2026 the company said the first launch is now expected in 2027 (8-K). On the November 9 call Beck said the timeline shifted "after voids were found in the propellant tanks forward dome," and that a replacement dome has since been made; the Q3 release says assembly, testing and delivery of the first-flight hardware to Launch Complex 3 are progressing. The order book moved the other way: in Q3 Rocket Lab signed more than $515M of Neutron launches with constellation operators, taking Neutron's backlog to 16 missions, several at a base price of $70M per launch. On the day of the results the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital announced a $150M conditional loan commitment to help Rocket Lab fly Neutron more often after its first launch (Defense Daily, November 10). The gates before a first flight are not minor: qualification of the rebuilt tank hardware, Archimedes engine qualification (running at NASA's Stennis Space Center), vehicle integration, static fire, wet dress rehearsal and regulatory approval. The recovery vessel Return On Investment, under construction at Bollinger Shipyards, is expected to support Neutron's second launch, not its first — so even the debut flight is not a reusability demonstration.

Contracted already: 16 Neutron missions in backlog at the end of Q3 2026, plus the $397M Space Force SB-AMTI award, which specifically requires Neutron. On the call CFO Adam Spice said Neutron pricing is already at $70M, not heading there, and that he expects it to keep rising. Backlog for a rocket that has not flown carries a schedule condition: the 10-Q expects only about 33% of the company's total backlog to become revenue within twelve months.

Geost — $275M, closed August 2025. Electro-optical and infrared sensor payloads. This is the acquisition that turned Rocket Lab from a bus-and-components supplier into a supplier of the actual mission payload for missile-warning and space-domain-awareness satellites — the highest-value content on an SDA spacecraft. Mynaric — $155.3M, closed April 2026. Laser optical inter-satellite communication terminals; the Q2 2026 release confirms the deal is closed. Mynaric was a distressed German listed company, and buying it takes the optical-crosslink content in-house rather than sourcing it. Motiv — $40M cash plus up to $20M stock earnout, closed May 26, 2026. Robotics (Q2 2026 10-Q). Iridium Communications — agreed, not closed. Rocket Lab agreed on June 28, 2026 (10-Q) to buy Iridium for $54 a share — $27.00 in cash plus Rocket Lab stock under a collared exchange ratio — an enterprise value of approximately $8.0 billion (announcement, filed as an 8-K exhibit). Since then the US antitrust waiting period has expired (August 12), the deal's registration statement has become effective (August 26), Iridium's stockholders have approved it (September 24), and on September 15 Rocket Lab completed a $1.94B share sale and said that, with earlier sales, other cash and Iridium's own $1.775B term loans (which stay in place after closing), it has enough to pay the cash portion, so it cancelled its $3.6B bridge loan before drawing on it (8-K). FCC consent and some foreign approvals remain; the 10-Q expects the deal to close in 2027. At that price Rocket Lab is buying a company worth roughly a fifth of its own market value — and Iridium is a mature, cash-generating satellite operator, which would change the character of the P&L more than anything else on this page.

Watch: the stock half of the Iridium price. The cash half is covered by shares already sold, other cash and Iridium's existing term loans (September 15 8-K); the other half is paid in new Rocket Lab shares under a collared exchange ratio (a formula that limits how far the number of shares moves with Rocket Lab's price), so Iridium's holders become Rocket Lab holders at closing.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
FLIGHT-PROVEN CADENCE (NEARLY 100 LAUNCHES) 117-LAUNCH BACKLOG VERTICAL INTEGRATION CLASSIFIED PAYLOAD ACCESS SDA PRIME + SUBCONTRACTOR OWN LAUNCH PADS FOUNDER-ENGINEER LEADERSHIP

The real moat is not the rocket — it is being one of the few US space companies outside SpaceX that build the satellites, their key parts and the rocket themselves. The Department of Defense has a structural need for a second supplier and cannot let SpaceX be the sole path to orbit or the sole builder of proliferated constellations. Rocket Lab is the beneficiary of that need, and the awards prove it: $515M on SDA Tranche 2 Transport Layer-Beta, $816M on Tranche 3 Tracking Layer, $397M from the Space Force for Flatellite spacecraft, $190M for 20 HASTE flights, $160M+ for GEO satellites. Layered on top is genuine vertical integration — after Geost and Mynaric, Rocket Lab supplies the bus, the sensor payload, the optical crosslinks, the solar arrays, the reaction wheels, the separation system and the ride. Very few companies can quote all of that on one contract, and each acquisition removes a supplier that could otherwise hold a programme schedule hostage. It also runs its own launch pads rather than booking slots on someone else's. What none of that establishes is pricing power against a reusable Falcon 9, and that is precisely the arena Neutron is being built to enter.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Neutron schedule risk is the entire equity story. A company at 39× EV/Sales with no earnings is being valued on a vehicle that has slipped from 2024 to 2025 to 2026 and, since October 14, 2026, to 2027. The January 2026 Stage 1 tank rupture during hydrostatic testing was a manufacturing failure, and the October delay followed voids found in a propellant tank's forward dome — a second tank manufacturing problem in one year. Qualification, Archimedes engine testing, static fire and wet dress rehearsal are all still ahead. Every quarter of slip hurts two ways: it delays revenue, and it gives customers weighing medium-lift rockets more time to sign with SpaceX instead.
▸
SpaceX is the incumbent and is now publicly traded. Falcon 9 is reusable, flight-proven at extreme cadence, and its economics are set by an operator with a launch business, a satellite constellation and a public currency of its own. Neutron's competitive proposition is "a second option at a comparable price" — which means Rocket Lab's medium-lift margins are, in the long run, set by a competitor's pricing decisions rather than its own cost curve. The stock fell from a $143.48 close at the end of May 2026 to $54.73 at the end of October.
▸
Government revenue concentration and appropriations risk. The contract list is overwhelmingly U.S. defence: SDA, Space Force, Department of War. That is high-quality, long-duration backlog. It is also subject to programme restructuring, continuing resolutions, protest, and the political durability of proliferated-constellation architectures. Cancelling the largest single award, the $816M SDA Tracking Layer contract, would remove contracted value equal to about 93% of Rocket Lab's trailing annual revenue.
▸
Steady dilution and a large acquisition. Common shares outstanding rose 20% in a year (stockanalysis) to 632.02M on November 4, 2026 (10-Q cover). Basic weighted-average shares were 652.3M in Q3 2026 against 528.7M a year earlier (+23%); the count runs above the common shares because, as in the company's Q4 guidance, it takes in about 41M convertible preferred shares (40.95M outstanding at September 30, 10-Q). Most of the recent increase is the $3.91B of shares sold in 2026 to fund the cash half of Iridium; the stock half is still to be issued at closing. Free cash flow is −$326M on a trailing basis.

Notable One-Off Items

Acquisition accounting. Four deals between August 2025 and June 2026 — Geost ($275M, closed August 2025), Mynaric ($155.3M, closed April 2026), Motiv ($40M cash plus up to $20M earnout, closed May 2026) and the pending Iridium agreement — mean the revenue comparisons on this page are partly bought rather than grown. Q3 2026 Space Systems growth of +88.1% includes acquired revenue that the company does not separate out, so organic Space Systems growth cannot be calculated. Treat the 73% headline growth rate as an upper bound on organic performance.

The Q3 2026 margin dip was smaller than guided. In August the company guided Q3 GAAP gross margin to 29–31%; it delivered 33.6%, down from 36.1% in Q2. Adjusted EBITDA was −$18.0M, inside the guided −$17M to −$23M. For Q4 the company guides revenue of $270–290M, GAAP gross margin of 34–36% and adjusted EBITDA of −$14M to −$20M (release).

Share issuance. Rocket Lab sold new shares through an at-the-market programme (selling into the market a little at a time) through 2026: $3.91B of gross proceeds in the first nine months, including 36.76M shares for $2.36B net of costs in Q3 alone (10-Q), and a $1.94B programme of 29.3M shares completed on September 15 (8-K). Basic weighted-average shares were 652.3M in Q3 2026 against 528.7M a year earlier; the company's Q4 guidance of 681M basic shares includes about 41M convertible preferred shares, which is why the weighted count runs above the 632.02M common shares outstanding on November 4. Q4 guidance also includes $45M of net interest income on the cash raised.

A low base for the net loss. The Q3 2025 net loss of $18.3M was far smaller than that quarter's $59.0M operating loss (release), so the jump in net loss to $79.7M overstates the change in the operating business: the operating loss rose 34%, from $59.0M to $79.2M.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 month-end close of $15.86 to $61.53 on November 13, 2026, RKLB returned +288% — a compound annual rate of roughly 31%, with no dividend — against +65.6% for SPY and +72.6% for QQQ over the same dates (price only). That is an outstanding five-year outcome and it hides one of the more violent price paths on the Nasdaq. The stock fell 76% from November 2021 to a June 2022 month-end close of $3.79, ground sideways for two years to a fresh low of $3.76 in April 2024, then rose 38-fold from that low to a May 2026 month-end close of $143.48 — including a single month, November 2024, in which it gained 155%. By the end of October 2026 it had given back 62% from that peak, to $54.73. Over one year it is still up 36.0% (from $45.25 on November 13, 2025; SPY +12.3%, QQQ +11.8%), which is the whole problem with describing this name: it is up about a third on the year and down 59% from its $151.00 intraday high, and which of those you emphasise determines the story you tell.

Relative performance. A five-year beta of 3.18 (stockanalysis) and a five-year path that includes a 76% drawdown, a 38× advance and a 62% five-month retreat are not index-like behaviour under any measurement. RKLB is a Nasdaq-listed aerospace and defence name that trades like a bet on a single engineering milestone, and its moves are dominated by Neutron news rather than by market direction.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with what is real. Rocket Lab generated $881.9M of revenue over the trailing twelve months, growing 59%, at a 36.2% gross margin that rose every year from FY2021 to FY2025 (−3.0% → 9.0% → 21.0% → 26.6% → 34.4%). It holds a $3.57B backlog, up 52% in one quarter, and $4.70B of cash and investments against $120M of debt. Electron is closing in on its 100th launch. Its defence contract sheet — $816M, $515M, $397M, $190M, $160M+ — is the contract sheet of a company the U.S. government has decided it needs, and on November 9 the Pentagon added a $150M conditional loan commitment for Neutron. This is a real, growing, well-funded aerospace business.

Now the arithmetic that governs the stock. There is no trailing P/E — the company lost $226.9M over twelve months, so the ratio does not exist. The relevant multiple is enterprise value to sales, and it is 38.9×: $34.30B of enterprise value against $881.9M of revenue. On 2026 consensus revenue of $980.0M it is still ~35×. For scale, the market is paying $44 of market value for every $1 of annual sales from a company with a −25.7% net margin and −$326M of free cash flow. Mature aerospace and defence primes trade at a small multiple of sales. Rocket Lab is not being valued as an aerospace company at all.

So what is being valued? Break the revenue down and the answer becomes unavoidable. Q3 2026: Space Systems $214.8M, up 88.1%; Launch Services $53.1M, up 29.7% (10-Q). The manufacturing business is growing fast, partly through acquisitions, and the launch business — the thing the company is named after — is a fifth of revenue. Put a generous multiple on both existing businesses and you do not approach $39B. The residual is Neutron. The market is paying tens of billions of dollars for a medium-lift rocket that has not flown and has slipped from 2024 to 2025 to 2026 to 2027 — but that already has 16 missions booked, several at $70M a launch.

The Neutron timeline, stated plainly. Original target: 2024. November 2025: pushed to 2026. January 21, 2026: Stage 1 propellant tank ruptures during hydrostatic testing — a manufacturing defect in hand-laid composite; production shifts to automated fibre placement. February 2026: first launch moved to Q4 2026. May 2026: 2026 debut reaffirmed. August 10, 2026: guidance narrowed to pad delivery in Q4 2026. October 14, 2026: first launch moved to 2027. Peter Beck on the November 9 call: the timeline shifted "after voids were found in the propellant tanks forward dome." Still ahead: qualification of the rebuilt hardware, Archimedes engine qualification at Stennis, integration, static fire, wet dress rehearsal, regulatory approval. And the recovery vessel Return On Investment will not be ready until the second flight — so the debut, whenever it comes, will not demonstrate the reusability that the entire cost argument depends on.

Cash and dilution — the funding of the wait. Trailing free cash flow is −$325.6M and operating cash flow −$191.8M (stockanalysis); over the first nine months of 2026 the operating outflow was −$127.3M with $84.2M of capex, and Q3 operating cash flow was positive at $7.2M (release). Against $4.70B of cash and investments at September 30, that is more than a decade of runway at the trailing burn — but much of that cash is set aside for the $27.00-a-share cash half of the Iridium price. It has been paid for by shareholders: $3.91B of new shares sold in the first nine months of 2026 (10-Q), common shares up 20% in a year to 632.02M, and the stock half of Iridium still to be issued at closing.

Where that leaves valuation. Three honest framings. (i) On today's business: 39× EV/Sales, no earnings, negative ROIC, negative FCF. Indefensible on any conventional aerospace comparison. (ii) On the backlog: $38.89B of market value against $3.57B of contracted backlog — 10.9× — and the 10-Q expects only about a third of that backlog to become revenue within twelve months; the $397M SB-AMTI award and the 16 Neutron missions cannot be delivered until Neutron flies. (iii) On the option: if Neutron flies successfully in 2027, demonstrates recovery on flight two and works through its booked missions at $70M or more a launch, the revenue base is transformed and the price is judged against a much larger revenue figure. That is the bull case, and the market has already paid for a good deal of it. The 62% slide from the $143.48 May month-end to the $54.73 October month-end happened without a single revenue miss — every quarter in that window set a revenue record. That is the tell: this share price is not a function of the financial statements. It is a function of a launch date.

Bull Case

One of the few US space companies outside SpaceX that build satellites and launch them too, and the government has decided it needs one: $816M on SDA Tranche 3, $515M on Tranche 2, $397M from the Space Force, $190M for HASTE, $160M+ for GEO satellites, and a $150M conditional Pentagon loan commitment for Neutron. Revenue grew 59% over the trailing year and 73% in Q3, backlog jumped 52% in one quarter to $3.57B, and $4.70B of cash and investments against $120M of debt pays for the wait. Customers have booked 16 Neutron missions, several at $70M a launch, before the first flight. Geost and Mynaric put the sensor payload and the optical crosslinks in-house — the highest-value content on a defence satellite. If Neutron flies in 2027, a second credible medium-lift provider enters a market with one dominant supplier.

Bear Case

39× EV/Sales and 44× sales for a company with a −25.7% net margin, −$326M free cash flow, negative ROIC and no trailing P/E because there are no earnings. The rocket the valuation depends on has never flown, has slipped from 2024 to 2027, ruptured a Stage 1 tank in January 2026 and had voids found in a tank dome before the October delay. Q3 EPS of −$0.09 on Nasdaq's adjusted basis missed the −$0.06 Nasdaq consensus, and the GAAP net loss more than quadrupled to $79.7M. Growth leans on a manufacturing segment boosted by four acquisitions whose organic contribution is not separated. Common shares are up 20% in a year, and an ~$8B Iridium acquisition, half paid in stock, is still to close. At a beta of 3.18, another Neutron slip does not cost 10%.

Another slip removes the main support for a 39× sales valuation, and a launch failure would do worse: it would reset qualification, delay the $397M SB-AMTI award and the 16 booked Neutron missions, and hand SpaceX more years of medium-lift demand.   Industry Commentary
One Reusable Rocket Sets Everyone's Price
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Medium-lift launch is a market with one dominant, reusable, flight-proven supplier. Every challenger — Neutron, ULA's Vulcan, Relativity's Terran R, Firefly, Blue Origin — is building toward a price point set by someone else's cost per flight. Rocket Lab's own position illustrates the squeeze from below as well: with nearly 100 Electron flights and a 117-launch backlog, Launch Services still brought in only $53.1M in Q3 2026, because a 300 kg-class vehicle caps revenue per flight no matter how often it flies. The economics of this industry do not reward being second unless being second is worth a strategic premium — which, for the U.S. government, it currently is. That premium, not commercial pricing power, is what Neutron is actually selling.
Proliferated Defence Constellations Are the Real Budget
EMERGING TAILWIND
The architectural shift from a few exquisite satellites to hundreds of cheap ones has moved money from traditional primes toward companies that can manufacture spacecraft at rate. Rocket Lab's awards trace that shift precisely: $515M on SDA Tranche 2 Transport Layer-Beta, $816M on Tranche 3 Tracking Layer, $397M from the Space Force for Flatellite spacecraft, $160M+ for GEO satellites including space domain awareness, and a $190M Department of War block buy for 20 HASTE hypersonic test flights at $9.5M each. Buying Geost ($275M) for infrared sensor payloads and Mynaric ($155.3M) for laser crosslinks was a direct bid for the highest-value content on those spacecraft. This is why Space Systems revenue rose 88% in Q3 2026 while launch revenue rose 30% — and it is the part of the story that is already signing backlog that becomes revenue as it is delivered, rather than promises.
Space Equities Are Priced on Milestones, Not Earnings
ONGOING HEADWIND
With SpaceX now publicly traded as SPCX, the sector has a very large listed company to compare against for the first time, and capital that once had nowhere else to go has somewhere else to go. Rocket Lab's own 2026 path shows what milestone-driven pricing does to a shareholder: $80.07 at the end of January, $143.48 at the end of May, $54.73 at the end of October, with record revenue in every quarter and a five-year beta of 3.18. Bull framing: that volatility is the price of owning a listed space company that can supply launch, satellite buses, payloads and crosslinks as an alternative to SpaceX — and at $61.53 the market prices the Neutron option far below where it did in May, while the Neutron order book has grown to 16 missions. Bear framing: the 62% slide happened while revenue kept setting records, which means the numbers on this page cannot support the price in either direction. Until Neutron flies, this is not a stock that can be valued on its financial statements — and any figure you compute from them, including the 39× EV/Sales above, is a description of sentiment rather than of value.
