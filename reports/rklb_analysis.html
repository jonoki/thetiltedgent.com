01 Company Overview

Rocket Lab designs and flies the small-lift Electron orbital rocket and its suborbital hypersonic-test variant HASTE, and it builds satellites, satellite subsystems and defence payloads through a business it calls Space Systems. FY2025 revenue was $601.8M, up 38% from $436.2M in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $769.2M, up 52.5%. It is, by mission count, the second-most active launch provider in the world behind SpaceX — the 93rd Electron flew in late August 2026 — and it carries a record $2.36B backlog at a market capitalisation of $38.46B.

That $38.46B is the whole argument. Rocket Lab has never earned a profit, and on trailing revenue the stock trades at 50× sales and 47× enterprise value to sales. Electron and Space Systems together produce roughly three-quarters of a billion dollars of revenue at a 37% gross margin and a $165M annual net loss. No arrangement of those numbers gets you to $38B. The multiple is Neutron — the medium-lift, reusable vehicle intended to compete for the payload class SpaceX's Falcon 9 owns. And as of this close, Neutron has never flown. It has not been to the pad. The first-launch target has slipped from 2024 to 2025 to 2026, and on the August 10, 2026 earnings call management stopped guiding to a launch date at all and guided instead to delivery to the launch pad in Q4 2026. Everything below is an attempt to size the gap between the business that exists and the business the price implies.

Electron is a 300 kg-class orbital launcher — the dedicated ride for a customer who does not want to wait for a rideshare slot and needs a specific orbit on a specific date. Rocket Lab flew 21 Electron/HASTE missions in FY2025, signed 36 new launch contracts in Q1 2026 alone, and passed its 93rd Electron launch in late August 2026. That cadence makes it the world's second-busiest launch provider. Pricing: Rocket Lab does not publish an average selling price. Two anchors exist. The verified one is the Department of War's $190M block buy for 20 HASTE suborbital test flights — $9.5M per flight. The derived one: if launch services was ~37.5% of FY2025's $601.8M revenue (midpoint of the third-party 35–40% estimate), that implies roughly $225M across 21 missions, or ~$10.7M per mission (derived, est.). Recent wins include three HASTE flights for Anduril from Launch Complex 2 at Wallops Island, and three 2027 NASA Electron missions carrying the PolSIR and TSIS-2 science payloads. The uncomfortable fact sits above: with 93 flights behind it and a full order book, this segment's revenue grew 1.7% year over year. Electron is a good business at a small scale. It is not a $38B business.

Structural ceiling: a 300 kg payload class caps revenue per flight. Growth here requires either more flights per year or a bigger rocket — which is what Neutron is for.

Realistic first flight

Verified status as of this close: Neutron has not launched, and has not yet been delivered to the pad. The schedule history is the story. The vehicle was originally targeted for a 2024 debut. In November 2025 the debut was pushed to 2026. On January 21, 2026 the Stage 1 propellant tank ruptured during hydrostatic pressure testing — attributed to a manufacturing defect in the hand-laid composite process, with future production moving to automated fibre placement. In February 2026 the first-launch target moved to Q4 2026. In May 2026 management confirmed the vehicle remained on track for a 2026 debut. Then on August 10, 2026 the language narrowed materially: the release states only that "production of the Stage 1 tank is currently aligned with the target delivery of Neutron to the launch pad in Q4 2026," Peter Beck acknowledged that "the window for an end-of-year launch is narrowing," and Adam Spice said the first test launch would "hopefully" occur later this year. Management now describes Neutron as "a revenue-generating story beginning in 2027 and beyond." The outstanding technical gates before a first flight are not minor: Stage 1 qualification, Archimedes engine qualification (running at NASA's Stennis Space Center), vehicle integration, static fire, wet dress rehearsal, and regulatory approval. Launch operations are based at Wallops Island, Virginia. The recovery vessel Return On Investment, under construction at Bollinger Shipyards, is expected to support Neutron's second launch, not its first — so even the debut flight is not a reusability demonstration.

Contracted already: at least seven Neutron launches for commercial customers between now and 2029, plus the $397M Space Force SB-AMTI award which specifically requires Neutron. Backlog denominated in a vehicle that has not flown is backlog with a schedule condition attached.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
FLIGHT-PROVEN CADENCE (93) #2 LAUNCH PROVIDER VERTICAL INTEGRATION CLASSIFIED PAYLOAD ACCESS SDA PRIME + SUBCONTRACTOR OWNED LAUNCH RANGES FOUNDER-ENGINEER LEADERSHIP

The real moat is not the rocket — it is being the only credible non-SpaceX vertically integrated space company in the United States. The Department of Defense has a structural need for a second supplier and cannot let SpaceX be the sole path to orbit or the sole builder of proliferated constellations. Rocket Lab is the beneficiary of that need, and the awards prove it: $515M on SDA Tranche 2 Transport Layer-Beta, $816M on Tranche 3 Tracking Layer, $397M from the Space Force for Flatellite spacecraft, $190M for 20 HASTE flights, $160M+ for GEO satellites. Layered on top is genuine vertical integration — after Geost and Mynaric, Rocket Lab supplies the bus, the sensor payload, the optical crosslinks, the solar arrays, the reaction wheels, the separation system and the ride. Very few companies can quote all of that on one contract, and each acquisition removes a supplier that could otherwise hold a programme schedule hostage. It also owns its launch sites rather than renting range time. What none of that establishes is pricing power against a reusable Falcon 9, and that is precisely the arena Neutron is being built to enter.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Neutron schedule risk is the entire equity story. A company at 47× EV/Sales with no earnings is being valued on a vehicle that has slipped from 2024 to 2025 to 2026 and is now guided only to a pad-delivery date. The January 2026 Stage 1 tank rupture during hydrostatic testing was a manufacturing failure, not a design failure — but it cost a year, and the qualification campaign, Archimedes engine testing, static fire and wet dress rehearsal are all still ahead. Every quarter of slip compounds two ways: it delays revenue, and it gives customers evaluating medium-lift capacity more time to sign with SpaceX instead.
▸
SpaceX is the incumbent and is now publicly traded. Falcon 9 is reusable, flight-proven at extreme cadence, and its economics are set by an operator with a launch business, a satellite constellation and a public currency of its own. Neutron's competitive proposition is "a second option at a comparable price" — which means Rocket Lab's medium-lift margins are, in the long run, set by a competitor's pricing decisions rather than its own cost curve. The stock's own May-to-September 2026 collapse from $143.48 to $64.26 coincided with the market re-rating exactly this question.
▸
Government revenue concentration and appropriations risk. The verified contract list is overwhelmingly U.S. defence: SDA, Space Force, Department of War. That is high-quality, long-duration backlog. It is also subject to programme restructuring, continuing resolutions, protest, and the political durability of proliferated-constellation architectures. A single SDA tranche cancellation would remove more contracted value than Rocket Lab's entire trailing annual revenue.
▸
Chronic dilution and a large M&A commitment. The share count has grown steadily every year since the SPAC — the loss-and-EPS arithmetic implies roughly 469M weighted-average shares in FY2022 rising to ~591M for the trailing twelve months, with 598.46M outstanding today, roughly +28% in four years. Free cash flow is −$371M on a trailing basis. Against that, the company has announced an agreement to acquire Iridium — reported at roughly $8B — whose funding structure is not disclosed in the primary release.

Notable One-Off Items

Acquisition accounting. Four deals in thirteen months — Geost ($275M, closed August 2025), Mynaric ($155.3M, closed April 2026), Motiv (closed, terms not disclosed) and the announced Iridium agreement — mean the revenue comparisons on this page are partly inorganic. The Q2 2026 product revenue growth of +95.6% includes acquired revenue that the company does not separate out, so organic Space Systems growth cannot be calculated from the sources fetched. Treat the 62% headline growth rate as an upper bound on organic performance.

The Q3 2026 gross margin step-down. Guidance is 29–31% GAAP gross margin against 36.1% delivered in Q2 — a six-point sequential decline on higher guided revenue of $250–265M. Guided adjusted EBITDA also worsens, from −$8.8M actual in Q2 to a guided −$17M to −$23M. Both are consistent with mix shifting toward lower-margin acquired hardware and with Neutron pre-launch spending landing in cost of revenue. This is the most concrete near-term negative in the disclosure.

Share issuance. Derived weighted-average share counts from reported net loss and EPS: FY2022 ~469M, FY2023 ~480M, FY2024 ~500M, FY2025 ~536M, TTM ~591M — against 598.46M outstanding now. That is roughly +28% dilution over four years, or about 6.4% a year compounding. A note on a discrepancy rather than smoothing it over: the Q2 2026 release as extracted shows weighted-average shares of 629.7M for Q2 2026 versus 515.1M for Q2 2025, which does not reconcile with the 598.46M outstanding figure. The discrepancy is flagged, not resolved; the direction — material and continuing dilution — is unambiguous either way. A share issuance programme (ATM) was reported terminated around the Q2 print.

No non-recurring gains. Nothing in the fetched disclosures indicates one-off items flattering earnings. The losses are operating losses.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with what is real. Rocket Lab generated $769.2M of revenue over the trailing twelve months, growing 52.5%, at a 37.26% gross margin that has improved every single year since 2021 (−3.0% → 9.0% → 21.0% → 26.6% → 34.4% → 37.3%). It holds a $2.36B backlog, up 137%, and $2.13B of cash against $133.7M of debt. It has flown Electron 93 times and is the second-most active launch provider on Earth. Its defence contract sheet — $816M, $515M, $397M, $190M, $160M+ — is the contract sheet of a company the U.S. government has decided it needs. This is a real, growing, well-capitalised aerospace business.

Now the arithmetic that governs the stock. There is no trailing P/E — the company lost $165.5M over twelve months, so the ratio does not exist. The relevant multiple is enterprise value to sales, and it is 47.07×: $36.20B of enterprise value against $769.2M of revenue. On 2026 consensus revenue of $958.3M it is still ~37.8×. For scale, that means the market is paying $50 for every $1 of annual sales from a company with a −21.5% net margin and −$371M of free cash flow. Mature aerospace and defence primes trade at a small multiple of sales. Rocket Lab is not being valued as an aerospace company at all.

So what is being valued? Break the revenue down and the answer becomes unavoidable. Q2 2026: product revenue $181.3M, up 95.6%; service revenue $52.7M, up 1.7%. The manufacturing business is compounding fast and the launch business — the thing the company is named after — did not grow. Space Systems is the majority of revenue on any measure: 77.4% of Q2 2026 on the disclosed product/service split, and roughly 60–65% of FY2025 on a third-party segment estimate. Put a generous multiple on both existing businesses and you do not approach $38B. The residual is Neutron. The market is paying tens of billions of dollars for a medium-lift rocket that has not flown, has not reached the pad, has slipped from 2024 to 2025 to 2026 and is now described by management as "a revenue-generating story beginning in 2027 and beyond."

The Neutron timeline, stated plainly. Original target: 2024. November 2025: pushed to 2026. January 21, 2026: Stage 1 propellant tank ruptures during hydrostatic testing — a manufacturing defect in hand-laid composite; production shifts to automated fibre placement. February 2026: first launch moved to Q4 2026. May 2026: 2026 debut reaffirmed. August 10, 2026: the company stops guiding to a launch and guides only to pad delivery in Q4 2026. Peter Beck: the end-of-year window "is narrowing." Adam Spice: the first test flight would "hopefully" happen this year. Still ahead: Stage 1 qualification, Archimedes engine qualification at Stennis, integration, static fire, wet dress rehearsal, regulatory approval. And the recovery vessel Return On Investment will not be ready until the second flight — so the debut, whenever it comes, will not demonstrate the reusability that the entire cost argument depends on. A first launch in 2027 is now the base case.

Cash and dilution — the funding of the wait. Trailing free cash flow is −$371.1M; operating cash flow −$222.5M; H1 2026 operating outflow −$134.4M against $53.1M of capex. Consensus expects −$220.4M of FCF in 2026. Against $2.13B of cash at June 30, that is roughly five to six years of runway at the current burn — genuinely comfortable, and the reason this is not a solvency story. It has, however, been funded by shareholders: derived weighted-average shares went from ~469M (FY2022) to ~591M (TTM), with 598.46M outstanding, roughly +28% in four years. And the announced Iridium acquisition — reported at ~$8B, terms unverified — is a commitment several times the cash balance whose financing mix is not yet disclosed. If it is equity-funded, the dilution history above is a prologue.

Where that leaves valuation. Three honest framings. (i) On today's business: 47× EV/Sales, no earnings, negative ROIC, negative FCF. Indefensible on any conventional aerospace comparison. (ii) On the backlog: $38.46B of market value against $2.36B of contracted backlog — 16.3× — and a meaningful slice of that backlog (the $397M SB-AMTI award, at least seven commercial Neutron flights through 2029) cannot be delivered until Neutron flies. (iii) On the option: if Neutron reaches the pad in Q4 2026, flies successfully in 2027, demonstrates recovery on flight two and captures even a modest slice of medium-lift demand, the revenue base is transformed and the multiple re-rates on a much larger denominator. That is the bull case, and the market has already paid for a good deal of it. The four-month, 55% de-rate from $143.48 to $64.26 happened without a single revenue miss — every quarterly print in that window was a record. That is the tell: this share price is not a function of the financial statements. It is a function of a launch date.

Bull Case

The only vertically integrated non-SpaceX space company in the United States, and the government has decided it needs one: $816M on SDA Tranche 3, $515M on Tranche 2, $397M from the Space Force, $190M for HASTE, $160M+ for GEO satellites. Revenue is compounding 52.5% with gross margin up every year for five years to 37.3%, backlog is up 137% to $2.36B, and $2.13B of cash against $134M of debt funds five-plus years of the current burn. Geost and Mynaric put the sensor payload and the optical crosslinks in-house — the highest-value content on a defence satellite. If Neutron reaches the pad in Q4 2026 and flies in 2027, a second credible medium-lift provider enters a market with one supplier and effectively unlimited demand.

Bear Case

47× EV/Sales and 50× sales for a company with a −21.5% net margin, −$371M free cash flow, negative ROIC and no trailing P/E because there are no earnings. The rocket the multiple depends on has never flown, has slipped three years, ruptured a Stage 1 tank in January 2026, and is now guided only to a pad-delivery date with the CEO conceding the launch window "is narrowing." Meanwhile the launch business grew revenue 1.7% year over year — all growth came from a manufacturing segment inflated by four acquisitions whose organic contribution is not separated. Q3 gross margin is guided down six points to 29–31%. Share count is up ~28% in four years and an ~$8B Iridium acquisition of undisclosed financing sits on the table. At a 2.61 beta, another Neutron slip does not cost 10%.

  Industry Commentary
One Reusable Rocket Sets Everyone's Price
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Medium-lift launch is a market with one dominant, reusable, flight-proven supplier. Every challenger — Neutron, ULA's Vulcan, Relativity's Terran R, Firefly, Blue Origin — is building toward a price point set by someone else's marginal cost. Rocket Lab's own position illustrates the squeeze from below as well: with 93 Electron flights and a full manifest, launch revenue still grew just 1.7% year over year, because a 300 kg-class vehicle caps revenue per flight no matter how often it flies. The economics of this industry do not reward being second unless being second is worth a strategic premium — which, for the U.S. government, it currently is. That premium, not commercial pricing power, is what Neutron is actually selling.
Proliferated Defence Constellations Are the Real Budget
EMERGING TAILWIND
The architectural shift from a few exquisite satellites to hundreds of cheap ones has moved money from traditional primes toward companies that can manufacture spacecraft at rate. Rocket Lab's verified awards trace that shift precisely: $515M on SDA Tranche 2 Transport Layer-Beta, $816M on Tranche 3 Tracking Layer, $397M from the Space Force for Flatellite spacecraft, $160M+ for GEO satellites including space domain awareness, and a $190M Department of War block buy for 20 HASTE hypersonic test flights at $9.5M each. Buying Geost ($275M) for infrared sensor payloads and Mynaric ($155.3M) for laser crosslinks was a direct bid for the highest-value content on those spacecraft. This is why product revenue nearly doubled while launch revenue stood still — and it is the part of the story that is already generating cash-convertible backlog rather than promises.
Space Equities Are Priced on Milestones, Not Earnings
ONGOING HEADWIND
With SpaceX now publicly traded as SPCX, the sector has a mega-cap reference asset for the first time, and capital that once had nowhere else to go has somewhere else to go. Rocket Lab's own 2026 path shows what milestone-driven pricing does to a shareholder: $80.07 in January, $143.48 in May, $64.26 in September, with record revenue in every one of those quarters and a 2.61 beta throughout. Bull framing: that volatility is the price of owning the only listed pure-play with a credible second-source position across launch, buses, payloads and crosslinks — and at $64.26 the market has repriced the Neutron option far below where it was paying in May, so the asymmetry has improved. Bear framing: the same 55% drawdown occurred with zero deterioration in results, which means the numbers on this page cannot support the price in either direction. Until Neutron flies, this is not a stock that can be valued on its financial statements — and any figure you compute from them, including the 47× EV/Sales above, is a description of sentiment rather than of value.
