01 Company Overview

Founded by Ralph Lauren in New York in 1967, the company sells apparel for men, women and children, footwear, eyewear, watches, jewellery, home furnishings and fragrance under Ralph Lauren Collection, Polo Ralph Lauren, Double RL and Chaps through its own stores, concession shops, digital sites, department stores, specialty retailers and golf shops. The fiscal year ends in March. For the year to March 2026 (FY2026) revenue crossed $8 billion for the first time — $8.115B, up 14.6% — with GAAP EPS of $15.11, up 30.2%.

The interesting number is not revenue. It is the price tag. Four years ago, in FY2022, Ralph Lauren kept 66.5 cents of gross profit on every dollar of sales and 10.4 cents of operating profit. In the latest quarter it kept 73.7 cents of gross profit and 17.5 cents of operating profit (18.7 cents adjusted). The engine is AUR: the average price a customer actually pays per item rose 16% in Q4 FY2026 and 15% in Q1 FY2027, and management says about half of that came from selling more at full price and discounting less. A brand that can raise what the customer pays year after year, while acquiring 1.5 million new customers in a single quarter, is doing the one thing apparel companies usually cannot.

The market paid for it. RL closed September 2021 at $111.04 and closed September 21, 2026 at $339.82 — roughly 3.1x in five years. And then, in August 2026, it did something odd: the company beat on its first-quarter numbers and raised its outlook, the stock rose 3.95% on the day to $395.83, peaked at $406.89 on August 10 — and has since fallen 16.5% to $339.82 with no new bad news in the sources fetched. The debate is no longer whether the pricing strategy works. It is how much further it can run: management itself expects AUR growth to slow to a "more normalized mid-single-digit pace" in FY2027, tariffs are a cost the company is absorbing through price, and the stock is priced at 21.4x trailing earnings.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Brand & pricing power Full-price selling discipline Three balanced regions Owned stores & digital Lean inventory Net cash $702.2M Founder-led creative identity

In apparel the moat is not a patent or a network; it is the customer's willingness to pay more for the same shirt because of the logo on it. Ralph Lauren's numbers say that willingness is growing: gross margin rose from 64.9% in FY2023 to 69.9% in FY2026 and 73.7% in the latest quarter, over the same years in which the company pulled back on discounting. The second leg is control of distribution — own stores and digital let the company set the price the customer sees, rather than leaving it to a department-store markdown calendar. The vulnerability is that a brand moat is maintained, not owned: it has to be re-earned every season, and pricing power that rests partly on mix can reverse faster than it was built.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Tariffs are being paid for with price. The Q1 FY2027 release describes strong full-price demand offsetting increased tariff pressure. That works while the customer keeps accepting higher prices. When the plan was unveiled in September 2025, Vietnamese exports faced a 20% US surcharge, indirectly affecting Asian-sourced production. If AUR growth slows to the mid-single digits management expects, the tariff cost has less room to hide.
02
Some of the growth is borrowed. About 15 points of North American wholesale growth in Q1 came from resumed shipments to one luxury wholesale account and shipments shifted out of Q4 FY2026. FY2027 also contains a 53rd week, worth about one point of revenue growth. Neither repeats.
03
Europe is stalling. Constant-currency growth of 4.6% and retail comps of +1% in a segment that is 30% of revenue. Europe's margin held, but a region that barely grows its comparable sales cannot keep carrying price increases indefinitely.
04
China is decelerating on purpose — and still has to deliver. From 40% growth in FY2026 to a guided mid-teens in FY2027. Asia is the highest-margin region; any shortfall there costs profit disproportionately.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, timing in wholesale: about 15 points of Q1 FY2027 North American wholesale growth came from resumed shipments to a luxury wholesale account and shipments moved out of Q4 FY2026 — flattering Q1 at Q4's expense. Second, the calendar: FY2027 includes a 53rd week, worth about one point of revenue growth, and currency helped Q4 FY2026 by about 450 basis points. Third, the GAAP–adjusted gap: Q1 FY2027 EPS was $4.28 reported versus $4.59 adjusted, and operating margin 17.5% versus 18.7% — a wider gap than a year earlier in absolute terms, though both measures grew 22%. Buybacks also help per-share figures: roughly $250M was repurchased in Q1 alone, and shares outstanding are down 2.32% YoY.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 close of $111.04 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $339.82, RL gained 206.0% on price — roughly a triple, before dividends. The path was not smooth. The stock fell to a window low of $84.93 in September 2022, spent most of 2023 between $106 and $145, then re-rated hard from early 2024 as margins widened year after year. The last twelve months have been a plateau: the stock has traded in a range between $299.98 and $421.60, and the month-end high of $401.41 in June 2026 has given way to three down months.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows a stock that spent most of 2024 and 2025 in overbought territory — peaking at 89.2 in February 2025 and 87.6 in November 2025 — and has now cooled to 45.3, its first sub-50 readings since October 2023. The 10-month moving average, at roughly $360.46, sits above the current price. The momentum that carried the stock for two years has faded to neutral; the fundamentals have not, which is the gap the next two earnings reports have to close one way or the other.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

This is a brand that has proven it can raise what customers pay — AUR +16% and +15% in the last two quarters — while gaining customers, shrinking inventory and widening gross margin to 73.7%. Operating margin has gone from 10.4% to 16.2% in four years, Asia is growing 24% at a 33.5% margin, the balance sheet carries $702.2M of net cash, and management has beaten and raised twice in a row. At 17.3x forward earnings after a 16.5% pullback with no new bad news in the sources fetched, 17 of 19 analysts rate it a Buy with an average target 31.5% above the price.

Bear Case

Pricing power is the whole thesis, and management itself says it is slowing — AUR growth to a "normalized mid-single-digit pace" in FY2027. Some of the latest quarter's growth does not repeat (about 15 points of North American wholesale from one account and timing; a 53rd week worth one point). Europe comped +1%. China is guided down from 40% to mid-teens growth. Tariffs are being absorbed through price at exactly the moment price growth decelerates. A stock that tripled on margin expansion is priced for more of it, and the market's reaction to a beat — up 3.95% on the day, then down 16.5% from the post-print high — says the easy re-rating is done.

Top 3 Risks

01
AUR deceleration meets tariff cost. Gross margin has been carried by double-digit price/mix gains. If those fall to mid-single digits while tariff costs persist, the gross-margin line stops expanding — and gross margin is what took the stock from $85 to $400.
02
Asia and China normalisation. Asia earns the highest margin (33.5%) and is growing fastest. Management already guides China from 40% to mid-teens growth; any miss beyond that hits the most profitable part of the business.
03
Discretionary spending cycle. With a beta of 1.35, RL is a consumer-discretionary stock that moves more than the market. Europe's +1% comp is the early-warning light; a broader slowdown in aspirational spending would test the full-price strategy when discounting is most tempting.
Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the price of the shirt
ONGOING CONSTRAINT

American fashion brands have been exposed to geopolitical and trade-policy uncertainty since 2025. When Ralph Lauren unveiled its plan in September 2025, Vietnamese exports faced a 20% US surcharge, indirectly affecting its Asian production. The company's answer has been price: in Q1 FY2027 it describes strong full-price demand offsetting increased tariff pressure, and gross margin still rose 140 bps to 73.7%. That is the best available evidence that the brand has pricing power. It is also a strategy with a ceiling — each round of price increases is a test of how far the customer will go.

Full price vs. the markdown habit
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Apparel's oldest problem is the markdown: make too much, discount the rest, train customers to wait for the sale. Ralph Lauren's five-year story is the reverse — inventory down 5% while revenue rose 14% in the latest quarter, AUR up double digits, gross margin from 64.9% in FY2023 to 69.9% in FY2026. Management attributes about half of Q4 FY2026's AUR gain to full-price selling and reduced discounting and half to mix. The discipline is the moat; the risk is that it is easiest to keep when demand is strong, and the harder test comes when it is not.

Asia as the new centre of gravity
EMERGING TAILWIND

Asia is now 30.1% of revenue and the most profitable region, with Q1 FY2027 comps of +23% and digital +32%. China grew 40% in FY2026. The bull framing: an under-penetrated market for the brand is still early, and every point of mix shifted toward a 33.5%-margin region lifts group margin without a price increase. The bear framing: growth rates like these rarely persist; management is already guiding China to mid-teens, and a region that grows this fast also has the most room to disappoint. Both are testable on November 5.
