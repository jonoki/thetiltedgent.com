01 Company Overview

Founded by Ralph Lauren in New York in 1967, the company sells apparel for men, women and children, footwear, eyewear, watches, jewellery, home furnishings and fragrance under Ralph Lauren Collection, Polo Ralph Lauren, Double RL and Chaps through its own stores, concession shops, digital sites, department stores, specialty retailers and golf shops. The fiscal year ends in March. For the year to March 2026 (FY2026) revenue crossed $8 billion for the first time — $8.115B, up 14.6% — with GAAP EPS of $15.11, up 30.2%.

The interesting number is not revenue. It is the price tag. Four years ago, in FY2022, Ralph Lauren kept 66.5 cents of gross profit on every dollar of sales and 10.4 cents of operating profit. Over the last twelve months it kept 70.9 cents of gross profit and 16.3 cents of operating profit, both before counting $82.3M of tariff refunds. The engine is AUR: the average price a customer actually pays per item rose 16% in Q4 FY2026, 15% in Q1 FY2027 and 9% in Q2 FY2027. On the November call the finance chief split that 9% into three equal parts: selling more expensive products, selling more in higher-priced regions and channels, and price rises with fewer promotions. A brand that can raise what the customer pays year after year, while adding 1.7 million new customers to its own stores and sites in a single quarter, is doing the one thing apparel companies usually cannot.

The market paid for it. RL closed November 2021 at $116.04 and closed November 6, 2026 at $376.56 — about 3.2x in five years. Then came an odd reaction. On November 5 the company beat its own outlook and raised its full-year forecast for the second time this fiscal year, and the stock fell 7.35% that day, from $412.06 to $381.78, having risen 4.5% the session before. Reuters put the fall down to the holiday-quarter forecast: sales growth of 3% to 4%, below the 4.2% analysts expected. The debate is no longer whether the pricing strategy works. It is how much further it can run: AUR growth has slowed from 16% to 9% in three quarters, management is planning for "mid-single-digit AUR growth" in the holiday quarter, tariffs are a cost the company is absorbing through price, and the stock is priced at 21.6x trailing earnings.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Brand & pricing power Full-price selling discipline Three balanced regions Owned stores & digital Lean inventory Net cash $581.8M Founder-led creative identity

In apparel the moat is not a patent or a network; it is the customer's willingness to pay more for the same shirt because of the logo on it. Ralph Lauren's numbers say that willingness is growing: gross margin rose from 64.9% in FY2023 to 69.9% in FY2026, and the latest quarter's adjusted 70.5% was 2.5 percentage points above a year earlier, over the same years in which the company pulled back on discounting. The second leg is control of distribution — own stores and digital let the company set the price the customer sees, rather than leaving it to a department-store markdown calendar. The vulnerability is that a brand moat is maintained, not owned: it has to be re-earned every season, and pricing power that rests partly on selling more expensive products and more in higher-priced regions can reverse faster than it was built.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Tariffs are coming back. The quarter's reported gross margin of 74.2% includes $82.3M of refunds of tariffs paid under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, which the Supreme Court ruled against; the company gave $33.2M of the refunds to charity and leaves all of it out of its adjusted results and guidance. The underlying cost has not gone away: the outlook assumes a tariff rate of about 10% through December, then "reciprocal rates in the high teens" in the fiscal fourth quarter. If AUR growth slows to the mid-single digits management expects, the tariff cost has less room to hide.
02
Some of the growth is borrowed. About 4 points of North American wholesale growth in Q2 came from shipments pulled forward from the second half, after about 15 points in Q1 from resumed shipments to one luxury account and shipments shifted out of Q4 FY2026. The payback shows in the third-quarter guide, which carries about 1 point of drag from wholesale timing and deliberate cuts to sales through off-price stores. FY2027 also contains a 53rd week, worth an additional 1 point of revenue growth. None of this repeats.
03
Europe is stalling. Constant-currency growth of 2.5% and comparable store sales of −1% in a segment that is 31.7% of revenue, with store sales down 4%. Europe's margin held, but management says the region has become more promotional, the opposite of the full-price selling behind the rest of this story, and it cut its full-year Europe outlook to low-single-digit growth, from low to mid single digits.
04
China is slowing — and still has to deliver. Revenue rose more than 35% in Q2; management's full-year call for FY2027 is mid-to-high-teens growth. Asia earned the highest first-half margin of the three regions (30.5%), so any shortfall there costs profit disproportionately.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, tariff refunds: Q2 FY2027 gross profit includes $82.3M of refunds of tariffs paid under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, worth about 3.7 points of gross margin (74.2% reported vs 70.5% adjusted), and the company gave $33.2M of the proceeds to charity, booked as an operating expense. Second, timing and the calendar: about 4 points of Q2 North American wholesale growth came from shipments pulled forward from the second half (after about 15 points of Q1 growth from a resumed luxury account and shipments moved out of Q4 FY2026), and FY2027 has a 53rd week worth an additional point of revenue growth. Third, the reported–adjusted gap: Q2 EPS was $4.87 reported and $4.64 adjusted, with reported above adjusted this time because the refunds outweighed restructuring and transformation charges and the donation. Buybacks also help per-share figures: about $250M was repurchased in Q2 and $500M so far this fiscal year, which completes the plan for FY2027, and diluted shares averaged 60.9M against 62.4M a year earlier.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

This is a brand that has proven it can raise what customers pay — AUR +16%, +15% and +9% in the last three quarters — while gaining customers, shrinking inventory and widening adjusted gross margin to 70.5%. Operating margin has gone from 10.4% to 16.3% in four years, Asia is growing 28% with China above 35%, the balance sheet carries $581.8M of net cash, management has raised the full-year outlook twice this fiscal year, and EPS has beaten Nasdaq consensus in each of the last four quarters. At 19.6x this fiscal year's estimated earnings, 16 of 18 analysts rate it a Buy with an average target 18.6% above the price.

Bear Case

Pricing power is the whole thesis, and it is slowing: AUR growth has gone from 16% to 9% in three quarters, and management is planning for mid-single digits in the holiday quarter. Some of the latest quarter's growth does not repeat (about 4 points of North American wholesale pulled forward), and this fiscal year has a 53rd week worth one point. Comparable store sales fell 1% in Europe, which has turned more promotional. China is guided from above 35% in the latest quarter to mid-to-high teens for the full year. Reported margins were flattered by $82.3M of tariff refunds, and the outlook has tariffs back in the high teens by the fiscal fourth quarter. A stock that tripled on margin expansion is priced for more of it, and a 7.35% fall on the day of better-than-expected results and a higher outlook shows how high the bar has become.

Top 3 Risks

01
AUR slows as tariffs return. Gross margin has been carried by double-digit gains in the price paid per item. That growth is already down to 9% and management plans for mid-single digits in the holiday quarter, while the tariff rate in its outlook rises from about 10% to the high teens in the fiscal fourth quarter. If those two meet, the gross-margin line stops expanding — and the stock's climb from $85 to $400 came with that margin expansion.
02

Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the price of the shirt
ONGOING CONSTRAINT

American fashion brands have been exposed to trade-policy uncertainty since 2025. The Supreme Court ruling against tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act has meant money coming back: Ralph Lauren booked $82.3M of refunds in the September quarter and donated $33.2M of them. Its answer to the tariff cost itself has been price: adjusted gross margin rose 2.5 points to 70.5%, on higher prices per item, more sales in higher-margin regions and lower product costs. That is the best available evidence that the brand has pricing power. It is also a strategy with a ceiling: each round of price increases tests how far the customer will go, and the company's outlook has tariffs back in the high teens by its fiscal fourth quarter.

Full price vs. the markdown habit
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Apparel's oldest problem is the markdown: make too much, discount the rest, train customers to wait for the sale. Ralph Lauren's five-year story is the reverse — inventory down 9% while revenue rose 9% in the latest quarter, AUR up between 9% and 16% in each of the last three quarters, gross margin from 64.9% in FY2023 to 69.9% in FY2026. On the November call the finance chief said Q2's AUR gain came in equal parts from product mix, from geographic and channel mix, and from pricing and fewer promotions. The discipline is the moat; the risk is that it is easiest to keep when demand is strong, and Europe, where discounting has increased, is where the harder test has begun.

Asia as the new centre of gravity
EMERGING TAILWIND

Asia was 26.0% of revenue in the latest quarter (27.9% in the first half) and earned the highest first-half margin of the three regions, with Q2 comparable store sales of +24% and digital +31%. China's revenue rose more than 35% in the quarter. The bull framing: the region is still early for the brand, and every point of sales shifted toward a region that earned 30.5% in the first half lifts group margin without a price increase. The bear framing: growth rates like these rarely persist; management's own full-year call on China is mid-to-high teens, and a region that grows this fast also has the most room to disappoint. Both are testable at the next print.
