01 Company Overview

ResMed makes the machines and masks that keep people with obstructive sleep apnea breathing through the night, and it wires almost all of them to the cloud. It reports two segments — Sleep & Breathing Health (devices, masks and accessories) and Residential Care Software. Fiscal 2026 (year ended June 30, 2026) revenue was $5.653B, up 10% reported and 8% in constant currency, with GAAP diluted EPS of $10.43 and non-GAAP EPS of $11.17 (+17%). In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 (to September 30, 2026), revenue rose 6% to $1,415M (7% in core constant currency) and non-GAAP EPS 14% to $2.91; GAAP EPS was $2.92. Its fiscal 2026 annual report (10-K) counts more than 35 million patients using cloud-connected devices on AirView, its online platform for care providers, and more than 12 million registered on the myAir patient app.

This is the hand the market keeps misreading. In 2021 ResMed's closest rival, Philips Respironics, recalled millions of breathing devices over degrading foam, and a 2024 US consent decree restricted its sale of new CPAP machines in the United States. ResMed was left holding most of the table. Then, in August 2023, the fear flipped: weight-loss drugs (GLP-1s) might shrink the patient pool itself, and the stock fell from a $222.35 July close to $159.59 in a single month. It climbed back to $274.51 by August 2025, only to lose 30.6% into a $190.57 May 2026 close — through a quarter of 11% revenue growth, an earnings beat, a CFO handover and fresh GLP-1 worry. It climbed again to $252.83 on October 29, 2026, the day of the first-quarter print, then fell 9.1% the next session while the S&P 500 fell 1.8%: earnings beat forecasts, but revenue growth slowed to 5.9%, the slowest of the last nine quarters. The settled close is now $232.94. The company's own counter-evidence: on the Q1 call CEO Mick Farrell said patients with prescriptions for both CPAP and a GLP-1 are more than 11% more likely to start CPAP therapy (The Motley Fool's call summary). The debate is whether GLP-1s are a leak in the funnel or a new door into it — and whether the July 2026 ventilator recall is a one-year dent or a crack in the quality story.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

35M+ patients on connected devices Recurring mask & accessory resupply AirView / myAir adherence data Rival weakened by 2021 recall FDA-cleared product portfolio 62.5% non-GAAP gross margin Net cash balance sheet (D/E 0.10)

The moat is not the blower — it is the loop. A patient is diagnosed, a provider sets up a connected device, AirView reports nightly usage to the provider and, in many cases, the insurer, and the resupply schedule for masks and cushions runs off that data. Every part of the loop makes the next part cheaper for ResMed and more annoying to replace for the provider. The 2021 Philips recall and the 2024 consent decree handed ResMed years in which its main rival was restricted from selling new CPAP machines in the US. The vulnerability is that the loop starts with a diagnosis — and anything that shrinks the number of people who need one attacks the top of the funnel, not the margin.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
GLP-1 drugs and the size of the patient pool. Some research suggests weight-loss drugs may reduce sleep-apnea severity over time. ResMed's own data points the other way — GLP-1 patients are more likely to start PAP — but that is company-sourced and early. This is the thesis behind the August 2023 crash and a large part of the 2026 drawdown.
02
Quality is now a two-sided story. ResMed's franchise was built partly on a rival's recall. The FDA Class I recall of the Astral 100/150 ventilators in July 2026 — a component that may leak and damage circuitry — puts the same scrutiny on ResMed. The ventilator line is small, but a second quality event in the sleep line would hit the moat itself.
03
Growth is slowing in fiscal 2027. Q1 revenue grew 5.9% reported (7% core constant currency), the slowest of the nine quarters StockAnalysis lists. FY2027 revenue guidance of $5.75–$5.85B, held in October, compared with an LSEG consensus of $5.92B when it was issued in August, per Medical Device Network. The MatrixCare sale and the Astral suspension explain much of the gap, but the headline growth rate investors see will be low single digits.
04
Philips' eventual return. The consent decree restricts Philips until requirements are met; it is not a permanent exit. When the rival is back at full strength in the US, some of the share ResMed gained by default is contestable.

Notable One-Off Items

Fiscal 2026 GAAP results carry three items the company excludes from non-GAAP figures: a $41.9M Astral field-safety provision (all in Q4), $21.7M of restructuring expense, and $11.5M of acquisition and portfolio-review expense ($5.6M in Q4). They explain most of the gap between GAAP EPS of $10.43 and non-GAAP EPS of $11.17, and why Q4 GAAP EPS grew only 2% while non-GAAP grew 16%. In Q1 FY2027, GAAP EPS ($2.92) and non-GAAP EPS ($2.91) nearly match because a $62M pre-tax gain on the MatrixCare sale ($0.38 a share after tax) offset restructuring and business-transformation costs ($29M, $0.16 a share), amortization of acquired intangibles (the gradual write-off of patents and customer lists that came with acquisitions; $14M, $0.07), acquisition and divestment costs ($10M, $0.07) and a $0.07 tax item tied to interest on tax refunds. From Q1 the company also books restructuring costs and that amortization inside its cost of sales and expense lines and has restated earlier quarters; operating income, net income and EPS are unchanged by the move. The portfolio has been reshaped: the Noctrix Health acquisition ($340M, a restless-legs-syndrome treatment company) is in the base, and the Q1 core growth rate leaves it out; the MatrixCare sale closed in the quarter for $450.8M of proceeds net of cash divested. Capital returns were $1.0B in fiscal 2026 (buybacks plus dividends) and $1.03B in Q1 alone ($936M of buybacks, including the $450M accelerated repurchase, plus $95M of dividends); management guides to more than $1.85B for fiscal 2027. Shares outstanding were 140.66M on October 26, 2026 (10-Q cover), down 1.35% on the year (StockAnalysis).

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $254.85 to the November 2, 2026 settled close of $232.94, RMD lost 8.6% of its price, while SPY (an S&P 500 fund) gained 65.1% and QQQ (a Nasdaq-100 fund) 72.9% on price over the same dates. Adding back the 19 dividends with ex-dates in the window ($9.70 a share) brings RMD's total return to roughly −5% (estimate, dividends not reinvested). The window opens five months after the June 2021 Philips recall, while the market still priced in a rival's absence at close to full value. The five years since have been a range with two trap doors — the August 2023 GLP-1 scare that took the stock to a $141.22 October 2023 low, and the 2026 slide from $274.51 (August 2025) to $190.57 (May 2026). The first recovery set the window's high; the second reached a $253.20 close on October 26, 2026 before the Q1 print knocked it back.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 21.2 times trailing and 19.2 times fiscal 2027 earnings for the category leader in sleep-apnea therapy: 62.5% non-GAAP gross margin, 27% ROIC, net cash, $1.61B of free cash flow, a 10% dividend raise and more than $1.85B of planned capital returns in fiscal 2027, $1.03B of it already paid out in Q1. Earnings roughly doubled over fiscal 2022–2026 while the stock fell. The company's own data says GLP-1 patients are more likely to start PAP, not less; the Astral recall is a known, sized ~$75M headwind in a small ventilator line; and the fiscal 2027 revenue "miss" is largely a portfolio choice (the MatrixCare sale). Masks grew 10% again in Q1; if that holds, the 8.5% of shares sold short may have to be bought back.

Bear Case

The bear case does not dispute this year's numbers; it disputes the size of the patient pool in five years. If GLP-1 drugs reduce sleep-apnea severity at scale, fewer people get diagnosed, fewer devices get placed, and the mask annuity slowly runs off. The stock's best years were priced on a rival's recall that will not last forever, and ResMed now has a Class I recall of its own. Q1 revenue grew 5.9%, the slowest of the last nine quarters, guidance was only held, the stock fell 9.1% on the first session after the print, and 10 of 20 analysts rate it Hold. About 21 times earnings is not cheap for a business whose long-run demand curve is genuinely in question.

Top 3 Risks

01
GLP-1 demand erosion. The thesis behind both big drawdowns in the window (August 2023, March–May 2026). The evidence so far is company-sourced and runs the other way, but it is early, and the market will not give credit until diagnosis and new-patient device growth prove it over several years.
02
Quality and regulatory exposure. The July 2026 Astral Class I recall has a $41.9M provision and a ~$75M revenue impact expected in fiscal 2027. A quality problem spreading to the core sleep devices or masks would damage the franchise that benefited from Philips' recall.
03
Competitive normalisation. Philips operates under a consent decree that restricts, rather than ends, its US sleep business. As it re-qualifies, the share ResMed gained by default becomes contestable on price and features.
Industry Commentary
GLP-1 drugs vs. the sleep-apnea funnel
CRITICAL DEBATE

Weight-loss drugs that treat obesity and type 2 diabetes have been the overhang on this stock since 2023, because some researchers suggest they may reduce the severity of sleep apnea over time. The market acted on that twice: a 28.2% one-month fall in August 2023 and a 30.6% slide from August 2025 to May 2026. ResMed's counter is data from its own ecosystem — on the Q1 call CEO Mick Farrell said patients with prescriptions for both CPAP and a GLP-1 are more than 11% more likely to start CPAP therapy — which reads as GLP-1s pulling people into doctors' offices where sleep apnea gets diagnosed. Both can be true at once: more diagnoses in the near term, less severe disease in the long term. The quarterly tell is Americas sleep-device growth: +8% to $436M in Q1 FY2027 (+6% in constant currency), per the release. At a Morgan Stanley investor discussion, Farrell said GLP-1s could add 0.5 to 1.25 percentage points to Americas sleep-device growth, as summarised by MarketBeat on September 21, 2026.

Recalls cut both ways
ONGOING

The Philips Respironics recall of June 2021 — driven by degrading sound-abatement foam — and the April 9, 2024 consent decree restricting its new CPAP and BiPAP sales in the US reshaped this industry in ResMed's favour. The July 2026 FDA Class I recall of ResMed's own Astral 100 and 150 ventilators is far smaller in scale — a single ventilator line, five serious injuries and no deaths reported as of June 23, 2026 — but it reminds investors that the same regulatory machinery applies to the leader. The provision was taken in Q4 FY2026; the revenue cost lands in fiscal 2027.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At the June 2022 close of $209.63 the stock cost about 40 times the $5.30 of GAAP EPS it earned that fiscal year, and at its August 2025 high of $274.51 about 29 times fiscal 2025's $9.51 (our calculation, price ÷ GAAP EPS); at $232.94 it is 21.2 times trailing GAAP EPS and 19.2 times fiscal 2027 consensus. The bull framing: this is a category leader with 60%-plus gross margins, a recurring consumables stream and net cash, priced at about half its 2022 multiple because of a theme rather than a result. The bear framing: the old 29–40 times multiples were the anomaly — a premium paid for a rival's absence and an uncontested patient pool, neither of which is guaranteed. The way to decide is not the P/E; it is several consecutive quarters of Americas device growth, which is the closest public read on new-patient starts.
