01 Company Overview

Rolls-Royce is the London-listed engine maker behind the Trent family of engines on Airbus and Boeing widebody jets, the EJ200 in the Eurofighter Typhoon, the nuclear reactors in Britain's submarines and, through its German mtu brand, diesel and gas engines that keep data centres running when the grid fails. It reports three divisions — Civil Aerospace, Defence and Power Systems — and owns 57.8% of Rolls-Royce SMR, its small-modular-reactor company (December 2025). In 2025 underlying revenue was £20.06B and underlying operating profit £3.46B, a 17.3% margin. The first half of 2026, reported on July 30, went further: revenue £11.28B, up 26% organically, operating profit £2.53B, up 46%, margin 22.5%, free cash flow £1.96B, and full-year guidance raised to £4.7–4.9B of operating profit from £4.0–4.2B. At the October 2, 2026 close the company was worth £122.6B, or $161.8B.

The story is a turnaround that became a compounding machine. Tufan Erginbilgic took over as chief executive on January 1, 2023; the board's remuneration committee wrote in the 2025 annual report that since then "over £88bn of shareholder value has been created as at 31 December 2025". The share price tells it more bluntly: 140.14p at the end of September 2021, 69.59p at the September 2022 low, 1,477.20p now — up 954% in five years against 47.6% for the FTSE 100. The engine of the change is the aftermarket. Airlines pay Rolls-Royce by the hour their engines fly under long-term service agreements, and management has spent three years renegotiating those contracts, extending engine life and cutting overhaul costs. That is also the question for the valuation: at 41.1× trailing underlying earnings, how much of the 2026 profit jump is repeatable? Of H1's £2.53B, £497M came from contract catch-ups and released loss provisions, and the CFO told analysts the second half would carry less of it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Installed widebody fleet on flying-hour contracts 2,266-engine order book £17.5B defence backlog Sovereign nuclear capability mtu data-centre framework deals to 2030 Net cash £2.1B, A3 / A- ratings

The moat is the fleet. Every engine delivered joins a fleet Rolls-Royce services under long-term agreements: under its TotalCare model the airline pays per flying hour while Rolls-Royce controls when and how the engine is overhauled — "we have discretion over when engines come into the shop and the work scope to be done," as the CFO put it on July 30. That makes the revenue stream long and hard to dislodge, and it means every gain in engine life or overhaul cost flows straight into contract margins. Defence adds sovereign programmes with decade-long budgets: the UK's submarine reactors, GCAP and the B-52 re-engining. Moody's and Fitch upgraded the group to A3 and A- in the first half of 2026, both with stable outlooks; S&P affirmed BBB+ and moved its outlook to positive.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Part of the profit jump is accounting catch-up. £497M of H1 2026's £2,534M operating profit — about 20% — came from contract catch-ups and released provisions on loss-making contracts. Management says the improvements are genuine and catch-ups will recur "at least at some level", but expects less in H2; the CEO expects some more onerous-contract releases next year but not in the mid-term. Investors paying 41× trailing earnings need the recurring part to keep growing.
02
One customer type, one aircraft class. Civil Aerospace is 55% of revenue and depends on widebody flying. The 2026 Middle East conflict showed the exposure: the CEO said the largest impact on Rolls-Royce came through Middle Eastern airlines, and the company now guides flying hours towards the low end of its range. Fuel-driven cancellations have so far been mostly narrowbody; as the CEO put it, "If I was running narrowbody, probably I would worry about it a lot more."
03
Supply chain and product-cost inflation. The 2026 cash guidance includes a £150–200M supply-chain cost, and H1 carried £77M of extra contract charges from higher product costs; the CFO named rare earths and elementals as pressure points. Management expects the cost to fall in 2027 and be gone by 2028.
04
Re-entering narrowbody is a big, unpriced bet. The CEO confirmed talks with "multiple parties" about a partner for a narrowbody engine and ruled out a pure financial partner. Any programme would mean years of development spending before revenue.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, the currency hedge book: Rolls-Royce sells mostly in dollars and reports in pounds, so statutory results swing with the market value of its US$22B of hedges — statutory profit for H1 2026 was £1.6B against £4.4B a year earlier, which had a £1.6B hedge gain and a £679M gain on deconsolidating Rolls-Royce SMR. Second, tax credits: H1 2026 underlying tax included a £181M one-off credit (re-recognised advance corporation tax), excluded from underlying EPS and the dividend; 2025 had a £277M equivalent. Third, contract catch-ups and provision releases of £497M in Civil Aerospace, which are part of underlying profit and which management expects to be smaller in H2. Statutory operating profit also absorbed a £117M pension past-service charge.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of 140.14p to the October 2, 2026 close of 1,477.20p, Rolls-Royce rose 954% on price alone — more than ten times the starting value. The path had two halves. Through September 2022 the stock fell to 69.59p, the low month-end of the window. From there it rose in twelve consecutive quarters, to 1,190.00p at the end of September 2025 (299.70p at the end of 2023, 568.60p at the end of 2024), then slipped to 1,150.00p by December. 2026 has been choppier: 1,333.50p in February, 1,132.00p in March (−15.1%), a month in which the conflict in the Middle East cut flying by Middle Eastern airlines, then a recovery to the window's highest month-end of 1,530.20p in August.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how one-way the climb was: it stayed above 80 every month from July 2023 to October 2025, peaking at 99.5 in July 2025. The March 2026 drop pulled it to 55.2; it now reads 69.3, just under the overbought line. The price sits above its 10-month moving average of about 1,357.73p, so the long-term trend is still up; the stock has gone sideways since the August peak.

Over the last year the index gained 11.0% and Rolls-Royce 26.5%. That outperformance has made Rolls-Royce the FTSE 100's fourth-largest member, at 4.99% of the index on August 28, 2026, behind HSBC, Shell and AstraZeneca. The comparison is price only; neither series includes dividends, and Rolls-Royce paid none for more than five years before reinstating dividends in 2025.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Every division is growing and widening margins at the same time: Civil 25.3%, Defence 21.0%, Power Systems 20.3% in H1 2026. 2026 guidance of £4.7–4.9B operating profit already reaches the 2028 target range set only in February, and the CEO has said the targets may be revisited. The service-contract repricing has years to run — the CEO said only half of in-production contracts will be on new terms even by 2028, and only 25% of the cash value created by improving them will be realised by then. Power Systems has a data-centre order book stretching to 2030, Defence a £17.5B backlog, SMR contracts in the UK and the Czech Republic and a selection in Sweden, and the balance sheet holds net cash while returning almost 90% of 2026 free cash flow. At 29.7× FY2027 consensus earnings, continued upgrades would bring the multiple down quickly.

Bear Case

The price already assumes it all. It trades at 41.1× trailing and 33.8× forward earnings, with a 3.0% free-cash-flow yield and a 0.74% dividend yield — about a quarter of the FTSE 100's 3.05% — for a business whose biggest division depends on widebody flying hours. Roughly a fifth of H1's operating profit came from catch-ups and provision releases that management itself expects to shrink. 2026 showed the exposure: the shares fell 15.1% in March, the month the Middle East conflict disrupted airline flying, and flying-hour guidance is now towards the bottom of its range. Supply-chain inflation, a possible narrowbody programme and the long road to SMR revenue are all costs before they are profits.

Top 3 Risks

01
Quality of the 2026 profit jump. £497M of net contractual improvements in H1 (£372M catch-ups, £125M provision releases) is about 20% of operating profit. If H2 and 2027 carry much less of it, growth in reported profit slows even as the underlying business improves — and the valuation leaves little room for a slowdown.
02
Widebody flying hours. Civil Aerospace is 55% of revenue and paid by the hour. A further shock — conflict, fuel, recession — feeds straight into revenue and into the cash airlines prepay. Flying-hour guidance is already towards the low end of 115–120% of 2019.
03
Execution on new programmes. The Trent XWB-97 durability upgrade (service entry 2028), the B-52 and MV-75 engines, GCAP, the SMR builds and any narrowbody engine all need years of engineering spend, and the supply chain is still adding £150–200M of cost in 2026.
Industry Commentary
Selling engines by the hour
CORE ECONOMICS

For Rolls-Royce, servicing engines is the bigger business: H1 2026 Civil Aerospace services revenue was £4.2B against £2.0B from new engines. Under its TotalCare agreements the airline pays for every hour the engine flies, and Rolls-Royce carries the cost of keeping it running. Two numbers decide the profit: how long an engine stays on the wing between overhauls, and what each overhaul costs. The CEO told analysts the time-on-wing programme targets more than doubling durability across in-production engines by the end of 2027, with the majority already delivered, and that "only 25% of the cash value that we created through improving our LTSA contracts and only 30% of the cash value of our OE renegotiations will be realised by the end of 2028." If that holds, the aftermarket keeps compounding well after the current guidance period.

Power for data centres
EMERGING TAILWIND

Data centres need backup generators in case the grid fails, and increasingly their own continuous ("prime") power while they wait for a grid connection. That has made mtu's engines one of the fastest-growing lines in the group: power-generation revenue +41% and Power Systems order intake +50% in H1 2026, with framework agreements running to 2030. The CEO said prime power is under 10% of the power-generation business today and should reach 15–20% by 2030, with far higher aftermarket revenue because those engines run continuously. The risk, raised by an analyst on the call, is that competitors are adding capacity for the same demand; the CEO said he did not see an overcapacity risk.

Defence budgets and nuclear
LONG-DATED

UK defence plans have firmed up, and Rolls-Royce sits on the programmes the Defence Investment Plan names: submarine reactors under AUKUS and the UK deterrent, the GCAP fighter engine, and propulsion for autonomous aircraft, where the UK's Defence Investment Plan earmarks £5B. In civil nuclear, the company describes Rolls-Royce SMR as the only SMR company with multiple contractual commitments in Europe. These are decade-long stories: they support the backlog and the multiple today, while the cash arrives late in the decade and beyond.
