01 Company Overview

Ross Stores is the second-largest off-price retailer in the United States, selling branded apparel, footwear, accessories and home fashions at 20–60% below department-store prices through 2,328 stores under two banners: Ross Dress for Less (1,952 stores) and dd's DISCOUNTS (376 stores). Fiscal 2025, the 52 weeks ended January 31, 2026, produced record revenue of $22.75B, up 7.7%, on comparable-store sales of +5%; the trailing twelve months through August 1, 2026 have accelerated sharply to $24.51B, up 14.0%. The company ranks roughly #349 globally by market capitalisation at $73.67B and is a member of the NASDAQ-100.

Off-price is traded on exactly two numbers — comparable-store sales (comps: sales growth from stores open at least a year, stripping out new-store growth) and merchandise margin (what the buyer pays for goods versus what they sell for). Ross has just printed +10% comps for a second straight quarter while TJX's comparable U.S. apparel division, Marmaxx, comped +1% and Burlington comped +2% in the same thirteen weeks. That gap is the whole page. Either Ross has captured a genuine trade-down wave from full-price retail under new CEO Jim Conroy, or it is taking share from two competitors who stumbled at the same time — and the distinction determines whether 28× trailing earnings is a bargain or a peak-cycle multiple. The complicating detail: $253M of one-time tariff refunds sit inside the Q2 print, worth $0.60 of the $2.66 reported EPS. Section 04 separates the two.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
OPPORTUNISTIC BUYING SCALE VENDOR RELATIONSHIPS PACKAWAY INVENTORY TREASURE-HUNT TRAFFIC LOW-COST STORE MODEL NO E-COMMERCE DRAG NET-CASH BALANCE SHEET

The off-price moat is a buying moat. Ross employs a large merchant organisation that buys close-outs, cancelled orders and overruns from thousands of vendors, often in-season, for cash, with no return privileges and no advertising allowances — terms full-price retailers cannot offer. The bigger the buyer, the more of a vendor's problem it can take in one call, so scale compounds: Ross at $24.5B of revenue and TJX at $62.4B sit in a two-player tier that Burlington ($12.2B) is still climbing toward. Packaway — buying goods now and warehousing them for next season — converts vendor distress into future margin and is why merchandise margin can expand (+110bps in Q2 ex-refunds) in a quarter when input costs were supposedly rising. The stores are cheap to run (no fixtures to speak of, minimal marketing, no e-commerce fulfilment cost — Ross does not sell online), which is how a 33% gross margin produces a 12.7% operating margin and a 36% ROIC. The vulnerability of the moat is that it is shared: TJX has every one of these advantages at 2.5× the scale. Ross wins when it executes better — which this year it clearly has — not because it holds something TJX lacks.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Sourcing concentration and tariff exposure. In May 2025 CEO Conroy told investors that "more than half of the goods we sell originate from China" and withdrew full-year guidance, guiding Q2 2025 EPS to $1.40–1.55 with a $0.11–0.16 per-share tariff hit. The full-year 2025 tariff cost came in at about $0.16 per share. The refund of those IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) duties — $253M in Q2 2026 — is what makes the current quarter look so good. Off-price buys what vendors over-ordered, so Ross's sourcing mix is the industry's sourcing mix; it cannot easily diversify it.
▸
Competitors reinvesting refunds into price. Burlington received $55M of refunds and stated it will "fully invest these refunds back into the business in the back-half of the year, to deliver even sharper values" — 40% in Q3, 60% in Q4. TJX received $331M (net $219M after compensation accruals). Ross took its $253M to earnings. If competitors use their windfall to cut prices while Ross books its as profit, Ross's Q4 comp guide of +4–5% is the number at risk.
▸
Comp cycle mean reversion. Ross is lapping a +2% comp with a +10%; next year it laps a +10%. Management's own guide already steps down — Q3 +6–7%, Q4 +4–5% — and the original FY2026 guide in March was only +3–4%. TJX's Marmaxx comp of +1% was blamed on "self-inflicted" merchandising errors that management says are "within our control"; if TJX fixes them, some of Ross's share gain reverses.
▸
New leadership, thin track record at this scale. The CEO and CFO both changed within roughly a year of each other. The results under Conroy have been excellent, but one strong year against a weak comparison and a stumbling rival is not yet a cycle. Ross also has no e-commerce channel — a cost advantage today, and an unhedged bet that the treasure hunt stays a store experience.

Notable One-Off Items

There is one large distortion and it sits right in the headline quarter.

IEEPA tariff refunds, ~$253M pre-tax, recognised in Q2 FY2026. Duties paid under the 2025 emergency tariffs were refunded and booked through cost of goods sold, adding about 405 basis points to Q2 operating margin and $0.60 to EPS. Reported Q2 EPS was $2.66 (+70.5%); excluding the refund it was roughly $2.06, +32% — still a big number, and still well above the company's own $1.85–1.93 pre-refund guidance. Reported operating margin expanded 610bps to 17.6%; the underlying 205bps expansion beat the 130–150bps plan. The full-year EPS guide of $8.61–8.77 includes the $0.60, so the clean FY2026 number is about $8.01–8.17 against $6.61 last year, roughly +21–24%. Ross, unlike Burlington, is not committing to reinvest the refund into price.

Tariff costs in FY2025 were about $0.16 per share — so the FY2025 base was slightly depressed and the FY2026 comparison slightly flattered even before the refund.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The two numbers, side by side. Off-price is a simple machine: buy goods cheaply because someone else made a mistake, sell them quickly in a cheap box. So the market watches whether customers are showing up (comps) and whether the buyers are getting good deals (merchandise margin). In Q2 FY2026 both went the right way at once — comps +10%, traffic-led, in every region, and merchandise margin +110bps excluding the refund. When traffic is that strong, the temptation is to chase it with worse-quality inventory and give the margin back; Ross did the opposite. That is the strongest single argument that this is execution, not luck.

The peer test. In the identical thirteen weeks to August 1, 2026, TJX's Marmaxx comped +1% (TJX consolidated +4%, carried by HomeGoods at +7%) and Burlington comped +2%. TJX's CEO called the Marmaxx miss "self-inflicted" — the wrong apparel in the wrong stores at the wrong time. That is both the bull and the bear case in one quote. Bull: Ross took share from a wounded competitor with better merchandising and is keeping the customers it gained (management says lapsed customers are returning and existing ones visiting more often — habits, not a one-time event). Bear: TJX is the best operator in the industry, says the problem is within its control, and has a $331M refund it has not yet spent on price. Burlington has already said it will spend its $55M on "even sharper values" — 40% this quarter, 60% in the holiday quarter — while Ross booked its $253M as profit. Ross is choosing margin; its competitors are choosing traffic. The Q3 (+6–7%) and Q4 (+4–5%) comp guides are where that choice gets marked.

The trade-down question. The macro version of the thesis is that a stretched consumer trades down from department stores to off-price. The dd's evidence cuts against a pure distress reading: the lower-income banner comped below Ross on a one-year basis and only in line on two-year. If the customer were simply poorer, dd's would lead. Instead the Ross customer — middle-income, brand-aware — is the one accelerating, and home and cosmetics (discretionary categories) led the quarter. That looks more like share gain from full-price and from Marmaxx than macro trade-down, which is better for durability (habits stick) but worse for the "this works in a recession" framing.

Tariffs — the round trip. The 2025 episode cost about $0.16 per share for the year, forced a guidance withdrawal, and knocked the stock to $127. The 2026 refund returned $0.60 in a single quarter. Net, tariffs have been a positive for reported earnings across the two years — which is absurd, and exactly why the clean numbers matter: ex-refund FY2026 EPS of roughly $8.01–8.17 versus $6.61 is the real +21–24%, and that is the number the forward multiple should be applied to. The structural exposure has not changed: more than half of what Ross sells originates in China because that is what its vendors make. Any re-escalation lands on Ross first.

Growth algorithm and capital return. Store growth of 115 a year on 2,328 is ~5% unit growth; add a mid-single-digit comp and modest margin expansion and the algorithm is low-double-digit EBIT growth. Buybacks of $1.275B a year retire roughly 1.7% of shares, the dividend pays 0.77%, so shareholder yield is about 2.6%. On $2.79B of free cash flow and $3.3B of net cash the programme is easily covered, and the March 2026 authorisation resets every two years like clockwork. The one criticism is that it is calendar-driven rather than price-driven: the company bought no more aggressively at $127 than at $228.

Valuation. At $230.69: 28.0× trailing EPS of $8.27, or about 30× ex-refund; ~28.5× the clean FY2026 guide of ~$8.01–8.17; 25.8× consensus FY2027 EPS of $8.94; 26.5× free cash flow; EV/EBITDA 20.3×. TJX trades at 24.5× trailing and Burlington at 23.9×. Ross has flipped from a discount to a premium versus TJX, and the premium is roughly the size of the comp gap. If Ross sustains a 3–4 point comp advantage, the premium is deserved; if Marmaxx normalises to mid-single-digit comps and Ross to the +3–4% it originally guided, the two should trade in line and ROST has ~15% of multiple to give back before any earnings miss. A PEG of 1.87 on refund-inflated growth, or ~4 on consensus FY2027 growth of 6.9%, says the market is not pricing in a structural re-rating — it is pricing in that FY2027 consensus is too low. That is a reasonable bet given how conservatively this management guides, but it is a bet on continued outperformance, not on cheapness.

Bull Case

Two consecutive +10% traffic-led comps with expanding merchandise margin, across every region and led by discretionary categories, while the two direct competitors comped +1% and +2% — this is share gain that sticks, not a macro blip. The clean FY2026 earnings guide is up 21–24%, consensus for FY2027 (+6.9%) is almost certainly too low given how conservatively this management guides (March's +3–4% comp guide is now tracking roughly +8%), and 36% ROIC with $3.3B of net cash funds 5% unit growth plus a 2.6% shareholder yield without strain. A defensive 0.86-beta retailer growing earnings 20%+ deserves the premium to TJX it now carries.

Bear Case

Reported EPS growth of 70% is 40 points of one-time tariff refund; the stock trades at ~30× clean trailing earnings, a premium to the better operator (TJX at 24.5×) for the first time in memory, and 53% above where it was a year ago while peers went nowhere. Management's own guide steps comps down to +4–5% by the holiday quarter, exactly when Burlington deploys 60% of its refund into price and TJX has fixed a Marmaxx problem it calls self-inflicted. More than half of merchandise originates in China, the CEO and CFO are both new, there is no e-commerce channel, and the short interest at 3% means no squeeze fuel. A return to in-line multiples is ~15% downside before any operational miss.

Top 3 Risks

01
Comp deceleration into the holiday quarter. Q4 laps a +9% comp with a +4–5% guide while both competitors have announced or hold un-deployed tariff refunds to spend on price. A Q4 comp below +4% would break the "share gain sticks" narrative and, at a premium multiple, the stock reaction would be disproportionate to the miss.
02
Tariff re-escalation. More than half of Ross's merchandise originates in China and the company cannot diversify what its vendors over-produce. The 2025 episode cost $0.16 per share and forced a guidance withdrawal; a repeat would hit at a time when the stock no longer carries a valuation cushion versus peers.
03
Margin give-back. Inventory is up 18% YoY against 13% sales growth and the packaway share has fallen to 36% from 38% — more goods on the floor. That is fine while traffic is +10%; if traffic slows to the guided +4–6% the markdown risk rises, and merchandise margin — the second number the market trades on — would reverse just as the refund comparison turns unfavourable in Q2 FY2027.
  Industry Commentary
The Refund Is Becoming a Price War
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
All three U.S. off-price chains received IEEPA tariff refunds in the same quarter — TJX $331M (net $219M), Ross $253M, Burlington $55M — and they are doing three different things with them. Ross booked its to earnings. TJX reported adjusted figures excluding it and has not said how it will use the cash. Burlington said explicitly it will "fully invest these refunds back into the business in the back-half of the year, to deliver even sharper values," 40% in Q3 and 60% in Q4. In a business where the value gap versus department stores is the product, a competitor announcing it will widen its gap with found money is a direct threat to Ross's Q4 comp guide of +4–5% — and Ross has already spent its ammunition on the P&L.
China Sourcing Is the Industry's Fixed Exposure
ONGOING HEADWIND
Off-price does not choose its sourcing — it buys what full-price vendors over-ordered, so its country mix is a lagged copy of the apparel industry's. Ross disclosed that more than half its goods originate in China; the 2025 tariff round cost it about $0.16 per share and its guidance for a quarter. The same duties were refunded a year later, but the policy volatility itself is the cost: it forced a guidance withdrawal, a $50 drawdown in the stock and a year of hedged commentary. Ross's advantage is the packaway model — it can buy the vendor's tariff-stranded inventory at distress prices, as it did in 2022 with the freight glut. Its disadvantage is that it cannot pre-buy its way out of a duty it does not know is coming.
Off-Price Keeps Taking the Department Store's Customer
EMERGING TAILWIND
The three chains are opening a combined 250+ stores this year (Ross 115, Burlington ~115, TJX targeting 4% annual store growth toward 7,500 stores from 2028) into a mall-anchor base that keeps shrinking. Ross's Q2 traffic gains came from new customers, lapsed customers and higher frequency — a customer-acquisition story, not a ticket story. Bull framing: the structural shift from full-price to off-price is a decade-long trend with 5% unit growth on top, and Ross has just proven it can out-merchandise TJX; the premium multiple is the price of the best comp in retail. Bear framing: the trend is shared by all three and Ross's relative outperformance rests on one competitor's admitted execution error and a refund the others are turning into price cuts. The industry tailwind is real; the Ross-specific premium is the part that has to be re-earned every quarter.
