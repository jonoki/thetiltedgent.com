01 Company Overview

Ross Stores is the second-largest off-price retailer in the United States, selling branded apparel, footwear, accessories and home fashions at 20–60% below department-store prices through 2,377 stores under two banners: Ross Dress for Less (1,993 stores) and dd's DISCOUNTS (384 stores) at October 31, 2026. Fiscal 2025, the 52 weeks ended January 31, 2026, produced record revenue of $22.75B, up 7.7%, on comparable-store sales of +5%; the trailing twelve months through October 31, 2026 reached $25.17B, up 14.3%. At the November 23, 2026 close the company was worth $69.33B (319.45M shares × $217.04, our calculation).

Off-price is traded on exactly two numbers — comparable-store sales (comps: sales growth from stores open at least a year, stripping out new-store growth) and merchandise margin (what the buyer pays for goods versus what they sell for). Ross followed two +10% quarters with +8% in the third quarter, against a +7% a year earlier and above its own +6–7% guide, while TJX's U.S. apparel division, Marmaxx, comped +2% in the same thirteen weeks. That gap is the whole page. Either Ross has captured a genuine trade-down wave from full-price retail under CEO Jim Conroy, or it is taking share from competitors who stumbled at the same time — and the distinction decides whether 25× trailing earnings is fair or a price that assumes this peak lasts. Q3 was the first quarter this year with no tariff refund in it: EPS rose 21% to $1.91 on its own. The $253M refund booked in Q2 (worth about $0.60 a share) still sits in the full-year numbers; Section 04 separates the two. Reuters tied the November 20 fall to the holiday quarter: a +4–5% comp guide against +9% a year ago, and a profit range the CFO said reflects higher fuel costs.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
OPPORTUNISTIC BUYING SCALE VENDOR RELATIONSHIPS PACKAWAY INVENTORY TREASURE-HUNT TRAFFIC LOW-COST STORE MODEL NO E-COMMERCE DRAG NET-CASH BALANCE SHEET

The off-price moat is a buying moat. Ross employs a large merchant organisation that buys close-outs, cancelled orders and overruns from thousands of vendors, often in-season, for cash, with no return privileges and no advertising allowances — terms full-price retailers cannot offer. The bigger the buyer, the more of a vendor's problem it can take in one call, so scale builds on itself: Ross at $25.2B of revenue and TJX at $63.2B sit in a two-player tier that Burlington ($12.2B) is still climbing toward. Packaway — buying goods now and warehousing them for next season — converts vendor distress into future margin and is why merchandise margin kept expanding this year (+1.1 points in Q2 excluding refunds, +0.2 in Q3) while tariffs and freight pushed costs up. The stores are cheap to run (no fixtures to speak of, modest marketing, no e-commerce fulfilment cost — Ross does not sell online), which is how a 34% gross margin (stockanalysis.com's trailing basis) produces a 13.9% operating margin and a 36% ROIC (return on invested capital: after-tax operating profit per dollar of capital in the business). The vulnerability of the moat is that it is shared: TJX has every one of these advantages at 2.5× the scale. Ross wins when it executes better — which this year it clearly has — not because it holds something TJX lacks.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Sourcing concentration and tariff exposure. In its May 2025 first-quarter release Ross said that "more than half of the goods we sell originate from China" and withdrew full-year guidance, guiding Q2 2025 EPS to $1.40–1.55 with a $0.11–0.16 per-share tariff hit. The full-year 2025 tariff cost came in at about $0.16 per share. The refund of those IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) duties — $253M in Q2 2026 — is about $0.60 of this year's EPS guide. Off-price buys what vendors over-ordered, so Ross's sourcing mix is the industry's sourcing mix; it cannot easily diversify it.
▸
Competitors reinvesting refunds into price. Burlington received $55M of refunds and stated it will "fully invest these refunds back into the business in the back-half of the year" to "deliver even sharper values" — 40% in Q3, 60% in Q4. TJX received $331M in Q2 (net $219M after compensation accruals) and another $132M in Q3 ($77M net), which it leaves out of its adjusted results. Ross took its $253M to earnings. So far it has not hurt: Ross comped +8% in Q3, the quarter in which Burlington said it would spend 40% of its refund, and CFO Bill Sheehan said on the call, "I do not think we have seen any competitive pressure from a pricing standpoint that has been hurting our business." The heavier 60% of Burlington's spend lands in the holiday quarter, where Ross's +4–5% comp guide is the number at risk.
▸
Comps falling back to normal. Ross comped +10%, +10% and +8% in the first three quarters; next year each quarter is measured against those. Management's own guide steps down to +4–5% for Q4 against a +9% a year earlier, and the original FY2026 guide in March was only +3–4%. TJX's Marmaxx improved from +1% in Q2 to +2% in Q3; TJX had called the Q2 miss "self-inflicted" and "within our control", and if it fixes it, some of Ross's share gain reverses.
▸
Freight and fuel. Higher fuel prices pushed freight costs up by 0.25 points of sales in Q3, and Reuters reported after the call that higher domestic freight costs are pressuring margins. Conroy said on the call that Ross wanted to keep its prices low for customers, "particularly in today's environment", and the CFO said the trimmed Q4 earnings range reflects higher fuel costs.
▸
New leadership, thin track record at this scale. The CEO and CFO both changed within roughly a year of each other, and two directors joined the board on October 1, 2026. The results under Conroy have been excellent, but one strong year against a weak comparison and a stumbling rival is not yet a cycle. Ross also has no e-commerce channel — a cost advantage today, and an unhedged bet that the treasure hunt stays a store experience.

Notable One-Off Items

There is one large distortion, booked in the second quarter and carried in every year-to-date and trailing figure until Q2 FY2027, when the year-earlier quarter includes it too.

IEEPA tariff refunds, ~$253M pre-tax, recognised in Q2 FY2026. Duties paid under the 2025 emergency tariffs were refunded and booked through cost of goods sold, adding about 4 points to Q2 operating margin and about $0.60 to EPS. Reported Q2 EPS was $2.66; excluding the refund it was roughly $2.06. Q3 carried no refund: EPS of $1.91 (+21%) and the 12.3% operating margin (+0.7 points, above the 11.7–12.0% plan) are clean. Nine-month EPS of $6.59 against $4.61 includes the $0.60. The full-year EPS guide of $8.75–8.84 includes it too, so the clean FY2026 number is about $8.15–8.24 against $6.61 last year, roughly +23–25%. Ross, unlike Burlington, did not commit to reinvest the refund into price.

Tariff costs in FY2025 were about $0.16 per share — so the FY2025 base was slightly depressed and the FY2026 comparison slightly flattered even before the refund.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 close of $109.09 to $217.04 on November 23, 2026, ROST has roughly doubled — +99.0% in price terms, about 14.8% a year before dividends, against +58.6% for SPY (an S&P 500 fund) and +65.3% for QQQ (a Nasdaq-100 fund) over the same dates. But most of the gain arrived in the last seventeen months. The stock spent June 2022 to June 2025 between $70 and $155 at month-ends — it closed June 2025 at $127.58, only 17% above where it closed November 2021 — and then rose +70% from that June 2025 close to today. Over the past year (Nov 21, 2025 close to Nov 23, 2026 close) ROST rose +24.7% while TJX fell 8.0%, Burlington 20.7%, and SPY rose 9.6%: investors paid more for Ross, not for off-price as a group, and the gap maps onto the comp gap. Since the July 2026 month-end high of $251.07 the stock has given back 13.6%, and at $217.04 it closed below its 50-day ($230.64) and 200-day ($225.67) daily averages, with a 14-day daily RSI of 40. A 0.85-beta retailer has delivered growth-stock returns, which means the next twelve months are being asked to justify the last eighteen rather than the last sixty.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The two numbers, side by side. Off-price is a simple machine: buy goods cheaply because someone else made a mistake, sell them quickly in a cheap box. So the market watches whether customers are showing up (comps) and whether the buyers are getting good deals (merchandise margin). In Q3 FY2026 both went the right way again — comps +8%, traffic-led, in every region, and merchandise margin +0.2 points on top of Q2's +1.1 excluding the refund. When traffic is that strong, the temptation is to chase it with worse-quality inventory and give the margin back; Ross has not, and inventory grew in line with sales. That is the strongest single argument that this is execution, not luck.

The peer test. In the identical thirteen weeks to October 31, 2026, TJX's Marmaxx comped +2% (TJX consolidated +4%, HomeGoods +5%), after +1% in Q2. TJX's CEO had said the Q2 issues were "self-inflicted and within our control" (missing basic and impulse items, a store mix he said could have been executed better), and Q3 showed only a small improvement. Bull: Ross took share from a wounded competitor with better merchandising and is keeping the customers it gained. Bear: TJX is the best operator in the industry, says the problem is within its control, and now guides its own holiday quarter at +2–3% consolidated. Burlington said in August it would spend its $55M of refunds on "even sharper values" — 40% in Q3, 60% in the holiday quarter — while Ross booked its $253M as profit; Ross's Q3 comp beat its guide anyway, and its CFO said price competition has not been hurting the business. The holiday quarter (+4–5% guide) is where that choice gets marked. Burlington had not reported its Q3 at this edition's date.

The trade-down question. The macro version of the thesis is that a stretched consumer trades down from department stores to off-price. The dd's evidence cuts against a pure distress reading: the lower-income banner comped slightly below Ross on a one-year basis in Q2 and was described as similar in Q3. If the customer were simply poorer, dd's would lead. Instead the Ross customer — middle-income, brand-aware — is keeping pace, and cosmetics and home (discretionary categories) led again. That looks more like share gain from full-price and from Marmaxx than macro trade-down, which is better for durability (habits stick) but worse for the "this works in a recession" framing.

Tariffs — the round trip. The 2025 episode cost about $0.16 per share for the year, forced a guidance withdrawal, and knocked the stock to $127. The 2026 refund returned $0.60 in a single quarter. Net, tariffs have added to reported earnings across the two years — which is absurd, and exactly why the clean numbers matter: ex-refund FY2026 EPS of roughly $8.15–8.24 versus $6.61 is the real +23–25%, and that is the number a forward price-to-earnings multiple should be applied to. The structural exposure has not changed: more than half of what Ross sells originates in China because that is what its vendors make. Any new round of tariffs lands on Ross first. Fuel is the new cost: freight took 0.25 points off Q3 gross margin.

Growth plan and capital return. Store growth of 115 a year on about 2,375 is ~5% more stores a year; add a mid-single-digit comp and modest margin expansion and operating profit grows low double digits. Buybacks of $1.275B a year retire roughly 1.8% of shares at today's price, the dividend pays 0.82%, so shareholder yield is about 2.5% (stockanalysis.com: 2.54%). On $2.54B of free cash flow and $3.16B of net cash the programme is easily covered, and the authorisation has been renewed for two years at a time. The one criticism is that it is set in dollars per year rather than stepped up when the price falls: Q2 and Q3 each spent $319M on about 1.4M shares.

Valuation. At $217.04: 25.3× trailing EPS of $8.59, or about 27× ex-refund; ~26.5× the clean FY2026 guide of ~$8.15–8.24; 24.1× consensus FY2027 EPS of $9.02; 27.3× free cash flow; EV/EBITDA 17.4× (enterprise value, which adds debt and leases and subtracts cash, over operating profit before depreciation; stockanalysis.com). TJX trades at 25.3× trailing and Burlington at 21.1× (our calculation at the Nov 23 closes). The premium to TJX that the last edition flagged at the September price is gone: the two now trade level on trailing earnings, with Ross still comping several points faster. If Ross keeps a 3–4 point comp advantage, that looks cheap relative to TJX; if Ross's comps fall back to the +3–4% it originally guided, level with TJX is about right. A PEG of 1.87 (stockanalysis.com) says the market is still paying for growth: it is betting that FY2027 consensus (+7.5%) is too low, a reasonable bet given how conservatively this management guides, but a bet on continued outperformance, not on cheapness.

Bull Case

Three straight comps of +8% or better (+10%, +10%, +8%; the last two led by more transactions) with merchandise margin still expanding and inventory in line with sales, while TJX's Marmaxx comped +1% and +2% — this is share gain that sticks, not a one-season blip. Q3 beat its own comp, EPS and margin guides with no refund help, the clean FY2026 earnings guide is up 23–25%, consensus for FY2027 (+7.5%) looks low given how conservatively this management guides (March's +3–4% comp guide is tracking +11% through nine months), and 36% ROIC with $3.16B of net cash funds 5% store growth plus a 2.5% shareholder yield without strain. After the November sell-off the stock trades level with TJX on trailing earnings despite growing faster.

Bear Case

Comps have already stepped down from +10% to +8% and the guide takes them to +4–5% in the holiday quarter against +9%, just as Burlington spends 60% of its refund on price and Marmaxx edges back. Freight costs are rising with fuel, management is holding prices rather than passing them on, and the Q4 profit range was trimmed. About $0.60 of this year's EPS is a one-time refund that the FY2027 comparison has to lap, more than half of merchandise originates in China, the CEO and CFO are both new, there is no e-commerce channel, and with only 2.8% of shares sold short there are few short sellers who would have to buy shares back and lift the price. The stock is still up 25% in a year while TJX fell 8% and Burlington 21%; a return to where it traded before this comp run is a long way down.

  Industry Commentary
The Refund Price War: Not Yet Visible
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
All three U.S. off-price chains received IEEPA tariff refunds in the second quarter — TJX $331M (net $219M), Ross $253M, Burlington $55M — and TJX took another $132M ($77M net) in Q3. They are doing three different things with them. Ross booked its to earnings. TJX reports adjusted figures excluding them. Burlington said explicitly it will "fully invest these refunds back into the business in the back-half of the year" to "deliver even sharper values", 40% in Q3 and 60% in Q4. In a business where the value gap versus department stores is the product, a competitor widening its gap with found money is a direct threat. So far the threat has not shown in Ross's numbers: Q3 comps beat the guide, and the CFO said price competition has not been hurting the business. The heavier 60% lands in the holiday quarter, against Ross's +4–5% comp guide.
China Sourcing Is the Industry's Fixed Exposure
ONGOING HEADWIND
Off-price does not choose its sourcing — it buys what full-price vendors over-ordered, so its country mix is a lagged copy of the apparel industry's. Ross disclosed that more than half its goods originate in China; the 2025 tariff round cost it about $0.16 per share and its guidance for a quarter. The same duties were refunded a year later, but the policy volatility itself is the cost: it forced a guidance withdrawal, a $50 drawdown in the stock and a year of hedged commentary. Ross's advantage is the packaway model — it can buy the vendor's tariff-stranded inventory at distress prices, as it did in 2022 with the freight glut. Its disadvantage is that it cannot pre-buy its way out of a duty it does not know is coming.
Off-Price Keeps Taking the Department Store's Customer
EMERGING TAILWIND
Ross and Burlington alone are opening about 230 stores this year (Ross 115 new, Burlington about 115 net new), and TJX added 64 stores in its third quarter, into a mall-anchor base that keeps shrinking. Ross's gains this year have come from more shopper visits rather than bigger baskets — management credited new customers, lapsed customers returning and higher frequency in Q2, and traffic again in Q3 — a customer-acquisition story, not a ticket story. Bull framing: the shift from full-price to off-price is a decade-long trend with 5% store growth on top, and Ross has shown for three quarters that it can out-merchandise TJX. Bear framing: the trend is shared by all three and Ross's relative outperformance rests partly on one competitor's admitted execution error and a refund the others are turning into price cuts. The industry tailwind is real; Ross's lead over its peers has to be re-earned every quarter.
