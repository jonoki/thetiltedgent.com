01 Company Overview

Republic Services is the second-largest of North America's publicly traded solid waste companies by revenue: 377 collection operations, 255 transfer stations, 207 active landfills and 79 recycling centers across the United States and Canada as of the end of FY2025. FY2025 revenue was $16.59B, up 3.5% year over year; the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $17.24B, and the third quarter alone grew 8.2%. At a $75.20B market capitalisation (our calculation: 304.64M shares × the $246.85 close) it ranks #165 in the S&P 500. The business is not complicated — a truck picks up a container, drives to a transfer station or straight to a hole in the ground that Republic owns, and tips it. What is complicated is why a business that simple earns a ROIC of 11.1% and trades at 34.2× earnings.

The answer, and the central debate of this page, is price versus volume. In Q3 2026 Republic put through 5.3% core price — the pure rate increase it pushed onto existing customers — while volume fell 0.6% (Q2: −1.6%). That is the whole industry in one line: a customer base that is barely growing in tonnage bringing in more revenue each year, because the sites that receive the waste cannot be replicated. A new landfill in a populated county is close to impossible to get permitted; the ones that exist were permitted long ago, and people keep throwing things away whatever the economy does. The question is not whether that pricing power is real — the 31.8% adjusted EBITDA margin says it is, even though it was a point lower than the 32.8% of a year earlier — but whether a mid-single-digit grower with a 1.09% dividend yield deserves 34.2× earnings and a 3.80 PEG when the price ceiling is set by what municipalities and franchise regulators will tolerate.

Three very different businesses under one label. Small-container (commercial dumpsters) is the crown jewel: $1,379M in Q3 2026, up 8.8% year over year, mostly open-market contracts where Republic sets the rate. Large-container (roll-offs for construction and demolition) is $859M, up 7.8%, and is the cyclical piece. Residential is $782M, up 3.7%, and is the weakest of the three: municipal franchise contracts, often with CPI-linked escalators that cap price below true cost inflation. Residential volume fell 4.3% in both Q2 and Q3 2026 on contract losses; the CEO said on the Q2 call that Republic will always take price over volume. On related revenue, Q3 small-container and large-container volume each rose 0.5% (large-container fell 2.2% in Q2 on construction softness), and total volume was down 0.6%.

The pricing figures are where the story is. Core price — the rate increase applied to the existing book, before any mix or volume effect — was 5.3% on total revenue and 6.3% on related revenue in Q3, the 6.3% split 7.6% in open market and 4.1% in restricted (franchise and municipal contracts where increases are formula-bound). Average yield — revenue growth from the change in the average price per unit of service — was 2.9% of total revenue, against 3.4% in Q2. As long as average yield sits above cost inflation, margin can expand even with volume flat to falling; in Q3 the adjusted EBITDA margin was 31.8%, down from 32.8% a year earlier, so this quarter did not show that expansion (the CFO's breakdown is in Section 04).

This is the reason the whole thing works. As of December 31, 2025 Republic operated 207 active landfills spanning 41,158 estimated permitted acres with 5.0 billion in-place cubic yards of estimated total available disposal capacity, plus 255 transfer stations. What matters is that this capacity exists because permits were granted, not because Republic chose to spend money: building a new landfill near a major city is, for practical purposes, no longer possible. Existing space is a shrinking resource that cannot be recreated, and the need for it does not go away.

Internalisation — the share of collected waste that Republic disposes of in landfills it owns or operates — ran approximately 67% in fiscal 2025. That is the key number: on two-thirds of tonnage, the disposal fee stays inside the house rather than being paid to a third party, which is why the collection business earns what it does. It also means reported landfill revenue understates the asset's economic contribution by roughly a factor of three, because intercompany volume is eliminated. Landfill revenue, net of intercompany, was $516M in Q3 2026, the same as a year earlier, and transfer revenue was $242M, up 8.0%. In Q2 the flat landfill line ($517M) came against a comparison that included wildfire-driven special waste; excluding those volumes, landfill special waste grew 10.7%.

Read this line for what it is: a commodity pass-through, not a management scorecard. Recycled commodity prices averaged $142 per ton in Q3 2026, up $16 from a year earlier (Q2: $136, down from $149). Recycling processing and commodity sales revenue was $122M, up 14.0% from $107M. When old corrugated cardboard and mixed paper rally, this line inflates revenue and flatters yield; when they fall, it drags. Neither direction says anything about whether the underlying collection franchise got better or worse. Analysts who cheer a commodity-driven beat and then punish a commodity-driven miss are judging the same business two different ways.

The renewable natural gas (RNG) story deserves the same discipline, and here it is important to separate what is running from what is promised. Republic operated 77 landfill gas-to-energy projects at its landfills as of the FY2025 10-K, with more than 30 in development. On RNG specifically, per the Q2 2026 call, two projects commenced operations during Q2 2026 and two more were expected to be operational by year-end. The broader sustainability portfolio — RNG plus the Polymer Centers (plastics recovery facilities; two operating, a third under construction in Allentown, Pennsylvania and slated for commissioning in early 2027) — is guided to contribute roughly $40M of incremental revenue and $20M of incremental EBITDA in 2026. On the $17.45B revenue and $5.56B adjusted EBITDA midpoints of the raised 2026 guidance, that is 0.23% of revenue and 0.36% of EBITDA. It is a real, capital-intensive, credit-and-offtake-dependent business — it is not, on today's numbers, a reason to own the stock.

Sustainability capex reality check: the entire sustainability portfolio adds ~$20M of 2026 EBITDA against $5.550–5.575B guided. Electric collection fleet: more than 280 vehicles at Q3 2026, targeting more than 300 by year-end, out of a fleet serving 377 collection operations.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
UN-PERMITTABLE LANDFILL AIRSPACE 67% INTERNALISATION ROUTE DENSITY FRANCHISE & MUNICIPAL CONTRACTS INELASTIC DEMAND SERIAL TUCK-IN ACQUISITION PFAS DISPOSAL PERMITS

Solid waste is the closest thing in the public equity market to a local toll road, and the toll booth is the landfill. Anyone can buy a truck; nobody can buy a new hole. The permitting process for a modern lined landfill near a population centre involves state environmental review, county zoning, groundwater monitoring commitments and thirty years of post-closure care liability — and it often dies at the first public hearing. So the 207 active sites Republic operates, holding 5.0 billion in-place cubic yards of permitted capacity, are a fixed, depleting, non-reproducible resource sitting in front of a demand curve that does not bend. That is why the industry raises prices faster than inflation year after year, and why core price of 5.3% against volume of −0.6% is a feature rather than a warning.

The second layer is internalisation. At ~67%, two-thirds of every tonne collected is tipped into Republic's own airspace, so the disposal margin that an independent hauler pays away is captured internally. Layer on route density — the marginal stop on an existing route costs almost nothing, so the incumbent in a zip code can always underbid a challenger and still earn more — and you have a business where market share is won street by street and, once won, is very hard to take back. The financial evidence: a 31.8% adjusted EBITDA margin in Q3, an ROE of 18.48%, and a beta of 0.32 — this stock has moved about a third as much as the market, which is exactly what an inflation-linked toll road should do.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The restricted book caps the pricing engine. Core price was 7.6% in open market in Q3 but only 4.1% in restricted — franchise and municipal contracts with formula escalators. If cost inflation speeds up again, those contracts cannot reprice fast enough, and margins stall in exactly the part of the business that is politically hardest to fix. Municipalities under budget pressure are also the counterparty most likely to litigate, re-tender, or municipalise.
▸
Organised labour is escalating. The July 2025 Teamsters strike, which Waste Dive described as the largest conflict yet between Republic and the union, began July 1 in the Boston area (Local 25, about 450 workers) and spread to California, Illinois, Georgia and Washington; by July 10 the Teamsters counted more than 2,000 workers striking or honouring picket lines. Republic's Q3 2026 release puts the 2025 labour-disruption impact at $56 million ($16 million in customer credits and $40 million in cost of operations) and reports none in 2026 so far. The pressure has not gone away — in late July 2026 Teamsters Local 104 authorised a strike at the Buckeye, Arizona landfill, where the union wants the 20 workers in the bargaining unit, offered a 2%-a-year raise, enrolled in the Teamsters' Western States pension plan, and has filed an unfair labour practice charge. A labour cost step-change is the single most credible route to a falling profit margin in a business with no other obvious cost shock.
▸
Volume is slipping and price has to carry growth. Total volume was −0.6% in Q3 (−1.6% in Q2). Residential fell 4.3% in both quarters on contract losses, which the CEO has tied to choosing price over volume; large-container fell 2.2% in Q2 on construction softness, then rose 0.5% in Q3. Giving up contracts to protect pricing is discipline; construction is a cycle you do not control. Republic is pricing 5.3% into a book that is shrinking slightly — that works until the shrinkage exceeds the price, and there is no disclosure that tells you where that crossover sits.
▸
Leverage and the balance sheet. $14.27B of total debt (StockAnalysis's measure) against $92M of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.17 and a current ratio of 0.75. In the release, long-term debt (net of the current portion) was $14.007B at September 30, 2026, plus $48M due within a year, against $12.985B and $596M at the end of 2025, while the company spent about $1.12B on acquisitions and returned $1.43B to shareholders in the first nine months. The cash flows support this comfortably today, but the debt helps pay for a steady run of acquisitions and has to be refinanced at whatever rates are then on offer; borrowing like that is cheap right up to the point it is not.

Notable One-Off Items

The 2025 Teamsters strike. Republic's Q3 2026 release puts the 2025 labour-disruption impact at $56M ($16M of customer credits plus $40M of cost of operations) and reports none in 2026. Against 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance of $5.550–5.575B that is about 1% — a real number, but not one that changes the picture. It depresses the 2025 base, which mechanically flatters 2026 comparisons.

Landfill event volumes in the 2025 base. Prior-year landfill volumes included one-time special waste, including wildfire clean-up. Landfill revenue was flat in both Q2 and Q3 2026 ($517M, then $516M); excluding those volumes, Q2 landfill special waste grew 10.7%. On the Q2 call management had flagged a 40-basis-point margin headwind in Q3 from the prior-year landfill event comparison (a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point) and guided Q3 margins to roughly flat; the reported Q3 adjusted EBITDA margin was 31.8% against 32.8% a year earlier, 100 basis points lower. On the October 29 call the CFO broke that down as 50 basis points from net fuel costs, 40 from the prior-year landfill volumes and 60 from elevated health-care costs, with the rest of the business adding 50.

Recycled commodity prices. $142/ton in Q3 2026, up $16 from a year earlier, after $136 in Q2 (down $13). This is a price, not a performance: it flows straight through revenue and yield.

Acquisitions. Republic invested about $1.12B in acquisitions in the first nine months of 2026, against more than $1.2B guided for the full year on the Q2 call. Acquisitions added 1.3 points to Q3's 8.2% revenue growth. Management had cited acquisition integration costs as one reason Q3 margins would be flat. Serial small-deal acquirers carry a permanent, recurring "one-off" integration line — it is a cost of the growth plan, not an aberration.

Organic versus acquired growth. Q3 2026 revenue grew 8.2%, which the release splits into 4.8 points from recycling and waste, 2.1 points from Environmental Solutions and 1.3 points from acquisitions. Average yield was +2.9% and volume −0.6% on total revenue. Over FY2021–FY2025 revenue rose from $11.3B to $16.59B, a 10.1% compound rate, with acquisitions a regular part of it. Acquisition spend of $1.12B in nine months equals about 49% of the $2.28B of adjusted free cash flow over the same period. Whether that steady buying builds value or only keeps revenue climbing is the open question on this stock, and no disclosure on the page settles it.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 conventionally suggest the stock has run hard and may be overbought; below 30 suggests it has been sold hard and may be oversold. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a network of 207 landfills holding 5.0 billion cubic yards of permitted airspace that cannot be replicated at any price, feeding 377 collection operations that internalise 67% of what they pick up. That structure produced 5.3% core price against −0.6% volume and a 31.8% adjusted EBITDA margin in Q3 2026, with management raising full-year guidance for the second quarter running and adding a new $5 billion buyback for 2027–2029. Net margin (12.92%) beats both public peers, debt-to-equity (1.17) is the lowest of the three, ROIC (11.14%) is second only to WM, and beta is 0.32. The stock is up 20.87% over twelve months and still 4.1% below its May 2025 monthly high.

Bear Case

Acquisitions are a regular part of the growth: revenue went from $11.3B to $16.59B over four years — a 10.1% compound rate — and $1.12B went on deals in the first nine months of 2026, about half of that period's adjusted free cash flow. Q3 revenue grew 8.2%, but the adjusted EBITDA margin fell to 31.8% from 32.8% and volume was still negative at −0.6%. For that you pay 34.2× trailing earnings, 30.8× FY2027 consensus and a 3.80 PEG, and take a 1.09% yield. Four in ten analysts rate it Hold, one rates it Sell, and the 4.1% price rise on restricted contracts limits the pricing the whole case rests on.

Top 3 Risks

01
Labour cost step-change. The 2025 Teamsters action, which the union said drew more than 2,000 workers across five states, cost $56M by Republic's count; the Local 104 strike authorisation at Buckeye, Arizona in late July 2026 shows the pressure is live. In a business whose whole appeal is "price exceeds cost inflation," a permanent reset in wage and pension cost is the one shock that breaks the sentence.
02
Investors paying less for the same earnings. A 0.32-beta, 1.09%-yield, 3.80-PEG stock at 34.2× earnings is being valued like a bond whose payments rise with inflation. The 22% drop from May 2025 to May 2026 happened with earnings rising — proof that the price investors will pay per dollar of profit, not the business, set the return. Q3's adjusted EBITDA margin was a full point below a year earlier; at this valuation a repeat would be hard to shrug off.
03
The acquisition treadmill. Spending $1.12B in nine months — about 49% of the $2.28B of adjusted free cash flow over the same period — buys 1.3 points of Q3's 8.2% revenue growth. It works only while the prices paid for small haulers stay below the return the assets earn. If competitors bid those prices up and the return on acquired assets drifts toward the cost of capital, the reported growth rate stays intact while the value creation quietly stops — and nothing in the disclosure would tell you.
Industry Commentary
Price above inflation is now a decade-long industry habit
EMERGING TAILWIND

The defining feature of North American solid waste is that the three public majors have collectively stopped chasing volume. Republic's Q3 2026 disclosure is the cleanest statement of it available: core price 5.3%, open-market core price 7.6%, volume −0.6%, after Q2's −1.6% and a deliberate shrinking of the residential book. The company is choosing to shrink the worst-priced part of its book. That behaviour only survives in an industry where the alternative disposal option does not exist — and where all three majors behave the same way. WM's 30.26% EBITDA margin and WCN's 19.31% operating margin show the peers earning comparable profits. The tailwind is that as long as this discipline holds, revenue and margin can expand without a single new customer. The weak point is that this discipline depends on everyone keeping to it: it takes only one large player deciding to win customers on price to unwind it.

Recycled commodities and RNG: the two lines investors most consistently misread
ONGOING HEADWIND

Recycled commodity prices averaged $142/ton in Q3 2026, up $16 from a year earlier ($136 in Q2). That swing moves revenue and average yield without telling you anything about operating quality — it is a bet on paper and cardboard demand embedded inside a utility. Renewable natural gas invites the mirror-image error in the other direction. Be precise about what exists: 77 landfill gas-to-energy projects operating with 30+ in development per the FY2025 10-K, and on RNG specifically two projects that entered service in Q2 2026 with two more expected by year-end, per the Q2 call. The entire sustainability portfolio — RNG plus two operating Polymer Centers plus a third under construction in Allentown, PA — was guided to ~$40M of incremental 2026 revenue and ~$20M of incremental EBITDA. That is 0.23% of guided revenue. It is money being spent today for a possible payoff later, and it should be valued as such rather than as a green-growth story.

Bull vs bear: it comes down to whether you are paying for the asset or for the growth
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Both sides of this argument agree on the facts. The landfill network is irreplaceable; the pricing power is real and documented quarter after quarter; volume is flat to slightly down; the acquisition spend is $1.12B in nine months against $2.28B of adjusted free cash flow. The bull says: pay 34.2× for a monopoly toll road with an inflation escalator, a 0.32 beta and 11% ROIC, and let the acquisitions add up quietly on top. The bear says: 34.2× and a 3.80 PEG for mid-single-digit growth is a bond with equity risk, and the shares lost 22% from May 2025 to May 2026 while earnings rose. Both are true. What settles it for any given investor is the discount rate they apply and whether they believe the labour cost base is stable — because if wages step up 4–5% a year against a 4.1% restricted-book price cap, the sentence "pricing in excess of cost inflation" stops being true, and everything on this page is downstream of that sentence.
