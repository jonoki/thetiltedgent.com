01 Company Overview

Republic Services is the second-largest solid waste company in North America: 377 collection operations, 255 transfer stations, 207 active landfills and 79 recycling centers across the United States and Canada as of the end of FY2025. FY2025 revenue was $16.59B, up 3.5% year over year; the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $16.89B, up 3.2%. At a $68.20B market capitalisation it ranks roughly #381 globally. The business is not complicated — a truck picks up a container, drives to a transfer station or straight to a hole in the ground that Republic owns, and tips it. What is complicated is why a business that simple earns a ROIC of 11.1% and trades at 31.5× earnings.

The answer, and the central debate of this page, is price versus volume. In Q2 2026 Republic put through 5.3% core price — the pure rate increase it pushed onto existing customers — while volume fell 1.6%. That is the whole industry in one line: a shrinking book of business generating expanding revenue and expanding margin, because the assets that receive the waste cannot be replicated. A modern landfill in a populated county is functionally un-permittable; the ones that exist are grandfathered options on an inelastic need. The question is not whether that pricing power is real — the 32.1% adjusted EBITDA margin says it is — but whether a 3–4% organic grower with a 1.2% dividend yield deserves 31.5× earnings and a 3.47 PEG when the price ceiling is set by what municipalities and franchise regulators will tolerate.

Three very different books wearing one label. Small-container (commercial dumpsters) is the crown jewel: $1,369M in Q2 2026, up 8.7% year over year, mostly open-market contracts where Republic sets the rate. Large-container (roll-offs for construction and demolition) is $839M, up 5.7%, and is the cyclical piece — its volume fell 2.2% on construction softness. Residential is $767M, up 2.0%, and is the worst of the three: municipal franchise contracts, often with CPI-linked escalators that cap price below true cost inflation. Republic is deliberately shrinking it — residential volume fell 4.3% on contract exits management describes as prioritising returns over tonnage. That is the single clearest evidence that this management team is running for price, not for share.

The pricing disclosure is where the analytical action is. Core price — the rate increase applied to the existing book, before any mix or volume effect — was 5.3% on total revenue and 6.4% on related revenue, split 7.8% in open market and 4.1% in restricted (franchise and municipal contracts where increases are formula-bound). Average yield — the revenue-weighted realised price change after mix — was 3.4% (4.0% related). The gap between 5.3% core price and 3.4% average yield is the tax the restricted book levies on the whole enterprise. As long as average yield sits above cost inflation, margin expands even with volume falling; management guides to 60–70 basis points of underlying margin expansion in FY2026.

This is the reason the whole thing works. As of December 31, 2025 Republic operated 207 active landfills spanning 41,158 estimated permitted acres with 5.0 billion in-place cubic yards of estimated total available disposal capacity, plus 255 transfer stations. Remaining airspace expressed in average years of life was not disclosed in the portion of the 10-K retrieved and is therefore not stated here — the cubic-yard figure above is the verified capacity disclosure. What matters analytically is that this capacity is a permitting artefact, not a capital-spending decision: siting a greenfield landfill near a major metro is, for practical purposes, no longer possible. Existing airspace is a depleting, non-reproducible option on a service demand that does not decline.

Internalisation — the share of collected waste that Republic disposes of in landfills it owns or operates — ran approximately 67% in fiscal 2025. That is the vertical-integration number: on two-thirds of tonnage, the disposal fee stays inside the house rather than being paid to a third party, which is why the collection business earns what it does. It also means reported landfill revenue understates the asset's economic contribution by roughly a factor of three, because intercompany volume is eliminated. Q2 2026 landfill revenue was flat at $517M against a hard comparison — municipal solid waste volume rose 1.1%, but the prior year included wildfire-driven special waste; excluding those volumes, landfill special waste grew 10.7%.

Read this line for what it is: a commodity pass-through, not a management scorecard. Recycled commodity prices averaged $136 per ton in Q2 2026, down from $149 a year earlier; management assumes roughly $140 per ton for the second half, implying a $135 full-year average. When old corrugated cardboard and mixed paper rally, this line inflates revenue and flatters yield; when they fall, it drags. Neither direction says anything about whether the underlying collection franchise got better or worse. Analysts who cheer a commodity-driven beat and then punish a commodity-driven miss are marking the same business to two different narratives.

The renewable natural gas (RNG) story deserves the same discipline, and here it is important to separate what is running from what is promised. Verified: Republic operated 77 landfill gas-to-energy projects at its landfills as of the FY2025 10-K, with more than 30 in development. On RNG specifically, two projects commenced operations during Q2 2026 and two more are expected to be operational by year-end. The broader sustainability portfolio — RNG plus the Polymer Centers (plastics recovery facilities; two operating, a third under construction in Allentown, Pennsylvania and slated for commissioning in early 2027) — is guided to contribute roughly $40M of incremental revenue and $20M of incremental EBITDA in 2026. On a $17.25B revenue base and $5.54B EBITDA base, that is 0.23% of revenue and 0.36% of EBITDA. It is a real, capital-intensive, credit-and-offtake-dependent business — it is not, on today's numbers, a thesis.

Sustainability capex reality check: the entire sustainability portfolio adds ~$20M of 2026 EBITDA against $5.525–5.550B guided. Electric collection fleet: 250+ vehicles at Q2 2026, targeting 300+ by year-end, out of a fleet serving 377 collection operations.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
UN-PERMITTABLE LANDFILL AIRSPACE 67% INTERNALISATION ROUTE DENSITY FRANCHISE & MUNICIPAL CONTRACTS INELASTIC DEMAND SERIAL TUCK-IN ACQUISITION PFAS DISPOSAL PERMITS

Solid waste is the closest thing in the public equity market to a local toll road, and the toll booth is the landfill. Anyone can buy a truck; nobody can buy a new hole. The permitting process for a modern subtitle-D landfill near a population centre involves state environmental review, county zoning, groundwater monitoring commitments and thirty years of post-closure care liability — and it fails at the first public hearing. So the 207 active sites Republic operates, holding 5.0 billion in-place cubic yards of permitted capacity, are a fixed, depleting, non-reproducible resource sitting in front of a demand curve that does not bend. That is why the industry compounds price above inflation year after year, and why core price of 5.3% against volume of −1.6% is a feature rather than a warning.

The second layer is internalisation. At ~67%, two-thirds of every tonne collected is tipped into Republic's own airspace, so the disposal margin that an independent hauler pays away is captured internally. Layer on route density — the marginal stop on an existing route costs almost nothing, so the incumbent in a zip code can always underbid a challenger and still earn more — and you have a business where market share is won street by street and, once won, is very hard to take back. The financial evidence: a 32.1% adjusted EBITDA margin, an ROE of 18.15%, and a beta of 0.40 — this equity moves less than half as much as the market, which is exactly what an inflation-linked toll road should do.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The restricted book caps the pricing engine. Core price was 7.8% in open market but only 4.1% in restricted — franchise and municipal contracts with formula escalators. That mix is why 5.3% core price becomes only 3.4% average yield. If cost inflation reaccelerates, the restricted book cannot reprice fast enough, and the margin-expansion story stalls in exactly the half of the business that is politically hardest to fix. Municipalities under budget pressure are also the counterparty most likely to litigate, re-tender, or municipalise.
▸
Organised labour is escalating. The July 2025 Teamsters strike was the largest sanitation action in decades: it began July 1 in Boston (Local 25) and spread to Ottawa IL, Manteca CA, Thurston County WA and Cumming GA, with over 2,000 workers at peak and 450+ still picketing in late August. Republic estimated a $25–50M hit to 2025 and spent about $100,000 a day on security for replacement workers. Most locals settled by mid-August; Boston did not. The pressure has not gone away — on July 30, 2026 Teamsters Local 104 authorised a strike at the Southwest Regional Landfill in Buckeye, Arizona, where roughly 20 workers rejected a 2%-a-year offer, demanded entry to the Western States Pension Plan, and filed an unfair labour practice charge. The union has funded a strike fund paying up to $1,000 a week. A labour cost step-change is the single most credible route to margin compression in a business with no other obvious cost shock.
▸
Volume is falling and the comparison base is deteriorating. Large-container volume −2.2% on construction softness, residential −4.3% on deliberate exits. Deliberate exits are good discipline; construction softness is a cycle you do not control. Republic is currently pricing 5.3% into a shrinking book — mathematically that works until the shrinkage exceeds the price, and there is no disclosure that tells you where that crossover sits.
▸
Leverage and the balance sheet. $14.30B of total debt against $107M of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.19 and a current ratio of 0.64. Long-term debt rose from $12.99B at the end of 2025 to $13.52B at June 30, 2026 while the company spent $860M on acquisitions and $651M on buybacks. The cash flows support this comfortably today; it is a rate-sensitive structure funding an acquisition programme, and rate-sensitive structures are cheap right up to the point they are not.

Notable One-Off Items

The 2025 Teamsters strike. Republic estimated a $25–50M impact to 2025 from the July 2025 work stoppage, plus roughly $100,000 a day in security costs during the action. On a ~$5.3B EBITDA base that is under 1% — it is a real number but not a thesis-changing one. It does depress the FY2025 base, which mechanically flatters the FY2026 comparison.

Wildfire special waste in the 2025 base. Prior-year landfill volumes included wildfire-driven special waste. Q2 2026 landfill revenue looked flat at $517M as a result — but excluding those volumes, special waste grew 10.7%. Management also flagged a 40 basis point landfill event comparison headwind in Q3 2026, which is why it guides Q3 margins roughly flat year over year with the full-year expansion concentrated in Q4. Anyone reading the Q3 print in isolation will get the wrong answer.

Recycled commodity prices. $136/ton in Q2 2026 versus $149 a year earlier — a $13/ton headwind that flows straight through revenue and yield. This is a price, not a performance. Full-year assumption: ~$135/ton.

Acquisition accounting and integration cost. $860M deployed on acquisitions in the first half of 2026, with the full-year figure raised to more than $1.2B. Management explicitly cited acquisition integration costs as one reason Q3 2026 margins should be flat. Serial tuck-in acquirers carry a permanent, recurring "one-off" integration line — it is a cost of the growth algorithm, not an aberration.

Organic versus acquired growth — the number that matters. Q2 2026 revenue grew 4.6%, built from +3.4% average yield and −1.6% volume — that is roughly +1.8% organic. The remaining ~2.8 points is acquisition. Extend it: revenue rose from $11.3B in FY2021 to $16.59B in FY2025, a 10.1% compound rate against an organic engine running under 2%. The overwhelming majority of Republic's five-year revenue growth was bought, not grown. At >$1.2B of acquisition spend a year against $2.65B of free cash flow, roughly 45% of the cash the business produces is being spent to buy the next couple of points of growth. Whether that is compounding or a treadmill is the honest open question on this stock, and no disclosure on the page settles it.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 conventionally suggest the stock has run hard and may be overbought; below 30 suggests it has been sold hard and may be oversold. On a monthly series each reading covers six months, so this is a slow, strategic gauge — not a trading trigger.

Over the five years from the October 2021 close of $134.60 to the September 2026 close of $222.71, RSG returned +65.5% on price alone, plus a dividend that grew from a lower base to today's $2.68 annualised. That is a respectable compounding record — roughly 10.6% a year before dividends — earned with a beta of 0.40 and almost no drama through the 2022 bear market, where the stock's window low of $120.28 in February 2022 represented a drawdown of barely 14%. That is the defensive proposition working exactly as advertised.

The problem is where the return was concentrated. Essentially all of it landed between late 2023 and May 2025, when the stock peaked at $257.29. Since that peak RSG has fallen 13.4%, and over the trailing 52 weeks it is down 4.21% — while EPS rose from $6.85 to $7.06 and adjusted EBITDA margin expanded. The last sixteen months have been a multiple compression, not an earnings problem. That is what happens to a bond proxy: the 2024–25 melt-up priced RSG as a rate-cut beneficiary and a defensive haven simultaneously, and the reversal since has been the market taking back the rate-driven half of the re-rating. The May 2026 low of $200.44 marked the bottom of that adjustment; the stock has recovered 11.1% from there and now sits 68% of the way up its 52-week range.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a network of 207 landfills holding 5.0 billion cubic yards of permitted airspace that cannot be replicated at any price, feeding 377 collection operations that internalise 67% of what they pick up. That structure produced 5.3% core price against −1.6% volume and a 32.1% adjusted EBITDA margin in Q2 2026, with management raising full-year guidance and guiding 60–70bp of margin expansion. Net margin (12.94%) beats both public peers, leverage (1.19× D/E) is the lowest of the three, ROIC (11.06%) is second only to WM, and beta is 0.40. The stock is down 4.21% over twelve months while earnings rose — you are entering after a multiple compression, not after a run.

Bear Case

Strip out acquisitions and this is a business growing under 2% a year. Revenue went from $11.3B to $16.59B over four years — a 10.1% compound rate — but Q2's 4.6% growth decomposes to +3.4% yield and −1.6% volume, roughly 1.8% organic, with the rest bought for >$1.2B of acquisition spend in 2026, nearly half of free cash flow. For that you pay 31.5× trailing, 29.2× forward, a 3.47 PEG and take a 1.20% yield — the highest forward multiple of the three majors on the slowest growth. Half the analyst panel is on Hold, four of the last six actions raised targets without upgrading, and 4.1% restricted-book pricing caps the engine that the whole thesis rests on.

Top 3 Risks

01
Labour cost step-change. The 2025 Teamsters action put 2,000+ workers on picket lines across five states and cost $25–50M; the Local 104 strike authorisation at Buckeye, Arizona on July 30, 2026 shows the pressure is live and the union's tactics have escalated (an enhanced strike fund paying up to $1,000/week, unfair labour practice filings). In a business whose entire equity story is "price exceeds cost inflation," a permanent reset in wage and pension cost is the one shock that breaks the sentence.
02
Multiple compression on rates or on a growth disappointment. A 0.40-beta, 1.20%-yield, 3.47-PEG stock at 31.5× is being valued as a bond substitute with an inflation escalator. The 22% drawdown from May 2025 to May 2026 happened with earnings rising — proof that the multiple, not the business, sets the return here. Guidance for a roughly flat Q3 2026 margin gives the market an easy excuse to test that again.
03
The acquisition treadmill. Spending >$1.2B a year — roughly 45% of free cash flow — to buy 2–3 points of growth works only while tuck-in multiples stay below the return the assets earn. Competition for the same private haulers from WM and a WCN paying 39.8× its own earnings bids those multiples up. If acquired ROIC drifts toward the cost of capital, the reported growth rate stays intact and the value creation quietly stops — and nothing in the disclosure would tell you.
Industry Commentary
Price above inflation is now a decade-long industry habit
EMERGING TAILWIND

The defining feature of North American solid waste is that the three public majors have collectively stopped chasing volume. Republic's Q2 2026 disclosure is the cleanest statement of it available: core price 5.3%, open-market core price 7.8%, volume −1.6%, residential volume −4.3% on deliberate contract exits. The company is choosing to shrink the worst-priced part of its book. That behaviour only survives in an industry where the alternative disposal option does not exist — and where all three majors behave the same way. WM's 30.29% EBITDA margin and WCN's 18.94% operating margin confirm the pattern is industry-wide, not company-specific. The tailwind is that as long as this discipline holds, revenue and margin expand without a single new customer. The fragility is that it is a coordinated equilibrium: it takes one large player deciding to buy share to unwind it.

Recycled commodities and RNG: the two lines investors most consistently misread
ONGOING HEADWIND

Recycled commodity prices averaged $136/ton in Q2 2026 against $149 a year earlier, with a ~$135 full-year assumption. That $13/ton swing moves revenue and average yield without telling you anything about operating quality — it is a bet on Chinese and domestic paper demand embedded inside a utility. Renewable natural gas invites the mirror-image error in the other direction. Be precise about what exists: 77 landfill gas-to-energy projects operating with 30+ in development per the FY2025 10-K, and on RNG specifically two projects that entered service in Q2 2026 with two more expected by year-end. The entire sustainability portfolio — RNG plus two operating Polymer Centers plus a third under construction in Allentown, PA — is guided to ~$40M of incremental 2026 revenue and ~$20M of incremental EBITDA. That is 0.23% of guided revenue. It is capital being spent today for optionality later, and it should be valued as such rather than as a green-growth narrative.

Bull vs bear: it comes down to whether you are paying for the asset or for the growth
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Both sides of this argument agree on the facts. The landfill network is irreplaceable; the pricing power is real and documented quarter after quarter; the organic growth rate is under 2%; the acquisition spend is over $1.2B a year against $2.65B of free cash flow. The bull says: pay 31.5× for a monopoly toll road with an inflation escalator, a 0.40 beta and 11% ROIC, and let the M&A compound quietly on top. The bear says: 31.5× and a 3.47 PEG for 1.8% organic growth is a bond with equity risk, and you have just watched it lose 22% peak-to-trough while earnings rose. Both are true. What settles it for any given investor is the discount rate they apply and whether they believe the labour cost base is stable — because if wages step up 4–5% a year against a 4.1% restricted-book price cap, the sentence "pricing in excess of cost inflation" stops being true, and everything on this page is downstream of that sentence.
