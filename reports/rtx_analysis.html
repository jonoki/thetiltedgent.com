01 Company Overview

RTX Corporation is one of the world's largest aerospace and defense companies by revenue, formed by the April 2020 merger of Raytheon Company and United Technologies' aerospace businesses and renamed from Raytheon Technologies to RTX in July 2023. It sells through three segments — Collins Aerospace (aircraft systems and interiors), Pratt & Whitney (commercial and military engines), and Raytheon (missiles, air-and-missile defense, radars and effectors) — giving it a roughly balanced commercial-aerospace / defense mix that few peers can match.

FY2025 revenue was $88.60B, up 9.7% YoY from $80.74B, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through Q3 2026 has reached $95.99B. Q3 2026 (reported before the open on October 20, 2026) delivered sales of $24.97B, +11% YoY (+12% organic), with adjusted EPS of $2.26 versus a $1.78 Nasdaq consensus, a 27% beat; RTX has topped that consensus in each of the last four quarters. Management raised its full-year outlook for the third quarter running, to $97.0–97.5B of adjusted sales and $7.65–7.75 of adjusted EPS. Backlog, the value of signed orders not yet delivered, reached $340B at September 30, lifted by multi-year US missile contracts for Tomahawk and AMRAAM; the SM-6 and SM-3 Block IB contracts were signed in October, after the quarter closed. At $191.60 the company is worth about $258.6B (1.349B shares outstanding at September 30, 2026 × the October 21 close).

Collins is one of the broadest aerospace systems franchises in the industry: avionics and flight controls, power and electrical systems, environmental control systems, landing gear and wheels/brakes, cabin interiors and seating, and connected-aviation software. Content sits on essentially every Western commercial airframe plus military platforms including the F-35, CH-47 Chinook and the Army's rotorcraft fleet. P&W is RTX's largest segment, up 17.3% in FY2025; in Q3 2026 its sales rose 9.6% to $9.23B and adjusted operating margin improved to 10.5% from 8.9%, including about $160 million of tariff-cost recovery. It builds the GTF (geared turbofan) family powering the A320neo, A220 and Embraer E2, the V2500 through the IAE consortium, business-jet engines, and — critically — the F135 as the sole engine supplier for all three F-35 variants, a monopoly position extended by the multi-billion-dollar Engine Core Upgrade programme. The economics are classic razor-and-blade: new engines are sold at or below cost, with value recovered over decades of shop visits and spare parts. That model was badly disrupted in July 2023 when RTX disclosed a powder-metal contamination defect in GTF high-pressure turbine and compressor discs, forcing accelerated fleet-wide inspections, a peak of roughly 350 grounded aircraft (AOG) and a $5.4B gross / ~$3.0B RTX-share charge. By 2026 the remediation is past its crisis phase: on the Q3 call Calio said output of the GTF shops that do MRO (maintenance, repair and overhaul) on the A320neo engine was up more than 35% from a year earlier, and that he expects the count of grounded aircraft to fall in the fourth quarter. Leadership changes at the turn of the year: Jill Albertelli, who ran Pratt's military-engines business, becomes president of Pratt & Whitney on January 1, 2027, succeeding the retiring Shane Eddy. The installed GTF base is now large enough that aftermarket shop visits are becoming a genuine profit engine rather than a warranty liability.

The Raytheon segment — formed in 2023 by merging the old Raytheon Missiles & Defense and Raytheon Intelligence & Space businesses — is the Western world's reference supplier of integrated air and missile defense. The franchise centres on the Patriot system and its GEM-T interceptor (now being produced in Europe via the COMLOG joint venture in Germany for NATO customers), the SM-3 and SM-6 naval interceptors, AMRAAM and StormBreaker air-to-air and air-to-ground effectors, the low-cost Coyote counter-UAS interceptor family, the LTAMDS radar replacing legacy Patriot sensors, and SPY-6 shipboard radars for the US Navy. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOURCES OF MOAT
Installed-base aftermarket Sole-source F135 $340B backlog Certification barriers Classified & ITAR access Patriot / SM-family lock-in Vertical rocket-motor capacity

RTX's moat is fundamentally an installed-base moat with a regulatory perimeter around it. Once a GTF or F135 engine is on wing, or a Collins wheel-and-brake or avionics box is certified onto an airframe, the part is locked in for the 25–35 year life of the aircraft and the manufacturer captures a recurring, high-margin spares and overhaul stream that no competitor can bid away without an airframer-level requalification. The same dynamic applies in defense with more force: Patriot, SM-3/SM-6 and AMRAAM are embedded in the doctrine, training pipelines and fire-control networks of dozens of allied militaries, so switching costs are measured in decades and treaty relationships rather than in price. Layer on ITAR and classified-facility requirements that exclude non-US entrants, a $340B backlog that pre-sells several years of output (about 3.5 times trailing sales), and multi-year vertical investment in solid rocket motors, energetics and forged/cast structural parts that are the industry's true bottleneck — and the result is a business where the binding constraint is factory throughput rather than customer demand. That is a rare and valuable place to sit. The offsetting truth is that this moat did not prevent the GTF powder-metal failure, which demonstrated that in aerospace the deepest moat can still be breached from the inside by a metallurgical process problem.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Residual GTF fleet-management liability. The powder-metal remediation is progressing, but RTX still carries multi-year customer-compensation obligations and elevated warranty accruals tied to grounded A320neo and A220 fleets. Any slippage in disc production, MRO turn times or inspection findings re-opens a cash-flow and reputational wound that already cost roughly $3.0B of RTX-share charges and years of narrowbody share momentum to CFM.

2. Fixed-price and international contract risk. A meaningful slice of the Raytheon segment sits on fixed-price development contracts, several of them international, where inflation, redesign and schedule slip land entirely on RTX. The company has taken repeated contract charges on these programmes since 2022, and the current surge in European orders is being written in an environment of rising input and labour costs.

3. Customer and political concentration. The US government is roughly 40% of RTX sales, with the Department of Defense the single dominant customer. That exposes the company to continuing-resolution funding gaps, procurement reprioritization toward attritable/autonomous systems and away from exquisite platforms, and to periodic export-licence and foreign-policy interruptions in the Middle East and Asia that can strand a booked order.

4. Supply chain, tariffs and structural castings. Titanium and nickel forgings, castings, and specialty energetics remain the throttle on both the engine and the missile ramp. Tariff regimes introduced in 2025 added a gross cost headwind measured in the hundreds of millions of dollars annually before mitigation, and RTX's ability to pass this through varies sharply between commercial aftermarket (good pricing power) and firm fixed-price defense (essentially none).

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

RTX's reported results carry more non-recurring noise than most industrials, and the gap is material: Q3 2026 GAAP EPS of $1.89 versus adjusted EPS of $2.26 is a $0.37 (16%) gap; for full-year 2025 RTX itemized $1.15 a share of acquisition-accounting adjustments (mainly amortization of intangibles from the 2020 Raytheon/UTC merger) and $0.14 of restructuring. Investors should be explicit about which basis they are valuing: the 31× trailing P/E is on GAAP earnings; on adjusted TTM earnings of about $7.48 the multiple is about 26×.

Tariff-cost recovery. Q3 2026 adjusted EPS of $2.26 included about $0.25 a share of what RTX calls IEEPA cost recovery: costs recovered on tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. On the call, CFO Neil Mitchill put the rest of the 33% rise down to about $0.43 of segment profit growth, partly offset by about $0.12 of other items, mainly a higher tax rate and lower pension income. He said about another $50 million of the recovery comes in the fourth quarter, after which it is largely behind the company.

GTF powder metal. The Q3 2023 charge of $5.4B gross / ~$3.0B RTX share remains the single largest distortion in the five-year record — it is why FY2023 net margin collapsed and why the FY2024–FY2026 margin recovery looks so steep. Residual customer-compensation payments continue to flow out of operating cash through 2026 and suppress reported FCF relative to underlying earnings power.

Tariffs. The 2025 tariff regime imposed a gross annual cost headwind on RTX in the mid-hundreds of millions of dollars; management has offset a substantial share through pricing, duty drawback and supply-chain reconfiguration, but the residual is embedded in current margins and the mitigation is not fully repeatable.

Divestitures. The 2024 sale of Collins' actuation and flight-control business to Safran (~$1.8B) and of the cybersecurity/intelligence services business (Nightwing) produced one-time gains and lowered reported growth in the periods after each sale; organic growth, which excludes divested businesses, is the cleaner comparison.

Contract charges. Periodic EAC adjustments on fixed-price Raytheon development programmes have recurred often enough since 2022 that they should be treated as a semi-permanent cost of doing business rather than as true one-offs.

Five-year trajectory. RTX closed at $88.86 at the end of October 2021 and at $191.60 on October 21, 2026 — a price gain of +115.6% (dividends not included), about 16.7% a year compounded. That headline number conceals two completely different regimes. From late 2021 through 2023 the stock went essentially nowhere and then broke, closing its months between $72 and $103 as the post-COVID commercial recovery was offset by supply-chain inflation and then shattered by the GTF powder-metal disclosure. From the September 2023 month-end low of $71.97 the stock has compounded at about 38% a year to the October 21, 2026 close, driven first by the credibility of the fleet-management plan and then, from 2025, by the European rearmament re-rating of defense stocks generally.

Relative performance. On price alone and over the same dates (October 29, 2021 close to October 21, 2026 close), RTX's +115.6% beats the S&P 500 ETF SPY's +68.3% and the Nasdaq-100 ETF QQQ's +87.8%, with all of the lead built after September 2023. The last year tells a different story: from October 21, 2025, RTX is up +10.7%, behind SPY (+15.2%) and QQQ (+18.6%), and year to date in 2026 it is up 4.5% from the $183.40 year-end close. At the end of July the stock had been up 17.4% for 2026 ($215.22); the August–October slide took back most of that, as the shares fell from a $225.49 close on August 18 to $180.26 on October 7 even as the missile contracts came in. RTX's appeal is that it owns both the rearmament theme and the commercial aftermarket recovery — the reason for the multiple premium in Section 04.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

RTX owns both structural growth stories in aerospace at once: a $340B backlog at September 30, anchored by multi-year US contracts for Tomahawk and AMRAAM, with SM-6 and SM-3 contracts signed in October on top, and a commercial aftermarket franchise with near-universal systems content that compounds with global flight hours. With the GTF powder-metal remediation moving from cash cost to cash generation, all three segments growing sales and margins in Q3 and FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance raised three times this year, from $6.60–6.80 in January to $7.65–7.75, earnings are growing into the multiple — the stock now trades at about 25× this year's guided earnings, down from about 31× at the last edition ($222.76 on the then $7.10–7.25 guide) — while the 0.31 beta, 0.55 debt-to-equity and $9.5B of trailing free cash flow make it a defensive way to own that growth.

▼ Bear Case

At 31× trailing GAAP earnings and about 25× guided 2026 earnings, RTX still trades at a premium to the S&P 500 for a business earning about 10% ROIC on 8.8% net margins. The Q3 beat leaned partly on about $0.25 a share of one-off tariff-cost recovery, and the stock slid 20% from August to October even as its order book swelled — a reminder that good news alone does not lift a stock already priced for it. A GTF inspection setback, a fixed-price charge on the new multi-year missile contracts, a tariff escalation or a US budget continuing resolution would all land on that premium — and the thin current ratio of 1.05 leaves little cushion if working capital turns against the ramp.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
Valuation risk. A 31× trailing multiple on a hardware manufacturer with single-digit net margins requires the rearmament and aftermarket cycles to run for years without interruption. The August–October slide shows how quickly that premium can shrink even on good news; analysts' average target sits 18.9% above the price, but with no sell ratings at all, the consensus leaves little room for disappointment.
02
GTF execution and residual liability. The powder-metal programme is on plan but not finished. Slippage in full-length disc output, worse-than-expected inspection findings, or renewed customer compensation would directly hit free cash flow and would strike at the exact narrative — competent execution — that drove the 2024–26 re-rating.
03
Budget, tariff and fixed-price contract shocks. Roughly 40% of sales come from the US government, exposing RTX to continuing resolutions and procurement reprioritization, while fixed-price international development work and tariff-inflated input costs have repeatedly produced charges that cannot be passed through. Political timelines, not order books, are the binding uncertainty here.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters Most Right Now
Global Rearmament: The Structural Demand Shock
EMERGING TAILWIND
The 2025 NATO framework pointing European members toward 5% of GDP on defense-related spending is a large peacetime shift in Western procurement, and its composition favours RTX disproportionately. The priority categories — integrated air and missile defense, deep-strike and long-range munitions, counter-UAS — map almost exactly onto Patriot/GEM-T, SM-3 and SM-6, AMRAAM, StormBreaker and Coyote. The global missiles, munitions and air-defense TAM of roughly $140B is compounding at 9–12% annually, versus low-single-digit growth for the platform-heavy parts of defense where Lockheed and Northrop are more exposed. Evidence of conversion is already visible: RTX's total backlog reached $340B at September 30, 2026, $164B of it defense; Raytheon's Q3 sales rose 14%; and between August and October the US signed multi-year contracts for Tomahawk ($22.9B over seven years), AMRAAM (up to $20.7B), SM-6 (up to $24.4B) and SM-3 Block IB ($6.3B). European localization — GEM-T production through the COMLOG joint venture in Germany — is the mechanism that keeps RTX inside orders that might otherwise be re-shored to MBDA, Kongsberg or Rheinmetall.
Supply Chain, Castings and Tariffs: The Throughput Ceiling
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The defining feature of this cycle is that aerospace and defense is demand-rich and supply-poor. Titanium and nickel forgings, single-crystal castings, solid rocket motors and energetics are all capacity-limited, and the qualification cycle for a new supplier runs years. That is why the Raytheon segment grew only 5.0% in FY2025 despite a record order book — output, not demand, set the number. Layered on top, the 2025 tariff regime added a gross cost headwind in the mid-hundreds of millions of dollars annually. RTX has mitigated much of it through pricing, duty drawback and re-sourcing, but the asymmetry matters: commercial aftermarket has real pricing power, firm fixed-price defense contracts have none. Raytheon's sales rose 14% in Q3 2026, against 5.0% for FY2025, but the supply constraint still caps how fast the backlog can be converted.
The Commercial Aftermarket Cycle — Where Bull and Bear Actually Meet
ONGOING SWING FACTOR
A large share of RTX's profit traces to aftermarket spares and shop visits across Collins and Pratt & Whitney, and that stream is being amplified by an ageing global fleet: airframers have persistently under-delivered new aircraft, so operators are flying older equipment harder, and every extra flight hour on an existing wing is high-margin revenue for RTX. The bull framing is that this is a multi-year annuity with pricing power, that GTF shop visits convert a warranty liability into the industry's best installed-base economics, and that at about 25× this year's guided earnings you are underpaying for a business whose backlog covers about three and a half years of sales. The bear framing is that aftermarket strength is partly a symptom of the same supply dysfunction that eventually normalizes — when Boeing and Airbus rates finally recover, older aircraft retire and the aftermarket tailwind reverses precisely when the defense budget cycle may also be peaking. Which of those is right is essentially the whole RTX debate at $191.60; nothing else in the model moves the answer as much.
