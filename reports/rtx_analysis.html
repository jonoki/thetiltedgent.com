01 Company Overview

RTX Corporation is the world's largest pure-play aerospace and defense company by revenue, formed by the April 2020 merger of Raytheon Company and United Technologies' aerospace businesses and renamed from Raytheon Technologies to RTX in July 2023. It sells through three segments — Collins Aerospace (aircraft systems and interiors), Pratt & Whitney (commercial and military engines), and Raytheon (missiles, air-and-missile defense, radars and effectors) — giving it a roughly balanced commercial-aerospace / defense mix that few peers can match.

FY2025 revenue was $88.60B, up 9.7% YoY from $80.74B, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through Q2 2026 has reached $93.50B (+11.8%). At a ~$300B market capitalization RTX is the largest aerospace & defense company in the world by market value — bigger than Lockheed Martin ($138B) and Northrop Grumman ($82B) combined — and ranks roughly in the top 50 US-listed companies. Q2 2026 (reported July 23, 2026) delivered sales of $24.71B, +14.5% YoY, with adjusted EPS of $1.89 versus a $1.66 consensus — the largest beat in the current cycle, and the stock rose 7.3% that session.

P&W is now RTX's largest segment and its fastest-growing, up 17.3% in FY2025 and reaching $34.98B on a trailing-twelve-month basis through Q2 2026. It builds the GTF (geared turbofan) family powering the A320neo, A220 and Embraer E2, the V2500 through the IAE consortium, business-jet engines, and — critically — the F135 as the sole engine supplier for all three F-35 variants, a monopoly position extended by the multi-billion-dollar Engine Core Upgrade programme. The economics are classic razor-and-blade: new engines are sold at or below cost, with value recovered over decades of shop visits and spare parts. That model was badly disrupted in July 2023 when RTX disclosed a powder-metal contamination defect in GTF high-pressure turbine and compressor discs, forcing accelerated fleet-wide inspections, a peak of roughly 350 grounded aircraft (AOG) and a $5.4B gross / ~$3.0B RTX-share charge. By 2026 the remediation is in its harvest phase rather than its crisis phase: MRO (maintenance, repair and overhaul) turnaround times have improved materially from the 2024 peak, AOG counts are declining, additional full-length disc capacity has come online, and customer-compensation cash outflows are being absorbed within guidance. The installed GTF base is now large enough that aftermarket shop visits are becoming a genuine profit engine rather than a warranty liability.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOURCES OF MOAT
Installed-base aftermarket Sole-source F135 $289B backlog Certification barriers Classified & ITAR access Patriot / SM-family lock-in Vertical rocket-motor capacity

RTX's moat is fundamentally an installed-base moat with a regulatory perimeter around it. Once a GTF or F135 engine is on wing, or a Collins wheel-and-brake or avionics box is certified onto an airframe, the part is locked in for the 25–35 year life of the aircraft and the manufacturer captures a recurring, high-margin spares and overhaul stream that no competitor can bid away without an airframer-level requalification. The same dynamic applies in defense with more force: Patriot, SM-3/SM-6 and AMRAAM are embedded in the doctrine, training pipelines and fire-control networks of dozens of allied militaries, so switching costs are measured in decades and treaty relationships rather than in price. Layer on ITAR and classified-facility requirements that exclude non-US entrants, a $289B backlog that pre-sells several years of output, and multi-year vertical investment in solid rocket motors, energetics and forged/cast structural parts that are the industry's true bottleneck — and the result is a business where the binding constraint is factory throughput rather than customer demand. That is a rare and valuable place to sit. The offsetting truth is that this moat did not prevent the GTF powder-metal failure, which demonstrated that in aerospace the deepest moat can still be breached from the inside by a metallurgical process problem.

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Residual GTF fleet-management liability. The powder-metal remediation is progressing, but RTX still carries multi-year customer-compensation obligations and elevated warranty accruals tied to grounded A320neo and A220 fleets. Any slippage in disc production, MRO turn times or inspection findings re-opens a cash-flow and reputational wound that already cost roughly $3.0B of RTX-share charges and years of narrowbody share momentum to CFM.

2. Fixed-price and international contract risk. A meaningful slice of the Raytheon segment sits on fixed-price development contracts, several of them international, where inflation, redesign and schedule slip land entirely on RTX. The company has taken repeated contract charges on these programmes since 2022, and the current surge in European orders is being written in an environment of rising input and labour costs.

3. Customer and political concentration. The US government is roughly 40% of RTX sales, with the Department of Defense the single dominant customer. That exposes the company to continuing-resolution funding gaps, procurement reprioritization toward attritable/autonomous systems and away from exquisite platforms, and to periodic export-licence and foreign-policy interruptions in the Middle East and Asia that can strand a booked order.

4. Supply chain, tariffs and structural castings. Titanium and nickel forgings, castings, and specialty energetics remain the throttle on both the engine and the missile ramp. Tariff regimes introduced in 2025 added a gross cost headwind measured in the hundreds of millions of dollars annually before mitigation, and RTX's ability to pass this through varies sharply between commercial aftermarket (good pricing power) and firm fixed-price defense (essentially none).

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

RTX's reported results carry more non-recurring noise than most industrials, and the gap is material: Q2 2026 GAAP EPS of $1.57 versus adjusted EPS of $1.89 is a $0.32 (20%) wedge, driven principally by amortization of acquisition-related intangibles from the 2020 Raytheon/UTC merger and by restructuring. Investors should be explicit about which basis they are valuing: the headline 39x trailing P/E is GAAP; on adjusted TTM earnings of roughly $7.00 the multiple is closer to 32x.

GTF powder metal. The Q3 2023 charge of $5.4B gross / ~$3.0B RTX share remains the single largest distortion in the five-year record — it is why FY2023 net margin collapsed to 4.9% and why the FY2024–FY2026 margin recovery looks so steep. Residual customer-compensation payments continue to flow out of operating cash through 2026 and suppress reported FCF relative to underlying earnings power.

Tariffs. The 2025 tariff regime imposed a gross annual cost headwind on RTX in the mid-hundreds of millions of dollars; management has offset a substantial share through pricing, duty drawback and supply-chain reconfiguration, but the residual is embedded in current margins and the mitigation is not fully repeatable.

Divestitures. The 2024 sale of Collins' actuation and flight-control business to Safran (~$1.8B) and of the cybersecurity/intelligence services business (Nightwing) produced one-time gains and reduced the FY2025 revenue base by roughly a point of growth — organic growth is correspondingly better than reported.

Contract charges. Periodic EAC adjustments on fixed-price Raytheon development programmes have recurred often enough since 2022 that they should be treated as a semi-permanent cost of doing business rather than as true one-offs.

Five-year trajectory. RTX closed at roughly $85.83 at the end of September 2021 and at $222.76 on August 12, 2026 — a price gain of about +160%, or roughly +190% total return including reinvested dividends, equivalent to about a 23% compound annual return. That headline number conceals two completely different regimes. From the September 2023 low of about $71.90 the stock has compounded at close to 45% annually, driven first by the credibility of the fleet-management plan and then, from 2025, by the European rearmament re-rating of the entire defense complex.

Relative performance. Over the five years RTX's ~190% total return comfortably exceeds the S&P 500's roughly 95–100% total return over the same span, with the entire outperformance generated after September 2023. 08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

RTX is the only large-cap that owns both structural growth stories in aerospace at once: a $289B backlog levered to NATO rearmament and munitions replenishment, and a commercial aftermarket franchise on 20,000-plus engines and near-universal systems content that compounds with global flight hours. With the GTF powder-metal remediation moving from cash cost to cash generation, revenue accelerating to +14.5% and FY2026 EPS guided to $7.10–7.25, earnings can grow into the multiple while the 0.29 beta, 0.57 debt-to-equity and $11.4B of free cash flow make it a defensive way to own that growth.

▼ Bear Case

At 39x trailing and 30x forward earnings with a 2.78 PEG, RTX trades at a premium to both its own history and its peer group for a business earning 9.4% ROIC on 8.3% net margins. Consensus upside is just 4.3% and the shares sit 17% above their 200-day average with monthly RSI overbought. Any of a GTF inspection setback, a fixed-price contract charge, a tariff escalation, or simply a US budget continuing resolution would hit a stock priced for flawless execution — and the low current ratio of 1.01 leaves little cushion if working capital turns against the ramp.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
Valuation and positioning risk. A 39x trailing multiple on a hardware manufacturer with single-digit net margins requires the rearmament and aftermarket cycles to run for years without interruption. With only 4.3% average implied upside and no sell ratings, the marginal buyer is already in — the asymmetry has shifted against new capital at this level.
02
GTF execution and residual liability. The powder-metal programme is on plan but not finished. Slippage in full-length disc output, worse-than-expected inspection findings, or renewed customer compensation would directly hit free cash flow and would strike at the exact narrative — competent execution — that drove the 2024–26 re-rating.
03
Budget, tariff and fixed-price contract shocks. Roughly 40% of sales come from the US government, exposing RTX to continuing resolutions and procurement reprioritization, while fixed-price international development work and tariff-inflated input costs have repeatedly produced charges that cannot be passed through. Political timelines, not order books, are the binding uncertainty here.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters Most Right Now
Global Rearmament: The Structural Demand Shock
EMERGING TAILWIND
The 2025 NATO framework pointing European members toward 5% of GDP on defense-related spending is the largest peacetime shift in Western procurement since the early 1980s, and its composition favours RTX disproportionately. The priority categories — integrated air and missile defense, deep-strike and long-range munitions, counter-UAS — map almost exactly onto Patriot/GEM-T, SM-3 and SM-6, AMRAAM, StormBreaker and Coyote. The global missiles, munitions and air-defense TAM of roughly $140B is compounding at 9–12% annually, versus low-single-digit growth for the platform-heavy parts of defense where Lockheed and Northrop are more exposed. Evidence of conversion is already visible: RTX's total backlog has climbed to roughly $289B, the Raytheon segment's trailing sales have accelerated to $29.92B, and August 2026 alone brought a $745M SM-3 Block IIA award. European localization — GEM-T production through the COMLOG joint venture in Germany — is the mechanism that keeps RTX inside orders that might otherwise be re-shored to MBDA, Kongsberg or Rheinmetall.
Supply Chain, Castings and Tariffs: The Throughput Ceiling
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The defining feature of this cycle is that aerospace and defense is demand-rich and supply-poor. Titanium and nickel forgings, single-crystal castings, solid rocket motors and energetics are all capacity-limited, and the qualification cycle for a new supplier runs years. That is why the Raytheon segment grew only 5.0% in FY2025 despite a record order book — output, not demand, set the number. Layered on top, the 2025 tariff regime added a gross cost headwind in the mid-hundreds of millions of dollars annually. RTX has mitigated much of it through pricing, duty drawback and re-sourcing, but the asymmetry matters: commercial aftermarket has real pricing power, firm fixed-price defense contracts have none. The constraint is easing — trailing Raytheon sales have re-accelerated as new capacity came online — but it caps how fast the backlog can be converted, and it is the single most common reason RTX has missed its own delivery plans since 2022.
The Commercial Aftermarket Cycle — Where Bull and Bear Actually Meet
ONGOING SWING FACTOR
Roughly two-thirds of RTX's profit dollars ultimately trace to aftermarket spares and shop visits across Collins and Pratt & Whitney, and that stream is being amplified by an ageing global fleet: airframers have persistently under-delivered new aircraft, so operators are flying older equipment harder, and every extra flight hour on an existing wing is high-margin revenue for RTX. The bull framing is that this is a multi-year annuity with pricing power, that GTF shop visits convert a warranty liability into the industry's best installed-base economics, and that at 30x forward earnings you are underpaying for a business whose backlog covers three years of sales. The bear framing is that aftermarket strength is partly a symptom of the same supply dysfunction that eventually normalizes — when Boeing and Airbus rates finally recover, older aircraft retire and the aftermarket tailwind reverses precisely when the defense budget cycle may also be peaking. Which of those is right is essentially the whole RTX debate at $222.76; nothing else in the model moves the answer as much.
