01 Company Overview

Royal Bank of Canada is Canada's largest bank and its largest listed company: #1 by market capitalisation in Canada on companiesmarketcap's country ranking (a TSX and S&P/TSX Composite heavyweight) and #57 globally, at a $285.5B US-dollar market value. It runs five businesses — Personal Banking, Commercial Banking, Wealth Management (including the US bank City National), Insurance and Capital Markets. Fiscal 2025 (year to October 31, 2025) produced record net income of C$20.4B, up 25%, and diluted EPS of C$14.07, up 25%. The latest reported period is Q3 FY2026 (May–July 2026), released August 27, 2026: revenue C$18.54B (+9%), net income C$6.02B (+11%), diluted EPS C$4.23 (+13%), ROE 17.9%.

The premise for this report is the Canadian bank oligopoly, and the market-cap ranking backs it: five of Canada's six most valuable companies are banks — RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank and CIBC — with only Shopify breaking the run at #3. A concentrated, heavily regulated deposit market lets the leader earn an ROE near 18% while holding 13.5% CET1 capital. The second premise — that the debate is a mortgage renewal wall — needs correcting. On RBC's own Q4 2024 disclosure (as reported by Canadian Mortgage Trends), 2025 was the heavy year: 60% of renewing uninsured clients faced higher rates, at an average +$513 a month (+22%). For 2026 the figure was 31% of renewals, at +$458 a month (+18%). The wall has mostly been climbed. What remains is a slow credit bleed (Personal Banking PCL +17% YoY in Q3, driven by Canadian cards and personal loans) and a spread question: management has pointed to the back half of 2026 as the moment when renewals originally booked at "very thin spreads" reprice. The third thread is HSBC Canada, acquired in 2024. Its cost synergies are "met and on pace to exceed" per management, but the accounting tailwind is fading: the purchase-price-accounting (PPA) accretion that flattered net interest income is running off at roughly C$100M a quarter, and RBC expects to lap that headwind in Q2 FY2027.

FX exposure: RBC reports in Canadian dollars, while the NYSE line trades in US dollars, so a US investor carries the CAD/USD rate on top of the bank. The Bank of Canada rate on September 21, 2026 was C$1.4021 per US$1 (US$0.7132 per C$). Most revenue is earned in Canada. The biggest foreign pieces are the US (City National and US wealth management, US$1.96B of Q3 revenue) and global Capital Markets.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Big Six oligopoly #1 by market cap in Canada Low-cost deposit franchise (C$1.64T) C$5.84T assets under administration Canada's leading dealer HSBC Canada client base Scale in technology spend

The moat is structural rather than clever. Canadian banking is a regulated oligopoly in which a handful of national banks hold the deposit base, and RBC is the largest of them. It funds a C$1.11T loan book with C$1.64T of deposits (a 67% loan-to-deposit ratio), so it rarely has to pay up for wholesale funding. The HSBC Canada deal removed the one foreign-owned bank with a meaningful Canadian retail and commercial franchise, and every client it brought over is a client no competitor can win at acquisition-level prices. The scale shows in the numbers: an ROE near 18% while holding capital at the top of management's range. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin. The franchise is tied to a domestic consumer carrying C$473B of RBC mortgages alone, and to an economy that US trade policy keeps knocking about.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Consumer credit is deteriorating slowly, not suddenly. Personal Banking PCL rose 17% YoY on higher impaired provisions in Canadian cards and personal loans, and gross impaired loans rose to 0.91% of loans from 0.85%. Management cited elevated unemployment in Ontario (the Greater Toronto Area) and parts of British Columbia at an investor conference this year. The mortgage renewal peak was 2025, not 2026, but households that took the 2025 payment shock are the ones now showing up in card and unsecured-loan losses.
02
The HSBC Canada accounting tailwind is running off. Fair-value accretion on the acquired HSBC Canada book boosted net interest income after the deal. That accretion is now falling, a drag of roughly C$100M a quarter (about 4 bps of margin), which RBC expects to lap in Q2 FY2027. The Q3 report cites the lower accretion as the reason Personal Banking NIM stayed flat despite better product mix. The cost synergies are real; the NII flattery was temporary.
03
Growth depends on markets. Wealth Management and Capital Markets produced C$2.99B of Q3 net income, 50% of the group's C$6.02B, and grew profit 32% and 16%. Both scale with asset prices and deal activity. A market drawdown would hit the fastest-growing half of the bank at the same moment the consumer book is softening.
04
Tariffs and the Canadian economy. The Q3 report notes new US tariffs imposed in August 2026 on about 5% of US imports from Canada, on top of existing sector tariffs. It also shows Canadian GDP contracting 0.1% in the first calendar quarter of 2026 before an expected 3.4% rebound in the second. RBC's nine-month FY2025 PCL was inflated by performing-loan provisions for "trade disruptions (including tariffs)", so this risk has already moved the credit line once.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items to adjust for. First, the HSBC Canada purchase-price accounting. Fair-value accretion on the acquired loans inflated NII after the deal and is now running off at about C$100M a quarter, which RBC expects to lap in Q2 FY2027. Integration costs were a "specified item" in adjusted earnings through Q2 FY2025 (C$43M in the first nine months of FY2025) and were zero in Q3 FY2026. Second, adjusted versus reported: the gap is now small (C$4.28 vs C$4.23 of diluted EPS). It consists mainly of amortisation of acquisition intangibles, so adjustments are not doing heavy lifting. Third, a pending gain. On August 10, 2026, RBC and BMO agreed to sell their jointly owned payments processor Moneris to Francisco Partners for about C$2B. RBC's 50% share should produce a gain of about C$475M after tax (C$560M pre-tax) on closing, expected by the end of Q1 FY2027 — roughly C$0.34 per share of one-off EPS to strip out of that quarter (C$475M ÷ 1,384.6M shares). Buybacks also matter for per-share figures: 17.2M shares were repurchased for C$4.24B in the nine months to July 31, 2026.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF SEP 21, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been, on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It measures momentum, not value — a strong stock can stay overbought for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 month-end close of $99.48 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $206.19, the NYSE line gained 107.3% on price, before dividends, in US dollars. A US holder's return includes the CAD/USD move, because the NYSE price is roughly the TSX price divided by the exchange rate. The shape is two regimes. From 2021 to 2023 the stock went nowhere: rate hikes and housing fears kept it between about $80 and $114, and it bottomed at $79.88 in October 2023 (RSI 28.9, the only oversold reading in the window). From late 2023 it roughly doubled in two and a half years, through the HSBC Canada close, a string of record quarters, and the market's growing willingness to pay for an 18% ROE.

The monthly 6-period RSI has been above 70 for most of the past year and reads 80.4 now, after peaking at 89.4 in July 2026. The 10-month moving average is about $186.21, 10% below the price, and the 3-month average is $206.62, so the trend is up but has flattened since July. On StockAnalysis's daily data the price sits below its 50-day average ($209.12) and well above its 200-day ($184.78). Momentum is stretched and has stalled. It has not reversed.

Against its domestic peers, RY's $285.45B market cap is 1.4x TD's $203.24B and larger than BMO ($123.16B) and Scotiabank ($116.21B) combined. At 18.2x trailing earnings and roughly 3x book, the market already prices it as the premium Canadian bank.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

RBC is the biggest player in an oligopoly and is compounding. It earns an ROE of 17.9% with CET1 at 13.5%, the top of its own range, while returning C$4.0B a quarter. Its efficiency ratio improved 160 bps in a year, and PCL of 36 bps is contained. The mortgage renewal wall peaked in 2025 and the book absorbed it. From here renewals are a spread opportunity, with thin-spread vintages repricing in the back half of 2026. The HSBC Canada PPA drag gets lapped by Q2 FY2027, the Moneris gain (~C$475M) lands in Q1 FY2027, and City National has finally become a contributor (US$184M a quarter). Wealth, a C$5.84T-AUA fee machine, has become the largest segment by revenue, so the bank is less of a leveraged bet on Canadian housing than its reputation suggests.

Bear Case

You are paying 18.2x trailing and about 3x book for a bank. That is a premium multiple, and it assumes the 18% ROE lasts. Half of Q3 profit came from Wealth and Capital Markets, which is market-sensitive revenue at a market high. The domestic consumer franchise shrank its profit (−1%), consumer PCL rose 17% and impaired loans are drifting up, and new US tariffs on Canadian imports were added in August 2026. The HSBC accounting tailwind is gone, and a 1.49% group NIM leaves little slack. The consensus average target is only 1.8% above the price, and a Hold from KBW carries the highest named target. A great bank at a full price is not the same as a great investment.

Top 3 Risks

01
Canadian consumer credit. Not a mortgage cliff but a slow bleed. Personal Banking PCL +17% YoY, gross impaired loans 0.91% (from 0.85%), and elevated unemployment in the GTA and parts of BC. RBC's uninsured Canadian mortgages total C$383B, 81% of its Canadian book.
02
Markets-dependent earnings at a markets high. Wealth Management (+32% profit) and Capital Markets (+16%) together earned about half of Q3 net income. A drawdown in asset prices or deal activity hits both at once, and the multiple would likely compress at the same time.
03
Valuation and FX for a US holder. 18.2x trailing, ~3x book, a 2.4% yield, and a consensus average target barely above the price. On top of the bank's own risk, a US-dollar investor carries the CAD/USD rate: every C$ of earnings is worth fewer US$ if the loonie weakens.
Industry Commentary
The mortgage renewal wall — mostly behind, not ahead
PREMISE PARTLY OUTDATED

Canadian mortgages are typically fixed for five years and then renewed at the prevailing rate, so the 2020–21 cohort of ultra-low-rate borrowers faced a "renewal wall" as those terms rolled. RBC's own numbers, disclosed with Q4 2024 results and reported by Canadian Mortgage Trends, put the heaviest year in 2025 (60% of renewing uninsured clients facing a higher rate, averaging +$513/month or +22%). 2026 was lighter: 31% of renewals, +$458/month (+18%). The Q3 FY2026 report shows no mortgage-led credit event. The PCL increase came from cards and personal loans, and Personal Banking still earns a 24.5% ROE. The next debate is about spread rather than default: management has said competition for mortgages is "intense", with weak purchase activity pushing borrowers to switch lenders, even as older thin-spread renewals come up for repricing.

HSBC Canada — from accretion to organic
INTEGRATION MATURING

RBC bought HSBC's Canadian bank in 2024: a commercial, trade-finance and affluent-retail franchise with an internationally connected client base. By this year management said the cost targets were "met and on pace to exceed", C$300M of revenue synergies were "on track", and more than 90% of the acquired HSBC mortgage clients hold core chequing accounts at RBC. Client retention is "at or slightly ahead" of plan (Q3 call). The accounting now turns against the headline numbers. Purchase-price fair-value accretion added to NII in the early quarters and is now running off (about C$100M a quarter, roughly 4 bps), until the comparison is lapped in Q2 FY2027. Integration costs are no longer excluded from adjusted earnings because there are none left.

Oligopoly economics — capital return as the dividend of scale
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Five of Canada's six most valuable companies are banks. In a concentrated, tightly supervised market the largest player gets the cheapest deposits, the most technology spend per client, and the capital generation to fund both growth and returns. RBC's Q3 shows it: CET1 flat at 13.5% after C$4.0B of buybacks and dividends in a single quarter, and a stated intent to drift toward the 13.0% midpoint of its range "over time", which means more room for capital return. The bull framing: an 18% ROE bank with excess capital, growth in fee businesses and a fading accounting drag deserves a premium. The bear framing: the premium is already there at 18x earnings and 3x book, and oligopoly protects margins but does not protect a bank from its own domestic credit cycle.
