01 Company Overview

Safran is the Paris-based aerospace and defence group that makes, among much else, half of the engine on every Boeing 737 MAX and on every LEAP-powered Airbus A320neo. Through CFM International, a 50–50 joint venture with GE Aerospace, it builds the LEAP engine: the LEAP-1A is one of two engine choices on the Airbus A320neo family, the LEAP-1B is the only engine on the Boeing 737 MAX, and the LEAP-1C powers China's COMAC C919. Around that sits a second business of landing gear, nacelles (the engine housings), brakes, wiring, navigation systems and guided bombs, and a third of aircraft cabins and seats. FY2025 adjusted revenue was €31.33B, up 14.7%, with recurring operating income of €5.20B (16.6% of sales) and adjusted EPS of €7.60. At the October 27, 2026 close the company was worth €114.9B, or $128.4B.

The economics are the story. A new engine earns little when it leaves the factory; the money comes over the following decades from spare parts and overhaul contracts, every time the engine flies. Safran's own first-half 2026 release shows both halves of that bargain: Propulsion's margin rose to a record 24.5% on spare-parts sales for older CFM56 engines, while more LEAP deliveries with fewer spare engines held it back. Management called the first half of 2026 a record — revenue €17.57B, +19.0%; recurring operating income €3.24B, +29.0%; margin 18.4% — and on July 28 management raised every line of its full-year outlook. The third quarter, reported on October 23, showed the other side. Revenue rose 14.7% to €9,003M, in line with expectations, but civil-engine spare parts grew 17.0% in dollars, down from 27.9% in the first half; the full-year outlook was confirmed, not raised; and on the call management said capital spending will run above 6% of revenue every year in the years to come. The stock fell 10.0% that session (€302.00 → €271.80). Over the chart's five years it has still compounded: €116.04 at the end of October 2021, €274.60 now, +137%, against +15% for the CAC 40 over the same window. But 2026 has been a round trip — €340.00 at the end of February, €273.00 at the end of April, €345.00 at the end of June, €274.60 now. Jefferies named high fuel prices and the bigger investment plan as the reasons for the October fall, and at 32.6× trailing adjusted earnings the price still assumes years of growth. The question underneath the valuation: how long does the CFM56 spare-parts windfall run, and how fast does the LEAP fleet, 1,565 new engines in the first nine months of 2026 alone, start paying the same way?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Sole engine on the 737 MAX (LEAP-1B) LEAP-1A on the A320neo family Decades-long spare-parts annuity 50–50 partnership with GE Aerospace Engine certification barriers Rafale M88 & guided-weapon franchise Net cash €1.67B

The moat is the installed base. A narrowbody engine flies for decades, and every overhaul needs parts that only the maker can certify; Safran's CFM56 engines are paying for themselves again: the FY2025 release credits strong traffic and low aircraft retirement levels for an aftermarket that ran well above plan. Behind them sits the LEAP fleet, which already runs past 1,000 deliveries a half and whose shop visits are only now starting to rise. Any new engine must be certified before an airline can fly it, which keeps the field of rivals narrow. The vulnerability is timing: the profit today comes from the old engine, and the new one has to take over before the old one retires.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The CFM56 windfall has a shelf life. The H1 2026 release credits spare-parts growth "largely" to the CFM56, helped by a "favorable workscope mix and comparison base". Those engines retire as A320neos and 737 MAXs replace them. If LEAP aftermarket profit ramps slower than CFM56 spares fade, Propulsion margins fall back from the record 24.5%. The third quarter showed how fast the pace can change: spare-parts growth slowed to 17.0% in dollars from 27.9% in the first half, though management expects the fourth quarter to pick up again.
02
LEAP-1B profit waits on a blade. The CFO said the trigger to book profit on LEAP-1B rate-per-flight-hour contracts "has not yet been reached"; on the October 23 call management said the new high-pressure turbine blade, already certified by GE, now enters service in early 2027 rather than 2026, which moves that profit from 2026 into 2027. Until then the 737 MAX side of the fleet earns cash but not booked margin.
03
Fuel prices and flying hours. Aftermarket revenue tracks how much airlines fly. Safran lists the "potential impact of the Middle East conflict" as a watch item in its outlook, and Berenberg's September note said renewed Middle East tensions are clouding the outlook for civil aviation services. The stock fell 14.4% in the week to April 24, 2026, the same week Safran reported Q1 revenue up 18.8%, and 10.0% on October 23, the day of the Q3 release.
04
Investment, tax and supply chain. On the Q3 call management said capital spending will be above 6% of revenue each year in the years to come, including about €100M for a new optronics plant near Stuttgart, Germany; JPMorgan responded by cutting its 2027–2030 free-cash-flow forecasts by up to 12%. The French corporate surtax cost €322M in H1 2026 and is expected to take about €500M out of 2026 free cash flow; supply-chain capacity is the other standing watch item, and helicopter-engine deliveries were held back by it in the half.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, hedge accounting: IFRS profit swings with the market value of the dollar hedge book ($59.5B at June 30, $61.1B at the end of September), which is why H1 2025 IFRS EPS was €12.07 and H1 2026 €4.21, against adjusted EPS of €3.80 and €4.63. Second, the French corporate surtax took €322M from H1 2026 net income; the company expects about €500M for the year. Third, one-off items of €(177)M in the half, covering the end of commercial discussions on past transactions, impairments on several programmes and M&A costs. Per-share figures also benefit from buybacks: 2.8 million shares (0.7% of the capital) repurchased for €875M between January and July 27, 2026, and roughly 4 million (1% of the capital) for €1.25B by October 20 (Q3 release), all to be cancelled before year-end.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Safran owns half of the engine on every 737 MAX and on every LEAP-powered A320neo, and earns on it for decades. The aftermarket is running ahead of plan — spare parts +27.9% and services +40.4% in dollars in H1 2026 — and management raised 2026 guidance on July 28 to €6.4–6.5B of recurring operating income, on the way to a 2028 ambition of €7.0–7.5B. The LEAP fleet, 1,030 deliveries in a single half, has its most profitable years ahead, and the LEAP-1B blade fix, now due in early 2027, should start booking profit on 737 MAX service contracts. Defence adds a second engine of growth as Rafale output doubles. The balance sheet carries net cash, the dollar is hedged at 1.12 through 2028, and about €2.35B of the €5B buyback is still to come. Management expects spare parts to grow again in the mid to high twenties in the fourth quarter. On that path 28× forward earnings shrinks quickly, and the October fall has already taken the multiple down.

Bear Case

At 32.6× trailing adjusted earnings and 7.6× book, the price still discounts years of high-teens profit growth. Today's record margins rest on the CFM56 — an engine being retired — and on a "favorable workscope mix and comparison base" that, by the company's own wording, flatters the comparison. If oil stays high and airlines cut flying, the aftermarket slows first and fastest, exactly the risk Berenberg flagged in September. The third quarter showed the first slowdown: spare parts +17.0% in dollars against 27.9% in the first half. The LEAP-1B profit trigger has slipped to 2027, capital spending is set above 6% of revenue a year, and the French surtax takes about €500M out of 2026 cash. The stock is down 20.4% from its June month-end through first-half results that beat expectations and raised the outlook in July, and an in-line quarter in October.

Top 3 Risks

01
Flying hours and fuel. Spare parts and service contracts are paid per flight hour or per shop visit; a sustained oil-price shock that grounds capacity hits the most profitable line first. The stock fell 14.4% in the week to April 24, 2026, and in September Bernstein wrote that civil aviation service names were being punished for high oil prices despite excellent results.
02
The handover from CFM56 to LEAP. Current Propulsion margins lean on old-engine spares. If CFM56 shop visits fall before LEAP aftermarket profit — including the LEAP-1B contracts still waiting for their profit trigger — ramps up, the record 24.5% margin reverses.
03
Valuation leaves little room. A 32.6× trailing and 27.9× forward multiple against a CAC 40 yielding 3.18% means any guidance miss, delivery shortfall from the supply chain, or tax increase lands on a price that assumes none of them.
Industry Commentary
The engine aftermarket boom
STRUCTURAL TAILWIND

Safran's FY2025 release explains the boom: strong air traffic and low aircraft retirement levels kept older engines flying and coming in for overhaul, and aftermarket activity "trended strongly above initial expectations", supported by strong traffic and low retirement levels. On the H1 call the CEO said civil aftermarket activity remained "well above our pre-conflict forecast". The constraint is the supply chain, not demand: Safran keeps "supply chain production capabilities" as a watch item and, with Airbus, bought out Tikehau's stake in the supplier Aubert & Duval in July, which the CEO tied to building a more resilient supply chain.

Fuel prices and the Middle East
ONGOING HEADWIND

Analysts place the 2026 drawdown in fuel, not in results. Bernstein's Adrien Rabier wrote in September that companies with civil aviation service businesses were being punished for high oil prices despite excellent results, while air traffic held up and order books were full for the next twelve months. Berenberg's George McWhirter saw the recovery in flight hours stall. Safran's own position, from the CEO in the April Q1 release: "Despite current uncertainties related to the conflict in the Middle East, first quarter performance in both the civil aerospace and defense sectors reinforces our confidence in our ability to reach the high end of our full-year guidance." The H1 numbers bore that out. On the October 23 call management said kerosene prices had doubled from pre-crisis levels, which will eat into airlines' 2026 profits, though it expects them to stay positive overall; the same day Safran reported spare-parts growth of 17.0% in dollars for the quarter, and the stock fell 10.0%.

Defence re-armament
EMERGING TAILWIND

Defence is a second growth leg. The CFO said Rafale production rises from 2 aircraft a month in 2025 to 4 a month by 2029, and M88 engine deliveries more than tripled in H1 2026 to 33. Safran expects to have tripled its missile turbojet capacity against 2022 by the end of this year, and on October 1 it said it plans to double capacity at its Texas turbojet plant by 2028. Its defence electronics — inertial navigation, optronics, the Hammer guided bomb — grew strongly in the half, and on the Q3 call management announced about €100M for a new optronics plant near Stuttgart. Equipment & Defense revenue rose 20.6% in the third quarter (+15.7% organic); Berenberg's George McWhirter wrote that defence grew while the engine division disappointed somewhat. Defence is a smaller profit pool than civil spares, but it does not depend on the price of jet fuel.
