01 Company Overview

Santander is a Spanish bank with a global footprint: 188.8 million customers (September 30, 2026) across Spain, the UK, Portugal, the US, Mexico, Brazil, Chile, Argentina and a consumer-finance arm spanning Europe and the Americas, run as five global businesses — Retail & Commercial Banking, Openbank (consumer finance and digital banking), Corporate & Investment Banking, Wealth Management & Insurance, and Payments. Reuters calls it the euro zone's largest bank by market value, and it carries an 18.2% weight in Spain's IBEX 35 index (September 30). FY2025 attributable profit was €14.1B. The latest reported period is the first nine months of 2026 (9M'26), published October 28, 2026: revenue €46.8B (+8%), record underlying profit €11.18B (+16%), reported profit €12.41B (+20%) including a €1.9B gain on selling its Polish bank less €666M of acquisition charges, and an underlying RoTE of 16.0%. The third quarter alone made €3.85B of underlying profit (+19%) but €3.43B reported (−2%), after €416M of charges tied mainly to the Webster deal.

2026 is the year Santander reshaped its map. It sold Santander Bank Polska to Erste Group (completed January 9, ~€7B cash), bought TSB in the UK (completed April 30), bought Webster Financial in the US (announced February 3, completed August 20, adding 4.2 million customers, paid partly in new Santander shares), and on July 30 announced an all-share offer for the ~10% of Santander Brasil it does not own. Meanwhile it keeps buying back its own stock: the share count fell from 14,885 million in September 2025 to 14,556 million after the September 2026 cancellation, even after issuing 330 million new shares for Webster. Executive chair Ana Botín on the nine months: "We once again delivered strong results in the first nine months, with earnings per share up 25% and growing momentum across the group." Over five years the NYSE ADS went from a $3.79 month-end in October 2021 to $11.75, +210.0% — but it fell 12.1% between this report's October 2 edition and October 29, as Brazil's election and a weak Brazilian quarter weighed. The question now is whether 2028's targets — RoTE above 20%, profit above €20B — can carry it further, or whether investors have already paid for them.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

188.8M customers €1.22T customer deposits Shared global tech platform Europe–Americas diversification Spain retail scale Capital-light wealth & CIB

Santander's edge is scale in many markets, run on one machine. Most global banks either stay home or run each country as its own bank. Santander's ONE Transformation programme puts every market on common platforms — the global app now runs in five countries — so it builds a product once and deploys it across its markets. That is how revenue grew 8% in the first nine months while costs, excluding the two acquisitions, fell 1%. Diversification is the other half: a bad quarter in Brazil or Argentina is absorbed by Spain, the UK and the US. In Ana Botín's words in the 9M'26 release: "At a time of heightened volatility, our combination of at-scale global businesses and diversification across Europe and the Americas is a distinctive source of strength."

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Latin American credit and currencies. Brazil and Argentina carry the group's highest credit costs, and their earnings are translated into euros at volatile exchange rates. In the third quarter Brazil's profit fell 15% as provisions rose 23% (Reuters), and the real slid after President Lula's re-election. Asked about Brazil on the October 28 call, CEO Grisi said: "I agree with you that rates are not going to come down as much as we thought in the plan" — and Santander Brasil's funding costs move with those rates. On Argentina he said loan-loss provisions "remain impacted by Argentina, reflecting sector-wide trends in the country."
02
Integration load. TSB (UK, from May) and Webster (US, from August 20) are being absorbed at the same time, while the Santander Brasil buy-in is pending. Charges for integration and written-down intangible assets reached €666M in the second and third quarters. Each deal promises cost savings — at least £400M for TSB — but integrations are where banks lose customers and add costs.
03
Capital tighter after Webster. CET1 fell from 14.0% in June to 12.7% in September after Webster's 147bps, just below the 12.8–13% year-end target, leaving less room above the 12–13% operating range for extra buybacks.
04
UK conduct and consumer finance. The UK car-finance commission issue cost €245M gross in the first half and still weighs on Openbank's profit, and Openbank has the highest cost of risk of the businesses that report one (2.10%). Consumer lending is the part of the group that turns first in a downturn.

Notable One-Off Items

Santander strips "non-recurring items" out of its underlying numbers, and in 2026 they are large. Q1'26: a €1,895M net capital gain on selling ~49% of Santander Bank Polska to Erste (completed January 9). Q2–Q3'26: €666M of charges for written-down intangible assets and TSB and Webster integration costs, €416M of them in Q3. 9M'25 comparatives carry €713M of Poland's own results in the same line. That is why reported nine-month profit grew 20% while underlying grew 16%. Inside underlying profit, €245M gross of UK motor-finance provisions in the first half weighed on Openbank. Capital: TSB cost 55bps of CET1 in Q2; Webster cost 147bps in Q3. Share count: 329,846,438 new shares were issued at an effective €10.7896 on August 20 to pay for Webster, and 462,683,139 bought-back shares were cancelled (registered September 9), leaving 14,556,482,801 shares.

RELATIVE STRENGTH INDEX (RSI) — 6-PERIOD, MONTHLY
⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF OCT 29, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end of $3.79 to the October 29, 2026 settled close of $11.75, the Santander ADS rose 210.0% on price alone. The first half of the window went nowhere: the ADS sank to $2.32 in September 2022, the five-year low, and was still near $4 at the end of 2023. The move came in 2025 — a 25.3% jump in February 2025, the largest monthly gain in the series, and a climb from $4.56 at the end of 2024 to $11.73 at the end of 2025. 2026 has been choppier: the Webster announcement month (February) and March gave back ground, then the ADS made its five-year month-end high of $14.56 in August as Webster closed, before slipping 5.0% in September and another 15.0% by October 29, through Brazil's election and the third-quarter print.

The 6-period monthly RSI peaked at 96.1 in September 2025, an extreme reading, and was above 80 from June to August 2026. It has dropped to 42.4, below the neutral 50 — neither overbought nor oversold on this measure. Both moving averages now sit above the price: the 3-month average at about $13.38 and the 10-month average at about $12.91, so the short-term and the longer-term trends on this chart have both turned down.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Santander grew revenue 8% in the first nine months while costs excluding its two acquisitions fell, took its efficiency ratio to 42.6%, and printed record underlying profit, with reported EPS up 25% as buybacks shrink the share count. TSB and Webster add scale in the UK and US, with at least £400M of TSB cost savings and a 2028 plan for RoTE above 20% and profit above €20B. At 9.8x trailing and 10.1x forward earnings and 1.78x tangible book, the €153.0B market value is about 7.7 times the 2028 profit target of €20B (our arithmetic). The payout shifts toward cash dividends from 2027, and in Santander's September 30 coverage table 20 of 25 firms rated it a Buy, with a Bloomberg average target 32% above the October 29 Madrid price.

Bear Case

The ADS has fallen 21.9% from its 52-week high, and the price to tangible book Santander reports rose from 1.60x to 2.08x between September 2025 and September 2026 at quarter-end prices — a lot of the plan was paid for in advance. Q3 beat on underlying profit but missed slightly on revenue and net interest income, provisions ran above forecasts, guidance was reiterated rather than raised, and Brazil — its second-largest market, per Reuters — saw profit fall 15% with the real weakening after the election. CET1 is 12.7%, below the year-end target range for now; two integrations and a Brazil buy-in run at once; Argentina keeps lifting credit costs; and reported earnings flatter the trend — the €1.9B Poland gain inflates trailing EPS by about €0.13 a share. Jefferies moved to Hold on October 20, saying the earnings prospects are already in consensus forecasts.

Top 3 Risks

01
Execution on two integrations at once. TSB (UK) and Webster (US) are both in their first year, and their charges reached €666M in the second and third quarters; missing the cost-savings and RoTE milestones would undercut the 2028 plan, which the market is already paying for.
02
Latin American credit and currency. Brazil's profit fell 15% in the third quarter on higher provisions, and its rates look set to stay higher for longer after the election; Argentina has already raised the group's cost of risk. Weaker local currencies shrink those profits in euros.
03
Less capital headroom. CET1 at 12.7% after Webster, against a 12.8–13% year-end target, leaves less excess to distribute above the 50% payout; a credit shock or regulatory change would compete directly with buybacks.
Industry Commentary
Rates stayed higher for longer — good for deposit-funded banks
TAILWIND

Santander's H1 report (July 2026) described a rate backdrop that turned rather than eased: the ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.25% in June 2026 as energy prices rose with the conflict in the Middle East, the Bank of England held at 3.75%, and the Federal Reserve held at 3.50–3.75%. For a bank that earns most of its revenue from the spread between loans and deposits, that is supportive. On the October 28 call CFO García Cantera said: "NII is proving stronger than anticipated at Investor Day." Brazil is the exception: there high rates raise Santander's funding costs, and CEO Grisi conceded they will not fall as much as the plan assumed. Santander says around 95% of group revenue is linked to customer activity.

European bank consolidation, with Santander as buyer and seller
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

In twelve months Santander sold its Polish bank at 2.2x tangible book, bought TSB and Webster, and bid for the rest of Santander Brasil — capital moved out of a market where it was a minority-listed player and into markets where it wants scale (the UK and the US) or full ownership (Brazil). Spain's banks trade as a group: on October 1 BBVA, CaixaBank, Sabadell and Santander all fell about 4% in the same session. And diversification cuts both ways — on October 26 it was Santander's Brazilian exposure, not Spain, that hit the shares.

Valuation after the run-up
KEY DEBATE

At 1.78x tangible book on a 16.0% underlying RoTE, Santander is priced for its returns to keep climbing. The bull framing: management's own plan — RoTE above 20% by 2028, EPS growth boosted by buybacks — would make today's multiple look modest, and the October sell-off has already taken the price-to-book ratio down from the 2.08x the company reported at the end of September. The bear framing: every lever in that plan (TSB, Webster, Brazil, ONE Transformation) has to work at once, and Brazil is now working against it. The sell side was firmly in the bull camp at September 30 — 20 Buys among the 25 firms in Santander's coverage table, with a Bloomberg average target of €13.85, 32% above the October 29 Madrid close — but those targets predate the election and the print, and Jefferies has since moved to Hold.
