01 Company Overview

SBA Communications is a landlord whose tenants hang radios in the air. It owns 46,390 wireless sites at June 30, 2026 — 17,362 in the US and its territories and 29,028 internationally — and rents vertical space on them to mobile carriers under long, escalating leases. It was founded in 1989 as Steven Bernstein and Associates, went public in June 1999, and is structured as a REIT. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $2.87B (+5.4%), and the number that matters for a tower REIT is not GAAP EPS but AFFO per share: $3.05 in Q2 2026, down 3.8% from $3.17 a year earlier, against a full-year 2026 guide of $11.95–$12.40.

The 2026 story is a round trip. On April 2, 2026, Bloomberg reported that SBA was exploring a potential sale after preliminary takeover interest from large infrastructure funds; the stock closed that day at $204.04, up 18.93%. Barron's then reported (as relayed by TIKR and Yahoo Finance) interest from KKR and Brookfield centred on about $250 per share. On May 12 Green Street reported KKR had paused exclusive talks over the size of the equity cheque and valuation; on June 15 Green Street reported the take-private process had ended, and the shares fell 4%. The stock now sits at $176.28 — just 2.4% above the $172.11 March close, before anyone mentioned a bid. Meanwhile the operating question is unchanged: domestic leasing revenue is shrinking (−3.7% YoY in Q2) as the Sprint and EchoStar churn washes through, and international revenue is growing fast (+30.5%) on the Millicom towers. Which one wins the next two years decides whether 14.5x AFFO is cheap.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

46,390 permitted sites Long leases with escalators Zoning & permitting barriers Operating leverage (68% EBITDA margin) Largest tower owner in Central America Investment-grade balance sheet (BBB)

A tower's moat is where it stands. A carrier that needs coverage in a specific spot either rents space on the structure already there or tries to permit a new one — and SBA's model is to keep adding tenants to towers whose cost is already sunk, with an average of 1.8 tenants per tower at the end of 2025. That is why the Adjusted EBITDA margin sits at 68%. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: the US customer base is three carriers, and when two of the historic tenants disappear — Sprint into T-Mobile, EchoStar out of the network business — there is nobody to replace the rent at the same site. The moat protects the tower; it does not protect the tenant count.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Customer concentration and consolidation churn. The 2026 outlook carries $56M of Sprint consolidation churn and $56M of EchoStar churn, all domestic. Every carrier merger removes a tenant from overlapping sites. The domestic line fell 3.7% YoY in Q2 despite escalators and new leasing.
02
Leverage in a rate-sensitive structure. Net debt is 6.4x annualised Adjusted EBITDA, and FY2026 net cash interest is guided at $490–$498M against $1.92–$1.94B of Adjusted EBITDA — roughly a quarter of EBITDA (computed at midpoints). The July notes priced at 4.875%–5.450%; refinancing older, cheaper debt at those rates is a slow drag on AFFO.
03
Emerging-market exposure. International is now 29.6% of revenue and growing, but it brings currency swings (FX added 2.2 points to Q1 2026 AFFO-per-share growth — −4.7% reported vs −6.9% ex-FX, computed from the disclosed figures — and can subtract as easily) and churn from carrier bankruptcies and restructurings.
04
The failed bid left a reference price. The market was shown ~$250 a share in April and got nothing. The stock has since given back almost the whole move, to within 2.4% of its pre-bid March close; with the process declared dead on June 15, there is no longer an M&A floor under the stock.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported figures. First, Q4 2025 net income of $370.4M ($3.47 per share) was roughly double the prior-year quarter's $178.8M ($1.61); the company's Q4 2025 release says net income included a $226.3M gain on the sale of substantially all of its operations in Canada (and the intercompany-loan FX loss shrank to $10.3M from $77.8M). That one-off sits inside the trailing EPS and explains why consensus expects FY2026 GAAP EPS to fall 21% to $7.71 while AFFO per share guidance is roughly flat-to-down. Treat the 19.0x trailing P/E with care. Second, EchoStar: beginning in 2026 all EchoStar revenue has been removed from reported results while the contract dispute is litigated, so any recovery would be upside not in guidance. Third, buybacks: SBA bought back about $500M of stock in 2025 at around $200 a share (per TIKR), bought none in Q2 2026, and says it plans to resume in the second half of 2026 with $1.1B of authorisation remaining — a lever that is not in the per-share guidance.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 14.5x this year's guided AFFO per share for 46,390 towers, a 68% Adjusted EBITDA margin, a new investment-grade rating and a dividend that is only ~41% of AFFO, raised 13% this year with management expecting low-teens growth (per TIKR). Two infrastructure buyers looked hard enough in April to talk about ~$250 a share; the talks broke on the size of the equity cheque, not on the assets. The Sprint and EchoStar churn is a known, finite, disclosed number in the 2026 bridge — once it has passed, escalators and new leases are left, and a restarted buyback with $1.1B authorised works harder at $176 than it did at ~$200 in 2025.

Bear Case

AFFO per share is falling (−3.8% in Q2, and the FY2026 guide midpoint of $12.175 is below the trailing $12.57), domestic leasing is shrinking, and three US carriers hold the pricing power. Growth now depends on emerging markets with elevated churn and currency risk. Leverage of 6.4x means interest cost — about a quarter of EBITDA — reprices higher as cheap debt rolls. And the private-equity bid is gone: the people with the most diligence on the assets decided not to pay ~$250, which makes that number a ceiling reference, not a floor.

Top 3 Risks

01
Domestic churn outlasts the plan. The 2026 outlook assumes $112M of Sprint and EchoStar churn. Any further carrier consolidation, or slower leasing from AT&T (currently inactive under an agreement running to mid-2028), would extend the domestic decline beyond 2026.
02
Rates and refinancing. At 6.4x net debt / EBITDA with FY2026 net cash interest guided at $490–$498M, every refinancing at today's 4.9%–5.5% coupons compresses AFFO per share. The stock also trades as a long-duration income asset, so higher rates hit the multiple and the cash flow at once.
03
Emerging-market execution. International is 29.6% of revenue and the growth engine, but it carries currency translation, carrier bankruptcies and consolidation churn, and peak Brazil churn (~15% of revenue) in 2026. Management's "near the end of this heightened international churn" is a forecast, not a result.
Industry Commentary
Carrier consolidation: fewer tenants per tower
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A tower's economics are a function of how many carriers hang equipment on it. The US market's move to three national carriers, and EchoStar's exit, removes tenants that cannot be replaced one-for-one at the same site. SBA's 2026 bridge quantifies it: escalators add $71–74M group-wide and new leases $52–58M, while Sprint churn (−$56M), EchoStar churn (−$56M) and regular churn (−$57M to −$64M) take nearly all of it back. Non-organic revenue — acquisitions and builds, $82M of it international — is what makes the total grow.

Private capital and the public multiple
ONGOING DEBATE

The April–June 2026 episode was a live test of whether private infrastructure capital values towers above the public market. The answer was "yes, but not enough": interest around ~$250 a share versus a $172.11 March close, but not at a price that worked once a roughly $37B enterprise value and ~$15.2B of net debt had to be financed. The stock is now back within a few dollars of the pre-bid level. The same arithmetic applies to any future bidder, and to the company itself when it decides between buybacks and deleveraging.

Satellites, edge compute and the next tenant
EMERGING TAILWIND

Management's view is that "satellites are a complement to terrestrial wireless networks, not a substitute", and that any new entrant would need "a terrestrial component to their networks" — i.e. towers. The more speculative upside is edge compute: Cavanagh says about half the US portfolio could host "a disaggregated architecture for AI-oriented applications". The bull framing: a new tenant class arrives just as consolidation churn ends. The bear framing: it is a conversation, not a contract, and none of it is in the numbers. The way to judge it is simple — watch for a signed agreement and a line in the guidance bridge.
