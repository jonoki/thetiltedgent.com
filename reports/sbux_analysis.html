01 Company Overview

Starbucks is the world's largest coffeehouse operator, running 41,304 stores globally as of its fiscal third quarter (ended June 28, 2026) — roughly one-third company-operated and two-thirds licensed — across three reporting segments: North America, International, and Channel Development (packaged coffee, ready-to-drink beverages and single-serve pods sold through grocery and foodservice channels rather than through Starbucks stores). Fiscal 2025 revenue was $37.18B, up 2.8% YoY, and trailing-twelve-month revenue is $38.34B (+4.5%). It is the second-largest restaurant company in the world by market value behind McDonald's ($192.9B), ranked roughly #190 globally and #137 among US-listed companies by market capitalization.

This is not a quality-compounder story right now — it is a turnaround. Fiscal 2025 was the worst operating year in the company's modern history: comparable store sales fell 1% globally, GAAP operating margin collapsed 710 basis points to 7.9%, and GAAP EPS fell 51% to $1.63. Everything below is best read against that baseline: what CEO Brian Niccol's "Back to Starbucks" plan has actually fixed, what it cost to fix it, and what the market is now paying for the fix.

The engine, and the segment where the turnaround is actually being decided. 16,933 U.S. stores (41% of the global portfolio) plus Canada. Q3 FY2026 segment revenue was $7.40B, +6.8% YoY, at a 13.6% segment operating margin. The diagnosis a year ago was operational, not brand-level: mobile order-and-pay had congested store floors, service times had stretched, the menu had bloated, and management had been renting sales with discounts. The fix — branded "Green Apron Service" — deliberately adds labor hours, re-sequences order handling, simplifies the menu, and strips out promotional discounting. Management called Green Apron Service the primary driver of sustained sales performance on the Q3 FY2026 call, and reported improving throughput specifically during the morning peak. Two supporting programs: the Coffee House Uplift store-remodel effort passed 1,000 locations ahead of schedule with transaction growth across all dayparts, and digital menu boards are targeted to reach 80–90% of stores by September 2026 to merchandise the weak afternoon daypart. Net North America stores were down 14 in Q3 FY2026 — the company is deliberately slowing unit development and closing "stores in the wrong locations" after over-building two to three years ago.

The single largest structural change in the story. On April 2, 2026 Starbucks closed a joint venture with Chinese private-equity firm Boyu Capital: Boyu took 60% of the China retail operations, Starbucks retained 40% plus ownership of the brand and the intellectual-property licence to the JV. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Global Brand Equity 41,304-Store Density Rewards Program Scale Mobile Order & Pay Green Coffee Sourcing Nestlé / PepsiCo Distribution Real-Estate Site Selection Pricing Power

Starbucks' moat is a habit moat rather than a technology moat. Its durable advantages are location density (16,933 U.S. stores means it is physically closest to the most morning commuters), a loyalty program that pre-loads customer cash and biases repeat visits, and enough brand permission to raise price without losing the customer — the last five years of comps were carried almost entirely by ticket increases, which is only possible with real pricing power. The Channel Development segment extends that brand into grocery aisles at 52.1% operating margin without owning the distribution. Crucially, the moat was never the thing that broke. FY2025's collapse came from operations — congested stores, long service times, a bloated menu, and discount-dependent traffic — not from customers deciding the brand no longer had a claim on them. The Q3 FY2026 result, in which transactions turned decisively positive while discounting was being removed, is the strongest available evidence that the problem was execution and not structural brand decay. The bear side of that argument is in the first vulnerability below, and it is not dismissible.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Generational trade-down — structural or fixable? The genuine bear thesis is that younger consumers have permanently reset what they will pay for a coffee, and that Starbucks' $6–8 handcrafted beverage is on the wrong side of that reset. The evidence for it: Dutch Bros compounding revenue at +29.6% off a much cheaper, drive-thru-first format, and Luckin at 36,000+ stores and +36.4% revenue growth on a price point a fraction of Starbucks China's. The evidence against it: Q3 FY2026 North America transactions grew 4.5% while discounting was being pulled, which is not what a brand losing a generation looks like. One clean quarter is not proof — this needs three or four more.
02
Labor cost, and an unresolved union. Starbucks Workers United represents about 11,000 baristas across 550+ unionized U.S. locations and has been without a first contract for over four years. An open-ended unfair-labor-practice strike began November 13, 2025, and the union sent a contract proposal to the company in March 2026 with bargaining still unresolved. Separately and independently, the turnaround itself is a deliberate labor-cost increase: Green Apron Service adds hours to store schedules. Both vectors push the same direction on store-level margin.
03
Balance sheet: negative equity and an over-covered dividend. Total debt is $22.45B against $3.61B cash, and total stockholders' equity is negative $7.67B (book value per share −$6.73) after a decade of aggressive buybacks. That makes ROE and D/E mathematically meaningless. The current ratio is 0.76 and the dividend payout ratio is 142% of trailing GAAP EPS — the dividend is currently being funded out of cash flow, not earnings. It is covered by $3.64B free cash flow against roughly $2.8B of dividends, but the margin of safety is thinner than the 2.33% yield suggests.
04
China is now an equity stake, not a controlled business. Retaining 40% converts China from an operating problem into a line-item Starbucks no longer controls. That de-risks the P&L and lifts International margin to 19.1%, but it also caps the upside: if the JV succeeds in reaching 20,000 stores, Starbucks captures 40% of the economics plus a royalty rather than 100%. China comps are also no longer separately reported, which permanently reduces disclosure on what was the second-most-watched number in the story.
04 Key Financial Metrics

Start with comparable store sales, because that is the metric the market actually trades. Comps break into two components that most write-ups collapse into one number: transactions (how many customers came in) and average ticket (how much each spent). A comp held up by ticket while transactions fall means you are raising price on a shrinking customer base — that is materially worse than the headline. A comp led by transactions means people are coming back. That distinction is the entire argument.

Premise correction: the "comps propped up by ticket while transactions fall" framing was accurate for fiscal 2025 and is no longer accurate as of the most recent reported quarter. Q3 FY2026 North America transactions grew 4.5%, more than the 3.5% ticket contribution. Live filings, not prior expectations, drive that conclusion. The China leg of the split has also been structurally removed by the April 2026 joint venture rather than fixed.

Now the cost of the fix — and why the trailing P/E is not what it looks like. Adding labor hours and removing discounts works, but it is expensive. GAAP operating margin went 16.18% (FY21) → 15.36% (FY23) → 14.18% (FY24) → 7.9% (FY25). GAAP EPS went $3.58 (FY23) → $3.31 (FY24) → $1.63 (FY25), a 51% decline; non-GAAP EPS fell 36% to $2.13. TTM EPS is $1.74. So the trailing P/E of 61.7x is arithmetic on trough earnings, not a statement about how expensive the franchise is. Margin is already recovering: Q3 FY2026 GAAP operating margin was 10.5% (+60 bps) and non-GAAP was 14.4% (+430 bps), with EPS +70% YoY to $0.85 non-GAAP.

Illustrative normalization — the number that matters. The following is arithmetic on stated inputs, not a forecast. On roughly $38B of revenue: at FY2024's 14.2% operating margin, operating income is ~$5.4B; at FY2023's 15.4%, ~$5.85B. Applying the 68–75% net-income-to-operating-income conversion Starbucks ran in FY2023–FY2024 (which embeds interest on today's $22.45B debt load at the low end) gives net income of roughly $3.7B–$4.4B, or $3.20–$3.85 of normalized EPS on 1.14B shares. At $106.53 that is 28x–33x fully normalized earnings — against McDonald's at 22.1x, the Restaurants industry aggregate at 28.2x, and the NASDAQ-100 at 32.5x. Management's own FY2026 guidance of $2.55–$2.65 non-GAAP EPS puts the stock at ~41x this year. The honest conclusion is not "SBUX is expensive." It is that the market has already paid for a substantially complete recovery, and the remaining return has to come from either exceeding normalized margin or growing units — not from the multiple compressing back toward a rebound.

Notable One-Off Items — these are material and they distort both years badly.

Fiscal 2025: roughly $892M of restructuring and impairment costs tied to the Back to Starbucks restructuring announced in September 2025, including the closure of 627 stores, over 90% of them in North America, plus a simplification of the support organization. That charge is most of the gap between FY25 GAAP operating margin of 7.9% and the underlying run-rate.

Q3 FY2026: a $536.3M net gain on the China divestiture, offset by $302.6M of restructuring and impairments, $44.1M of China transaction costs and $18.1M of transformation costs. Net of those, GAAP EPS of $0.91 came in above non-GAAP EPS of $0.85 — an unusual inversion that means the headline GAAP number flatters the quarter. The effective tax rate also fell to 26.4% from 31.8%. Anyone modelling forward earnings should work from the $0.85 non-GAAP figure and the $2.55–$2.65 FY26 guide, not from the GAAP print.

Five-year trajectory. SBUX closed July 2021 at $121.43 and trades at $106.53 — a price return of −12.3% over roughly five years, during which the NASDAQ-100 roughly doubled. Adding back dividends (approximately $10–11 per share cumulatively over the period at the current $2.48 annual rate and lower rates earlier — approximate, not verified from a dividend-history source) brings the total return to somewhere near flat. That is the fact that frames everything else: this stock has been dead money for half a decade, and the 2026 rally is a recovery off a five-year base, not a breakout to new highs. The path had three distinct phases: a 2021–2022 de-rating from the reopening peak as Howard Schultz returned as interim CEO and buybacks were suspended; a long 2023–2024 grind lower as U.S. transactions deteriorated; and the Niccol era, which produced a violent one-day repricing in August 2024, a false dawn into February 2025, a capitulation to $80.05 in April 2025 and $80.87 in October 2025, and then the sustained 2026 recovery on actual operating evidence.

Relative performance. Over the trailing 52 weeks SBUX returned +20.55% in price against the NASDAQ-100 (via QQQ) at +24.68% including dividends — a slight lag despite one of the best operating quarters in the company's recent history, which is itself informative about how much recovery was already discounted. Against its own industry, SBUX has re-rated well past the Restaurants aggregate P/E of 28.2x on trailing earnings while McDonald's sits at 22.1x. On a five-year view the gap versus the NASDAQ-100 is enormous, and closing it requires the earnings normalization in Section 04 to actually land.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The diagnosis was right and the fix is working. Q3 FY2026 North America comps of +8.1% were led by transactions (+4.5%), not ticket, achieved while discounting was being removed — that is the highest-quality comp composition Starbucks has printed in years, and it argues the problem was throughput and service, not brand erosion. Non-GAAP operating margin expanded 430 bps to 14.4% and non-GAAP EPS rose 70% in the same quarter, so the margin cost of adding labor hours is already being out-earned by the volume it generates. China has been converted from an unwinnable price war into a 40% equity stake plus a royalty, lifting International segment margin to 19.1% and removing the biggest source of forecast risk. Free cash flow of $3.64B never broke even in the trough year. If margin normalizes toward the FY2023–24 band, normalized EPS of $3.20–$3.85 makes today's 61.7x trailing multiple look like an artifact rather than a valuation.

Bear Case

The recovery is real and already priced. At $106.53 the stock trades at ~41x management's own FY2026 guide of $2.55–$2.65 and 28–33x a fully normalized $3.20–$3.85 — a premium to McDonald's 22.1x and roughly at the NASDAQ-100's 32.5x, for a business growing units in the low single digits. That is precisely why four firms, including Morgan Stanley and Wells Fargo, double-downgraded to Underweight four days after a quarter that beat. One clean quarter does not settle the structural question either: Dutch Bros is compounding at +29.6% and Luckin at +36.4% on cheaper price points, and Starbucks now captures only 40% of whatever China becomes. The dividend consumes 142% of trailing GAAP EPS, equity is negative $7.67B, and the current ratio is 0.76 — there is no balance-sheet cushion if comps decelerate before margin fully recovers.

Top 3 Risks

01
Comp deceleration before margin normalizes. Q3's +7.9% global comp laps an easy prior-year base; the FY2027 comparisons will be brutal. If transaction growth fades back toward zero while the added Green Apron labor hours stay in the cost structure, the company ends up with FY2025's margin and FY2026's expectations embedded in the share price.
02
Multiple compression on normalized earnings. The market is paying 28–33x a normalized earnings figure that does not yet exist. A de-rating to McDonald's 22.1x on $3.50 of normalized EPS implies roughly $77 — near the 52-week low — without requiring the turnaround to fail at all. The valuation is doing more work than the operations.
03
Coffee input costs and labor, compounding. Arabica futures are at 341.65 US¢/lb, up 5.9% in a session and 5.3% over the past month, with ICE inventories at a 2.75-year low of 231,445 bags. Management flagged coffee inflation as a Q3 headwind expected to moderate — if it does not, and an unresolved Workers United contract lands as a wage step-up in the same year, the margin bridge to normalized earnings gets materially longer.
  Industry Commentary
Green Coffee Costs — Still Elevated, Finally Deflating
ONGOING HEADWIND

Arabica futures sit at 341.65 US¢/lb (≈$3.42/lb) as of August 25, 2026 — up 5.89% on the session and 5.27% over the past month, but down 12.39% over twelve months. The near-term tightness is real: ICE arabica inventories have fallen to a 2.75-year low of 231,445 bags, Brazil's 2026/27 harvest was only 90% complete as of August 12 versus 97% a year earlier (arabica specifically 86% versus 95%), and earthquake damage has limited Colombian exports through the port of Buenaventura. Management flagged coffee inflation as a Q3 FY2026 cost headwind that it expects to moderate in Q4. The year-over-year decline is what makes the FY2026 margin guidance of >11% credible; the month-over-month spike is what makes it fragile. For a company whose gross margin is 22.34% and whose entire investment case rests on a margin bridge, the direction of this one commodity carries unusual weight.

Labor: The Turnaround Is a Deliberate Cost Increase, and the Union Is Unresolved
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Two labor pressures push the same direction. First, by design: Green Apron Service adds staffing hours, routines and coaching to store schedules, and management said explicitly on the Q3 FY2026 call that labor hours are being managed "without capping growth" — meaning hours are being spent to buy throughput. That is the mechanism by which FY2025's GAAP operating margin fell to 7.9% from 14.18% and GAAP EPS halved to $1.63. Second, involuntarily: Starbucks Workers United represents about 11,000 baristas at 550+ U.S. stores (roughly 3% of the 16,933-store U.S. base) and has gone more than four years without a first contract. An open-ended unfair-labor-practice strike began November 13, 2025, and the union tabled a contract proposal in March 2026 with over 100 unfair-labor-practice charges outstanding. The union's headline demands — more hours and better staffing — are directionally the same thing Green Apron Service is already doing, which is why a settlement is plausible; it is also why a settlement would likely be priced into wages rather than absorbed for free.

The Price-Point War — and What It Means for Bulls and Bears
EMERGING TAILWIND / STRUCTURAL QUESTION

The competitive set has bifurcated by price. Luckin surpassed 36,000 stores globally with TTM revenue of $8.24B growing 36.4% — larger than Starbucks' entire International segment run-rate — on a price point a fraction of Starbucks China's. In the U.S., Dutch Bros is compounding revenue at +29.6% to $1.88B from a cheaper, drive-thru-first format that skews younger. The bear reads this as structural: a generation has repriced what coffee is worth, Starbucks' premium positioning is on the wrong side of it, and the company has already conceded China by selling 60% of it. The bull reads it as containment: Starbucks exited a price war it could not win at an acceptable structural outcome (40% of the equity, plus brand royalties, plus a path to 20,000 JV stores, plus International segment margin up to 19.1%), and then proved in North America — the market that actually matters at 73.9% of revenue — that it can grow transactions 4.5% while raising ticket 3.5% and removing discounts. Both readings fit the same data. The tiebreaker is Q4 FY2026 and the FY2027 comparisons: if transaction-led comps survive a hard lap, the structural thesis is dead. If they do not, the discount-dependency was never actually cured.
