01 Company Overview

Charles Schwab is a savings-and-loan holding company that runs the largest publicly traded retail brokerage and the largest custodian for independent registered investment advisors (RIAs) in the United States. It is structurally a hybrid broker-bank: it earns fees on assets it manages and trades it executes, and it earns a spread on the cash its clients leave idle, which Schwab Bank invests in securities and loans. That second engine — net interest revenue — is why Schwab trades more like a bank than a broker on rate headlines.

FY2025 net revenues were $23.92B, up 22.0% YoY, with GAAP EPS of $4.65 (+56%) and adjusted EPS of $4.87 (+50%). The third quarter of 2026, reported on October 15, kept the pattern: net revenues of a record $7.17B, +17% YoY, GAAP EPS of $1.62 (+29%) and adjusted EPS of $1.69 (+29%), against a $1.66 consensus (Nasdaq earnings calendar). Total client assets were $13.33 trillion at September 30, 2026 (+15% YoY) across 48.6 million client accounts (40.26 million brokerage, 2.43 million banking and 5.87 million workplace-plan accounts). The stock fell 2.7% on the day of the release and has slid since: it closed at $89.58 on October 19, 19.2% below the $110.88 of the previous edition. Schwab ranks #78 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization.

The TTM picture through September 2026 shows $27.06B of revenue and $10.26B of net income (StockAnalysis) — a 37.9% net margin (net income ÷ revenue) — and a 21.1% ROE on a trailing basis. Schwab's own annualized figure for the third quarter alone was 25% (21% a year earlier), and 44% on tangible common equity (equity excluding goodwill and other intangibles; 38% a year earlier).

Schwab's moat is a funding-cost advantage wrapped in a distribution monopoly. Because it custodies $13.33 trillion of client assets across 48.6 million client accounts, a persistent slice of that money sits as uninvested sweep cash — $492.8B at September 30, 2026 — which Schwab funds at an administrative rate far below wholesale. No competitor can replicate that deposit base without first replicating the distribution. On the advisory side, the RIA custody franchise is a genuine two-sided network: advisors bring clients, Schwab provides trading, custody, reporting, lending, and practice-management technology, and re-papering an entire book to another custodian is a months-long operational ordeal. The TD Ameritrade integration — completed after moving millions of accounts and the thinkorswim platform — removed the last credible scale challenger in RIA custody and is now producing cross-sell evidence. Layered on top is a cost structure that lets Schwab charge $0 equity commissions and still post a 54.8% adjusted pre-tax margin in 3Q26.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Cash sorting and yield competition. The 2023 crisis proved that Schwab's core profit engine is a spread it does not fully control. When short rates rose, clients moved sweep cash into money-market funds and Treasuries, forcing Schwab to replace cheap deposits with expensive Federal Home Loan Bank borrowing. Client cash fell to 8.9% of total client assets, down 50bps YoY at September 30 — the sorting pressure has moderated but has not disappeared, and rivals like Interactive Brokers advertise materially higher cash yields as a competitive wedge. Any regulatory or litigation push toward higher sweep rates hits earnings directly.

2. Duration and AOCI on the securities portfolio. Schwab invested pandemic-era deposits into long-dated mortgage-backed securities at low yields. A large held-to-maturity portfolio carries unrealized losses that do not flow through earnings but do sit in AOCI and in the fair-value footnote, constraining book value and creating the perception that Schwab is capital-impaired if forced to sell. That perception is what turned a rate cycle into a bank run in March 2023.

3. Fee and price compression. Equity commissions already went to zero. The next fronts are advisory program fees, money-market expense ratios, and custody economics, where Altruist and other low-cost challengers are aggressively targeting small and mid-sized RIAs. Schwab's fee-based revenue grows with markets, but the rate it charges is under permanent structural pressure.

4. Regulatory and concentration risk. As a $521B savings-and-loan holding company, Schwab is subject to bank capital, liquidity, and stress-testing regimes that pure brokers avoid, and to FDIC assessments — including the special assessment levied after the 2023 regional bank failures. Trading revenue is also concentrated in options and retail engagement, which can normalize sharply.

Notable One-Off Items

Schwab's reported numbers over the last three years contain several material non-recurring distortions, and the gap between GAAP and adjusted results is not trivial:

Acquisition and integration costs. 3Q26 GAAP results absorbed $151M of pre-tax transaction-related costs ($141M amortization of acquired intangible assets and $10M of acquisition and integration costs), which is the $0.07 gap between GAAP EPS of $1.62 and adjusted EPS of $1.69; the figure was $170M in 2Q26. Prior years carried far heavier TD Ameritrade integration costs, which have now largely rolled off following completion of the client-account conversions — a meaningful tailwind to the GAAP-to-adjusted convergence.

FDIC special assessment. Following the March 2023 regional bank failures, the FDIC levied a special assessment on large insured institutions to replenish the Deposit Insurance Fund. Schwab's 2023 expenses included a $172 million FDIC special assessment (Q4 2023 earnings release, January 17, 2024); it is non-recurring and depresses the FY2023 base against which current growth is measured.

Securities repositioning and AOCI marks. Schwab has periodically sold or repositioned lower-yielding securities, realizing losses in order to reinvest at higher yields. Separately, the held-to-maturity portfolio carries unrealized losses that never touch the income statement but suppress tangible book value and were the analytical core of the 2023 bear case. Because Schwab has now repaid nearly all of its supplemental funding, it is no longer a forced seller of those securities — but the marks remain a balance-sheet overhang until the portfolio rolls down.

Capital structure actions. In 2Q26 Schwab redeemed $2.1B of Series I preferred stock and issued $1.5B of Series L, plus repurchased $1.0B of common (11.2 million shares). In 3Q26 it repurchased $0.5B (4.7 million shares) and declared preferred dividends of $95M. Preferred dividend changes shift EPS at the margin and should be normalized when comparing quarters.

Expense guidance distortion. At the July update management guided FY2026 total expense growth to 9.5%–10.5% versus underlying 5.5%–6.5%, a roughly 400bps gap driven by volume-related costs and the inclusion of Forge (~100bps). On October 15 the CFO said expenses should come in at the low end of that range; 3Q26 expenses excluding interest rose 9% YoY to $3.394B. Comparing headline expense growth to prior years without this adjustment overstates cost inflation.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been up versus down. Above 70 means the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 means it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

The five-year trajectory is one of the more dramatic round trips in US large-cap financials. SCHW closed September 2021 at $72.84 and closed October 19, 2026 at $89.58 — a price gain of roughly +23% over five years, or about 4.2% annualized before dividends, after a run to $109.82 at the end of August that has since been given back. That headline number conceals almost everything that mattered. The stock peaked at $87.70 in January 2022 as the market anticipated Fed tightening (rising rates were, at that point, unambiguously good for Schwab's spread). It then fell through 2022 as the rate shock hit asset values, recovered to $83.26 by December 2022 — and then, in a single month, collapsed 33% from $77.92 at end-February 2023 to $52.38 at end-March 2023, with the intraday low in the mid-$40s. Silicon Valley Bank's failure turned the market's attention to Schwab's held-to-maturity securities portfolio, its unrealized losses, and the accelerating flight of client cash out of low-yielding sweep accounts. Schwab did not fail — it plugged the gap with expensive Federal Home Loan Bank borrowing — but the earnings power was gutted and the multiple never fully recovered until the funding cost came back out.

The bottom was $52.04 at end-October 2023, coincident with the peak in long-term rates. From there the recovery has been a mechanical function of one variable: the paydown of supplemental funding. Bank supplemental funding fell by $9.7B to $5.1B at year-end 2025 and then to just $0.5B of FHLB borrowings at June 30, 2026 — a 94% decline from $9.0B a year earlier. Every dollar retired swapped a high-cost liability for cheap deposits, and NIM followed: from the crisis trough up to 2.74% for FY2025, 3.00% in 2Q26 and 3.04% in 3Q26. That single line explains the earnings recovery — FY2025 EPS +56% — far better than any narrative about markets or trading. The third quarter is the first sign of friction: FHLB borrowings rose to $2.0B at September 30, and management now expects full-year NIM toward the lower end of the 3.00%–3.10% range given in July.

Layered on top was a genuine engagement boom. Total client assets reached $13.33T (+15% YoY) at September 30, 2026; daily average trades ran 10.7 million in 3Q26, +44% YoY; margin loan balances reached $187.9B. The stock cleared its pre-crisis high in 2025 and made a new one at $114.53 intraday on August 24, 2026; the close of $89.58 is 21.8% below that. From the October 2023 month-end low of $52.04 to $89.58, the position is up +72%.

Relative performance: a +23% five-year price return has materially lagged the S&P 500, which rose +78.6% on price over the same window (SPY, September 30, 2021 close $429.14 to October 19, 2026 close $766.48, Yahoo Finance), a run driven by mega-cap technology through 2023–2025 — SCHW spent two of those five years underwater from a self-inflicted balance-sheet wound while the index compounded. Schwab has also lagged the online brokers Interactive Brokers (+422%) and Robinhood (+129%) on price over the same window (Yahoo Finance closes), whose profits never depended on a book of long-dated bonds bought at low rates. The counterpoint: the return on tangible common equity is 44% annualized and the trailing P/E has compressed to 15.3, but over the last twelve months SCHW is −4.8% on price (October 17, 2025 close $94.14 to $89.58), so the market is not paying for that normalization yet.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The balance-sheet repair is essentially finished and the earnings power it suppressed is coming back mechanically: FHLB borrowings are far below the $9.0B of June 2025 (at $2.0B on September 30), NIM has recovered to 3.04% (2.88% a year earlier) and adjusted pre-tax margin is 54.8%. That is happening alongside a genuinely strong franchise — $13.33T of client assets (+15%), a record $190.4B of quarterly core net new assets (+38%), 1.2 million new brokerage accounts in the quarter, and 25% annualized ROE — on an 11.4x forward P/E and a 0.52 PEG. With Tier 1 Leverage at 8.9% and almost $4B of buybacks in nine months, a 19% fall from the previous edition makes capital return a bigger lever.

Bear Case

Most of the recovery is a one-time normalization, not a growth rate — you cannot pay down $9B of supplemental funding twice. Management said on October 15 that full-year revenue and NIM should land toward the lower end of the July ranges, and FHLB borrowings rose to $2.0B from $0.5B in the quarter. Trading revenue fell to $1.158B from $1.215B in 2Q26 as DATs eased to 10.7M from 11.9M, and the July guide for FY2026 DATs was 10.6M. Client cash has fallen to 8.9% of total client assets, down 50bps YoY, and the July guidance assumed a rate hike, which would restart sorting pressure. Goldman Sachs cut its target after the print, citing the net interest income outlook, and Deutsche Bank, Evercore ISI and BMO cut theirs too; the stock is at $89.58, 18% below its August month-end.

Top 3 Risks
01
Cash sorting reaccelerates. Sweep cash of $492.8B funds the entire net interest engine at below-market cost. Client cash is already down to 8.9% of total client assets. A rate hike — which management's July outlook assumed — or aggressive yield competition from Interactive Brokers and money-market funds could pull balances out again, forcing Schwab back toward wholesale funding and stalling the NIM path; management already guides to the lower end of the July range.
02
Trading and engagement normalize hard. Trading revenue of $1.158B (+16%) on 10.7M DATs (+44%) and $187.9B of margin loan balances (+93% YoY) reflects an exceptional retail environment. Options volumes and long/short strategy demand are cyclical. A quiet market removes several hundred million dollars of quarterly revenue at very high incremental margin, and the expense base does not shrink with it.
03
Duration and AOCI overhang on the securities book. Unrealized losses on the held-to-maturity portfolio remain a live constraint on tangible book value and a latent confidence risk. Schwab is no longer a forced seller, but if long rates rise materially the marks widen again, and the 2023 experience showed how quickly a securities-portfolio narrative can become a deposit-flight narrative for a broker-bank with $521B of assets.
  Industry Commentary
The Rate Path and Deposit Betas
ONGOING HEADWIND
Schwab's July outlook assumed an unusual base case: one 25bp Fed hike before year-end 2026, with minimal impact on 2026 but meaningful NIM expansion in 2027. For a broker-bank this is genuinely two-sided. Higher short rates lift the yield on a $521B balance sheet — but they also widen the gap between Schwab's administrative sweep rate and what clients can earn in a money-market fund, which is precisely the mechanism that produced the 2023 crisis. The deposit beta Schwab actually realizes on the next move is the whole ballgame. Evidence is mixed: sweep cash rose $7.1B in 3Q26 to $492.8B, but FHLB borrowings rose to $2.0B from $0.5B and client cash, at 8.9% of total client assets (down 50bps YoY), shows the structural drift is still one-directional.
Retail Investor Engagement & Trading Volumes
EMERGING TAILWIND
The retail participation cycle is running at levels that would have been considered a blow-off top in a prior era and have simply persisted. Schwab opened 1.243 million new brokerage accounts in 3Q26 (+9% YoY; 48.6 million client accounts in total), booked $190.4B of core net new assets (+38% YoY) including $67.5B in September, ran 10.7 million DATs (+44%), and ended with margin loan balances of $187.9B (+14% from June). Industry-wide this is being driven by options, leveraged and long/short products, and a generation of investors who default to self-directed platforms. It is a tailwind — but it is a cyclical tailwind, and the third quarter's 10.7M DATs already sit below the 2Q peak of 11.9M.
Competition on Cash Yields — Bull vs Bear
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The permanent tension in this business is that Schwab's largest profit pool is money it pays clients almost nothing for. Interactive Brokers ($965B of client equity and 35% more accounts than a year earlier at September 30, 2026) competes explicitly on cash yield; Fidelity ($17.5T, September 2025) defaults clients into a money-market fund rather than a low-rate sweep; Vanguard ($11.6T, September 2025) competes on cost. Bull framing: Schwab has held sweep balances at $492.8B and grown them through a full rate cycle, proving that convenience, breadth, and the advisor channel outweigh a few hundred basis points of yield for most clients — and with supplemental funding retired, the spread now drops straight to the bottom line at a 54.8% adjusted pre-tax margin. Bear framing: every year of investor education, every fintech that automates cash management, and every regulatory nudge toward sweep-rate disclosure chips away at the pool. If sweep cash structurally reprices toward market yields, Schwab's earnings power resets lower permanently — and an 11.4x forward multiple on earnings lifted by a one-time recovery would look expensive in hindsight.
