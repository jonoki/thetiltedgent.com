01 Company Overview

Shin-Etsu Chemical makes two very different things at very large scale. One is the silicon wafer — the polished disc of ultra-pure silicon that every chip is built on — where it ranks No.1 in the world, alongside photoresists and photomask blanks (both No.2), the light-sensitive coatings and patterned plates used to print circuits. The other is PVC, where its US subsidiary Shintech is the world's largest producer and the group has 4.84 million tons a year of capacity in the US, Europe and Japan (company rankings, Annual Report 2026). In between sit silicones (No.1 in Japan), cellulose derivatives for medicines and food, rare-earth magnets and synthetic quartz. In the fiscal year to March 2026 (Shin-Etsu calls it FY2025) it booked ¥2,573.9B of sales, up 0.5%, operating income of ¥635.2B (−14.4%) and net income of ¥474.5B (−11.2%). Overseas customers bought 79% of it; the US alone took 28%.

The two halves are still moving in opposite directions. In July–September 2026 (Q2 FY2026) the group earned ¥212.1B of operating income, up 26.9%, and ¥385.9B for the first half, up 15.6%. The semiconductor side earned ¥218.9B in the half, up 28%, on AI-driven demand and price increases, with some products short of supply; the PVC side earned ¥86.2B, down 16%, after Asian prices softened in late May and steadied following a July price rise (Media-IR's report on the October 27 results). The full-year forecast of ¥700B of operating income, first issued on July 24 when it came in below analyst expectations and the stock fell 12.4% over two sessions, was left unchanged on October 27; it implies a second half about 18.6% below the first. At ¥5,966 the market values Shin-Etsu at ¥10.8 trillion ($68.7B), 22.0 times trailing earnings and 20.9 times the company's own forecast for this year. The question is whether the wafer upcycle can carry the group while the PVC cycle sits near the bottom — and whether the balance sheet, which held about ¥1.35 trillion more cash than debt at September 30 after the July buyback, keeps going back to shareholders.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

No.1 silicon-wafer maker Lithography materials (No.2 resists & blanks) World's largest PVC capacity US-integrated Shintech Long-term wafer agreements AAA (R&I) balance sheet Specialty silicones

The moat is process scale in materials that customers cannot easily requalify. A chipmaker that has qualified a wafer or a photoresist for a production line does not switch suppliers lightly, and Shin-Etsu leads in wafers and is second in the lithography consumables that sit on top of them. In PVC the advantage is cost: Shintech is the world's largest producer and makes its own caustic soda, vinyl chloride monomer and part of its ethylene in the US, and the Louisiana project will more than double that ethylene capacity (0.5 to 1.125 million t/year). Behind both sits a balance sheet that lets the company build through downturns — only ¥401.6B of debt against about ¥1,753B of cash and short-term investments at September 30, 2026 (StockAnalysis, S&P Global balance-sheet data).

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The PVC cycle is set in Asia. Infrastructure Materials fell from ¥52.8B of quarterly operating income to ¥18.5B in a year before recovering to ¥34.8B in Q1 FY2026 and ¥51.4B in Q2. Management traces Asian weakness to inventories and supply from Chinese producers; Shin-Etsu cannot control either, and its own forecast leans cautious for the second half.
02
Wafer capacity costs more than twice what it did. Management told analysts that building a new plant would cost more than twice its previous investment, so it will not rush to add capacity and is pricing to justify it. That protects returns, but it leaves room for rivals such as GlobalWafers, which signed a 10-year agreement with Micron; management suggested GlobalWafers' large US investment and Buy American policy may have helped it win that deal.
03
Contract renewals. Current long-term wafer agreements were signed around 2020–21; some expire by the end of next year, others run three to four more years (Q1 call). The price at renewal sets Electronics Materials' margin for years.
04
Raw materials and the Middle East. The company's own Q1 commentary names the armed conflict involving Iran as a source of concern for crude and petrochemical prices; magnets depend on heavy rare earths whose supply the company is working to reduce its reliance on.

Notable One-Off Items

Four things bend the figures. First, the Shintech lag: the US PVC business is consolidated one quarter late, so the April–June PVC rollback in Asia reached the reported numbers in Q2 FY2026 (July–September). Infrastructure Materials still earned ¥51.4B in that quarter (our arithmetic: first-half ¥86.2B less Q1's ¥34.8B), but the first half is down 16%, and Shintech's July–September quarter arrives only in Q3 results. Second, buybacks: Shin-Etsu spent ¥500.0B on its own shares in FY2025 (cash flow statement) and ¥250B more by July 30, 2026 — about ¥1,250B since April 2022 by management's count on April 28, before this year's ¥250B — so per-share growth outruns profit growth. Third, the ¥20 commemorative dividend is a one-off; the ordinary dividend is ¥116. Fourth, the yen: management put the sensitivity of FY2026 ordinary income at ¥3.7B a year for each yen of movement against the dollar on the July 24 call — a weaker yen adds profit — and the forecast now assumes about ¥157 per dollar from October; the rate on October 30 was ¥157.80. In January 2026 some existing shareholders also sold ¥140B of shares in a secondary offering agreed with the company.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The wafer business — 54% of segment profit at a 34% margin — is in the tightest market in years: all 300 mm makers at full capacity, a record shipment month in June, customers building inventory for AI, a rival's 10-year contract with Micron showing how long buyers expect demand to last, and new plants costing more than twice as much, which keeps rivals' capacity slow and pricing firm into the renewals that start next year. The Isesaki lithography site adds capacity in the materials AI chips use most. PVC is near a cyclical low and management says Chinese producers are already raising prices, so the segment that missed the forecast has room to recover. Meanwhile an AAA-rated (R&I) balance sheet returns cash steadily: ¥250B of buybacks this year, a ¥136 dividend and a 40% payout policy. At 21 times its own forecast, every target in the consensus sits above the price.

Bear Case

The company's own forecast was 8% below what the market expected when it was issued in July, and it came from management, not a competitor; the unchanged ¥700B after a ¥385.9B first half implies a second half 18.6% lower, so either management is cautious or something is expected to weaken. Infrastructure Materials earns about a fifth of profit (22.3% in the first half) and its price is set by Asian supply and Chinese producers Shin-Etsu cannot control; because of the Shintech lag, Shintech's own July–September quarter reaches the reported numbers only with the third-quarter results. The stock has lagged the Nikkei 225 by almost 72 percentage points over five years. Management says it will not rush wafer investment, which protects margins but leaves volume growth to rivals, and the yen at ¥157.80 per dollar sits close to the ¥157 now assumed — a stronger yen costs profit.

Industry Commentary
Wafers: from units to contracts
EMERGING TAILWIND

On the July 24 call management described a change in how chips are bought: the market used to be read in units — phones, PCs, cars — but AI behaves like infrastructure, so the business increasingly runs on firm contractual commitments. Its evidence was the 10-year agreement between Micron and GlobalWafers, which management read as customers expecting strong device demand for a long time. Slightly under 10% of 300 mm wafers now go into devices that use two wafers, a share management expects to grow. The constraint is cost: construction, equipment, materials and freight have all risen, and management estimates a new plant would cost more than twice its previous investment — so it will price for that rather than chase volume.

PVC: two markets, one price-setter
ONGOING SWING FACTOR

North American PVC demand is holding up — housing is not strong, but management says industrial, data-center and infrastructure investment are supporting it — and Shintech has passed on price increases there. Asia is different: in April management argued that the price fall was only a temporary setback; by July, inventories in Asia had risen more than expected, demand had weakened and prices had fallen back to earlier levels. Its explanation is supply-side overreaction rather than collapsing demand, and Chinese producers started raising prices again in July. The Plaquemine project is the long-run answer: more in-house ethylene and VCM makes Shintech less dependent on the merchant market for its main raw materials.

Capital returns — bull and bear framing
DEBATE

Shin-Etsu has turned from a cash hoarder into a steady buyer of its own stock: ¥500.0B in FY2025, ¥250B more by July 2026, the January secondary offering to widen its shareholder base, and a 40% payout policy. The bull framing: with about ¥1.35T more cash than debt at September 30 and ROE of 10.4% in FY2025 (12.8% annualised in the first half), every buyback lifts per-share earnings, and management says it weighs ROE and the cost of capital explicitly. The bear framing: buybacks lift EPS without lifting the business — FY2025 net income fell 11% while EPS fell 6% — and the cash is still earning interest income rather than funding wafer capacity, which management says it will add only at prices that justify the doubled cost.
