01 Company Overview

Skydance Corporation is what Paramount became on October 6, 2026, the day it completed its purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery. It now owns two Hollywood studios (Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros.), two streaming services (Paramount+ and HBO Max, which the company says will be merged into a single service over time), the CBS network and HBO, both companies' cable channels — Nickelodeon, MTV and Comedy Central on one side, the Discovery networks on the other — and two national newsrooms, CBS News and CNN. The closing release counts more than 200 million streaming subscribers and calls Skydance a company with nearly $70 billion in revenue. On the pro forma figures filed with the closing 8-K, the two businesses together had $66.1B of revenue in 2025 and $31.6B in the first half of 2026.

The company is the product of two deals in fourteen months. On August 7, 2025, David Ellison's Skydance Media merged with Paramount Global, and the old PARA Class B shares carried on, one for one, as PSKY on Nasdaq. On February 27, 2026, Paramount agreed to buy WBD for $31.00 a share in cash, after outbidding a Netflix deal the WBD board had already signed. Twelve states sued to block it in July, a court paused it, and a September 21 settlement with the states and the Writers Guild cleared the way. At closing, WBD holders received $31.01666668 a share: the $31.00 plus a "ticking" payment for each day past September 30. On the share count implied by the 8-K's ticking total (about 2.51B WBD shares), that is about $78B for WBD's equity (our arithmetic). The same day the company renamed itself Skydance, and its Class B shares moved to the NYSE as SKYD.

It paid with new owners and new debt. The Ellison family, RedBird Capital, the Public Investment Fund (PIF), L'IMAD, the Qatar Investment Authority and LionTree put in $47B for 3.92B new Class B shares at $12.00; the company sold $41.4B of dollar secured notes and €885M of euro notes and took $8.5B and €850M of term loans. The new shares are 78% of the roughly 5.04B now outstanding, so the shares that existed before the deal are about 22% of the company (our arithmetic). At the October 8 close the equity was worth about $46.8B, and the pro forma balance sheet carries $82.5B of debt against $7.9B of cash. Everything that follows comes back to one question: can the combined company grow its cash flow fast enough to carry that debt while its biggest profit source, cable and broadcast TV, shrinks?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Two studios' film & TV libraries CBS and HBO Franchise IP (Star Trek, Top Gun, Mission: Impossible) 200M+ streaming subscribers Multi-year carriage contracts Controlling shareholder with deep pockets

A media company's moat is what it owns, not what it makes this year. Skydance now owns a century of Paramount films, the Warner Bros. library, HBO, a broadcast network with live sports, and franchises people pay to see again. That is Ellison's argument: a company this size can compete with Netflix, Amazon, Apple and Disney as a peer, and it can cut the costs of running two of everything. The weakness is that a library is a stock of value, while the channels that turn it into cash today — cable bundles — are a shrinking flow, and that flow now has to pay interest on $82.5B of debt. More scale bought with borrowed money raises the stakes; it does not widen the moat by itself.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Leverage on a declining base. Pro forma net interest of $3.09B in the first half of 2026 was eighteen times pro forma operating income of $172M. Management's own target is to bring net leverage down to 3.0x only by the end of 2029. The debt is serviced mostly from TV networks whose revenue fell 9% and 17% in Q2.
02
Losses before the savings. On the pro forma figures the combined company lost $6.08B in 2025 and $2.09B in the first half of 2026, after acquisition accounting and the new interest. Standalone Paramount lost $621M over the four quarters to June. The $6B-plus of synergies has to arrive before the pro forma losses turn into profits.
03
The settlement is a five-year rulebook. The states' consent decree sets minimum film counts, at least $300M a year of extra U.S. production spending, $9.5M a year for workforce training and $5M a year for an independent film fund; the company also reimburses up to $40M of the states' legal fees and pays $17.5M to the Writers Guild health fund (8-K, September 30). Each limits the cost-cutting a leveraged buyer usually relies on.
04
Public holders don't vote and were diluted. SKYD Class B shares carry no votes, and the Ellison family controls the voting stock. The new investors — the Ellison family, RedBird, sovereign funds and LionTree — hold about 78% of the shares, issued at $12.00. The warrants give the old public holders a ten-year claim at that same price.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

A note on the series first. One security runs through the whole chart under three tickers. Before August 7, 2025 it is Paramount Global's Class B stock, PARA; old Class B holders could take one PSKY share for each PARA share (or $15 cash, prorated), so the price level carries across that merger without a conversion factor. On October 6, 2026 the same Class B shares were renamed SKYD and moved to the NYSE, with no exchange of shares; Yahoo Finance serves SKYD with the PARA and PSKY history attached, and its monthly closes match the PSKY series to the cent. One caveat: before August 2025, part of each PARA holding carried the $15 cash election, so those closes are not a pure read on the business.

With that said, the five years are brutal. From the October 2021 close of $36.22 to the October 8, 2026 settled close of $9.28, the Class B line lost 74.4% of its price (dividends excluded). It was not a straight line down. The stock fell to the $10–13 range by early 2024 and stayed there until the Skydance deal was done — then a bidding war turned it into a momentum stock. It rallied from $12.57 at the end of July 2025 to $18.92 at the end of September 2025, when the 6-period monthly RSI hit 89.3, the highest reading in the series. Everything since has been the market pricing in the cost of winning. March 2026, the first full month after Paramount won WBD, was down 33.2%; July 2026, the month of the states' suit and the court pause, was down 19.3% to $7.96, the lowest month-end close in the window. September 2026 closed at $10.33, down 5.3%, and October so far is down another 10.2%.

Momentum is soft: the monthly RSI is 41.3. The 10-month moving average is about $10.29 and the 3-month average $10.17, both above the $9.28 price. Over the last twelve months the stock is down 49.8% (from the $18.50 close of October 8, 2025) and 30.7% year-to-date from $13.40; over the same windows SPY rose 15.0% and 13.5% (Yahoo Finance closes, our calculation).

Relative performance

The Class B line is down 74.4% over five years and down 49.8% over one, while SPY gained 15.0% over the last year. The share price no longer tells the size of the company: with 5.04B shares, Skydance's $46.8B of equity at the October 8 close compares with $185.1B for Disney, $297.7B for Netflix and $75.3B for Comcast (our calculations) — and Skydance also carries $82.5B of pro forma debt. Per-share history before October 6 belongs to a company with about 1.12B shares; the same price now buys a smaller slice of a much larger and more indebted business.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The deal is done, financed and cleared, and the uncertainty that hung over the stock for a year is gone. Skydance owns two studios, HBO and CBS, two streamers with more than 200 million subscribers between them, and the Paramount and Warner Bros. libraries, and management targets more than $6B of savings over three years on top of Paramount's own $2.7B programme. Streaming is already growing at both companies (Q2: +9% and +10%) and WBD's streaming margin is near 17%. The new owners paid $12.00 a share, 29% above the October 8 close, and public holders get ten-year warrants at that same price. At 0.71x pro forma sales the market prices a lot of failure; if the company reaches its $10B-plus free cash flow target by 2030, that is about a fifth of today's market value each year.

Bear Case

This is a leveraged bet on legacy media. Pro forma, the company carries $82.5B of debt, paid $3.09B of interest in the first half of 2026 against $172M of operating income, and lost $2.09B. About 72% of segment profit comes from TV networks whose revenue fell 9% and 17% in Q2. The consent decree requires minimum film output and extra production spending for five years, which rules out the cost cuts a leveraged buyer usually depends on. Public shareholders don't vote; the holders eligible for warrants own about 471M shares, under a tenth of the company (our arithmetic), beside the Ellisons and the sovereign funds, and face an average Street target barely above the price, with as many Sells as Holds. The market's verdict on closing week: down 2.56%, then 6.72%.

Top 3 Risks

01
Debt load. $82.5B of pro forma debt at coupons up to 9.125%, and a 3.0x net leverage target not due until the end of 2029. If cable declines faster than streaming and studios grow, interest cover gets tight fast.
02
Integration under a consent decree. Five years of minimum film counts and extra U.S. production spending, while two studios, two streamers and two newsrooms are combined. The synergies must come from elsewhere, and the merged streaming service has yet to be built.
03
Governance and dilution. Non-voting public shares, a controlling family, 3.92B new shares, and 471.3M warrants that add about 9% more if exercised. Minority holders have no say in the next capital decision.
Industry Commentary
Linear TV decline vs. the streaming land-grab
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Every legacy media company has the same problem: the cable bundle that pays the bills is shrinking, and streaming, which is meant to replace it, earns less per viewer. Q2 2026 shows it at both halves of Skydance — network revenue down 9% at Paramount and 17% at WBD, streaming up 9% and 10%. Ellison's answer is scale: combine two libraries, two studios and two streamers, then compete with Netflix, Amazon, Apple and Disney as a peer. Netflix's earlier signed deal for the Warner Bros. studio business and the bidding contest that followed show the industry believes the assets are worth owning. The question is the price, and the debt that paid it.

Regulation, politics and foreign capital
ONGOING HEADWIND

Twelve states sued to stop the deal and settled for a five-year consent decree rather than a divestiture; the Writers Guild settled alongside them. The decree's commitments run for five years and will be watched. The equity behind the deal includes sovereign wealth funds of Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates; the closing release names the Public Investment Fund (PIF), L'IMAD and the Qatar Investment Authority among the investors. Their stakes are non-voting shares, which the FCC cleared with a foreign-ownership waiver on September 17. One company now owns two national newsrooms, CBS News and CNN, under a controlling family; every editorial decision will be read politically.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The most unusual number in this report is $12.00: the price at which the controlling family and its partners bought $47B of new SKYD stock, against a $9.28 market close two sessions later. The bull framing: the best-informed buyers paid 29% above market, and public holders get a ten-year warrant at the same price on top of their shares. The bear framing: the price was the floor of a formula agreed in April, set by a share price that had fallen since February; the investors were contractually committed, and a floor reached through a falling stock says little about value. The way to decide is the first two combined quarters: EBITDA against the two companies' separate run-rates plus synergies, and whether the debt starts coming down.
