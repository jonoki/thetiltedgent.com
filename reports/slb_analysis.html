01 Company Overview

SLB is the world's largest oilfield services (OFS) company — the contractor that exploration & production (E&P) operators hire to find, drill, complete, and produce oil and gas wells. It sells measurement and evaluation services, drilling systems, subsea and surface production hardware, production chemicals, and a growing software/AI stack, across roughly 100 countries. FY2025 revenue was $35.71B, down 1.6% YoY, with GAAP EPS of $2.35 (−24%) and adjusted EPS of $2.93 (−14%); TTM revenue through June 2026 is $36.37B (+2.5%).

At a $79.48B market capitalization SLB is the #162 most valuable US-listed company and #323 globally, and it is worth more than Baker Hughes ($64.01B) and Halliburton ($29.18B) individually. The business is deliberately barbelled: an international/offshore franchise generating $27.94B (78% of FY2025 revenue) that is levered to long-cycle national-oil-company and deepwater spending, against a smaller North American position ($7.52B) that has been repositioned away from weak US land pressure pumping and toward production chemicals and artificial lift via the July 2025 ChampionX acquisition. Two structurally different growth engines sit on top: a Digital division carrying ~28% operating margins, and a fast-scaling Data Center Solutions business inside "All Other."

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
100-Country Footprint NOC Relationships Subsurface Data Archive Integrated Project Delivery Delfi / Lumi Switching Costs Subsea Installed Base R&D + Patent Depth

SLB's moat is logistical and relational before it is technological. Operating in roughly 100 countries with local content, customs, HSE certification and crew qualification already in place is a barrier that takes a decade and hundreds of millions of dollars to replicate — which is why national oil companies in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Brazil, and India award multi-year integrated contracts to a shortlist of three firms rather than tendering line items. On top of that sits a subsurface data asset: a century of well logs, formation evaluation, and petrophysical interpretation that is the training substrate for Delfi and Lumi, and that a software-native entrant simply cannot buy. Once an operator standardizes its subsurface workflows on Delfi, the switching cost is a re-training and re-validation exercise across a whole exploration organization, which is why ARR grew 15% YoY through a downcycle. Finally, subsea trees and control systems create a 20-year aftermarket annuity attached to the installed base. The counterweight is that in the highest-volume, lowest-differentiation lines — North American pressure pumping, commodity drilling fluids — the moat is thin to nonexistent, which is exactly why SLB has been retreating from them.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Customer concentration in state actors and geopolitical single points of failure. Middle East & Asia was $12.22B of FY2025 revenue, and a regional conflict cut Middle East revenue 38% sequentially to $1.66B in Q2 2026 — a swing SLB could neither hedge nor price around. Management expects only a gradual recovery to ~95% of Q4 2025 levels by year-end, and flagged a downside scenario that would trim a further $150M from Q3. When a handful of NOCs set your activity level, budget politics and armed conflict are operating variables, not tail risks.

2. Late-cycle dependence on E&P capital budgets that are being deliberately suppressed. Global upstream capex was roughly $420B in 2025 (−2.5%) and Wood Mackenzie expects a further 2–3% decline in 2026, leaving spending more than 5% below 2024 with sub-$60/bbl oil. Service companies are price-takers on volume. SLB's TTM operating margin has already fallen from 17.55% in FY2024 to 14.16%, and ROIC from mid-teens toward 11.59%.

3. Commoditization pressure and Chinese/regional competition. In wireline, drilling fluids and pressure pumping, regional players — including China Oilfield Services — compete credibly on price in exactly the markets SLB most wants to grow. Reservoir Performance's Q2 2026 operating margin of 14.9% against 18.3% for FY2025 is a live pricing signal, not a mix artifact.

4. Integration and dilution risk from ChampionX. The deal was struck largely in stock: shares outstanding rose 7.57% YoY, producing a negative buyback yield of −7.57% and a shareholder yield of −5.37% despite $2.41B of repurchases in FY2025. Purchase accounting (intangible amortization, inventory step-up) is a material drag on GAAP EPS — Q2 2026 GAAP EPS of $0.52 versus adjusted $0.55, and FY2025 GAAP $2.35 versus adjusted $2.93. The chemicals business also imports raw-material cost inflation SLB did not previously carry.

Notable One-Off Items. Several distortions matter for anyone comparing GAAP and adjusted figures:

ChampionX purchase accounting. The acquisition closed in July 2025 (Q3 2025), adding production chemicals and artificial lift. It contributed $1.46B of FY2025 revenue ($879M in Q4 2025 alone, of which $874M went to Production Systems and $28M to Digital) and $870M in Q2 2026. Because it was substantially stock-funded, shares outstanding rose 7.57% YoY — a real per-share headwind that shows up as a −7.57% buyback yield despite $2.41B of FY2025 repurchases and $648M (12M shares) in Q2 2026. Intangible amortization and inventory step-up sit inside the GAAP-to-adjusted bridge: FY2025 GAAP EPS $2.35 vs adjusted $2.93 (a $0.58 gap, ~20% of GAAP EPS), and Q2 2026 GAAP $0.52 vs adjusted $0.55.

Antitrust-driven divestitures. The FY2025 results release did not itemize material divestiture gains or losses tied to obtaining ChampionX clearance. Treat any specific divestiture figure you see elsewhere as unverified against the primary release; SLB did not flag one as material.

Restructuring, impairments and Russia-legacy items. No material impairment or restructuring charge was highlighted in either the FY2025 or Q2 2026 results releases, and no Russia-exit item was separately disclosed in those releases. The persistent GAAP-vs-adjusted gap (roughly $0.03/share in Q2 2026, $0.58/share for FY2025) is therefore dominated by acquisition accounting rather than by write-downs. Anyone underwriting this name should verify the charges-and-credits schedule in the 10-Q directly — the summary releases do not fully itemize it.

Bolt-on M&A. SLB acquired Tachyus Corp., a Houston reservoir-modeling technology firm, during Q2 2026 — small, but consistent with buying digital capability rather than building it.

Plain English: RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been. Above 70 the stock has run hot and may be due a pause; below 30 it has been beaten down and may be due a bounce. It is a timing gauge, not a valuation tool.

Five-year trajectory. On a dividend-adjusted basis SLB went from a $26.67 close in September 2021 to $53.55 on August 19, 2026 — a total return of roughly +101%, about 14.9% annualized. That headline hides two completely different regimes. From late 2021 through August 2023 the stock more than doubled (to $54.64) as the post-COVID restart, the 2022 energy shock and a genuine international upcycle drove revenue from $22.93B (2021) to $33.14B (2023). From that peak it then de-rated for nearly two years to a $31.90 monthly close in May 2025 — a 42% drawdown — as E&P capital discipline, sub-$60 oil and a collapsing US land market pushed FY2025 revenue down 1.6% and GAAP EPS down 24%. The last eight months are regime three: a violent re-rating from $37.96 (Dec 2025) to $56.58 (Apr 2026), driven not by the oil price but by ChampionX consolidation, digital margin expansion, and the emergence of Data Center Solutions. The June 2026 drop to $46.49 on Middle East disruption and the subsequent recovery to $53.55 show how much of the move is now headline-driven.

Relative performance. Over the trailing 52 weeks SLB returned +60.8% against +20.9% total return for the S&P 500 (SPY) — roughly 40 points of outperformance. Against oilfield-services peers the picture is far less flattering: HAL +65.1%, NOV +71.4%, WFRD +64.9%, BKR +50.4% over comparable windows. In other words SLB has been a sector trade, not an alpha trade: it is roughly middle-of-the-pack within its own industry, and the entire relative gain versus the index came from the OFS group re-rating together off a deeply depressed base. The stock still sits below its August 2023 adjusted peak on a five-year chart that has produced two 40%+ drawdowns — a reminder that this is a high-beta business regardless of what the 5-year measured Beta of 0.75 suggests.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

SLB is being repriced from a rig-count derivative into a diversified energy-infrastructure and industrial-software company. Digital carries ~28% operating margins with $1.04B of ARR growing 15%, Data Center Solutions is compounding at 80% YoY toward a >$2B run rate by end-2027, and ChampionX adds opex-funded chemicals and artificial lift revenue tied to producing barrels rather than new wells. Meanwhile deepwater FIDs are up 30% YoY, offshore could grow double digits in 2027, and Middle East activity is recovering off a conflict-depressed base. Forward P/E of 18.91 against consensus 2027 EPS of $3.23 (+29%) with a 2.20% dividend, a 0.47 debt-to-equity ratio and >$4B of committed 2026 capital returns is a reasonable price for that combination.

Bear Case

Strip out the acquisition and the business is shrinking: ex-ChampionX, Q2 2026 revenue fell 5% YoY. Operating margin has gone from 17.55% (FY2024) to 14.16% TTM, ROIC from mid-teens to 11.59%, and GAAP EPS is down 29.5%. Two of the four service segments — Well Construction (−7% YoY) and Reservoir Performance (−8% YoY, margin down to 14.9%) — are contracting with pricing pressure, and they are 51% of revenue. Global upstream capex is forecast to fall another 2–3% in 2026 under sub-$60 oil, so the demand pool is still eroding. The stock has already risen 61% in a year to 25.97x trailing earnings — a 33% premium to peers — on a data-center narrative that is currently under 3% of revenue. 87% of analysts rate it Buy, which is a crowded position, not a margin of safety.

01
Middle East concentration and geopolitical disruption. Middle East & Asia was $12.22B of FY2025 revenue; conflict cut Middle East revenue 38% sequentially to $1.66B in Q2 2026 and management has already flagged a scenario removing another $150M from Q3. Recovery timing is explicitly "uncertain," and there is no operational or financial hedge against it.
02
E&P capital discipline caps the addressable spend. Global upstream capex was ~$420B in 2025 and Wood Mackenzie projects a 2–3% decline in 2026, leaving spending >5% below 2024 levels. Operators are prioritizing returns over volume growth at sub-$60/bbl. Service pricing follows spending with a lag, so margin pressure in Reservoir Performance and Well Construction can persist even if oil rallies.
03
Narrative-driven multiple risk on Data Center Solutions. A meaningful part of the 61% twelve-month re-rating rests on a business that is not yet at a $1B run rate inside a $36B company, competing against established power and industrial infrastructure providers. Any slip in the Meta campus timeline or in the >$1B/>$2B run-rate milestones removes the justification for SLB's premium to HAL and BKR and exposes the underlying ex-acquisition revenue decline.
  Industry Commentary
International & Offshore Upcycle vs. Weak North American Land
ONGOING HEADWIND
The market has bifurcated. Offshore is genuinely improving — deepwater FIDs +30% YoY, Brazil pushing toward 4.0 mb/d on pre-salt, Guyana toward 1+ mb/d on Stabroek expansion, and management guiding to possible double-digit offshore growth in 2027. North American land is the opposite: sub-$60/bbl oil, capital-disciplined shale operators, and Wood Mackenzie flagging North America and Europe as the regions where cuts are offsetting gains elsewhere. SLB's positioning is the right side of this trade — 78% international, and its NAM growth (+36% YoY in Q2) comes from ChampionX chemicals rather than drilling. But note the asymmetry: ex-ChampionX global revenue still fell 5% YoY, so offshore strength is not yet large enough to offset onshore and Middle East weakness.
E&P Capital Discipline Caps Service Demand
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
This is the binding constraint on the whole sector. Global upstream investment was roughly $420B in 2025 (−2.5%) and is forecast down another 2–3% in 2026, more than 5% below 2024, with sub-$60/bbl crude as the operating assumption (Wood Mackenzie). Non-OPEC supply still grows ~1.5% because operators are getting more barrels per dollar — which is precisely the problem for service companies, since efficiency gains are deflationary for service revenue. Oilfield services is late-cycle and capex-dependent: SLB's revenue is a derivative of a spending pool that is shrinking, and no amount of share gain fixes a contracting TAM. It shows up directly in the numbers — operating margin 17.55% (FY2024) → 14.16% (TTM), ROIC → 11.59%.
Digital & AI in Oilfield Operations — and the Bull/Bear Frame
EMERGING TAILWIND
Digital and AI adoption is an identified top-5 upstream theme for 2026 (S&P Global), and SLB is the clear scale player: Delfi, Lumi, Agora and Tela underpin $1.04B of ARR growing 15% YoY at a 34.7% EBITDA margin, plus a Data Center Solutions business growing 80% YoY. Bull framing: if Digital plus All Other reach ~$6–7B of combined revenue at ~28% margins by 2028, roughly 20% of SLB's revenue would carry software-like economics and near-zero oil-price correlation — which would justify a permanent re-rating above HAL and BKR and change what the trough of the next cycle looks like. Bear framing: those two lines are 12.6% of FY2025 revenue today, All Other actually declined 6% in FY2025, and the 51% of revenue in Well Construction and Reservoir Performance is shrinking with margin compression. Buy the second story and 18.91x forward earnings is cheap; buy the first and you are paying 25.97x trailing for a business whose core is still contracting.
