01 Company Overview

SanDisk Corporation is a NAND flash memory and storage company and does nothing else — NAND is the non-volatile ("keeps data when the power is off") memory inside SSDs (solid-state drives), memory cards and USB drives. It designs and, through a long-running manufacturing joint venture with Japan's Kioxia, fabricates 3D NAND wafers, then sells them as raw components, client and enterprise SSDs, and retail-branded storage. SanDisk was carved out of Western Digital on February 21, 2025 as a standalone NASDAQ-listed company, so its entire public trading record spans about twenty months.

In the quarter to October 2, 2026 (fiscal Q1 2027) revenue was $11.03B, up 378% YoY and 23% on the previous quarter, with a gross margin of 85.0% and GAAP net income of $8.10B. Fiscal 2026 (ended July 3, 2026) revenue was $20.25B, up 175% from $7.36B, on a 71.5% gross margin. The growth is mostly price: the 10-Q says Edge revenue per gigabyte rose more than 450% on the year, while total exabytes (billions of gigabytes) sold rose almost 10%. By revenue SanDisk was the fifth-largest NAND supplier in 2Q26 (TrendForce: about $8.97B, up 50.7% on the quarter, behind Samsung, the SK hynix group, Micron and Kioxia). At a $160.3B market capitalisation it ranks #74 in the S&P 500 and #34 in the Nasdaq-100. The shares are up 374.6% in 2026 ($237.38 at the December 31 close to $1,126.52 on November 3) but 51.8% below the record close of $2,335.00 on June 25.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Kioxia JV Fab Scale BiCS 3D NAND Co-Development Yokkaichi + Kitakami $88.9B Contracted Backlog Enterprise SSD Qualifications SanDisk Retail Brand Zero Long-Term Debt

SanDisk's real moat is Flash Ventures, the manufacturing joint venture it has run with Kioxia (formerly Toshiba Memory) since 2000. The two companies jointly own and fund the Yokkaichi and Kitakami fabs in Japan and split the output. This is a genuinely unusual cost structure: SanDisk gets roughly half the scale economics of a top-two NAND producer while carrying only about half the wafer capex, and it shares R&D on the BiCS 3D NAND roadmap with a partner rather than funding a node transition alone. The result is visible in the numbers: gross capital spending (the company's measure, well above the $43M of equipment on its own books) was 6.3% of fiscal Q4 2026 revenue and management expects about 6% for FY2027 (Q4 call), while its long-term goal is to grow supply by a mid-to-high-teens percentage a year, mainly by moving to newer chip generations (BiCS8 → BiCS10). Layered on top of that is a genuinely new contractual moat: eight New Business Model customers have signed multi-year agreements with floor-and-ceiling pricing that management said in August cover more than half of FY2027 bits and about two-thirds of FY2028 bits; by October 2 the contracted revenue not yet recognised had reached $88.9B. If those contracts hold, SanDisk has turned a commodity into something closer to a utility with contracted volumes. If.

Key Vulnerabilities
▸
Commodity price exposure is the entire P&L. Two-thirds of fiscal Q4 2026's sequential growth came from price, not volume, and in Q1 FY27 Edge revenue per gigabyte was up more than 450% on the year. NAND is an interchangeable product with five large suppliers; the FY2026 gross margin swing of +41 points and the FY2023 operating loss of $2.04B are the same business under different pricing. A 30% fall in prices would cut Q1 FY27's $8.78B of operating income by about 38%, to roughly $5.47B (our arithmetic: revenue 30% lower, costs unchanged).
▸
No vertical integration, no DRAM, no captive demand. Samsung and SK hynix sell DRAM, HBM and NAND into the same customers and can cross-subsidise a NAND price war indefinitely; Samsung also consumes its own flash in phones. SanDisk is a single-product company with roughly half of one fab complex. It has no second leg to stand on if NAND alone rolls over, and no captive internal demand to absorb bits.
▸
The JV cuts both ways. Flash Ventures means shared capacity decisions, shared capital-spending obligations, and accounting that keeps the ventures off SanDisk's consolidated books (the equity method), which makes the true cost of each chip harder to see from the outside. SanDisk will pay Kioxia about $1.2B for manufacturing services and supply across 2026–2029, and the Flash Ventures agreements now run to December 31, 2034 (FY2026 10-K). Kioxia is simultaneously a partner and a direct competitor at 13.6% share (TrendForce, 2Q26) — the two co-develop BiCS and then fight for the same enterprise SSD sockets. Any change in Kioxia's ownership or strategy is a first-order risk to SanDisk's cost base.
▸
NBM contracts concentrate risk in a few customers. Ten agreements with eight customers underwrite $93.9B of minimum revenue (August 2026), and two customers were each 12% of Q1 FY27 revenue (10-Q). That ties SanDisk to a handful of AI infrastructure buyers whose own spending plans can change. SanDisk held $16.5B of deposits and guarantees against default in August — a sensible precaution that also tells you the company thinks default is a live scenario. Contract floors are only as good as the customer behind them.

Notable One-Off Items

Several distortions matter when reading these numbers. (1) Spin-off accounting. FY2025 was SanDisk's first year as a standalone entity and carried separation costs, costs left behind by the split and a $1.64B GAAP net loss; year-over-year growth rates are therefore measured off an artificially depressed base and overstate the underlying improvement. (2) GAAP above non-GAAP. Q1 FY27 GAAP net income of $8.10B exceeded non-GAAP net income of $7.62B (GAAP EPS $52.60 against $49.51), as it did in fiscal 2026. The cause is a $513M net gain on equity securities in the quarter, which GAAP counts and the non-GAAP figures exclude (Q1 FY27 release reconciliation). (3) Joint-venture accounting. Flash Ventures sits outside SanDisk's consolidated accounts, which is why the reported asset base and ROIC look far better than the economics of a company that depends on those fabs; the 10-Q puts SanDisk's maximum loss exposure to the ventures at $2.94B at October 2. (4) Inventory revaluation. A rising-price environment mechanically flatters gross margin as low-cost inventory is sold into high-priced contracts — and reverses just as mechanically when prices fall. (5) Buybacks and the split. $5.0B of Q1 FY27 repurchases (about 3.2M shares) cut the count to 142.3M shares outstanding on October 26, while Q1 diluted shares were 154M and Q2 is guided to about 151M. Every per-share figure on this page is before the 8-for-1 split; after it, divide by eight.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

AI infrastructure has changed NAND's demand curve: management expects datacenter to go from ~30% to ~50% of the market in a single year, and high-capacity QLC SSDs are taking over workloads hard drives cannot serve. SanDisk has turned that demand into $88.9B of contracted revenue not yet recognised at October 2 (10-Q), from long-term agreements with floor prices that management says cover more than half of FY2027 bits. The Kioxia JV lets it grow bits mid-to-high-teens by moving to newer chip generations while spending about 6% of revenue on equipment (management's FY2027 outlook), so most extra revenue becomes profit. On $17.4B of trailing free cash flow, no debt and $10.5B of buyback authorisation left, about 5x the FY2027 consensus EPS is not demanding if the contracts hold, and the shares are already 52% below their June peak. The bull case is simply that this is no longer a commodity business.

Bear Case

Every NAND up-cycle in history has ended the same way, and this one is more extreme than any of them: an 85.0% gross margin in a business that earned 7.1% three years ago, with growth coming mostly from price (Edge revenue per gigabyte up more than 450% on the year against exabytes up almost 10%). Extraordinary returns are precisely what triggers capacity — Samsung, SK hynix, Kioxia, Micron and YMTC all have the balance sheets to add wafers, and decisions made now become bits in 2027–28. The contract "floors" have never been tested through a downturn, and two customers were each 12% of Q1 revenue. At $160B the market already prices these profits at about 9x trailing earnings, a multiple that says it expects them to fall, and the shares dropped 13.5% on a beat-and-raise quarter. A fall in gross margin to 30–40% at today's revenue would cut operating income by roughly 55–70% (our arithmetic on Q1 FY27). July (−46.6%) and October (−33.9%) showed how fast the exit gets crowded.

Top 3 Risks
01
Cycle roll-over. NAND pricing is the single variable that determines whether SanDisk earns $200 per share or loses money. The FY2023 → FY2026 swing — from a $2.04B operating loss on 7.1% gross margin to $12.4B of operating income on 71.5% — shows how far it can go. There is no version of this business that is defensive.
02
Capacity additions across the industry. TrendForce says suppliers are putting capital spending into DRAM and HBM first, limiting new NAND capacity, which is why the shortage persists. But five suppliers are all earning record returns at once, and the industry's history of responding to that with wafer additions is essentially unbroken. Watch supplier capex guidance and any new fab announcement — including from China's YMTC, which competes on price rather than return on capital.
03
Contract and customer risk in the NBM model. The entire "this time is different" argument rests on a handful of long-term agreements (ten with eight customers at the August count). If AI infrastructure spending slows, customers will renegotiate, contracted volumes will be disputed, and the floor pricing will be tested for the first time. Concentration cuts both ways: a few customers can de-risk a cycle or become the cycle.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Drives This Stock
NAND Supercycle Economics
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Making NAND is mostly fixed cost: the fabs cost about the same to run whether prices are high or low, so profits swing with the balance between the bits (storage capacity) the industry can make and the bits customers want. In 2Q26 the top five suppliers grew revenue 77% on the quarter to $68.87B (TrendForce), on AI-server demand for enterprise SSDs and rising prices. SanDisk's own numbers tell the same story: in Q1 FY27 Edge revenue per gigabyte was up more than 450% on the year against total exabytes up almost 10%. The corollary is unforgiving — the FY2023 downturn took gross margin to 7.1% and produced a $2.14B net loss. This is a business whose earnings power is set almost entirely outside its own control.
AI Storage: HDD → SSD Substitution
EMERGING TAILWIND
The genuinely new element in this cycle is demand-side, not supply-side. AI training and inference generate data lakes that need capacity and throughput; shortages of high-capacity hard drives have pushed cloud giants toward high-capacity QLC enterprise SSDs as a substitute. That is why datacenter's share of the NAND market moves from ~30% in CY2025 to ~50% in CY2026 and why SanDisk's datacenter revenue grew 437% to $5.15B in fiscal 2026 and reached $4.80B in the September quarter alone — 43.5% of revenue, up from 12% a year earlier. Unlike price, this driver has a real physical basis — but it also depends on AI capex continuing, and on HDD supply staying short.
Supply Discipline — Bull vs. Bear
ONGOING HEADWIND
Bull framing: after SanDisk's three loss-making years (FY2023–FY2025) the industry has changed behaviour. TrendForce says suppliers are putting capital spending into DRAM and HBM first, limiting new NAND capacity; SanDisk is growing bits mid-to-high teens by moving from BiCS8 to BiCS10 while spending about 6% of revenue on equipment, and management said in August that bits stay on allocation beyond calendar 2027. Long-term contracts lock in that discipline on both sides. Bear framing: discipline is a function of profitability, and profitability is currently the highest in the industry's history. Five suppliers with record cash flow, a Chinese entrant indifferent to returns, and a 2027–28 demand forecast that assumes AI capex compounds — this is the exact set-up that has preceded every NAND crash. The question is not whether capacity comes; it is whether it arrives before or after the NBM contracts have paid for themselves.
