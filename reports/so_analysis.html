01 Company Overview

The Southern Company is one of the largest regulated electric and gas utility holding companies in the United States, serving retail and wholesale customers across Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and, through Southern Company Gas, natural gas distribution territories in Illinois, Georgia, Virginia and Tennessee. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $29.55B, up 10.59% YoY, with TTM revenue of $31.20B (+7.91%) and TTM EPS of $4.38. At a $107.48B market capitalization the company ranks #107 in the S&P 500, and it is the largest utility focused on regulated service in the Southeast by market value — ahead of Duke Energy ($99.63B) and well ahead of AEP ($68.90B).

What makes Southern unusual among regulated utilities right now is where it operates. Its Georgia and Alabama franchise territory sits in the middle of one of the fastest-growing data-center corridors in the country. In Q3 2026, data-center usage on Southern's system grew 59% YoY (52% year to date), and the company added about 4 GW of new large-load contracts (agreements to supply very large users such as data centers) since its Q2 call, bringing signed contracts to 21 GW through the mid-2030s, with another 7 GW in late stages and a prospective pipeline of over 75 GW. Weather-normalized retail electricity sales rose 3.5% in the quarter — the company's slides call it the highest in recent history, with growth across all customer classes and companies.

Georgia Power is the centre of gravity for the entire investment case. In Q3 2026 its revenue rose 18.6% to $4.47B and its net income rose 22.0% to $1.52B. It is a vertically integrated, rate-regulated monopoly overseen by the Georgia Public Service Commission (PSC), and it serves the Atlanta metro plus the coastal corridor around Savannah, where data-center building by the largest cloud and AI companies (hyperscalers) has concentrated. Its 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) — the triennial long-range supply plan every vertically integrated utility must file — was approved by the Georgia PSC on July 15, 2025, and forecasts roughly 8,500 MW of load growth over six years and a ~2,600 MW increase in peak demand by the end of 2030 versus the 2023 forecast. Approved additions include capacity upgrades (uprates) at existing nuclear units (including 54 MW from Vogtle Units 1 and 2), 268 MW of gas at Plant McIntosh, more than 1,500 MW of battery energy storage (BESS), 1,100 MW of initial renewable procurement scaling toward up to 4,000 MW by 2035, and more than 1,000 miles of new transmission. On July 28, 2026 Georgia Power broke ground on two combined-cycle gas units at Plant Bowen adding nearly 1,500 MW plus a 500 MW battery, supported by a US Department of Energy loan package of up to $26.5 billion. The company files quarterly Large Load Economic Development Reports with the PSC, which is the single most useful public dataset for tracking whether the AI demand story is turning into real contracts.

Southern's moat is the classic regulated-utility moat — an exclusive, state-granted franchise to serve a defined territory, with rates set to allow recovery of prudently incurred costs plus an authorized return on rate base — but it is currently being amplified by something genuinely scarce. The limit on AI infrastructure in 2026 is not capital or chips; it is deliverable, firm electric power on a known schedule. Southern controls grid connections and generation in a corridor where hyperscalers want to build, and it is the largest owner of the newest large-scale nuclear units in the United States: Plant Vogtle Units 3 and 4, two Westinghouse AP1000 reactors in which Georgia Power holds a 45.7% ownership interest (Oglethorpe Power 30%, MEAG Power 22.7%, Dalton Utilities 1.6%). Unit 4 was connected to the grid on March 1, 2024 and both units are now in service, supplying carbon-free, round-the-clock power of exactly the kind hyperscale customers are willing to pay a premium for. The commercial terms reinforce the moat: large-load contracts carry minimum bills covering at least 100% of the incremental cost to serve, long tenors (at least 15 years for data centers; the OpenAI agreement runs 25 years), and about $25 billion of collateral required by 2030 on the 17 GW contracted since mid-2025 — structures designed so that the customer, not the ratepayer or the shareholder, bears the risk if the load fails to show up.

Key Vulnerabilities

The risk that expected demand never arrives is documented, not hypothetical. Georgia PSC staff testimony filed in late 2025 found that data centers were "primarily underperforming expectations due to a mixture of lower materialization rates, project cancellations, and delays." Since 2023, 33 data-center projects totalling 11,332 MW were removed from Georgia Power's queue, representing 55% of all project removals. In Q3 2025 the total pipeline actually shrank by a net 6 GW to 50.9 GW: about 6.8 GW of new projects came in and existing projects added 1.6 GW, while 14.3 GW left. The headline 75 GW pipeline figure should therefore be read as the top of a funnel, not a forecast; contracted GW is the number that matters. Asked about that funnel on the Q3 2026 call, CEO Chris Womack said: "Now are we going to land 75 gigawatts of projects? Probably not."

Delayed recovery and stranded costs (spending left with no customers to pay it back). Georgia and Alabama base rates are expected to be held stable through 2029, which is excellent politics and mediocre economics: Southern is deploying an $85 billion capital plan through 2030 ($81B base plus about $4B of identified projects) into a rate base whose recovery is deferred. PSC staff flagged in that testimony that beyond about 1,900 MW backed by executed large-load contracts, the rest of the new generation Georgia Power wanted to certify was "speculative and exposes customers to the risk of stranded costs." A commission that later finds new generation imprudent can disallow recovery — as the $19M of Nicor Gas capital disallowances year to date illustrates in miniature.

Balance-sheet strain and the equity/rate arithmetic. Debt-to-equity is 1.68 versus a 1.63 average for the five regulated peers reviewed here, total debt is $75.50B, and free cash flow is -$3.76B on TTM capex of $14.24B. The company targets FFO-to-debt of about 17% by 2029 and says about $2B of equity is still to be raised through 2030, up from $1.1B at Q2, and plans to fund additional investment with about 40% equity. Every percentage-point move in long-term interest rates changes both the cost of that funding and what investors will pay for a stock valued largely on its steady payout.

The politics of who pays for AI power. Southern joined the "Ratepayer Protection Pledge" in July 2026 and has emphasised rate stability, but the underlying tension is unresolved across the industry: residential customers increasingly object to funding grid expansion driven by hyperscalers. Advocacy groups including the Southern Environmental Law Center have publicly challenged both the fossil capacity approved in the IRP and the adequacy of customer protections in Georgia Power's data-center framework. On the Q3 2026 call Womack acknowledged "a lot of conversation about potentially pausing" data-center development in Georgia, said there has "not been any talk about retroactive or looking backwards," and described the company's stance as "wait and see." Adverse tariff redesign — shifting more cost to shareholders or capping large-load returns — is the unlikely but severe outcome that would most directly damage the bull case.

Notable One-Off Items

The gap between Q3 2026 GAAP EPS of $1.77 (basic) and adjusted EPS of $1.94 is 17 cents, and almost all of it is two disclosed items, which investors should model explicitly:

Wind repowering accelerated depreciation (Southern Power). $118M pre-tax / $92M after-tax in Q3 2026, 8 cents a share, and $414M pre-tax / $322M after-tax year to date. The release projects about $100M pre-tax more in 2026 and about $135M in 2027, with the projects finishing by the third quarter of 2027.

Nicor Gas capital investment disallowances. $9M pre-tax / $7M after-tax in Q3; $19M pre-tax / $14M after-tax year to date. Small in dollars, but a live example of regulatory recovery risk on deployed capital.

Debt extinguishment costs. $43M pre-tax / $40M after-tax in Q3, from repurchasing convertible notes and redeeming junior subordinated notes, plus $57M from a change in tax benefits on 2025's debt extinguishment: 8 cents a share in total. Year to date the pre-tax cost is $54M.

Asset-sale tail item. A $9M state income tax refund ($7M after federal tax) at Southern Company Gas relating to the 2018 sale of Elizabethtown Gas, partly offset by $2M of related fees, all year to date with no effect on the third quarter.

Vogtle Units 3 & 4 tax remeasurement. A modest $4M tax charge year to date and nothing in the quarter. Notably, the enormous Vogtle construction charges that dominated Southern's non-GAAP reconciliations from 2018 through 2023 have now largely run their course; the remaining Vogtle items are tax true-ups rather than cost overruns.

Storm cost recovery: no storm-cost deferral or recovery item appears in the adjusted-earnings reconciliation of the Q3 2026 earnings release (Form 8-K, November 5, 2026). Southeast utilities routinely carry storm reserves and securitized storm recovery balances, so the absence of a Q3 2026 disclosure should not be read as an absence of exposure.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Southern controls the grid connections and generation that can run on demand in one of the fastest-growing large-load corridors in the US, with 21 GW contracted, a 75+ GW funnel, minimum bills covering at least 100% of incremental cost to serve and about $25B of collateral required by 2030. Weather-normalized retail sales grew 3.5% in Q3 — the company calls it the highest in recent history, with growth across every customer class and company — and data-center usage was up 59% YoY. An $85B capital plan through 2030, which management says could approach $100B, gives visible rate-base growth, while a 3.29% yield with 25 years of dividend increases pays you to wait. At a 21.08 trailing P/E — close to the peer average and below the S&P 500 — with a 2026 outlook just raised to $4.70, the bull case says the load growth is not yet in the price.

Bear Case

The pipeline is a funnel, not a backlog. Georgia PSC staff documented that data centers are "primarily underperforming expectations," with 33 projects totalling 11,332 MW removed since 2023 and the total pipeline shrinking by about 6 GW in a single quarter, and asked about the 75 GW on the Q3 call, the CEO said "Probably not." Meanwhile Southern is spending $14.24B a year in capex against $10.48B of operating cash flow — -$3.76B free cash flow — funded on the second most leveraged balance sheet in its peer group (1.68 debt-to-equity, $75.50B total debt) into a rate base whose base-rate recovery is frozen until at least 2029. Warmer weather added 17 cents to the quarter's adjusted EPS, a boost that does not carry forward, and talk of pausing data-center development in Georgia is a political risk to future load growth. With 12 Holds and 3 Sells against 8 Buys and a low target of $74, analysts as a group are saying the assets are fine and the price is not.

Top 3 Risks
01
Large-load attrition. The gap between 75 GW of prospective pipeline and 21 GW contracted is where the entire valuation debate lives, and the historical conversion record is poor — 11,332 MW of Georgia data-center projects removed since 2023, representing 55% of all removals. If contracted GW stops growing, Southern is left holding an $85B capital plan built for demand that did not arrive, and the regulatory question becomes who pays for it.
02
Interest-rate and refinancing sensitivity. With $75.50B of total debt, an enterprise value of $181.24B against a $107.48B market cap, negative free cash flow and a 0.29 beta, SO behaves like a long-term bond. Long-term interest rates that stay high reduce what investors will pay for its earnings and raise the cost of the very capital the growth plan depends on. The Sep–Oct 2022 drawdown of 15% in two months and the Oct–Dec 2024 giveback of 9.6% both came with rising rates and no company-specific trigger.
03
Regulatory and political recovery risk. Base rates in Georgia and Alabama are to be held flat until at least 2029, deferring recovery on a massive build. Commissions can and do disallow costs — the $19M of year-to-date Nicor Gas capital disallowances is a small live example — and public pressure to shield residential ratepayers from AI-driven grid costs is intensifying, with the Southern Environmental Law Center already challenging the adequacy of customer protections in Georgia Power's data-center framework and talk of pausing data-center development in Georgia. Adverse tariff redesign is the unlikely but severe outcome that would most directly break the bull case.
  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Load Growth in the Southeast
EMERGING TAILWIND

US electricity demand was structurally flat for roughly fifteen years; it is not flat any more, and the Southeast is where the turn is sharpest. Southern's data-center usage grew 59% YoY in Q3 2026 and is up 52% year to date; commercial sales rose 7.3% in the quarter on a weather-adjusted basis. Georgia Power's approved 2025 IRP forecasts approximately 8,500 MW of load growth over six years and a ~2,600 MW increase in peak demand by end-2030 versus the 2023 plan. Southern added about 4 GW of large-load contracts since its Q2 call, reaching 21 GW, with about 7 GW more in late stages. The OpenAI agreement — 3.2 GW over 25 years near Savannah with 1 GW of flexible demand response — is the marquee validation. Critically, this is regulated load: it converts into rate base and authorized returns rather than earnings that swing with wholesale power prices. Peers are chasing the same trend (AEP announced a $100B five-year plan through 2031 on Oct 28, 2026), which is itself evidence that the demand is real rather than a Southern-specific story.

Rate Base Growth, Heavy Spending and Delayed Recovery
ONGOING HEADWIND

Regulated utilities earn on rate base, so growth requires spending — and the spending comes first. Southern's five-year capital plan went from $76B to $81B in February 2026 and to about $85B at the Q3 2026 results, and management says further opportunities could push it toward $100B. The cost is visible in the cash statement: TTM capex of $14.24B against $10.48B of operating cash flow, producing -$3.76B of free cash flow, funded by $75.50B of debt at a 1.68 debt-to-equity ratio — the second highest of the six-name peer set, though the -$3.76B FCF is the best of the group (peer average -$8.32B), which tells you the entire sector is in the same position. The delay before spending can be charged to customers makes it worse in the near term: with Georgia and Alabama base rates held flat until at least 2029, capital goes into the ground years before it earns a full return. Management's target of about 17% FFO-to-debt by 2029 and its remaining equity need of about $2B through 2030 are the two credit metrics to monitor; the $2.731B of convertible notes issued in August 2026 shows the company actively managing that funding gap.

Interest Rates, Utility Valuations, and the Bull/Bear Frame
SETS WHAT INVESTORS PAY

A 0.29-beta stock yielding 3.29% with about 69% of earnings paid out and $75.50B of debt behaves much like a long-term bond with an added upside bet on load growth. The five-year price series makes this explicit: two of the sharpest month-end falls (Sep–Oct 2022, -15%; Oct–Dec 2024, -9.6%) came with rising interest rates and no company-specific trigger. The bull framing: if long-term rates fall, investors tend to pay more for steady earnings, and an accelerating earnings base plus a yield roughly three times the S&P 500's starts to look compelling — the setup in which SO's 21.08 P/E moves toward the index's rather than away from it, and the $115 high target comes into view. The bear framing: if rates stay elevated, what investors will pay stays capped regardless of how many gigawatts get contracted, the capital plan gets financed at a higher cost, and the stock does what it has done for the last twelve months — a +1.58% price change while SPY rose +9.69% and every peer did better. The market has shown this pattern twice this year: a Q2 report featuring the OpenAI contract was followed by a 1.78% decline that day, and a clean Q3 beat with a raised outlook moved the stock just 0.24%.
