01 Company Overview

The Southern Company is one of the largest regulated electric and gas utility holding companies in the United States, serving retail and wholesale customers across Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and, through Southern Company Gas, natural gas distribution territories in Illinois, Georgia, Virginia and Tennessee. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $29.55B, up 10.59% YoY, with TTM revenue of $30.18B (+6.40%) and TTM EPS of $4.15. At a $106.05B market capitalization the company ranks #222 globally and #112 among US companies, and it is the largest pure-play regulated electric utility in the Southeast by market value — ahead of Duke Energy ($96.37B) and well ahead of AEP ($68.74B).

What makes Southern unusual among regulated utilities right now is where it operates. Its Georgia and Alabama franchise territory sits in the middle of one of the fastest-growing data-center corridors in the country. In Q2 2026 alone, data-center usage on Southern's system grew 55% YoY to more than 1.2 GW, and the company signed 6 GW of new large-load contracts, bringing contracted demand to more than 17 GW through the mid-2030s against a prospective pipeline of over 75 GW. Weather-normalized retail electricity sales rose 2.3% — management characterized this as the strongest growth in nearly two decades for a business that had been effectively flat for fifteen years.

Georgia Power is the centre of gravity for the entire investment case. It is a vertically integrated, rate-regulated monopoly overseen by the Georgia Public Service Commission (PSC), and it serves the Atlanta metro plus the coastal corridor around Savannah where hyperscale data-center development has concentrated. Its 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) — the triennial long-range supply plan every vertically integrated utility must file — was approved by the Georgia PSC on July 15, 2025, and forecasts roughly 8,500 MW of load growth over six years and a ~2,600 MW increase in peak demand by the end of 2030 versus the 2023 forecast. Approved additions include extended power uprates at existing nuclear units (including 54 MW from Vogtle Units 1 and 2), 268 MW of gas at Plant McIntosh, more than 1,500 MW of battery energy storage (BESS), 1,100 MW of initial renewable procurement scaling toward up to 4,000 MW by 2035, and more than 1,000 miles of new transmission. On July 31, 2026 Georgia Power broke ground on two combined-cycle gas units at Plant Bowen adding nearly 1,500 MW plus a 500 MW battery, supported by a US Department of Energy loan package of up to $26.5 billion. The company files quarterly Large Load Economic Development Reports with the PSC, which is the single most useful public dataset for tracking whether the AI load thesis is converting.

Southern's moat is the classic regulated-utility moat — an exclusive, state-granted franchise to serve a defined territory, with rates set to allow recovery of prudently incurred costs plus an authorized return on rate base — but it is currently being amplified by something genuinely scarce. The binding constraint on AI infrastructure in 2026 is not capital or chips; it is deliverable, firm electric power on a known schedule. Southern controls interconnection and generation in a corridor where hyperscalers want to build, and it owns the only newly completed large-scale nuclear units in the United States: Plant Vogtle Units 3 and 4, two Westinghouse AP1000 reactors in which Georgia Power holds a 45.7% ownership interest (Oglethorpe Power 30%, MEAG Power 22.7%, Dalton Utilities 1.6%). Unit 4 was connected to the grid on March 1, 2024 and both units are now in service, supplying carbon-free baseload of exactly the round-the-clock character hyperscale customers are willing to pay a premium for. The commercial terms reinforce the moat: large-load contracts carry minimum bills covering at least 100% of the incremental cost to serve, long tenors (the OpenAI agreement runs 25 years), and $21 billion of aggregate collateral supporting the 17 GW portfolio — structures designed so that the customer, not the ratepayer or the shareholder, bears the risk if the load fails to show up.

Key Vulnerabilities

Load materialization risk is documented, not hypothetical. Georgia PSC staff testimony filed in late 2025 found that data centers were "primarily underperforming expectations due to a mixture of lower materialization rates, project cancellations, and delays." Since 2023, 33 data-center projects totalling 11,332 MW were removed from Georgia Power's queue, representing 55% of all project removals. In Q3 2025 the total pipeline actually shrank from 57.3 GW to 50.9 GW — 6.8 GW of inflows against 14.3 GW of outflows. The headline 75 GW pipeline figure should therefore be read as a funnel top, not a forecast; contracted GW is the number that matters.

Regulatory lag and stranded-cost exposure. Georgia and Alabama base rates are expected to be held stable through 2029, which is excellent politics and mediocre economics: Southern is deploying an $81 billion five-year capital plan into a rate base whose recovery is deferred. PSC staff have already flagged that beyond the ~1,900 MW under executed large-load contracts at the time of that testimony, remaining approved capacity was "speculative and exposes customers to the risk of stranded costs." A commission that later finds new generation imprudent can disallow recovery — as the $10M of Nicor Gas capital disallowances year to date illustrates in miniature.

Balance-sheet strain and the equity/rate arithmetic. Debt-to-equity is 1.82 versus a 1.66 average for the five regulated peers reviewed here, total debt is $77.09B, and free cash flow is -$3.27B on TTM capex of $13.92B. The company targets FFO-to-debt of 17% by 2029 and had $1.1B of remaining equity needs through 2030 as of Q2 2026. Every 100bp move in long rates changes both the cost of that funding and the discount rate applied to a 0.33-beta bond proxy.

Political pricing of AI load. Southern joined the "Ratepayer Protection Pledge" in July 2026 and has emphasised rate stability, but the underlying tension is unresolved across the industry: residential customers increasingly object to funding grid expansion driven by hyperscalers. Advocacy groups including the Southern Environmental Law Center have publicly challenged both the fossil capacity approved in the IRP and the adequacy of customer protections in Georgia Power's data-center framework. Adverse tariff redesign — shifting more cost to shareholders or capping large-load returns — is the tail risk that would most directly damage the bull case.

Notable One-Off Items

The gap between Q2 2026 GAAP EPS of $1.03 and adjusted EPS of $1.13 is not cosmetic — it is almost entirely one identifiable, disclosed item, and investors should model it explicitly:

Wind repowering accelerated depreciation (Southern Power). $143M pre-tax / $111M after-tax in Q2 2026 and $296M pre-tax / $230M after-tax year to date. Management has guided to a further $205M pre-tax remaining in 2026 and $120M in 2027, after which it runs off. This single non-cash charge is what turned Southern Power's segment result from a profit into a $(25)M quarterly loss.

Nicor Gas capital investment disallowances. $8M pre-tax / $6M after-tax in Q2; $10M pre-tax / $8M after-tax year to date. Small in dollars, but a live example of regulatory recovery risk on deployed capital.

Debt extinguishment costs. $11M pre-tax / $8M after-tax year to date, associated with liability management.

Asset-sale tail item. A $9M pre-tax state income tax refund relating to the previously completed Elizabethtown Gas disposition — a credit, not a charge.

Vogtle Units 3 & 4 tax remeasurement. A modest $4M charge in both the quarter and year-to-date periods. Notably, the enormous Vogtle construction charges that dominated Southern's non-GAAP reconciliations from 2018 through 2023 have now largely run their course; the remaining Vogtle items are tax true-ups rather than cost overruns.

Storm cost recovery: no material storm-cost deferral or recovery item was disclosed in the Q2 2026 Form 8-K or discussed on the Q2 2026 earnings call in the sources reviewed for this note. Southeast utilities routinely carry storm reserves and securitized storm recovery balances, so the absence of a Q2 2026 disclosure should not be read as an absence of exposure — it should be verified against the 10-Q before being relied upon.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward. Readings above 70 mean the stock has run hard and may be stretched; readings below 30 mean it has been sold off hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation tool.

Five-year trajectory. On a split- and dividend-adjusted basis SO went from $52.05 (Sep 2021) to $92.19 (Aug 2026) — a total return of roughly +77.1%, about 12.1% annualized. That is a strong outcome for a 0.33-beta regulated utility, but it was not earned evenly. The first three years were a rangebound grind: from Sep 2021 through Dec 2023 the stock oscillated between roughly $52 and $67 as rising real yields compressed the multiple on every bond-proxy equity. The entire five-year return was effectively generated in an eighteen-month window from spring 2024 through early 2026, when the market re-rated Southeast utilities on the AI data-center thesis and Vogtle Units 3 and 4 finally moved from cost overrun to earning asset. The last six months have been a consolidation: after peaking at $96.58 in Feb 2026, the stock has drifted to $92.19, below its 50-day moving average of $94.57 and marginally below its 200-day of $92.56, with a 52-week change of -0.71%.

Relative performance. Within its own sector the picture is also unflattering over that window: on one-year total returns, Entergy delivered +25.95%, Dominion +18.05%, AEP +17.60%, Duke +5.33%, and SO just +2.57% — last among the six-name peer group. The tension between those two frames is the whole debate: the five-year chart says the re-rating happened, and the one-year chart says the market has stopped paying for the next leg until contracted gigawatts show up as revenue.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Southern owns the interconnection rights and dispatchable generation in the fastest-growing large-load corridor in the US, with 17+ GW contracted through the mid-2030s, a 75+ GW funnel, and contracts structured with minimum bills covering at least 100% of incremental cost to serve plus $21B of aggregate collateral. Weather-normalized retail sales grew 2.3% — the best in nearly two decades for a business that was flat for fifteen years — and data-center load is up 55% YoY. An $81B five-year capital plan plus 10 GW of approved generation at roughly $2B per GW gives visible, regulator-blessed rate-base growth, while a 3.30% yield with 24 years of dividend growth pays you to wait. At a 22.20 trailing P/E — in line with peers and a 21% discount to the S&P 500's 28.00 — none of this is in the price.

Bear Case

The pipeline is a funnel, not a backlog. Georgia PSC staff documented that data centers are "primarily underperforming expectations," with 33 projects totalling 11,332 MW removed since 2023 and the total pipeline shrinking 6.4 GW in a single quarter. Meanwhile Southern is spending $13.92B a year in capex against $10.65B of operating cash flow — -$3.27B free cash flow — funded on the most leveraged balance sheet in its peer group (1.82 debt-to-equity, $77.09B total debt) into a rate base whose base-rate recovery is frozen until 2029. Revenue grew 6.40% versus an 11.18% peer average and Q2 revenue missed by $250M. With 14 Holds and 3 Sells against 7 Buys and a KeyBanc target of $79, the sell side is telling you the assets are fine and the entry price is not.

Top 3 Risks
01
Large-load attrition. The gap between 75 GW of prospective pipeline and 17 GW contracted is where the entire valuation debate lives, and the historical conversion record is poor — 11,332 MW of Georgia projects cancelled since 2023, representing 55% of all removals. If contracted GW stops growing, Southern is left holding an $81B capital plan built for demand that did not arrive, and the regulatory question becomes who pays for it.
02
Interest-rate and refinancing sensitivity. With $77.09B of total debt, an enterprise value of $180.15B against a $106.05B market cap, negative free cash flow and a 0.33 beta, SO behaves like a long-duration bond. Higher-for-longer long rates compress the multiple directly and raise the cost of the very capital the growth plan depends on. The Sep–Oct 2022 drawdown of 15% in two months and the Oct–Dec 2024 giveback of 9% are both pure rate events with no fundamental trigger.
03
Regulatory and political recovery risk. Base rates in Georgia and Alabama are expected to be held flat through 2029, deferring recovery on a massive build. Commissions can and do disallow costs — the $10M of year-to-date Nicor Gas capital disallowances is a small live example — and public pressure to shield residential ratepayers from AI-driven grid costs is intensifying, with the Southern Environmental Law Center already challenging the adequacy of customer protections in Georgia Power's data-center framework. Adverse tariff redesign is the tail risk that would most directly break the bull case.
  Industry Commentary
AI Data-Center Load Growth in the Southeast
EMERGING TAILWIND

US electricity demand was structurally flat for roughly fifteen years; it is not flat any more, and the Southeast is where the inflection is sharpest. Southern's data-center load grew 55% YoY in Q2 2026 to over 1.2 GW, up 500 MW in twelve months, with 49% growth year to date and commercial sales up 7.4% in the quarter. Georgia Power's approved 2025 IRP forecasts approximately 8,500 MW of load growth over six years and a ~2,600 MW increase in peak demand by end-2030 versus the 2023 plan. Southern signed 6 GW of new large-load contracts in Q2 alone, reaching 17+ GW through the mid-2030s, with 8 GW in late-stage discussions and 3 GW expected to finalize near-term. The OpenAI agreement — 3.2 GW over 25 years near Savannah with 1 GW of flexible demand response — is the marquee validation. Critically, this is regulated load: it converts into rate base and authorized returns rather than merchant price exposure. Peers are chasing the same trend (AEP has a $78B plan through 2030; Entergy has publicly targeted greater than 8% EPS growth through 2035), which is itself evidence that the demand is real rather than a Southern-specific story.

Rate Base Expansion, Capital Intensity and Regulatory Lag
ONGOING HEADWIND

Regulated utilities earn on rate base, so growth requires spending — and the spending comes first. Southern raised its five-year capital plan from $76B to $81B in February 2026, and 10 GW of newly approved generation at roughly $2B per GW implies $20B+ of further opportunity beyond that. The cost is visible in the cash statement: TTM capex of $13.92B against $10.65B of operating cash flow, producing -$3.27B of free cash flow, funded by $77.09B of debt at a 1.82 debt-to-equity ratio — the highest of the six-name peer set, though the -$3.27B FCF is actually the best of the group (peer average -$7.34B), which tells you the entire sector is in the same position. Regulatory lag makes it worse in the near term: with Georgia and Alabama base rates expected flat through 2029, capital goes into the ground years before it earns a full return. Management's stated FFO-to-debt target of 17% by 2029 and its reduced remaining equity need of $1.1B through 2030 are the two credit metrics to monitor; the $1.375B convertible priced in August 2026 shows the company is actively managing that funding gap rather than deferring it.

Interest Rates, Utility Valuations, and the Bull/Bear Frame
SETS THE MULTIPLE

A 0.33-beta stock yielding 3.30% with 73% of earnings paid out and $77B of debt is, mechanically, a long-duration bond with an equity option attached. The five-year price series makes this explicit: the two worst drawdowns (Sep–Oct 2022, -15%; Oct–Dec 2024, -9%) were both rate events with no company-specific trigger, and the cycle low of $58.85 in Sep 2023 coincided precisely with the peak in long-end yields. The bull framing: if long rates fall, SO gets a multiple expansion on top of an accelerating earnings base, and the 3.30% yield against SPY's 0.98% starts to look compelling on a relative basis — this is the setup in which a 22.20 P/E versus the index's 28.00 closes rather than widens, and the $114 high target becomes reachable. The bear framing: if rates stay elevated, the multiple stays capped regardless of how many gigawatts get contracted, the $81B capital plan gets financed at punishing cost, and the stock does what it has done for the last twelve months — deliver a -0.71% price change while the S&P 500 returns +20.87% and every peer outperforms it. Note that the market has already run this experiment once this year: the OpenAI contract, the largest single validation the thesis has received, produced a 2.19% decline on the day. Until the discount-rate regime cooperates, good news at Southern has been arriving into an unreceptive tape.
