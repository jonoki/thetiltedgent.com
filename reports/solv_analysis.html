01 Company Overview

Solventum is the old 3M Health Care business, set loose. On April 1, 2024 3M distributed 80.1% of the shares to its own stockholders and kept 19.9% to sell down later. The same day, S&P added SOLV to the S&P 500 in place of VF Corp. What came out of the door was a medtech company in name and a conglomerate fragment in practice: wound care and surgical supplies, a dental business, a hospital-coding software business, and a filtration unit that sold into biopharma and industry. FY2025 sales were $8.33B, up 0.9% reported and 3.3% organic (organic growth leaves out currency moves, acquisitions and divestitures). Adjusted EPS was $6.11. GAAP EPS was $8.88, flattered by a one-off gain.

The story since the spin is a company breaking itself into smaller pieces, on purpose. First it sold the filtration unit to Thermo Fisher. The deal was announced at $4.1B in February 2025 and closed on September 2, 2025, producing a $1,549M gain on sale for FY2025 ($1,518M booked in Q3, $31M in Q4), with the proceeds going to pay down debt. It bought Acera Surgical in December 2025. Then on August 5, 2026, alongside a quarter that beat hard, it said it intends to separate the Health Information Systems software unit within 12–18 months, by spin-off, sale or merger. CEO Bryan Hanson's aim is "a true medtech company" built on MedSurg and Dental. The stock has done its part: from a first-month close of $65.01 (April 2024) to $89.08, +37.0% since listing, with no dividend. Q3 2026, reported on November 5, was the other side of that flattering Q2: sales fell 7.0% (organic −2.8%) as customers worked off the stock they had ordered ahead of the ERP cutover, yet adjusted EPS of $1.45 still beat the consensus of $1.35 and the full-year adjusted EPS guide went up to $7.20–$7.30. Management says the underlying organic growth rate was about 4% (per MarketBeat's summary of the call). The question for the next year is how much of the improvement is structural now that the timing effects have washed through.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hospital consumables installed base Clinical habit & product familiarity HIS coding used by 75%+ of US hospitals Regulatory clearances Global manufacturing & distribution Long-term software contracts (HIS)

The moat is habit and plumbing. A nurse reaches for the dressing she was trained on. A hospital's coding team builds its workflow around the software it already runs. Neither is switched on price alone. That is why GAAP gross margin held near 55% (55.9% over nine months of 2026; 57.9% adjusted) even as the company went through a spin, a divestiture and an ERP migration. The weakness is also clear: GAAP operating margin was 5.3% over nine months of 2026 (3.5% in Q3), against 22.95% in FY2021. Some of that is stand-up and separation cost that should fade: $465M of 3M separation costs and $132M of restructuring in nine months. Some of it is the price of rebuilding, as a standalone company, the systems 3M used to provide. The adjusted operating margin was 22.8% over nine months, and management now expects the FY2026 figure near the high end of its 22.2–22.7% guide (per MarketBeat's call summary), so it thinks most of the gap is temporary. The GAAP line has not shown it yet. An amended raw-material supply agreement with 3M also removes a cost increase scheduled for April 2027, in exchange for a volume commitment (MarketBeat call summary).

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The borrowed quarter was paid back. Q2 2026's +9.5% organic growth was lifted by orders customers placed ahead of the ERP switch; Q3 gave them back (about 6.3 points of organic growth, per the release), with organic growth of −2.8% and sales down 7.0%. Adjusted EPS of $1.45 was 3.6% lower than a year earlier. The market took it in stride: the shares rose 3.7% on Nov 6, the first session after the release, to $90.38, though the Q3 print came after a 7.7% October drop to $81.33. Management says the underlying growth rate was about 4% (MarketBeat call summary); the test is whether Q4 and 2027 show it.
02
GAAP profitability is thin. Q3 GAAP EPS was −$0.07 against $1.45 adjusted, and the GAAP operating margin was 3.5%. Adjustments of $157M for 3M separation, $50M for restructuring, $90M of acquired-intangibles amortisation and others do heavy lifting, and each year of "one-off" separation cost makes it harder to argue they are one-off. Management says about 90% of the 3M transition services are exited, with the rest finishing by the end of 2027 (MarketBeat call summary).
03
Leverage and cash conversion. Debt-to-equity is 1.10 (including finance leases), net debt is about $4.6–$4.8B, the current ratio is 1.03 and free cash flow over the last four quarters is $0. The balance sheet is improving because of an asset sale, not because the business is producing cash. Q4 has to deliver most of the $200–$300M guide. The company bought back $403M of stock in the first nine months of 2026 (release), under a $1B buyback programme announced in November 2025, so cash returns currently depend on borrowing and asset sales.
04
Execution load. In under three years the company has spun out, stood up its own ERP (all cutovers now complete, per the Q3 release), sold a business, bought one, cut SKUs (a ~100bps drag on 2026 growth) and now started a second separation. Every one of those is a chance for customer disruption or a supply-chain miss, and Q3's Dental drop shows how an order shift can hit a segment.

Notable One-Off Items

Four items distort the ratios above. First, 3M separation costs: $157M in Q3 and $465M in nine months, against $433M in the same nine months of 2025. Second, restructuring under the "Transform for the Future" programme: $50M in Q3, $132M in nine months. Third, litigation costs of $178M in nine months. Fourth, the ERP advance-order swing: sales pulled into Q2 and out of Q3, which the company said cost about 630bps (6.3 points) of Q3 organic growth. Behind them sit the $1,518M gain on the filtration sale in Q3 2025, which left the trailing figures this quarter, and tariff refunds, which helped Q2 profit and Q3 cash flow. Take the timing items out and management puts underlying organic growth at about 4%, with margin repair still ahead.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

SOLV has been listed for about two and a half years, so there is no five-year record to judge. From the first month-end close of $65.01 (April 2024) to the November 13, 2026 settled close of $89.08, the stock gained 37.0%, with no dividends to add. The path mattered more than the endpoints. The first three months looked like classic post-spin selling, and SOLV closed June 2024 at $52.88, the lowest month-end in the series, having traded as low as $47.16 in July 2024. From there it has been a two-year rebuild in steps: to the high $70s by early 2025, a flat 2025 in the $66–$76 band, a jump on the Q3 2025 print, a spring 2026 slide to $65.30, and a five-month run to a $91.06 August 2026 close. Since then it has given back some of that: $88.15 at the end of September, $81.33 at the end of October (−7.7%), and $89.08 on November 13 after the Q3 print.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 73.6 in August 2026, its first reading above 70 in the series. It fell to 52.4 at the end of October and reads 63.1 with the November 13 close counted as the latest month. Its low was 35.7 at the March 2026 trough, which never reached oversold. The 10-month moving average sits near $79.40, below the price, and the 3-month average near $86.19. The trend is still up, but the August peak has not been regained, and the quarter that powered that leg was partly borrowed from the next.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

This is a spin-off doing what good spin-offs do: shrinking to its best assets and fixing the balance sheet. It is deleveraging, has sold a non-core unit for $4.0B, and has a second separation under way that could unlock value in a software business used by 75%+ of US hospitals. FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance has been raised twice, to $7.20–$7.30 from $6.40–$6.60 before August. At $89.08 that is about 12.3x FY2026 consensus, for a medtech consumables franchise with 55% GAAP gross margins and an adjusted operating margin management now expects near the high end of 22.2–22.7%. Q3 showed the order timing washing out, with the guide still going up. If the GAAP–adjusted gap closes as stand-up costs roll off, the reported numbers catch up with the story and today's multiple looks low.

Bear Case

Strip out the one-offs and this is a ~3% grower with a 5.3% GAAP operating margin (nine months), zero free cash flow over the last four quarters, a 1.03 current ratio and 1.10 debt-to-equity. The trailing P/E of 100 is an artefact of separation and restructuring costs, and nothing a buyer should pay for. Q3 organic growth was −2.8% and Dental fell 7.4%, and the separation will remove the steadiest-growing segment and about 4 points of operating margin. Consensus expects FY2027 EPS of $7.54, +4.1% on FY2026, and 3M still has a stake to sell. At 5.4% below the 52-week high the price already reflects most of the good news.

Top 3 Risks

01
Underlying growth after the payback. The Q3 payback is behind it: organic growth was −2.8%, about 3.5% with the 630bps added back (our addition) and about 4% on management's measure. If Q4 and 2027 show less than that, the market will re-read the first half as mostly timing.
02
Separation execution and the costs left behind. The HIS separation removes the segment that grew fastest organically in FY2025, and management expects it to take about 4 points off the remaining company's operating margin (MarketBeat call summary). The remaining company must show that MedSurg and Dental can grow and expand margins on their own, while absorbing the shared costs the departing unit used to carry and running its third major transaction in under three years.
03
Cash conversion and leverage. Free cash flow over the last four quarters is $0 and nine-month free cash flow is −$32M, against a $200–$300M FY2026 guide, and net debt is $4.6–$4.8B. If the cash does not come through, buybacks and further deleveraging depend on more asset sales rather than on the business.
Industry Commentary
The spin-off life cycle: forced sellers, then focus
STRUCTURAL PATTERN

SOLV's first three months follow a common spin-off pattern. Shareholders of a diversified industrial were handed a healthcare stock they had not chosen, and many sold it. The stock went from a $69.15 first-day open to a $52.88 June 2024 close. What followed was also typical: management simplifying the portfolio (the filtration sale, the HIS separation), paying down the debt it was spun out with, and asking to be valued as a focused medical-technology company. The remaining 3M stake means the forced-seller phase is not entirely over.

Tariffs and supply chains in medical consumables
ONGOING VARIABLE

Tariffs show up in Solventum's numbers in both directions. On the Q1 call the CFO said operational improvements mostly offset the impact of tariffs and inflation, while Q2 2026 gross margin was lifted by a refund of tariffs (release) that management does not expect to repeat in 2027 (MarketBeat's Q3 call summary). Neither is under management's control, and the refund is not recurring earnings. A consumables maker with global manufacturing lives with this exposure. The tariff-driven part of 2026 earnings deserves less weight than the recurring part.

AI in medical coding — the HIS asset
EMERGING TAILWIND

Hospital revenue-cycle management is labour-heavy, and autonomous coding is one of the clearer commercial uses of AI in healthcare administration. Solventum's HIS unit sits in the middle of it, with 40 years of coding content, most US hospitals as users and a ~$10B addressable market by the company's own sizing. Hanson's argument for separating it is that it needs "a different investment level" and "a different pace of innovation" to exploit that opportunity. The bull framing: a separation lets investors value the software business the way they value software companies, and interested buyers exist. The bear framing: the parent gives up its steadiest grower and its most AI-leveraged asset, and keeps the lower-growth consumables.
