01 Company Overview

S&P Global is the world's largest credit-ratings agency and the owner of the S&P 500 and Dow Jones index families, plus a large financial-data and commodity-pricing franchise. It generated $15.34B of revenue in FY2025, up 7.9% YoY, with GAAP net income of $4.47B and diluted EPS of $14.66. On a TTM basis revenue is $16.12B (+9.7%) and net income $4.92B (+22.7%).

The company ranks roughly #185 globally and #99 in the United States by market capitalization at $123.23B. That headline number is materially smaller than a year ago for a mechanical reason as much as a fundamental one: on July 1, 2026 S&P Global completed the spin-off of its Mobility division as an independent company, Mobility Global (NYSE: MBGL), distributing one MBGL share for every SPGI share held as of the June 15, 2026 record date. Post-spin S&P Global runs four divisions — Ratings, Market Intelligence, Energy (formerly Commodity Insights), and S&P Dow Jones Indices.

The stock has nonetheless de-rated on fundamentals too: shares set a one-year low after the February 10, 2026 Q4 print, when 2026 adjusted-EPS guidance of $19.40–$19.65 came in below the ~$19.96 consensus and the stock fell roughly 12% in a session. Shares now trade at 25.0x trailing earnings versus an S&P 500 trailing multiple near 29.9x — an unusual discount for a business that has historically commanded a premium.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
RATINGS DUOPOLY NRSRO REGULATORY BARRIER S&P 500 BRAND LICENSE INDEX OLIGOPOLY 72% INDEX MARGINS PLATTS BENCHMARKS 166 YEARS OF DATA HISTORY EMBEDDED WORKFLOW SWITCHING COSTS

Two structures do almost all the work. First, the ratings duopoly: S&P and Moody's together account for roughly 80–85% of global ratings revenue (est.), with Fitch a distant third. That concentration is not an accident of execution — it is enforced by demand. Bond mandates, insurance capital rules, central-bank collateral frameworks, and index inclusion criteria are written in terms of ratings from a small set of recognized agencies, and issuers overwhelmingly buy two ratings because investors expect two. A new entrant cannot manufacture the default-history dataset, the regulatory recognition, or the investor acceptance, and even if it could, the marginal issuer has no reason to substitute a cheaper rating that fewer buyers will honor. Pricing power follows: Ratings earned a 68% adjusted operating margin in Q2 2026.

Second, the index oligopoly. S&P DJI, MSCI, and FTSE Russell control the benchmarks against which most of the world's institutional money is measured. The S&P 500 in particular is a brand, not just a methodology — SPY, IVV, VOO and the listed futures/options complex are all licensed from S&P DJI, and an ETF sponsor cannot swap benchmarks without breaking its own product's identity and forcing clients to re-underwrite tracking error. Because the incremental cost of another dollar of indexed AUM is near zero, revenue growth converts to profit almost one-for-one: asset-linked fees +22% YoY drove segment operating profit +21% at a 72% margin. Layered on top are Platts commodity benchmarks (the same "written into contracts" dynamic in energy) and Market Intelligence's workflow entrenchment, which is real but the weakest of the four.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Private credit disintermediation. The moat protects the rated market; it does nothing if borrowing migrates outside it. Direct lending and private-credit funds increasingly finance mid-market and even large-cap borrowers with bilateral, unrated or privately-rated paper. Every dollar of syndicated bond issuance replaced by a private loan is a dollar of Ratings transaction revenue that never occurs. Transaction revenue was $746M in Q2 2026, +25% — so the effect is not yet visible in a strong issuance year, which is exactly when it is easiest to ignore.

2. Issuance cyclicality dressed as recurring revenue. Roughly 56% of Ratings revenue in Q2 2026 was transaction-based, i.e. tied to new debt volumes, which are a function of rates, spreads, refinancing walls, and M&A. Ratings is ~36% of revenue but roughly half of operating profit, so a single bad issuance year — 2022 is the template, when the stock fell 28% — compresses group earnings far more than the revenue mix implies. The market currently pays a subscription-like multiple for this stream.

3. Generative AI in data and analytics. Market Intelligence (33% of revenue, 36% margin) sells retrieval, aggregation, and summarization — precisely the tasks large language models compress in cost. The division grew 6% in Q2 2026 versus 20% at Indices and 17% at Ratings, and peer FactSet trades at just 18.9x earnings with a Hold consensus and an average target below its current price, which is the market pricing this risk explicitly in a close comparable.

4. Regulatory and reputational tail risk. The issuer-pays model carries a structural conflict of interest that regulators revisit after every credit cycle; the 2008 experience produced Dodd-Frank Title IX and eventually a $1.5B DOJ/state settlement. Ratings is also subject to EU and UK regimes with their own registration and fee-transparency requirements. A rating error at scale in a stressed cycle is the one event that can impair the franchise rather than merely the earnings.

Notable One-Off Items

Deal-related amortization (~$785M expected for full-year 2026). Almost entirely the legacy of the 2022 IHS Markit acquisition. This is the largest single reconciling item between GAAP and adjusted earnings and the main reason GAAP ROE of 14.25% understates the economics of the business.

Divestiture gains of $186M pre-tax in H1 2026, of which $172M ran through Market Intelligence, from the sales of the Enterprise Data Management and thinkFolio businesses plus a Colorado facility. These inflate TTM EPS and net margin and will not repeat.

Separation costs. Q2 2026 included $56M of disposition-related costs in Corporate and $43M of combined severance across divisions. A Transition Services Agreement with the spun-off Mobility Global reduced adjusted SG&A by $6M in the quarter — a temporary benefit that unwinds as the TSA rolls off.

Buybacks. $1.5B repurchased in H1 2026 through accelerated share repurchase agreements (≈3.5M shares), with more than $7B expected for the full year against a $123B market cap — roughly a 5.7% annualized reduction in share count at current prices. Buyback yield already stands at 2.79% on a trailing basis. Reported EPS growth in 2026–2027 will therefore materially exceed operating-profit growth.

Discontinued operations. Mobility's results are in reported figures through June 30, 2026 and absent thereafter. Any TTM metric on this page that includes Q3'25–Q2'26 still contains Mobility; forward multiples do not. Treat trailing-vs-forward comparisons with care.

In plain English: RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. Neither is a signal on its own.

Five-year trajectory. SPGI closed at $424.89 in September 2021 and $418.04 on August 18, 2026 — a price return of −1.6% over five years. That is the headline, and it is misleading in both directions. It excludes five years of dividends (currently $3.88 annualized, raised for 53 consecutive years) and, more importantly, it excludes the value of the Mobility Global shares distributed to every SPGI holder on July 1, 2026, which are not reflected anywhere in the price series above. Total shareholder return was therefore meaningfully positive; this build does not have a verified MBGL valuation with which to quantify it, and no estimate is offered.

The path was anything but flat. The stock peaked at $474 in October 2021, lost 36% to a $305 close in September 2022 as the rate shock froze debt issuance, then compounded steadily to an all-time monthly closing high of $551.10 in July 2025 — a 81% advance from the 2022 trough — on recovering issuance and index AUM growth. The 2026 leg down has two distinct causes: a fundamental de-rating in February (guidance miss, −12% in a session, one-year low) and a mechanical step-down around the July spin. At $418 the shares sit 24% below the July 2025 peak and 9.5% above the 52-week low of $381.61.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A cleaner, higher-margin company is now available at a discount to the market it indexes. Post-spin, Ratings (+17%, 68% margin) and Indices (+20%, 72% margin) are half the revenue and the majority of profit, both growing double digits, both protected by structures — a ratings duopoly and an index oligopoly — that no amount of capital can replicate. At 25.0x trailing and 21.7x forward earnings versus an S&P 500 at 29.9x, with >$7B of buybacks retiring ~5.7% of the float annually, $5.57B of free cash flow, and a consensus target 24% above the price, the market is treating a benchmark-monopoly business like a commoditizing data vendor.

Bear Case

The premium multiple was never justified by the earnings quality. Over half of Ratings revenue is transaction-based and therefore a leveraged bet on debt issuance, and 2022 showed what a closed primary market does (−28% in a year). Index revenue is a leveraged bet on the equity market level itself. Meanwhile the third of revenue in Market Intelligence grew only 6% and is the part of the business generative AI most directly attacks, and private credit is quietly moving financing outside the rated perimeter. Management has already missed guidance once in 2026, GAAP ROE is 14.3%, and the de-rating may simply be the market correctly repricing a cyclical as a cyclical.

Top 3 Risks
01
Debt issuance downturn. Transaction revenue was $746M of $1,339M in Ratings in Q2 2026 and carries near-incremental margins. A rate or spread shock that closes primary markets removes that revenue almost immediately, and Ratings supplies roughly half of divisional operating profit. This is the fastest-acting risk in the model.
02
Equity drawdown hitting index asset-linked fees. S&P DJI bills basis points on AUM; asset-linked fees of $348M in Q2 (+22%) scale directly with market levels. A sustained bear market compresses the company's highest-margin line and its multiple simultaneously — the two risks are correlated, not diversifying.
03
Structural erosion: private credit plus AI. Private credit shrinks the rated universe over time, while generative AI compresses the price of the data-retrieval work that underpins Market Intelligence's $1.2B quarterly revenue. Neither shows up cleanly in a single quarter, which is precisely what makes them dangerous to a multiple.
  Industry Commentary
The Debt Issuance Cycle Has Turned Up
EMERGING TAILWIND
Corporate borrowers are refinancing into a more accommodating rate environment and the volume is showing up directly in the numbers: billed issuance rose 28% in Q4 2025, Ratings transaction revenue rose 25% YoY in Q2 2026 to $746M, and H1 transaction revenue reached $1,458M versus $1,217M. Because a rated bond costs S&P Global almost nothing incremental to rate, this flowed through to a 28% increase in Ratings operating profit at a 68% margin. The dependency runs both ways — 2022's issuance freeze produced a 28% share-price decline — but the current cycle position is a tailwind, and it is the single largest reason 2026 guidance implies 6–8% organic growth despite a shrinking reported top line.
Private Credit Is Building a Market Outside the Rated Perimeter
ONGOING HEADWIND
The ratings duopoly is only valuable where ratings are required. Direct lending funds now finance borrowers that would once have issued syndicated high-yield bonds, often with bilateral documentation, no public rating, or a private rating that generates a fraction of the fee. The migration is slow and largely invisible inside a strong issuance year — which is what 2026 is — but it compounds. The metric to watch is whether Ratings transaction revenue keeps pace with reported market-wide issuance over a full cycle. A related pressure sits in Market Intelligence: at 6% growth versus 17–20% at the benchmark businesses, and with peer FactSet's market cap down 27% and trading at 18.9x, the market is already discounting generative AI's effect on the price of financial data.
Passive Investing: The Toll Booth That Cuts Both Ways
BULL VS BEAR FRAMING
Bull framing: every dollar that moves from an active manager to an S&P 500 tracker becomes a recurring basis-point toll for S&P DJI at a 72% operating margin. Asset-linked fees grew 22% and derivative royalties 14% in Q2 2026 — growth that requires no headcount, no capex (2026 group capex guidance is only $190–210M), and no new product. Bear framing: that same toll is a levered claim on the equity market's level and on ETF fee compression, and it arrives at a moment when the company has already cut its portfolio down to fewer, more cyclical engines. Post-spin, roughly half of profit is a bet on debt issuance and a meaningful further slice is a bet on the S&P 500 itself. At 25.0x trailing earnings versus the index's 29.9x, the market is pricing SPGI as a cyclical financial rather than a compounding franchise — the investment question is which of those two descriptions is actually correct.
