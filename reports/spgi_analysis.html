01 Company Overview

S&P Global is the world's largest credit-ratings agency and the owner of the S&P 500 and Dow Jones index families, plus a large financial-data and commodity-pricing franchise. In FY2025, before the Mobility spin-off, it reported $15.34B of revenue, up 7.9% YoY, with GAAP net income of $4.47B and diluted EPS of $14.66. For the first nine months of 2026, with Mobility treated as a discontinued operation, revenue from continuing operations was $11.07B, up 9%, and diluted EPS from continuing operations was $12.98, up 29% (including $386M of pre-tax gains on businesses it sold). On a TTM basis diluted EPS is $16.81.

The company ranks #89 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization at $135.25B (our calculation: 289.9M shares outstanding × the $466.55 close). On July 1, 2026 S&P Global completed the spin-off of its Mobility division as an independent company, Mobility Global (NYSE: MBGL), distributing one MBGL share for every SPGI share held as of the June 15, 2026 record date. Post-spin S&P Global runs four divisions — Ratings, Market Intelligence, Energy (formerly Commodity Insights), and S&P Dow Jones Indices. On October 27 it opened a strategic review of Capital IQ and associated data businesses, including a potential separation; the release says there is no set timeframe and no assurance of a transaction.

The shares set a one-year low after the February 10, 2026 Q4 print, when 2026 adjusted-EPS guidance of $19.40–$19.65 came in below the $19.94 analysts' average compiled by LSEG and the shares closed 9.7% lower that day (Reuters). The Q3 print on October 27 changed the tone: the company raised its 2026 adjusted-EPS guidance to $17.70–$17.95 from $17.50–$17.75, and the shares closed October 30 at $466.55, 11.6% above the prior edition's August 18 close. They trade at 27.8x trailing earnings; the S&P 500 column in section 04 shows 25.1x.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
RATINGS DUOPOLY NRSRO REGULATORY BARRIER S&P 500 BRAND LICENSE INDEX OLIGOPOLY 72% INDEX MARGINS PLATTS BENCHMARKS 166 YEARS OF DATA HISTORY EMBEDDED WORKFLOW SWITCHING COSTS

Two structures do almost all the work. First, the ratings duopoly: S&P and Moody's together account for roughly 80–85% of global ratings revenue (est.), with Fitch a distant third. That concentration is not an accident of execution — it is enforced by demand. Bond-fund investment rules, insurance capital rules, central-bank collateral rules and bond-index entry rules are written in terms of ratings from a small set of recognized agencies, and issuers overwhelmingly buy two ratings because investors expect two. A new entrant cannot manufacture the default-history dataset, the regulatory recognition, or the investor acceptance, and even if it could, a borrower has no reason to switch to a cheaper rating that fewer buyers will accept. Pricing power follows: Ratings earned a 66% adjusted operating margin in Q3 2026.

Second, the index oligopoly. S&P DJI, MSCI, and FTSE Russell control the benchmarks against which most of the world's institutional money is measured. The S&P 500 in particular is a brand, not just a methodology — SPY, IVV, VOO and the exchange-traded futures and options on the index are all licensed from S&P DJI, and a fund company cannot switch to another index without changing what its fund is and making its clients re-check how closely the fund follows the new benchmark. Because serving another dollar of indexed AUM costs almost nothing, revenue growth converts to profit almost one-for-one: asset-linked fees rose 24% YoY in Q3 2026 at a 72% adjusted margin. Layered on top are Platts commodity benchmarks (the same "written into contracts" dynamic in energy) and Market Intelligence's place in clients' daily work, which is real but the weakest of the four.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Private credit disintermediation. The moat protects the rated market; it does nothing if borrowing migrates outside it. Private-credit funds increasingly lend directly to mid-sized and even large companies, through one-to-one loans that carry no public rating or only a private one. Every dollar of syndicated bond issuance replaced by a private loan is a dollar of Ratings transaction revenue that never occurs. Transaction revenue was $677M in Q3 2026, up just 1%, and management said it expects a slight decline in the fourth quarter; no disclosed metric isolates how much of any slowdown is private credit.

2. Debt-issuance swings dressed up as steady revenue. Roughly 53% of Ratings revenue in Q3 2026 ($677M of $1,271M) was transaction-based, i.e. tied to how much new debt is sold, which depends on interest rates, the extra yield lenders demand over government bonds, how much existing debt comes due, and deal activity. Ratings is about 34% of segment revenue but roughly 42% of adjusted operating profit (our calculation from rounded segment margins), so a single bad issuance year — 2022 is the template, when the stock fell 29% (December 2021 to December 2022 month-end closes, price only) — compresses group earnings far more than the revenue mix implies. Yet investors value the stock as if this revenue were as steady as subscription income.

3. Generative AI in data and analytics. Market Intelligence (34% of segment revenue, 36% adjusted margin) sells retrieval, aggregation, and summarization — precisely the tasks large language models compress in cost. The division grew 7% in Q3 2026 versus 20% at Indices and 2% at Ratings, and on October 27 the company opened a strategic review of Capital IQ, its flagship data product. Peer FactSet trades at 22.6x trailing earnings (our calculation at the October 30 close) after a 22.5% gain in its share price over twelve months (October 30, 2025 to October 30, 2026 closes, Yahoo Finance), so the market is not pricing every data vendor the same way.

4. Regulatory and reputational tail risk. The issuer-pays model carries a structural conflict of interest that regulators revisit after every credit cycle; the 2008 experience produced Dodd-Frank Title IX and later settlements with US authorities. Ratings is also subject to EU and UK regimes with their own registration and fee-transparency requirements. A rating error at scale in a stressed cycle is the one event that can impair the franchise rather than merely the earnings.

Private Credit's Effect on Rated Issuance
The structural question overhanging the Ratings franchise. Growth of direct lending and privately-placed credit shifts financing away from the rated public markets that generate transaction fees. There is no clean disclosed metric for the leakage; the tell would be Ratings transaction revenue growing materially slower than reported market issuance in a strong credit year.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A cleaner, higher-margin company with a raised outlook. Post-spin, Ratings (66% margin) and Indices (+20% revenue, 72% margin) are about half the revenue and the majority of profit, both protected by structures — a ratings duopoly and an index oligopoly — that no amount of capital can replicate. Management lifted 2026 adjusted EPS guidance to $17.70–$17.95, Indices posted its 14th straight record quarter, more than $7B of buybacks are cutting the share count about 4% a year, free cash flow was $3.30B in nine months, and the average analyst target is 12.6% above the price. At 27.8x trailing and 26.2x guided 2026 earnings, the market is still weighing a benchmark business against a data vendor — and the Capital IQ review is a chance to sharpen that picture.

Bear Case

The stock's high price per dollar of earnings was never justified by how steady those earnings are. Over half of Ratings revenue is transaction-based and so rises and falls with debt issuance — it grew just 1% in Q3 and management guided to a slight decline in Q4 — and 2022 showed what a closed primary market does (−29% in a year). Index revenue rises and falls with the stock market itself. Meanwhile Market Intelligence, a third of segment revenue, grew only 7% and is the part of the business generative AI most directly attacks; its flagship Capital IQ is now under strategic review with no timetable, and private credit is quietly moving lending outside the market for rated debt. Initial 2026 guidance in February came in below consensus, GAAP ROE is 17.9%, and the market may simply be valuing a business whose earnings swing with the credit cycle as exactly that.

Top 3 Risks
01
Debt issuance downturn. Transaction revenue was $677M of $1,271M in Ratings in Q3 2026; management expects a slight decline in Q4. A jump in interest rates, or in the extra yield lenders demand, that stops new bond sales cuts that revenue within a quarter or two, and Ratings supplies roughly 42% of divisional adjusted operating profit. This is the fastest-acting risk in the model.
02
Equity drawdown hitting index asset-linked fees. S&P DJI bills a small percentage of assets under management; asset-linked fees of $375M in Q3 (+24%) scale directly with market levels. A sustained bear market shrinks the company's highest-margin line and the price investors pay per dollar of its earnings at the same time — the two risks are correlated, not diversifying.
03
Structural erosion: private credit plus AI. Private credit shrinks the rated universe over time, while generative AI compresses the price of the data-retrieval work that underpins Market Intelligence's $1.26B quarterly revenue. Neither shows up cleanly in a single quarter, which is precisely what makes them dangerous to the share price.
  Industry Commentary
Debt Issuance: Ratings Fees Slowed in Q3
COOLING
Ratings transaction revenue — fees earned when new debt is rated — was $677M in Q3 2026, up 1% from $668M, and non-transaction revenue (monitoring and subscriptions) was $594M, up 4%; the division grew 2%. CFO Eric Aboaf said on the call: "We expect billed issuance growth to generally mirror transaction revenue growth over time." and the fourth quarter "implies a slight decline year-over-year in transaction revenue…". The dependency runs both ways — 2022's issuance freeze produced a 29% share-price decline — but the group still grew 7% in Q3 because Indices (+20%), Market Intelligence (+7%) and Energy (+6%) carried it.
Private Credit Is Building a Market Outside Rated Debt
ONGOING HEADWIND
The ratings duopoly is only valuable where ratings are required. Direct lending funds now finance borrowers that would once have issued syndicated high-yield bonds, often as one-to-one loans with no public rating, or a private rating that generates a fraction of the fee. The shift is slow and adds up quietly. The metric to watch is whether Ratings transaction revenue keeps pace with reported market-wide issuance over a full cycle. A related pressure sits in Market Intelligence: it grew 7% in Q3 versus 20% at Indices, and on October 27 the company opened a strategic review of Capital IQ. CEO Martina Cheung said on the call: "The strategic review we announced this morning is about putting our investment where we are strongest."
Passive Investing: The Toll Booth That Cuts Both Ways
BULL VS BEAR FRAMING
Bull framing: every dollar that moves from an active manager to an S&P 500 tracker becomes a recurring toll for S&P DJI at a 72% adjusted operating margin. Asset-linked fees grew 24% and usage-based royalties 19% in Q3 2026 — growth that requires no headcount, almost no capex (2026 group capex guidance is $155–165M), and no new product. Bear framing: that same toll rises and falls with the stock market and shrinks as fund companies cut their fees, and after the spin-off the company has fewer businesses to lean on. Post-spin, a large part of profit is a bet on debt issuance and a meaningful further slice is a bet on the S&P 500 itself. At 27.8x trailing earnings, the market is deciding whether SPGI is a financial company whose earnings swing with markets or a steadily growing franchise — the investment question is which of those two descriptions is actually correct.
