01 Company Overview

Sempra is a San Diego–based energy holding company that owns three structurally different businesses: Sempra California (San Diego Gas & Electric and SoCalGas — roughly 3.6 million electric and 3.3 million gas customers), Sempra Texas Utilities (its interest in Oncor, the largest transmission-and-distribution utility in Texas, with about 18,418 circuit miles of transmission line and more than 4.1 million delivery points), and Sempra Infrastructure (LNG liquefaction and export — Cameron LNG, ECA LNG and Port Arthur LNG). FY2025 revenue was $13.70B, up 3.9% from $13.19B; TTM revenue through June 2026 is $13.55B, up 1.6%. Market cap of $54.95B ranks the company roughly #467 globally.

Revenue is close to meaningless here, and that is the first thing to internalise. A regulated utility earns an allowed return on the capital it has put in the ground — its rate base — so the number that drives the equity is the capital plan, not the top line. Sempra's plan is ~$64.9B over 2026–2030, with 59% at Oncor in Texas, 36% in California and just 5% at Infrastructure. And Sempra Texas contributes earnings but essentially no consolidated revenue, because Oncor is held as an equity-method interest — which is why $13.55B of revenue sits alongside a segment that produced $517M of first-half earnings.

The central debate on this page: Sempra is priced like a utility with a problem. It trades at 24.3× trailing P/E on depressed GAAP earnings but 16.0× forward, at a discount to the peer group on forward numbers, with a 3.13% yield and a 7–9% long-term EPS growth target. The discount exists because two of the three businesses carry risks that a normal regulated utility does not: California wildfire liability, which is an uncapped-looking tail on the SDG&E side, and an LNG development arm that is capital-hungry, commodity-adjacent and not rate-regulated at all. The bull case is that Oncor's Texas load growth is now the dominant earnings driver and the market has not repriced for it. Both cases are examined below.

San Diego Gas & Electric and Southern California Gas serve roughly 3.6 million electric and 3.3 million gas customers across about 4,100 square miles. This is textbook regulated utility economics — capital is invested, the California Public Utilities Commission sets an allowed return, and earnings follow rate base. The problem is the growth rate. On the Q2 2026 call management put California rate-base growth at roughly 5%, against about 11% for the utility platform overall. California earnings rose $24M year over year in the quarter. In a company targeting 7–9% EPS growth, the half that produces most of the earnings is growing at roughly half the corporate target, and the other half has to make up the gap. That is the structural tension in the equity.

Set against that, California is where the tail risk lives. SDG&E is an investor-owned utility operating in high fire-threat districts, and California's inverse-condemnation doctrine has historically exposed utilities to wildfire damages regardless of negligence. Sempra's balance sheet carried $235M of wildfire fund contributions at June 30, 2026 (down from $246M at year-end 2025) — the asset side of its participation in the state wildfire fund. Management's public position on the Q2 call was that "the status quo doesn't work," that the Governor and legislative leaders are actively working on a package, and that it was "premature to evaluate specific proposals without bill language." That is a company waiting on Sacramento.

Earnings Mix (6M)

Oncor is the largest transmission-and-distribution (T&D — the wires business; it delivers power but does not sell it or generate it) utility in Texas: about 18,418 circuit miles of transmission and 4.1 million-plus delivery points. Sempra Texas earnings rose from $208M to $346M in Q2 2026 (+66%) and from $354M to $517M across the half. Two things drove that. First, Oncor's new base rates took effect June 1, 2026, with an August 1 surcharge recovering the difference from the interim period — the rate case lifted the authorised ROE to 9.75% on a 43.5% equity layer, narrowing the gap between earned and allowed returns. Second, volume: Texas load is growing in a way no other US service territory is.

The numbers management put on the Q2 call are the bull case in a sentence. Oncor's "Batch Zero" screening identified 44 GW of large-load interconnection requests in its territory that met eligibility requirements — against a current peak load of 31 GW, i.e. a potential 140% increase. About 8 GW is already ramping onto the system, which is the part that matters: this is not a queue of speculative letters of intent, it is connected and energising load. Management's framing: demand growth in Texas "is not just a projection, but is actively occurring." Oncor also submitted 127 GW of substantiated large load into the 2026 regional transmission plan, and ERCOT has endorsed high-voltage transmission projects requiring more than $7B, most of which Oncor is expected to build.

That converts into capital. Oncor's approved five-year base plan is $47.5B, with roughly $10B of incremental opportunity through 2030 identified on top — a $57.5B total opportunity, comprising about $4B of Dallas–Fort Worth transmission upgrades, $3B of non-Permian reliability work and $3B of system resiliency. Note the arithmetic: Sempra's consolidated 2026–2030 plan assigns $38.2B to Sempra Texas, which is Sempra's share of Oncor's larger $47.5B programme, not the whole of it. Oncor funds itself and Sempra does not consolidate its revenue.

This is the segment that makes Sempra un-comparable to NextEra, Duke, Southern, AEP, Dominion, Exelon or Xcel. Sempra Infrastructure develops and operates LNG (liquefied natural gas) liquefaction and export terminals — Cameron LNG in Louisiana, ECA LNG on Mexico's Pacific coast, and Port Arthur LNG in Texas. Earnings more than doubled in the half, from $218M to $492M, and from $72M to $230M in Q2. But this is development-stage infrastructure with construction risk, not a rate-regulated business with a guaranteed return, and the quarterly numbers include mark-to-market noise from interest-rate swaps.

Port Arthur LNG Phases 1 and 2 were reported "on time and on budget" as of July 2026, with combined nameplate capacity of 26 Mtpa (million tonnes per annum) of new export capacity. Note what that phrasing implies and what it does not: the July 2026 and Q2 2026 disclosures treat Phase 2 as an under-construction asset, not a pending decision — but no Phase 2 FID date, project cost or financing structure was disclosed on any page fetched for this report, and none is asserted here. ECA LNG Phase 1 (3.25 Mtpa, single train) shipped its first cargo in July 2026 and then hit a problem: damage was discovered in the project's refrigeration compressors, pushing substantial completion to Q4 2026 with commercial operations shortly after. That is the LNG business in miniature — long-dated contracted cash flows, and equipment that can slip a quarter without warning.

The funding answer is the KKR transaction. Sempra agreed to sell a 45% equity interest in Sempra Infrastructure Partners to KKR affiliates for roughly $10B, implying about $22.2B of equity value for the whole platform, with closing expected in Q3 2026. The capital plan explicitly assumes 70% SI Partners ownership through Q1 2026 and 25% thereafter. On the call, management said the deal deconsolidates roughly $9B of debt, pays down parent debt, and supports the "displacement of the need for common equity in our current base capital plan" — that is the whole point. Sempra is selling a minority of the non-regulated business to avoid diluting shareholders while funding a $65B regulated build. Separately, the Ecogas México sale closed August 20, 2026 for about $500M. Management also expects a positive Moody's reassessment after close.

Watch item: the KKR close is the single most consequential near-term event. It is the funding mechanism for the capital plan; a delay reopens the equity-issuance question.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
REGULATED MONOPOLY FRANCHISE ONCOR T&D SCALE ERCOT LOAD-GROWTH POSITION RATE-BASE COMPOUNDING PERMITTED LNG SITES CONTRACTED LNG OFFTAKE CAPITAL-RECYCLING OPTIONALITY

The genuine moat is the one every wires utility has and almost nothing else in the economy does: a legal monopoly on delivery within a service territory, with a regulator that sets prices to recover prudently incurred capital plus a return. What makes Sempra's version unusually good right now is where the wires are. Oncor sits in the middle of ERCOT, the one US market where large-load demand is growing at a rate that turns a wires monopoly from a bond substitute into a growth business — 44 GW of eligible interconnection requests against 31 GW of peak load is not an incremental number, and 8 GW of it is already energising. A regulated monopoly with a genuine volume problem is a rare object.

The second, weaker moat is siting. Permitted, financed, under-construction LNG liquefaction on the US Gulf Coast is scarce — Port Arthur's 26 Mtpa across two phases is not something a competitor can replicate quickly. But it is a project moat, not a franchise moat: it depends on execution and on the counterparties honouring long-dated contracts, and ECA's compressor damage is a live reminder that the risk is real and mechanical.

Key Vulnerabilities
01
California wildfire liability is the tail that eats the equity. SDG&E operates in high fire-threat territory in a state whose liability doctrine has historically attached damages to utility equipment regardless of fault. Sempra carries $235M of wildfire fund contributions on the balance sheet at June 30, 2026 — a number that is small relative to what a catastrophic-fire scenario could cost. Management's own words on the Q2 2026 call were that "the status quo doesn't work" and that it is "premature to evaluate specific proposals without bill language." Until California legislates a durable liability framework, this risk is unquantifiable, and unquantifiable risk gets a valuation discount whether or not the fire ever happens.
02
The best asset is the one Sempra does not fully own or consolidate. Oncor is the growth engine, but Sempra holds it as an equity-method interest and Oncor has its own regulator, its own balance sheet and minority owners. Sempra shareholders get a share of Oncor's earnings and none of its cash-flow control. If the thesis is "buy Sempra for Texas load growth," the honest version is "buy a levered, wildfire-exposed holding company for a partial claim on Texas load growth."
03
Funding depends on selling pieces of the business. Free cash flow is −$5.24B TTM: operating cash flow of $5.42B against a capital programme that is far larger. The plan is funded by asset sales — 45% of Sempra Infrastructure to KKR for ~$10B, Ecogas for ~$500M — explicitly to displace the need for common equity. That works once. Buyback yield is already negative (−1.32%), meaning Sempra has been issuing shares. If the KKR close slips or the next $65B plan cannot be funded by selling another minority stake, the equity issuance returns and the growth rate is diluted.
04
California's rate base grows at half the corporate target. Management put California rate-base growth near 5% against ~11% for the utility platform. Half the earnings base compounds at roughly half the promised EPS growth rate, which means Texas and Infrastructure have to carry disproportionately. Any Texas regulatory disappointment — or a slower conversion of the interconnection queue into rate base — pressures the 7–9% target directly.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 the reverse. On monthly data it is a slow, coarse signal — it flags multi-month exhaustion, not entry points.

Over the five years from the October 2021 close of $63.82 (split-adjusted) to $84.04 today, the shares have returned +31.7% in price, roughly 5.7% annualised, before dividends — which over the same period ran between 2.9% and 3.8% a year. Call it high-single-digit total return over five years for a business that has told investors it can compound EPS at 7–9%. The gap between those two numbers is the report.

The shape of the chart matters more than the endpoints. There are three distinct regimes. From late 2021 into early 2022 the stock ran to $84.06 in March 2022 on the energy shock — the same level as today, four and a half years ago. It then spent 2022–2023 grinding lower as rates rose and utility bond substitutes were repriced, bottoming at $68.03 in September 2023, a 19% drawdown from the 2022 peak. From there it rebuilt steadily to $93.67 in November 2024 on the first wave of data-centre enthusiasm.

Then February 2025. The stock fell from $82.93 to $71.57 in a single month — a 13.7% decline, the largest monthly drop in the entire five-year series and larger than anything the 2022 rate shock produced. Sempra's full-year guidance refresh was scheduled for that month; Barclays had written in December 2024 that it expected "a full guidance refresh in February 2025." What the price series shows unambiguously is a step-change in valuation at that point, from which the stock has never fully recovered: the pre-February-2025 high of $93.67 was not exceeded until February 2026, a full year later. The specific contents of the February 2025 guidance revision were not verified from the sources fetched and are therefore not stated here — but the de-rating itself is visible in the data.

The recovery through 2025 into early 2026 took the stock to a five-year closing high of $97.17 in March 2026, and it has given most of that back since: −13.5% from March to the September 4 close, including a 7.7% fall in August alone despite a Q2 that beat on both GAAP and adjusted EPS and affirmed every element of guidance. At $84.04 the shares sit 16.8% below the 52-week high of $101.04 and just 6.4% above the 52-week low. The monthly RSI has spent the summer sliding toward oversold without reaching it — a slow bleed, not a capitulation.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Sempra is the cheapest way to own the best load-growth story in American utilities. Oncor faces 44 GW of eligible large-load interconnection requests against 31 GW of current peak load, with 8 GW already energising, a freshly reset 9.75% authorised ROE and a $57.5B identified capital opportunity through 2030. The $64.9B five-year plan is 95% regulated and is being funded by selling a minority of the LNG platform to KKR for ~$10B rather than by issuing equity. At 16.0× forward earnings with a 3.13% yield and an affirmed 7–9% growth target, the market is paying a discount for exactly the segment mix that produced +66% Texas and +219% Infrastructure earnings growth last quarter.

Bear Case

Half the earnings base sits in California, growing rate base at ~5% against an 11% platform average, and carrying a wildfire liability the company cannot quantify and Sacramento has not yet legislated. Free cash flow is −$5.24B, the funding plan depends on a transaction that has not closed, and buyback yield is already negative. Trailing ROE of 6.68% is below Oncor's own allowed return, and GAAP EPS fell 16% year over year. The de-rating since February 2025 has been persistent, not a dip: the stock is at the same price it reached in March 2022 and 16.8% below its 52-week high, and four desks cut targets in the week before this report despite a beat-and-affirm quarter. The market has seen the Texas numbers and is still not paying.

Industry Commentary
California Wildfire Liability — the sector's unresolved constitutional problem
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

California is the only major US jurisdiction where an investor-owned utility can face damages for a fire started by its equipment without a finding of negligence. That doctrine has already produced one utility bankruptcy in the state. Sempra's exposure runs through SDG&E, and the balance-sheet marker — $235M of wildfire fund contributions at June 30, 2026, down from $246M at year-end 2025 — is a participation asset, not a liability ceiling. On the Q2 2026 call management said the Governor and legislative leaders are "actively working" with "solid progress expected," that "the status quo doesn't work," and that it was "premature to evaluate specific proposals without bill language." Asked whether utilities might contribute to a second phase of fund financing, management framed reform as "public policy for livability, not a utility bailout" and said it would "evaluate the totality of the package." Read that as: a deal is being negotiated, utilities expect to pay something, and nobody will say how much. For the equity, the consequence is mechanical — an unquantifiable tail risk cannot be discounted, so it is capitalised as a permanent multiple discount on the California half of the business.

ERCOT Load Growth — the first real volume growth in US utilities in forty years
EMERGING TAILWIND

US electricity demand was flat to declining for a generation; efficiency gains offset economic growth and utilities grew earnings purely by spending capital on replacement and hardening. Data centres broke that. In Oncor's territory the numbers are extreme: 44 GW of large-load interconnection requests meeting eligibility screens against a 31 GW current peak — a potential 140% increase — with 8 GW already ramping onto the system, and 127 GW of substantiated large load submitted into the 2026 regional transmission plan. ERCOT has endorsed high-voltage transmission projects requiring more than $7B, most of it expected to be built by Oncor. The transmission-and-distribution utility is the one participant in the AI build-out with a legal monopoly and a regulator obliged to let it earn a return on what it builds — no technology risk, no chip cycle, no customer-concentration renegotiation. The caveat is conversion: interconnection requests are not signed load, queues are systematically overstated by speculative applications, and the gap between a 44 GW queue and 8 GW actually connecting is the entire uncertainty. Sempra's own plan assigns $38.2B to Texas out of Oncor's $47.5B approved base and $57.5B identified opportunity — the incremental $10B is optionality, not guidance.

The Conglomerate Question — bull and bear implications
ONGOING HEADWIND

Every comparison in this report is compromised by the same fact: Sempra is three businesses with three risk profiles wearing one ticker. NextEra, Duke, Southern, AEP, Dominion, Exelon and Xcel are all regulated utilities that trade on regulated-utility logic — rate base, allowed ROE, and a stable payout. Sempra has that in California and Texas, plus an LNG export developer with construction risk, commodity-adjacent contracts and a 45% partner arriving from private equity. The bear implication is that this is exactly why the discount exists and why it should persist: investors buying a utility for defensiveness do not want a $22.2B LNG platform attached, the parent structure burns $190M a half in costs, and the Q2 GAAP result had to be adjusted down for interest-rate-swap gains on Port Arthur — earnings quality that a pure utility does not have to explain. The bull implication is that the capital-recycling machine is real and repeatable: Sempra sold 45% of Infrastructure for ~$10B and Ecogas for ~$500M, deconsolidating ~$9B of debt and funding a $65B regulated plan without issuing equity — something a pure-play utility with the same growth would have to fund by diluting shareholders. Whether that is a value-creating structure or an expensive way to avoid the equity market is the argument, and at 16.0× forward earnings the market is currently voting for the second answer.
