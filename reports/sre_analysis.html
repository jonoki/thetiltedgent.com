01 Company Overview

Sempra is a San Diego–based energy holding company that owns three structurally different businesses: Sempra California (San Diego Gas & Electric and SoCalGas — about 1.56 million electric and 7.15 million gas customer meters), Sempra Texas Utilities (its interest in Oncor, the largest transmission-and-distribution utility in Texas, with about 18,418 circuit miles of transmission line and more than 4.1 million delivery points), and Sempra Infrastructure (LNG liquefaction and export — Cameron LNG, ECA LNG and Port Arthur LNG). FY2025 revenue was $13.70B, up 3.9% from $13.19B; TTM revenue through September 2026 is $13.59B, down 0.9%. The market cap at the November 6, 2026 close is $51.13B.

Revenue is close to meaningless here, and that is the first thing to internalise. A regulated utility earns an allowed return on the capital it has put in the ground — its rate base — so the number that drives the share price is the capital plan, not the top line. Sempra's plan is ~$64.9B over 2026–2030, with 59% at Oncor in Texas, 36% in California and just 5% at Infrastructure, and Oncor now projects its 2027–2031 base plan about 20% higher than its 2026–2030 plan. Sempra Texas contributes earnings but essentially no consolidated revenue, because Sempra books only its share of Oncor's profit (an equity-method interest) — which is why $13.59B of revenue sits alongside a segment that produced $977M of nine-month earnings.

The central debate on this page: the quarter was strong and the shares still fell. Adjusted EPS rose 26% to $1.40, Texas earnings rose 50%, and management now points to the high end of its 2026 range — yet the stock lost 3.7% on results day and another 2.1% the next. The same release carried the bad news: on October 29 California's regulator proposed denying a rate correction worth $188M of 2027 revenue, which could put 2027 EPS in the lower half of its range. The shares trade at 16.7× trailing P/E on GAAP earnings and 14.2× the analyst consensus for 2027, with a 3.36% yield and a 7–9% long-term EPS growth target. Two of the three businesses carry risks that a normal regulated utility does not: California wildfire liability, which has no clear ceiling on the SDG&E side, and an LNG development arm that is capital-hungry, tied to commodity markets and not rate-regulated at all. The bull case is that Oncor's Texas load growth is now the main earnings driver and the market has not paid for it. Both cases are examined below.

San Diego Gas & Electric and Southern California Gas together have about 1.56 million electric and 7.15 million gas customer meters (September 30, 2026). This is textbook regulated utility economics — capital is invested, the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) sets an allowed return, and earnings follow rate base. The problem is the growth rate. On the Q2 2026 call management put California rate-base growth at roughly 5%, against about 11% for the utility platform overall. Q3 California earnings fell to $223M from $370M, and the release shows why: $149M after tax of net regulatory disallowances (costs the regulator will not let the company recover). Before tax the 10-Q shows $205M: SoCalGas charges of $92M on its pipeline-integrity programme and $217M on Track 3 of the rate case, partly offset by a $104M credit at SDG&E. On October 29 the CPUC approved a revised decision that cuts the pipeline disallowance by about $30M. In a company targeting 7–9% EPS growth, the segment with the largest nine-month earnings is growing its rate base at about 5%, so the others have to make up the gap. That is the tension in the stock.

The new problem is a rate correction. In December 2025 SDG&E and SoCalGas asked the CPUC to fix how the 2024 rate case handled capital spending after its test year. On October 29, 2026 the CPUC issued a proposed decision denying the request, and the companies expect a final decision in Q4 2026. The 2027 revenue at stake is $188M ($79M at SDG&E and $109M at SoCalGas, per the 10-Q). If the proposal stands, Sempra says California's 2027 earnings would be at the low end of the segment's guidance range and company EPS could land in the lower half of its 2027 range unless mitigated. CFO Karen Sedgwick called it a one-year issue on the call, since new rates from the 2028 rate case are expected in January 2028.

Set against that, California is where the worst-case risk lives. SDG&E is an investor-owned utility operating in high fire-threat districts, and California's inverse-condemnation doctrine has historically exposed utilities to wildfire damages regardless of negligence. Sempra's balance sheet carried $226M of wildfire fund contributions at September 30, 2026 (down from $246M at year-end 2025) — the asset side of its participation in the state wildfire fund. On the Q2 call management said the current arrangement does not work, that the Governor and legislative leaders were working on a package, and that it was too early to judge proposals before bill language existed. A live example arrived in October: the Norte fire near San Clemente on October 16, 2026. A lawsuit filed October 19 against SDG&E, Enova Corporation and Sempra alleges SDG&E's electrical facilities caused it; the Orange County Fire Authority is still investigating the cause (Q3 2026 10-Q).

Oncor is the largest transmission-and-distribution (T&D — the wires business; it delivers power but does not sell it or generate it) utility in Texas: about 18,418 circuit miles of transmission and 4.1 million-plus delivery points. Sempra Texas earnings rose from $306M to $460M in Q3 2026 (+50%) and from $660M to $977M across the nine months. Two things sit behind that. First, Oncor's new base rates took effect June 1, 2026, with a surcharge recovering the difference for January 1 to June 1 — the rate case lifted the authorised return on equity (ROE, the profit rate the regulator allows on shareholders' capital) to 9.75%, with equity at 43.5% of the capital structure. Second, volume: Oncor set a new system peak of 33 GW this year, 6% above its previous record.

The bull case starts with large new requests for power. ERCOT, the Texas grid operator, is running a single study of them called Batch Zero. On the Q2 call Martin said 44 GW of requests in Oncor's territory were expected to qualify, against the 31 GW peak load quoted at the time, a potential 140% increase, with about 8 GW already connected. The Q3 release updates that: ERCOT has conditionally classified about 24 GW as base load, including 10 GW already energised, and 21 GW as studied load, the two categories in its study. The energised part is what matters most: it is connected and drawing power, not a queue of speculative letters of intent. Oncor also submitted 127 GW of substantiated large load into the 2026 regional transmission plan, and on the Q2 call Martin counted about $7B of ERCOT-endorsed transmission projects among Oncor's incremental opportunities ($4B of North and Central Texas upgrades and $3B of non-Permian projects). On the Q3 call Oncor CEO Allen Nye said ERCOT is sending a second request for information to about 80% of Batch Zero customers and still aims to finish validating them in mid-December.

That converts into capital. Oncor's existing five-year base plan is $47.5B, and it now projects its 2027–2031 base plan about 20% higher, roughly $57B on simple arithmetic. Nye attributed the rise to adding 2031, continued customer and load growth, and projects moving into the base plan; Martin said it leaves out the Eastern STEP 765-kilovolt projects, a system resiliency programme and projects tied to Batch Zero. Sempra will announce its full 2027–2031 plan on the February 2027 call. Note the arithmetic: Sempra's consolidated 2026–2030 plan assigns $38.2B to Sempra Texas, which is Sempra's share of Oncor's larger $47.5B programme, not the whole of it. Sempra does not consolidate Oncor's revenue.

This is the segment that makes Sempra hard to compare with NextEra, Duke, Southern, AEP, Dominion, Exelon or Xcel. Sempra Infrastructure develops and operates LNG (liquefied natural gas) liquefaction and export terminals — Cameron LNG in Louisiana, ECA LNG on Mexico's Pacific coast, and Port Arthur LNG in Texas. Earnings swung from a $362M loss in the first nine months of 2025 to $838M, and from a $580M loss to $346M in Q3; the prior-year loss came from $514M of tax expense tied to the planned sale, and Q3 2026 includes a $97M after-tax gain on selling Ecogas. This is development-stage infrastructure with construction risk, not a rate-regulated business with an allowed return, and the quarterly numbers also carry paper gains and losses on interest-rate hedges.

Port Arthur LNG Phases 1 and 2 were reported on time and on budget as of July 2026, with combined nameplate capacity of 26 Mtpa (million tonnes per annum) of new export capacity. The July 2026 and Q2 2026 disclosures treat Phase 2 as an under-construction asset, as it has been since Sempra Infrastructure Partners took the final investment decision on September 22, 2025: about 13 Mtpa, roughly $14 billion of estimated capital expenditure, and 49.9% of the project's equity sold to a Blackstone-led consortium (Sempra Form 8-K, Sept 23, 2025). ECA LNG Phase 1 (3.25 Mtpa, single train) shipped its first cargo in July 2026, and planned inspections then found damage in the project's refrigerant compressors. Sempra said in July that substantial completion would slip to Q4 2026, subject to the root-cause investigation and repairs, with sales under long-term contracts starting shortly after. The Q3 10-Q still lists the project as under construction and says Sempra keeps substantially similar funding obligations for its cost overruns after the KKR sale. That is the LNG business in miniature — long-dated contracted cash flows, and equipment that can slip a quarter without warning.

The funding answer is the KKR transaction. Sempra agreed in September 2025 to sell a 45% equity interest in Sempra Infrastructure Partners to KKR affiliates for roughly $10B (base price about $9.99B), implying about $22.2B of equity value for the whole platform. The closing has moved: previously expected in Q3 2026, it is now expected in November 2026, with the release saying within two weeks of November 5. The parties extended the date after which either can walk away to November 27, 2026, and KKR would owe a $414M termination fee if it failed to close once conditions were met (10-Q). On the call Justin Bird said the two remaining conditions are consents from the Cameron partners and from Japanese lending agencies. After closing, KKR's group will own 65%, Sempra 25% and ADIA 10%, and SI Partners leaves Sempra's consolidated accounts, taking $9.70B of debt now shown as held for sale with it. The estimated gain of $2.0–$2.4B is why 2026 GAAP EPS guidance jumped to $7.41–$8.41. Selling a minority of the non-regulated business to avoid diluting shareholders while funding a $65B regulated build is the whole point. Separately, the Ecogas México sale closed August 20, 2026 for $524M net of costs. On the Q2 call, asked about Moody's negative outlook on the company, CFO Sedgwick said she expected the outlooks to improve once more than $9B of debt leaves the balance sheet, probably early next year.

Watch item: the KKR close is the single most consequential near-term event. It is the funding mechanism for the capital plan; a further delay reopens the equity-issuance question.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
REGULATED MONOPOLY FRANCHISE ONCOR T&D SCALE ERCOT LOAD-GROWTH POSITION RATE-BASE GROWTH PERMITTED LNG SITES CONTRACTED LNG OFFTAKE ASSET SALES THAT FUND GROWTH

The genuine moat is the one every wires utility has and almost nothing else in the economy does: a legal monopoly on delivery within a service territory, with a regulator that sets prices to recover prudently incurred capital plus a return. What makes Sempra's version unusually good right now is where the wires are. Oncor sits in the middle of ERCOT, a market where large-load demand is growing fast enough to turn a wires monopoly from a steady, bond-like holding into a growth business — the Q3 release classifies about 24 GW of large load as base load, including 10 GW already energised, and another 21 GW as studied load, against a new system peak of 33 GW. A regulated monopoly with this much new volume is a rare object.

The second, weaker moat is siting. Permitted, financed, under-construction LNG liquefaction on the US Gulf Coast is scarce — Port Arthur's 26 Mtpa across two phases is not something a competitor can replicate quickly. But it is a project moat, not a franchise moat: it depends on execution and on the counterparties honouring long-dated contracts, and ECA's compressor damage is a live reminder that the risk is real and mechanical.

Key Vulnerabilities
01
California wildfire liability is the worst-case risk for shareholders. SDG&E operates in high fire-threat territory in a state whose liability doctrine has historically attached damages to utility equipment regardless of fault. Sempra carried $226M of wildfire fund contributions on the balance sheet at September 30, 2026 — a number that is small relative to what a catastrophic-fire scenario could cost. A lawsuit over the October 16, 2026 Norte fire alleges SDG&E's facilities caused it; the cause is still under investigation. Until California legislates a durable liability framework, this risk cannot be measured, and investors pay less for a stock with a risk they cannot size, whether or not the fire ever happens.
02
The best asset is the one Sempra does not fully own or consolidate. Oncor is the growth engine, but Sempra holds it as an equity-method interest and Oncor has its own regulator, its own balance sheet and minority owners. Sempra shareholders get a share of Oncor's earnings and none of its cash-flow control. If the thesis is "buy Sempra for Texas load growth," the honest version is "buy a debt-funded, wildfire-exposed holding company for a partial claim on Texas load growth."
03
Funding depends on selling pieces of the business. Free cash flow is −$4.87B TTM: operating cash flow of $5.87B against capital spending of $10.74B. The plan is funded by asset sales — 45% of Sempra Infrastructure to KKR for ~$10B, Ecogas for $524M — explicitly to displace the need for common equity. That works once. Buyback yield is already negative (−0.77%), meaning Sempra has been issuing shares. If the KKR close slips or the next five-year plan, which is set to be larger, cannot be funded by selling another minority stake, the equity issuance returns and the growth rate is diluted.
04
California's rate base grows more slowly than the corporate target. Management put California rate-base growth near 5% against ~11% for the utility platform. The segment with the largest nine-month earnings is growing its rate base more slowly than the 7–9% EPS target, which means Texas and Infrastructure have to carry disproportionately — and the October 29 proposed decision shows California can subtract as well. Any Texas regulatory disappointment, or a slower conversion of the interconnection queue into rate base, pressures the 7–9% target directly.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 the reverse. On monthly data it is a slow, coarse signal — it flags multi-month exhaustion, not entry points.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $59.94 (split-adjusted) to $78.17 on November 6, 2026, the shares have returned +30.4% in price, roughly 5.5% annualised, before dividends. Add a dividend yield of roughly 3% and call it a high-single-digit total return over five years. The company has told investors it can grow EPS at 7–9% a year; the shares have risen 5.5% a year. That gap is the report.

The shape of the chart matters more than the endpoints. There are three distinct phases. From late 2021 into early 2022 the stock ran to $84.06 in March 2022 on the energy shock, a level it is 7.0% below today, more than four and a half years later. It then spent 2022–2023 grinding lower as interest rates rose and investors who buy utilities for steady income, as an alternative to bonds, marked them down, reaching $68.03 in September 2023, a 19% fall from the 2022 peak. From there it rebuilt steadily to $93.67 in November 2024 on the first wave of data-centre enthusiasm.

Then February 2025. The stock fell from $82.93 to $71.57 in a single month — a 13.7% decline, the largest monthly drop in the entire series and larger than anything the 2022 rate shock produced. On February 25 Sempra lowered its 2025 EPS guidance to $4.30–$4.70. The month-end high of $93.67 was not beaten until November 2025 ($94.72), nine months after the drop.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Sempra offers a way to own one of the strongest load-growth stories in American utilities at a low price. Oncor's new system peak of 33 GW sits beneath about 45 GW of large load conditionally classified in ERCOT's Batch Zero study, 10 GW of it already energised, with a freshly reset 9.75% authorised return and a base capital plan now projected about 20% higher for 2027–2031. The $64.9B five-year plan is 95% regulated and is being funded by selling a minority of the LNG platform to KKR for ~$10B rather than by issuing equity, with closing expected within weeks. Q3 adjusted EPS rose 26% and management now points to the high end of its 2026 range. At 14.2× consensus 2027 earnings with a 3.36% yield and an affirmed 7–9% growth target, the market is paying a discount for exactly the mix that produced +50% Texas earnings growth last quarter.

Bear Case

The largest earnings segment sits in California, growing rate base at ~5% against an 11% platform average, and it has just taken $149M of net after-tax regulatory disallowances and a proposed decision that could push 2027 EPS into the lower half of guidance. California still has no lasting law on wildfire liability, and a lawsuit over the October Norte fire alleges SDG&E's facilities caused it. Free cash flow is −$4.87B, the funding plan depends on a transaction that has not closed and has already slipped from its original timetable, and buyback yield is negative. The slide since March has been persistent: the stock is 22.6% below its 52-week high and 2.6% above its low, and it fell after earnings beats in both August and November. The market has seen the Texas numbers and is still not paying.

Industry Commentary
California Wildfire Liability — the sector's unresolved constitutional problem
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

In California an investor-owned utility can face damages for a fire started by its equipment without a finding of negligence, under a doctrine called inverse condemnation. That doctrine has already produced one utility bankruptcy in the state. Sempra's exposure runs through SDG&E, and the balance-sheet marker — $226M of wildfire fund contributions at September 30, 2026, down from $246M at year-end 2025 — is a participation asset, not a liability ceiling. On the Q2 2026 call management said the current arrangement does not work, that the Governor and legislative leaders were working on a package and that it was too early to judge proposals without bill language. Asked whether utilities might contribute to a second phase of fund financing, management framed reform as public policy rather than a utility bailout and said it would weigh the package as a whole. On the Q3 call Martin said he could not say whether a bill would be filed before the legislature's deadline but saw a clear path to action this fall or next spring. Read that as: a deal is being negotiated, utilities may be asked to pay, and nobody will say how much. Meanwhile the October 16, 2026 Norte fire has already produced a lawsuit against SDG&E and Sempra, with the cause still under investigation. For shareholders, the consequence is straightforward: a risk that cannot be sized lowers the price investors will pay for the California half of the business.

ERCOT Load Growth — the first real volume growth in US utilities in forty years
EMERGING TAILWIND

US electricity demand was flat to declining for a generation; efficiency gains offset economic growth and utilities grew earnings purely by spending capital on replacement and hardening. Data centres broke that. In Oncor's territory the figures are extreme: about 45 GW of large load conditionally classified in ERCOT's Batch Zero study, 10 GW of it already energised (Q3 2026 release), and 127 GW of substantiated large load submitted into the 2026 regional transmission plan, against a new system peak of 33 GW. ERCOT has endorsed about $7B of transmission projects that Oncor counts among its incremental opportunities, and Oncor now projects its 2027–2031 base plan about 20% above the $47.5B 2026–2030 plan. Sempra's CEO also pointed to a significant amount of non-data-centre growth. The transmission-and-distribution utility is the one participant in the AI build-out with a legal monopoly and a regulator obliged to let it earn a return on what it builds — no technology risk, no chip cycle, no customer renegotiating a contract. The caveat is conversion: interconnection requests are not signed load, queues are overstated by speculative applications, and ERCOT is now sending about 80% of Batch Zero customers a second request for information. The gap between about 45 GW in the study and 10 GW already energised is the entire uncertainty.

The Conglomerate Question — bull and bear implications
ONGOING HEADWIND

Every comparison in this report is compromised by the same fact: Sempra is three businesses with three risk profiles wearing one ticker. NextEra, Duke, Southern, AEP, Dominion, Exelon and Xcel are all regulated utilities that trade on regulated-utility logic — rate base, allowed ROE, and a stable payout. Sempra has that in California and Texas, plus an LNG export developer with construction risk, commodity-linked contracts and a majority partner arriving from private equity. The bear implication is that this is exactly why the discount exists and why it should persist: investors buying a utility for defensiveness do not want a $22.2B LNG platform attached, the parent structure cost $345M in nine months, and Q3 GAAP results carried gains on Ecogas and on Port Arthur hedges that a pure utility does not have to explain. The bull implication is that selling assets to fund growth works: Sempra sold Ecogas for $524M and is selling 45% of Infrastructure for ~$10B, moving $9.70B of debt off its accounts and funding a $65B regulated plan without issuing much equity — something a utility with no such assets and the same growth would have to fund by diluting shareholders. Whether that is a value-creating structure or an expensive way to avoid the equity market is the argument, and at 14.2× consensus 2027 earnings the market is currently voting for the second answer.
