01 Company Overview

State Street is one of the three large Western custody and trust banks — the plumbing layer of institutional asset management. It holds, settles, values, reports on and lends out other people's securities, and separately manages money of its own through State Street Investment Management, the sponsor of the SPDR ETF family. At September 30, 2026 it held a record $59.17 trillion of assets under custody and administration (AUC/A — client assets it safekeeps and services, which it does not own), up 15% year over year, and managed a record $6.31 trillion of AUM, up 16%. FY2025 revenue was $13.89B, up 7.4%; the trailing twelve months through September 2026 is $15.50B, up 13.7% (stockanalysis.com). Market capitalisation is $45.73B (the $167.60 close times 272.83M shares), which places it #248 in the S&P 500.

The central debate is the same one as last edition: are the last twelve months the new run rate or the peak of one? The third quarter gave a mixed answer. On the company's own numbers it was strong: EPS of $3.69 (up 32.7%), a 34.6% pre-tax margin, a 25.1% ROTCE, no notable items (the one-off costs, such as restructuring, repositioning and legal charges, that management strips out of its comparisons), and full-year guidance raised again after a July raise. But growth slowed in every line: assets under custody up 14.5% (18.1% in Q2), assets under management up 15.8% (22.7%), fee revenue up 11% (17.2%), and revenue of $4,004M was 1.1% lower than Q2's $4,048M and slightly under forecasts of about $4.03B to $4.04B. Net interest margin (NIM) was 1.12%, up 16 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) on the year but down 1 basis point from Q2. The shares fell 4.14% on the day of the release and the company did not comment on the move. Both tailwinds that drove 2026 — rising asset prices and wider deposit spreads — still work; they are simply no longer accelerating.

This is the business that makes State Street systemically important. Asset owners and asset managers hand over securities; State Street safekeeps them, settles trades, calculates net asset values, handles corporate actions, produces regulatory and investor reporting, and increasingly runs the client's entire front-to-back technology stack through Alpha — the platform that bundles the custodian's back office with portfolio management and trading software. The ratio moves with the mix of deals, but both quarters show how thin the pricing is and how much volume is needed to move revenue. A custodian's deposits are a by-product, not a product: cash sits at the bank between trades and between fund subscriptions and redemptions, and State Street earns a spread on it. That makes NII the part of the P&L least under management's control and most exposed to the rate path and to client cash behaviour. Q3 NII was $864M, up 21% year over year and up from $860M in Q2, on a NIM of 1.12% (1.13% in Q2). Management raised full-year NII growth guidance to 15–16% from 14–15%, and the CFO said NII should rise from the third quarter again in the fourth, “towards the upper end of that range” (Q3 call, via the fool.com transcript). Note what a 1.12% NIM means in context: a commercial bank typically runs far higher, because it lends. State Street is not a lender; it is a very large, very low-risk balance sheet earning a thin spread on other people's transactional cash. Credit risk is correspondingly small: the Q3 provision for credit losses was a $4M release (a negative provision).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SWITCHING-COST LOCK-IN OLIGOPOLY OF THREE REGULATORY CAPITAL BARRIER SPDR BRAND & SPY LIQUIDITY ALPHA FRONT-TO-BACK STACK SCALE ECONOMIES IN OPS GLOBAL SETTLEMENT NETWORK

The moat is switching cost, and it is unusually literal. Moving $500B of assets from one custodian to another means re-papering every sub-custody relationship in every market, re-pointing every accounting and NAV feed, re-testing every regulatory report, and running both systems in parallel for months — all while the fund must continue to strike a daily price that is legally binding on investors. The cost is measured in years and reputational risk, not basis points, which is precisely why a business earning about 1.6 to 2.3 basis points on newly won assets (Q3 and Q2 2026) can still be a durable franchise. Regulation reinforces it: a global custodian must hold regulatory capital, maintain settlement infrastructure in dozens of jurisdictions, and satisfy supervisors in all of them, which is a barrier no software company can cross with code alone. The Alpha platform deliberately deepens the trench — once the client's portfolio management and trading systems also run on State Street's rails, the switching cost stops being operational and becomes existential.

What the moat does not do is protect pricing. Custody pricing is thin, as the basis-point figures above show; the franchise defends volume, not rate. And the moat protects almost nothing in Investment Management, where SPDR competes head-on with two larger sponsors on a product — index exposure — that is commoditised by construction. The evidence for the moat working is in the operating leverage: Q3 2026 revenue grew 12.9% while expenses grew about 8%, producing 531 basis points of positive operating leverage (revenue growth minus expense growth) — the eleventh consecutive quarter of positive operating leverage excluding notable items — and lifting the pre-tax margin to 34.6%, up 350 basis points from a year earlier (earnings release). Neither Q3 2026 nor Q3 2025 had notable items, so the figure needs no adjustment; Q2's headline figure was lifted by one-off charges in the year-earlier quarter.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Revenue is a levered bet on asset prices. Both halves of the company charge fees on market value. AUC/A rose 14.5% and AUM rose 15.8% in twelve months, and fee revenue rose 11%: fees follow asset values, but at a lower rate than assets grow, and the gap has widened. A 20% global drawdown would compress the fee base mechanically, with no execution failure required. This is the single most important structural fact about the name, and it is why a 13.7× trailing earnings multiple on near-record earnings is not obviously cheap.
▸
NII is exogenous. Net interest income is 21.6% of revenue and grew 21% in Q3 on 16 basis points of NIM expansion. State Street does not set policy rates and does not control whether clients leave cash on deposit or sweep it into money-market funds. The guidance raise to 15–16% NII growth is a forecast about the rate environment as much as about the business, and a cutting cycle plus deposit migration would reverse it without management doing anything wrong.
▸
Structural fee compression in custody, structural fee compression in passive. The newly-won-assets rate of roughly 1.6 to 2.3 basis points (Q3 and Q2 2026) shows how little each new dollar pays. In asset management, SPDR competes with two larger sponsors, BlackRock and Vanguard, which have more scale and, in Vanguard's case, a mutual ownership structure that lets it treat fees as a cost recovery rather than a profit centre. Growth in both segments has to come from volume.
▸
Operational and concentration risk, not credit risk. Credit losses are negligible (a $4M reserve release in Q3 2026). The tail risk is different in kind: a settlement failure, a NAV error across thousands of funds, a cyber incident in the servicing stack, or a control breakdown in FX pricing. These are low-frequency, high-severity events that damage the one asset the moat rests on — client trust — and they do not show up in any ratio on the table below.

Notable One-Off Items

Q3 had no notable items, and neither did the year-earlier quarter (earnings release), so its 531 basis points of operating leverage needs no adjustment. Q2's headline did: its 1,226 basis points of operating leverage was 645 basis points excluding notable items, because the year-earlier quarter, Q2 2025, carried $138M of them (Q2 release). On the like-for-like measure, operating leverage was 645bps in Q2 and 531bps in Q3, and the CFO said the company expects to deliver over 500bps of positive operating leverage in 2026 (Q3 call).

Credit provision. Q3 2026 recorded a $4M provision release (a negative provision). That helps profit slightly, but it is a release of reserves rather than an operating result, and it cannot repeat indefinitely.

The comparison base. FY2023 net income of $1,821M and EPS of $5.58 sit far below the later years (FY2024 $2,483M / $8.21; FY2025 $2,717M / $9.40). TTM EPS of $12.23 is 2.2 times the FY2023 figure, but only 49% above FY2024's. Any "EPS has doubled since 2023" framing measures from a depressed base.

Share count. Q3 EPS grew 32.7% while net income available to common shareholders grew 28.4% ($1,030M against $802M): average diluted shares fell 3.1% (279.3M against 288.2M), and a smaller share count lifts earnings per share faster than total profit (earnings release). Shares outstanding are 272.83M, down from 274.70M in the previous edition, a 0.7% fall. The company bought back $400M of stock in Q3.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — a stock can stay overbought for a long time in a genuine re-rating.

Digesting the print
08 Summary & Key Risks

Build the earnings from the top, the way a custodian actually earns. Start with client assets: $59.17T of AUC/A (up 14.5%) and $6.31T of AUM (up 15.8%). Both are charged in basis points on market value. That produced fee revenue of $3,140M in Q3 2026, up 11%, a slower rate than the asset base grew. Separately, $864M of net interest income, up 21%, at a 1.12% NIM (up 16bps from a year earlier). Add them and total revenue was $4,004M, up 12.9%. Expenses grew about 8% to $2,620M, so the pre-tax margin rose to 34.6% (up 350bps) and net income rose 26.6% to $1,090M. Buybacks turned that into 32.7% EPS growth, $3.69 versus $2.78. Every figure in that chain is from the earnings release (October 14, 2026).

Now ask what produced it. Fee revenue grew 11% because the asset base grew 14.5% and each new dollar of assets pays less. The asset base grew because global markets rose. NII grew 21% because the margin widened 16 basis points, which is a function of the yield curve and of clients' willingness to leave cash on deposit. Neither engine is something State Street built. What State Street contributed is the third term — expense discipline — holding cost growth to about 8% against 12.9% revenue growth is what converts a market tailwind into a 25.1% ROTCE. Management's medium-term targets of a 35% pre-tax margin and a mid-20s ROTCE are set at approximately where the company already is (34.6% and 25.1%). If the targets are the destination, the journey is largely complete.

What changed in the quarter. Growth slowed in every line: AUC/A growth fell from 18.1% to 14.5%, AUM from 22.7% to 15.8%, fee revenue from 17.2% to 11%. Fee revenue fell 1.5% from Q2 and total revenue 1.1%, though NII edged up from $860M to $864M. Net inflows into the asset manager dropped from $114B to $7B, which the CFO attributed in part to clients redeploying cash balances. On the other side, guidance was raised, there were no notable items, and Q3's operating leverage of 531 basis points was clean. The shares fell on the day anyway; the company did not explain the move, and the slower growth rates and the small revenue miss are the numbers that pointed the other way from the raised guide.

Valuation — and which earnings base. On TTM EPS of $12.23, STT trades at 13.70×, below the two-name custody-peer average of 14.99×. On the 2026 consensus of $13.80 it trades at 12.14×. On book value it is 1.85×. The PEG of 0.64 looks compelling until you notice that the growth in the denominator is built on market levels and rate spreads. The real question is which line you capitalise. If $12.23 is a cyclical peak, 13.7× is a full multiple on normalised earnings. If ROTCE genuinely holds in the mid-20s — as management's medium-term target asserts — then a business earning 25% on tangible equity, returning about 5.6% a year in dividends and buybacks, at 12–14× earnings, is cheap. The panel splits along that line: seven Holds against ten positive ratings, with targets from $152 to $230.

Where State Street sits versus the sector. On the October 15 closes STT has the lowest trailing multiple of the three custody banks (13.70× against BNY's 15.74× and Northern Trust's 14.23×) and the lowest net profit margin (22.26% against BNY's 29.74% and Northern Trust's 24.67%, stockanalysis.com). Its fee and NII growth rates are what the quarter's guidance supports; the peers' own comparable growth rates are not shown here.

Bull Case

A systemically entrenched custodian with switching costs measured in years is growing client assets at 14.5% while holding expense growth to about 8%, producing a 34.6% pre-tax margin and a 25.1% ROTCE — numbers that would flatter a specialty asset manager, let alone a trust bank. Record servicing and management fees, a clean quarter with no notable items, and guidance raised again after a July raise: fee growth to 13–14% and NII growth to 15–16%. Capital is being returned aggressively — a 10% dividend raise, about a 3.4% buyback yield, a payout approaching 80% — supported by an 11.0% CET1. And at 12.14× 2026 consensus EPS the shares are cheaper than the peer average and are 14.5% below their 52-week high.

Bear Case

Both engines are exogenous. Fee revenue rose 11% because client assets rose 14.5% on rising markets; NII rose 21% on 16 basis points of NIM that the Federal Reserve, not State Street, set. The growth rates are slowing in every line, revenue missed forecasts slightly and fell from Q2, and net inflows into the asset manager were $7B against $114B a quarter earlier. Management's medium-term targets are set where the company already stands, so there is little built-in improvement. The business carries a 1.42 beta and thin pricing on new custody business (about 1.6 basis points on Q3 wins), and the shares fell 4.1% on a beat-and-raise quarter, which suggests the market already paid for a lot.

Top 3 Risks

01
Market-value gearing works in reverse. Fees in both segments are charged on the market value of client assets. AUC/A and AUM are at records; a meaningful global drawdown compresses the entire fee base mechanically, with a lag of one quarter and no management error required. With a beta of 1.42, the stock would take that hit twice — once through earnings and once through the multiple.
02
The rate and deposit cycle turning. NII is 21.6% of revenue and just grew 21% on margin expansion. A cutting cycle compresses the asset yield faster than the deposit cost, and a risk-on environment pushes clients to sweep transactional cash into money funds, shrinking the deposit base. Guidance of 15–16% NII growth is a forecast about the macro environment; nothing in the franchise defends it. NIM already slipped 1bp from Q2 to Q3.
03
Slowing growth against a high earnings base. The bull case requires 2026's earnings to be a floor rather than a ceiling, while management's own medium-term targets (35% pre-tax margin, mid-20s ROTCE) sit essentially at today's level and the credit provision is already a small release. If growth in assets and fees keeps decelerating, the 13.7× trailing multiple will be tested, and the $152 low target — 9% below the price — is the number to watch.
  Industry Commentary
Custody Pricing Is Thin
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The structural fact of this industry is that custody is paid for in hundredths of a percent of assets. State Street's own disclosure quantifies it: $68M of new annual servicing fee revenue won on $429B of AUC/A in Q3 — roughly 1.6 basis points (our arithmetic), after $87M on $384B (about 2.3 basis points) in Q2. In Q3, servicing fees rose 9.9% on an asset base that rose 14.5%. The industry's answers are volume and cost: State Street is pursuing both, with a transformation programme targeting about $1B of annual run-rate benefits by 2029 and a Q3 efficiency ratio of 65.4%.
Two Custody Banks Reported Back to Back
NEW THIS QUARTER
State Street reported on October 14 and BNY on October 15. On October 14 State Street closed down 4.14%, BNY down 2.4% and Northern Trust down 2.9%; on October 15 BNY closed up 3.2%, Northern Trust up 1.7% and State Street up 0.43% (daily closes). The group moved together in direction on both days, but State Street fell furthest on its own results and recovered least. The factors that drive all three — asset prices and deposit spreads — are the same, so a drawdown in either would hit all three.
Tokenisation: Threat, or the Next Custody Product?
EMERGING TAILWIND
On-chain settlement is theoretically the technology that makes a custodian unnecessary — a distributed ledger is a record of who owns what. State Street is betting the opposite: on the Q3 call it said it was on track to deliver a tokenised fund servicing capability by year-end 2026 and that traditional and digital finance would operate alongside each other for a fairly long period (Q3 call). Bull framing: tokenised funds still need NAV calculation, regulatory reporting, corporate actions and a legally accountable custodian, so tokenisation adds an asset class to service. Bear framing: if institutional assets move to ledgers where settlement is instant and ownership is self-evident, the fee pool for reconciliation and settlement shrinks, and 1.6–2.3 basis points on new business leaves little room to absorb that.
