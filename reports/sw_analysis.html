01 Company Overview

Somebody has to make the box. Smurfit Westrock makes containerboard, corrugated boxes, paperboard and consumer packaging across roughly 40 countries, with about 97,000 employees. It is the product of a July 2024 combination: Ireland's Smurfit Kappa — a Dublin box-maker since 1934, acquired by Jefferson Smurfit in 1938 — bought Atlanta-area WestRock. The deal closed on July 5, 2024; the combined shares began trading on the NYSE and in London on July 8, 2024, the same day the stock entered the S&P 500. Smurfit Kappa holders ended up with about 50.4% of the new company.

FY2025 net sales were $31.18B with net income of $699M and Adjusted EBITDA of $4.94B (15.8% margin). The reported "+47.7% revenue growth" for 2025 is merger arithmetic, not demand: 2024 only includes WestRock from July 5. The real story is a cycle trade. Box demand in North America was soft through 2025 and into 2026 — North American corrugated volumes fell 4.8% in Q2 2026 — while freight and energy costs rose. Then, in about six weeks in mid-2026, the paper market turned. The CEO called global paper markets "as strong as I have seen in my lifetime within this industry", and the industry pushed through its third round of containerboard price increases of the year. The question at $45.41 is simple to state: do higher paper prices reach the income statement before freight and energy costs eat them?

The two operating metrics that actually matter

In a commodity paper business, profit is price minus cost, times volume. Price is being pushed up. Cost is rising too. Watch both lines.

Containerboard price action
+$100/ton
SW's containerboard and kraft paper increase from September 1, 2026. It followed Packaging Corporation of America's $140/ton announcement, which industry observers called unprecedented and about double a typical increase. International Paper followed with $80/ton. An announced increase only counts once customers actually pay it; Q3 and Q4 results will show how much sticks.
2026 cost inflation
~$520M
FY2026 guidance includes about $300M of extra freight cost and about $220M of extra energy cost versus 2025, with about $80M and $70M of that landing in Q3. That is roughly 10% of the $4.9–5.1B EBITDA guide. Packaging Dive reports input costs came in about $300M higher than management expected three months earlier.
Adj. EBITDA guidance
$4.9–5.1B
FY2026, cut in July from $5.0–5.3B set in February. Q3 guide is about $1.3B, against $1,076M in Q1 and $1,140M in Q2. Hitting the full-year range therefore depends on a strong Q3 and Q4.
Footprint rationalisation
~600k tons
About 600,000 tons of high-cost capacity closed and 3,000+ jobs removed through FY2025, with the $400M synergy target exceeded in 2025 (company FY2025 release). In 2026 so far: one UK mill and eight more converting sites in progress.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Integrated mill-to-box system Scale: ~40 countries Local box-plant density Mill capital intensity deters entry Multinational customer reach Paper-based substitution for plastic

A box does not travel well: it is bulky, cheap and heavy relative to its value. So the moat is local density: box plants within delivery distance of customers, supplied by the company's own mills. That structure is hard to copy, because a new containerboard mill costs billions and takes years to build. It protects market position. It does not protect price. Containerboard is a commodity, and SW's margins still follow the industry supply-and-demand balance. The mid-2026 tightening shows this: SW did not create it, but it gains from it.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Price takers on cost, price setters only when the cycle allows. Freight and energy were guided about $520M higher for 2026. Price increases only offset that if customers accept them. After two rounds earlier in the year, a third round of increases is a bigger ask of box buyers whose own volumes are soft.
02
Thin earnings under a large dividend. TTM net income is $497M, but the annual dividend of $1.81 per share costs about $0.95B, a payout ratio of 192% of GAAP earnings and 93% of free cash flow. It is covered by cash flow, not by accounting profit, and capex guidance of $2.4–2.5B leaves little room.
03
Leverage in a cyclical business. Total debt of $14.16B against $677M of cash, 2.77x debt/EBITDA, and interest coverage of 2.26x. That is manageable in a normal year. If EBITDA falls in a downturn, it gets tight quickly.
04
Guidance credibility. The FY2026 EBITDA range has already been cut once, from $5.0–5.3B to $4.9–5.1B. Q2 adjusted EPS of $0.35 missed the $0.42 consensus. The Q3 ~$1.3B guide is the next test.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The paper cycle is turning, and SW is the largest player. The CEO says markets are "as tight as I can remember", the industry is on its third round of price increases in 2026, and SW's $100/ton applies across a very large tonnage base. Around 600,000 tons of high-cost capacity are already closed, the $400M synergy target has been exceeded, and about 20 loss-making sites remain to fix or close. At 15x forward earnings, 8.2x EV/EBITDA, 1.29x book and a 3.98% yield, you are paying a cyclical-trough price for what could be a cyclical upswing. All 17 analysts rate it a Buy, with a $56.84 average target.

Bear Case

So far, the price increases are announcements, while the cost inflation has already hit the numbers: about $520M of extra freight and energy in 2026, a guidance cut in July, and a Q2 EPS miss. North American volumes are falling 4–5% a year. GAAP earnings of $0.94 a share support neither a $1.81 dividend nor a 48x trailing multiple. Debt is $14.2B at 2.8x EBITDA, and ROIC of 4.1% is below the cost of capital. With every analyst already at Buy, a weak Q3 would bring downgrades rather than upgrades. The 2030 plan of $7B EBITDA requires roughly 40% growth from here in a commodity business.

Top 3 Risks

01
Price increases fail to stick. The whole near-term case, including the Q3 ~$1.3B guide, assumes the industry's 2026 price increases are realised. If box demand stays soft, customers push back and the increases slip.
02
Cost inflation outruns price. Freight is guided about $300M higher and energy about $220M higher for 2026, and the CFO says this cost pressure "is not showing signs of abatement." Input costs have already come in about $300M above what management expected three months earlier.
03
Leverage plus dividend in a downturn. $14.16B of debt, 2.26x interest coverage, capex of $2.4–2.5B, and a dividend that uses 93% of free cash flow. If a recession hits box demand, the company would have to choose between the dividend and paying down debt.
Industry Commentary
A containerboard market that turned in six weeks
EMERGING TAILWIND

Containerboard prices are set by the balance between mill capacity and box demand, and that balance shifted sharply in mid-2026. PCA's $140/ton increase, about double a typical move, was followed by SW ($100), IP ($80) and Cascades ($110–140) within days. Tony Smurfit said on the Q2 call that "nobody would have been pre-buying". The move surprised buyers, which means inventories in the chain were not built ahead of it. Capacity closures across the industry, including SW's own 600,000 tons, are part of the reason. The open question is demand: SW's North American corrugated volumes were still falling in Q2.

Freight and energy inflation
ONGOING HEADWIND

Paper and boxes are heavy and low in value, so freight is a large share of delivered cost, and mills use a lot of energy. SW guided about $300M of extra freight and $220M of extra energy for 2026. Q2 alone carried about $90M of extra freight and $22M of extra energy. In the Q2 slides, management described "the tightest industry supply conditions in recent years" alongside rising costs. Both the price upside and the cost pressure are real; which one is larger will decide the next few quarters.

A merger still being digested
EXECUTION STORY

Two years in, Smurfit Westrock is still reshaping the WestRock system it acquired: about 80 loss-making facilities at the merger, about 20 now, more than 3,000 jobs removed, and synergies above the $400M target. GAAP earnings still carry closure and integration costs, which is why TTM EPS ($0.94) sits so far below FY2025 adjusted EPS ($2.05). If the restructuring finishes as planned and paper prices hold, the reported numbers should converge toward the adjusted ones. If not, the adjusted figures will keep looking better than the business.
