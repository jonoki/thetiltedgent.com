01 Company Overview

Walk into a furniture store, a vet's office, a Lowe's or a Sam's Club, and the store card you are offered at the till may well be a Synchrony card with somebody else's name on the front. Synchrony is the former retail-finance arm of GE Capital — it went public in July 2014 at $23 a share, raising $2.88B — and it is built around private-label and co-branded cards issued in partners' names. Its business is simple to describe: partner with a retailer or healthcare provider, lend to that partner's customers at high rates, share part of the profit back with the partner, and fund the whole thing with online bank deposits. At the end of Q3 2026 that meant $103.3B of loan receivables, 68.3 million average active accounts and $83.0B of deposits.

The numbers that matter are bank numbers, and they are unusual ones. Synchrony earns a net interest margin of 15.50% — a card lender's margin, far above a deposit-and-mortgage bank's — and pays for it with a net charge-off rate of 4.79%. It is a high-margin, high-loss machine; the investment case is always the spread between those two figures, after the partner's cut. In Q3 2026 net earnings were $892M, or $2.68 per diluted share (versus $2.86 a year earlier, a quarter that included a $152M reserve release), and management lifted full-year guidance to approximately $9.50 of diluted EPS, the top of the $9.25–$9.50 range it gave after Q2. On the market's side the story has been politics, not credit: in January 2026 a presidential call for a one-year 10% cap on credit-card interest rates knocked the stock 8% in a session, and SYF's month-end closes since have ranged from $68.02 to $77.08. At $74.11 it trades at 7.7x trailing earnings — priced like a business the market is not sure it will be allowed to keep.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Point-of-sale distribution via partners CareCredit provider network Underwriting data on 68M active accounts Deposit funding ($83.0B of deposits) Retailer share arrangements align partners Integration into partner checkout & apps

The moat is distribution at the till. Synchrony does not have to find its customers — its partners hand them over at the moment of purchase, and the retailer share arrangement (RSA) makes each partner a co-owner of the program's profit: $1.1B paid out in Q3 2026 alone (+7% YoY). That structure is sticky — renewing is almost always cheaper than rebuilding the underwriting, the systems and the customer base with a rival — and Synchrony added or renewed more than 15 partners in Q2 and 10 in Q3, including La-Z-Boy and Fender. The vulnerability is the other side of the same coin: Synchrony rents its customers. A big partner that walks takes the relationship with it, and a big partner that stays uses that leverage to take a larger slice of the RSA.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Price controls on the product. The business model is a high APR against high losses. A 10% rate cap, as proposed in January 2026, would in Wells Fargo's assessment "wipe out earnings" for card-focused lenders like Synchrony. The CEO's own read on the July 21 call: "price controls generally are bad… you will have unintended consequences. You're going to restrict credit."
02
Partner concentration. Programs like Walmart/OnePay, Amazon, PayPal and Lowe's are named by management or the company as major relationships. Renewals are negotiated when partners have the most leverage, and lost programs take their receivables with them. The PayPal Credit exclusivity runs through 2028 per Wikipedia.
03
Credit is the cycle. An allowance of 10.01% of loans — $10.3B on $103.3B — is the company's own estimate of lifetime losses on today's book. Q2 earnings were helped by a $163M reserve release; Q3 carried a $30M reserve build (against a $152M release a year earlier). In a consumer downturn the build grows, and charge-offs rise at the same time.
04
Expense creep. The efficiency ratio rose 330bps to 35.9% in Q3 on higher operational losses, technology investments and marketing, and the CFO said fourth-quarter other expense "will generally be in line with the third quarter" — no operating leverage this year.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items colour the Q3 2026 print. First, the reserve swing: provision for credit losses was $1.27B, up $120M (10%) from a year earlier, "primarily driven by a reserve build of $30 million versus a $152 million release in the prior year" (Q3 2026 release). Releases are real earnings but not recurring ones, and the comparison flipped from a $152M tailwind to a $30M cost. Second, expenses: other expense rose $123M, or 10%, to $1.4B on higher operational losses, technology investments and marketing, and the efficiency ratio widened 330bps to 35.9%. Third, the share count: weighted diluted shares fell 12.8% in a year (369.9M to 322.4M), so EPS fell 6.3% ($2.68 vs $2.86) while net earnings fell 17% ($892M vs $1,077M).

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $48.88 to the October 21, 2026 settled close of $74.11, SYF gained 51.6% on price. Dividends add to that: annual dividends per share over FY2022–FY2025 alone sum to $4.01 (StockAnalysis). But the path was anything but smooth. It now reads 52.5 — dead neutral. The 10-month moving average sits at about $73.14, just below the current price: after ten months of chop between $68 and $77, trend and price have converged. Momentum is waiting for a verdict, and the verdict is more likely to come from Washington than from the loan book.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 7.7x trailing earnings and about 1.7x tangible book for a business earning a 25.2% ROTCE, with charge-offs (4.79%) down 37bps year on year, purchase volume up 8%, a 15.50% margin, and a CET1 of 12.9% against an 11% target — nearly two points of capital still to be returned, on top of a $4.6B authorisation worth about 20% of the market cap. Diluted shares already fell 12.8% in a year. Management now expects about $9.50 of EPS for 2026, and consensus has $10.47 in 2027 (about 11% growth). Analyst targets average $87.88, 18.6% above the price.

Bear Case

The low multiple is not an oversight; it is a discount for regulatory tail risk on the one thing Synchrony sells — high-rate credit to consumers who cannot get cheaper credit elsewhere. Wells Fargo's own January framing was that a 10% cap would "wipe out earnings" for lenders like SYF. Meanwhile the earnings being capitalised are under pressure: Q3 net earnings fell 17% year on year, with EPS down only 6% because the share count shrank; the year-ago quarter's $152M reserve release became a $30M build; the efficiency ratio rose 330bps; and loans grew 3.2% against 8% spend growth. Evercore ISI moved to Hold the day after the print, and 8 of 24 analysts now rate the stock Hold. When the consumer cycle turns, reserve releases become builds and the buyback slows at exactly the wrong moment.

Top 3 Risks

01
APR caps or fee regulation. The January 2026 proposal took 8% off in a session and set the tone for the year. A concrete cap would reprice the whole model; the CEO's warning is that it would restrict credit to exactly the customers the business serves.
02
Consumer credit turning. The book is reserved at 10.01% of loans and losing 4.79% a year in a benign environment. A rise in unemployment would push charge-offs up and turn reserve releases (Q2: $163M) into larger reserve builds (Q3: $30M) — hitting earnings twice.
03
Partner leverage. The customers belong to the partners. Renewals of the largest programs, and the size of the RSA share paid to keep them ($1.1B in Q3; the CFO expects a higher full-year total that stays within the long-term target of 4%–4.5% of average loans), set how much of the margin Synchrony actually keeps.

The price of credit is now a political variable
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The January 2026 call for a one-year 10% cap on credit-card interest rates hit the pure-play card lenders hardest: SYF −8%, Capital One −6%, American Express −4%, versus about −1% for JPMorgan and Bank of America. The ordering is the lesson — the more of a company's revenue comes from revolving card balances, the more of its value is exposed. Wells Fargo estimated such a cap would cut large-bank pre-tax earnings by 5%–18% and "wipe out earnings" for card-focused lenders. The economics are straightforward: a book that charges off 4.79% a year and pays partners an RSA cannot be run at a 10% APR for the customers it currently serves. Whether the cap happens is a political question this report cannot answer.

Payers paying faster, spending more
MIXED SIGNAL

Synchrony's Q3 again shows a consumer who is spending — purchase volume up 8% to $49.7B — but also paying down balances quickly: the CFO put the Q3 payment rate (the share of balances repaid each month) at 16.9%, about 60bps higher than a year earlier, and the company expects it to stay elevated. For credit quality that is good news (delinquencies down 5bps and 6bps year on year, charge-offs down 37bps). For revenue it is less so: interest and fees on loans grew 1.5% to $5.59B against 8% spend growth. Management's year-end goal of "mid-single-digit" loan growth (Q3: +3.2% YoY) depends on balances building.

Capital return as the valuation floor — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: at 7.7x earnings a company that spent $1.1B on buybacks in a single quarter (4.8% of its market cap, our calculation: $1.1B ÷ $23.08B) compounds per-share value fast, and a CET1 almost two points above target means the pace can continue without new earnings. The bear framing: buybacks turned a 17% fall in Q3 net earnings into a 6% fall in EPS; they mask the absolute trend, and they are the first thing regulators or a credit downturn would slow. The way to adjudicate is not the P/E — it is whether net earnings, not EPS, grow over the next four quarters.
