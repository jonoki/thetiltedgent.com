01 Company Overview

Stryker is one of the world's largest pure-play medical technology companies, selling orthopaedic implants (knees, hips, trauma and extremities), the Mako robotic-arm surgical platform, surgical instruments and visualization systems, hospital beds and emergency-care equipment, and neurovascular and peripheral-vascular thrombectomy devices in roughly 61 countries. Fiscal 2025 net sales were $25.12 billion, up 11.2% reported and 10.3% organic — the company's fourth consecutive year of double-digit or near-double-digit organic growth, driven by 9.9% unit volume growth against just 0.4% of price.

At a $127.1 billion market capitalisation Stryker is the 181st most valuable listed company in the world and roughly the 94th largest in the United States, placing it just behind Intuitive Surgical (#87 US, $139.8B) among large-cap medical-device names and well ahead of Zimmer Biomet and Globus Medical. The stock has de-rated sharply over the past twelve months — down roughly 13% — after a March 2026 cyber incident disrupted manufacturing, even as the underlying business re-accelerated to 9.0% organic growth in Q2 2026.

A note on Spinal Implants. Stryker sold its U.S. spinal implants business — roughly $707 million of 2024 sales — to Viscogliosi Brothers, which renamed it VB Spine; the divestiture completed in 2025 and included a binding offer for the French spinal products business. Reported Spinal Implants revenue collapsed to $185M in FY2025 (−73.9%) and just $3M in Q2 2026 (−40.9% cc). This is the single largest reason Orthopaedics' reported FY2025 growth (+5.4%) badly understates its organic growth (+9.5%), and it is why reported-versus-organic reconciliation matters more for SYK than for most large-cap medtech.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
~3,000 MAKO INSTALLED BASE 2.5M+ ROBOTIC PROCEDURES SURGEON SWITCHING COSTS RAZOR/RAZOR-BLADE PULL-THROUGH FULL-OR VENDOR BUNDLING SERIAL M&A ENGINE ASC CHANNEL POSITION #1 EXTREMITIES SCALE

Stryker's moat is not the implant — knees and hips are increasingly commoditised, with industry pricing running −1% to −2% per year — it is the robot that decides which implant gets used. A Mako placement is a multi-hundred-thousand-dollar capital sale that comes bundled with surgeon training, hospital workflow integration and Stryker-specific software; once a surgeon has learned the haptic boundaries and planning workflow, switching to a Zimmer ROSA or Smith+Nephew CORI means re-training, re-credentialing and re-negotiating the whole implant contract. With roughly 3,000 systems placed globally and more than 2.5 million cumulative procedures in 47 countries, Stryker's installed base is larger than ROSA (1,300+) and CORI (1,100+) combined. The result is that Mako is now performing over two-thirds of Stryker's U.S. knee procedures and over a third of its hips — annuity revenue that grows with procedure volume and requires no new capital sale. Layer on top the fact that Stryker also sells the operating room's beds, power tools, visualization towers, waste management and, increasingly, its vascular consumables, and the company can bid whole-hospital contracts that a single-category competitor cannot match. The Mako RPS handheld launch in 2026 explicitly widens this moat downmarket, targeting the ambulatory surgery centers and cost-sensitive surgeons for whom a full Mako was uneconomic — precisely where Smith+Nephew has been placing 40% of CORI systems.

Robotic Competition Is Catching Up
Zimmer Biomet (ROSA, plus the Monogram robotics acquisition), Smith+Nephew (CORI, heavily ASC-weighted) and J&J MedTech (VELYS, now cleared for knees and launched in spine in 2025) are all placing systems. Stryker's lead is large but its growth in placements will increasingly come from replacing rivals rather than converting manual surgeons. Any perceived clinical parity in the handheld/ASC tier erodes the pricing premium Mako supports.
Acquisition Leverage & Amortisation Drag
Growth has been bought as well as built: Inari ($80/share, closed Feb 2025), Wright Medical, AVS and a "very robust pipeline" of further deals that CFO Preston Wells calls the number-one capital-deployment priority. Total debt is $15.45B against $3.48B cash, and Q2 2026 alone carried $175M of intangible amortisation plus $25M of acquisition and integration charges. That is a permanent wedge between GAAP and adjusted earnings.
Operational & Cyber Fragility
The March 11, 2026 cyber incident shut down manufacturing; facilities only ran consistently again from April 1, and remediation costs (manufacturing loss absorption plus IT and R&D expense) are still weighing on 2026 earnings. Separately, a peripheral vascular plant created a "meaningful backorder situation with lost sales" in Q2 2026. Two supply failures inside twelve months is a pattern, not a one-off, and in emergent-care categories lost cases are lost permanently.
Hospital Capital & Reimbursement Exposure
Medical ($4.2B, 16.7% of sales) and Mako capital placements both depend on hospital capital budgets rather than procedure volumes. Management currently benefits from an elevated backlog and "very strong" demand, but a health-system margin squeeze or a shift in U.S. reimbursement would hit the capital line first and with a lag that guidance tends not to anticipate. Pricing is already negative across orthopaedics.

Notable One-Off Items

Stryker's reported results for 2026 are unusually distorted, and the distortions run in both directions.

Tariffs (favourable, non-recurring). Q2 2026 gross profit included a $158 million benefit from the reversal of 2025 tariffs, worth roughly $0.34 per share and about 60 basis points of the quarter's gross margin expansion. This will not repeat, and management explicitly flagged that the benefit was largely consumed by ongoing cyber remediation costs.

Cyber incident (unfavourable, partly ongoing). The March 11, 2026 incident shut manufacturing; plants only ran consistently from April 1. Q1 2026 was severely impacted by production stoppages, and Q2 still carried loss absorption from under-utilised manufacturing plus incremental IT and R&D expense. Cyber costs are the stated reason 2026 adjusted EPS guidance was held at $14.95–$15.10 despite the tariff windfall.

Acquisition accounting. Q2 2026 adjustments to operating income included $175M of intangible amortisation, $95M of structural optimisation charges, $25M of acquisition and integration costs (employee retention and manufacturing integration), $5M of medical device regulation costs, $1M of goodwill/impairment and a $2M net benefit from recall-related matters. Amortisation is recurring in practice even though it is excluded from adjusted figures — it is the ongoing price of the M&A strategy, not a true one-off.

Portfolio changes. The VB Spine divestiture (~$707M of 2024 U.S. spinal implant sales) removed roughly three points of reported Orthopaedics growth in FY2025 and makes reported segment growth non-comparable; the Inari acquisition (closed February 19, 2025) added the reciprocal distortion in Vascular, where FY2025 reported growth of +50.6% flipped to −1.8% constant currency in Q2 2026 once the acquisition lapped and supply problems hit. Currency has been a minor factor — 40 bps of headwind on total Q2 2026 sales, with management guiding to a slightly favourable FX contribution for the full year if rates hold.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. On monthly data these signals are slow and should be read as multi-month context, not trading triggers.

Five-year trajectory. SYK closed August 2021 at $250.97 and August 18, 2026 at $331.37 — a price return of +32.0%, or roughly 5.7% annualised. Adding back dividends of roughly 1% per year lifts the total return to approximately +37–38% (dividend contribution is an approximation, not a verified figure). That is a materially weaker outcome than the S&P 500 delivered over the same window, and essentially all of the underperformance was generated in the last eighteen months: from the June 2025 monthly-close peak of $391.64 the stock is down 15.4%, and the twelve-month change is −13.2%. The path was not linear. Stryker fell 25% from its December 2021 close of $255.14 to a June 2022 trough of $190.95 in the rate shock, then compounded steadily for two and a half years — from $190.95 to a $391.64 peak, a 105% advance — on Mako adoption and post-pandemic elective procedure recovery, before the 2026 operational shocks reversed a fifth of that gain.

What the chart is saying now. The price sits above its short and medium-term monthly moving averages but below the long one, the classic signature of a bottoming attempt inside a downtrend. Four consecutive higher monthly closes (May $304.28 → June $314.84 → July $325.70 → August $331.37) have lifted the 6-period RSI out of oversold territory into neutral ground. Bulls will point out the stock is 17.9% off the low with fundamentals re-accelerating to 9% organic; bears will note it is still 16.5% below the 52-week high and that the recovery has been driven by a non-recurring tariff benefit.

Relative performance. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Stryker is compounding organic revenue at 9–10% in a 4–5% market, which means it is taking share every single year, and the mechanism is durable: ~3,000 installed Mako systems and 2.5 million cumulative procedures lock surgeons into Stryker implants for the life of each placement, with Mako RPS now extending that lock downmarket into ASCs. At 20.7x forward earnings the stock trades below the medtech average despite a 1.93 PEG and 12.5% ROIC, because trailing GAAP earnings are artificially depressed by amortisation, structural optimisation and one-time cyber costs. If 2027 simply looks like a normal Stryker year — 8–9% organic, 27%+ adjusted operating margin, no plant outages — the $382.88 consensus target implying 15.5% upside is not aggressive.

Bear Case

Two manufacturing failures in twelve months — a cyber incident that halted production for three weeks and a vascular plant problem that cost emergent-care sales — suggest operational fragility in a company whose whole premium rests on reliability. The Q2 2026 "beat" leaned on a $158M non-recurring tariff reversal worth $0.34 per share; strip it out and the margin story is far less impressive. Meanwhile the growth is increasingly bought rather than built: $15.45B of debt, $175M of quarterly amortisation, a CFO who calls M&A the number-one capital priority, and a Vascular business that went from +50.6% reported to −1.8% organic the moment the acquisition lapped. Robotic competition from ROSA, CORI and VELYS is narrowing the technology gap in exactly the ASC tier Stryker is now chasing.

Orthopaedic pricing is already running −1% to −2% annually, hospital capital budgets are cyclical, and the stock has already shown it will de-rate 22% from peak on operational news without any change to the competitive position.
  Industry Commentary
Robotic Orthopaedic Surgery Adoption
EMERGING TAILWIND
Robotics has moved from novelty to standard of care in joint replacement, and the installed-base race is effectively decided at the top: Mako ~3,000 systems versus ROSA 1,300+ and CORI 1,100+, with J&J's VELYS cleared for knees and launched in spine in 2025. Stryker already runs over two-thirds of its U.S. knees and over a third of its U.S. hips robotically, and passed 2.5 million cumulative Mako procedures across 47 countries. The next phase is the ambulatory surgery center, where Smith+Nephew placed 40% of its 2025 CORI systems and where Stryker's handheld Mako RPS is now launching. Because knee and hip pricing runs −1% to −2% per year, robotics is the only mechanism left for a manufacturer to defend price and win share simultaneously — which is why every player is spending here.
Elective Procedure Demand & Hospital Capital Budgets
ONGOING HEADWIND
The demand backdrop is currently benign — Stryker management said it has "not observed meaningful changes in volume trends" in the U.S. procedural environment and exited Q2 2026 with an elevated capital backlog and production capacity, not customer demand, as the binding constraint. But the underlying market grows only 4–5% per year (knees and hips $18.4B, +5.6% in 2025; spine $11.4B, +4%; trauma $6.7B, +4%), and roughly 40% of Stryker's revenue — Medical at $4.2B plus Mako capital placements — depends on hospital capital budgets rather than on procedure counts. Health-system margin compression hits that line first and with a lag. Management's counter is portfolio mix: a meaningful share of sales are high-acuity, medically necessary or emergent, which insulates against pure discretionary softness.
Medtech Consolidation — Bull vs Bear Implications
ONGOING HEADWIND
Orthopaedics is consolidating hard: Stryker bought Inari ($80/share, Feb 2025) and sold spine to VB Spine; Zimmer Biomet bought Paragon 28 (~$1.2B) and Monogram; Globus absorbed NuVasive and bought Nevro. Further $2B+ transactions are expected, with earnings-generating targets clearing around a 12x EBITDA median. The bull reading: Stryker is the best-capitalised, best-integrated acquirer in the sector, ROIC of 12.5% proves the deals have earned their cost of capital, and $4.70B of free cash flow funds the pipeline without dilution. The bear reading: a company that needs acquisitions to sustain double-digit growth is a company whose organic engine is smaller than it looks — and $15.45B of debt, $175M of quarterly amortisation and a Vascular business that turned negative the moment Inari lapped are exactly what that looks like on the way down. Tariffs cut across both: industry-wide impacts range from $15M to $400M annually, and Stryker's own Q2 2026 benefited from a $158M reversal that will not repeat.
