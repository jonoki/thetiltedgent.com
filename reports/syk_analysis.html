01 Company Overview

Stryker is one of the world's largest pure-play medical technology companies, selling orthopaedic implants (knees, hips, trauma and extremities), the Mako robotic-arm surgical platform, surgical instruments and visualization systems, hospital beds and emergency-care equipment, and neurovascular and peripheral-vascular thrombectomy devices in roughly 61 countries. Fiscal 2025 net sales were $25.12 billion, up 11.2% reported and 10.3% organic (organic means excluding acquisitions, divestitures and currency), and sales for the twelve months to September 2026 reached $26.30 billion.

At the November 2, 2026 close Stryker was worth $116.8 billion (380.7 million shares). The stock is down 13.9% over the past twelve months while the S&P 500 (SPY) rose 10.3%: a March 2026 cyber incident disrupted manufacturing, a peripheral vascular supply problem has run longer than planned, and on October 29 the company cut its 2026 outlook as hip and knee procedures slowed. Organic growth was 7.7% in Q3 2026, down from 9.0% in Q2. On October 6 Stryker named President and COO Spencer Stiles to succeed Kevin Lobo as CEO from January 1, 2027.

Created by the 2026 segment recast, Ortho Tech combines Mako robotics and enabling technologies with orthopaedic power instruments. This is the competitive moat. Stryker had ~3,000 Mako systems installed globally at the end of 2025 (Gabelli), and Mako had passed 2.5 million procedures in 47 countries by July 2026 — versus roughly 1,300+ Zimmer ROSA and 1,100+ Smith+Nephew CORI systems, implying Stryker holds well over half of the installed robotic orthopaedic base. A note on Spinal Implants. Stryker sold its U.S. spinal implants business to Viscogliosi Brothers, which renamed it VB Spine (the Spinal Implants category had $707 million of sales in 2024); the divestiture completed in 2025 and included a binding offer for the French spinal products business. Reported Spinal Implants revenue collapsed to $185M in FY2025 (−73.9%) and just $4M in Q3 2026 (−45.4% in constant currency). This is the single largest reason Orthopaedics' reported FY2025 growth (+5.4%) badly understates its organic growth (+9.3% on the recast basis), and it is why reported-versus-organic reconciliation matters more for SYK than for most large-cap medtech.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
~3,000 MAKO INSTALLED BASE 2.5M+ ROBOTIC PROCEDURES SURGEON SWITCHING COSTS RAZOR/RAZOR-BLADE PULL-THROUGH FULL-OR VENDOR BUNDLING SERIAL M&A ENGINE ASC CHANNEL POSITION #1 EXTREMITIES SCALE

Stryker's moat is not the implant — knees and hips are increasingly commoditised, with prices falling about 1% a year (Gabelli, 2025) — it is the robot that decides which implant gets used. A Mako placement is a capital-equipment sale that comes bundled with surgeon training, hospital workflow integration and Stryker-specific software; once a surgeon has learned the haptic boundaries and planning workflow, switching to a Zimmer ROSA or Smith+Nephew CORI means re-training, re-credentialing and re-negotiating the whole implant contract. With roughly 3,000 systems placed globally by the end of 2025 and more than 2.5 million procedures in 47 countries, Stryker's installed base is larger than ROSA (1,300+) and CORI (1,100+) combined. The result is that by the end of 2025 Mako was performing over two-thirds of Stryker's U.S. knee procedures and over a third of its U.S. hips — annuity revenue that grows with procedure volume and requires no new capital sale. Layer on top the fact that Stryker also sells the operating room's beds, power tools, visualization towers, waste management and, increasingly, its vascular consumables, and the company can bid whole-hospital contracts that a single-category competitor cannot match. The Mako RPS handheld launch in 2026 is meant to widen this moat downmarket (management called its start slow in October), targeting the ambulatory surgery centers and cost-sensitive surgeons for whom a full Mako was uneconomic — precisely where Smith+Nephew has been placing 40% of CORI systems.

Robotic Competition Is Catching Up
Zimmer Biomet (ROSA, plus the Monogram robotics acquisition), Smith+Nephew (CORI, heavily ASC-weighted) and J&J MedTech (VELYS, now cleared for knees and launched in spine in 2025) are all placing systems. Stryker's lead is large but its growth in placements will increasingly come from replacing rivals rather than converting manual surgeons. Any perceived clinical parity in the handheld/ASC tier erodes the pricing premium Mako supports.
Acquisition Leverage & Amortisation Drag
Growth has been bought as well as built: Inari ($80/share, closed Feb 2025), Wright Medical and AVS, with more to come: Lobo said Stryker has an “active pipeline,” and CFO Preston Wells called M&A “the #1 priority.” In October Stryker also agreed a ~$400M preferred equity investment in CMR Surgical, maker of the Versius soft-tissue surgical robot, with an exclusive option to buy the rest of the company. Total debt is $14.93B against $3.27B of cash at September 30, 2026, and Q3 2026 carried $175M of intangible amortisation, $17M of acquisition and integration charges and $293M of goodwill and other impairments. That is a lasting wedge between GAAP and adjusted earnings.
Operational & Cyber Fragility
The March 11, 2026 cyber incident shut down manufacturing; Stryker said its global manufacturing network was fully operational again from the week of April 1, and remediation costs (manufacturing loss absorption plus IT and R&D expense) are still weighing on 2026 earnings. The peripheral vascular supply problem that followed has outlasted management's end-of-Q3 target, and Lobo now expects availability to keep normalizing through year-end. In emergency-care categories a lost case usually goes to a competitor's device and does not come back.
Hospital Capital & Reimbursement Exposure
Medical ($4.2B, 16.7% of sales) and Mako capital placements both depend on hospital capital budgets rather than procedure volumes. The order book was still “strong and elevated” on the Q3 call, but a health-system margin squeeze or a shift in U.S. reimbursement would hit the capital line first and with a lag that guidance tends not to anticipate. Procedures are the other exposure: Lobo tied the Q3 hip and knee slowdown to affordability concerns from higher insurance premiums and deductibles, and pricing across orthopaedics is already slightly negative.

Notable One-Off Items

Stryker's reported results for 2026 are unusually distorted, and the distortions run in both directions.

Tariffs (favourable, non-recurring). Q2 2026 gross profit included a $158 million benefit from the reversal of 2025 tariffs, worth roughly $0.34 per share. It sits in the nine-month 2026 figures and will not repeat.

Cyber incident (unfavourable, partly ongoing). The March 11, 2026 incident shut manufacturing; the global manufacturing network was fully operational again from the week of April 1. Q1 2026 was hit by production stoppages and Q2 still carried costs from under-used plants plus extra IT and R&D spending. On the Q3 call CFO Preston Wells said much of what was lost to the incident had since been made back up; the peripheral vascular supply problem that followed has not fully cleared.

Impairment and special charges (Q3 2026). GAAP results carried $293M of goodwill and other impairments, $153M of structural optimisation and other special charges (including $77M related to the Spinal Implants sale and $37M for ending sales relationships in certain countries) and $32M for regulatory and legal matters. With amortisation, these are why GAAP EPS fell 11.7% to $1.96 while adjusted EPS rose 10.3% to $3.52.

Acquisition accounting. Q3 2026 adjustments to operating income also included $175M of intangible amortisation, $17M of acquisition and integration costs, $6M of EU medical device regulation costs and a $4M net benefit from recall-related matters. Amortisation is recurring in practice even though it is excluded from adjusted figures — it is the ongoing price of the M&A strategy, not a true one-off.

Portfolio changes. The VB Spine divestiture (Spinal Implants sales fell from $707M in 2024 to $185M in 2025) is why acquisitions and divestitures took 4.8 points off Orthopaedics' FY2025 growth on the recast basis, and it makes reported segment growth non-comparable; the Inari acquisition (closed February 19, 2025) added the reciprocal distortion in Vascular, where FY2025 reported growth of +50.6% flipped to −1.8% constant currency in Q2 2026 once the acquisition lapped and supply problems hit, before recovering to +7.4% in Q3. Currency was neutral in Q3 2026 (+7.7% both reported and in constant currency), and guidance assumes a slightly favourable effect for the year if rates hold.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Even in a bad quarter Stryker grew 7.7% organically and adjusted EPS 10.3%, in a year when management puts hip and knee market growth at about 2.5%. The mechanism is intact: ~3,000 installed Mako systems lock surgeons into Stryker implants for the life of each placement, Ortho Tech grew 16.0% in Q3, Endoscopy 14.0%, and the hospital-equipment order book is still “strong and elevated.” At 20.7x the midpoint of 2026 adjusted EPS guidance the stock trades below the medtech average with a 12.5% ROIC, because GAAP earnings carry amortisation, restructuring and impairment charges. If the vascular supply problem clears by year-end and 2027 delivers the growth “at the high end of our markets” CFO Preston Wells said he expects, the $355.04 average target (15.7% above the November 2 close) is not demanding. The CMR Surgical option and a $3.0B buyback increase add ways to win.

Bear Case

2026 has gone wrong in three ways: a cyber incident that halted production for nearly three weeks, a vascular supply problem that has missed its end-of-Q3 fix, and now a hip and knee slowdown that management ties to patients facing higher premiums and deductibles. Guidance was cut on October 29, Mako RPS is off to a slow start, and Q2's beat had leaned on a $158M one-time tariff reversal. The growth is also partly bought: $14.93B of debt, $175M of quarterly amortisation, a $293M impairment in Q3, a ~$400M CMR Surgical stake with an option to buy more, and a CFO who calls M&A the number-one priority. A CEO change on January 1 adds uncertainty, and robotic competition from ROSA, CORI and VELYS is narrowing the gap in the ASC tier Stryker is now chasing.

01
Operational and cyber recurrence risk. The March 2026 incident halted most manufacturing for nearly three weeks, and its costs have weighed on 2026 earnings. The vascular supply problem has already run past management's end-of-Q3 goal; if it lasts beyond year-end, or another plant fails, the lost emergency cases may stay with competitors.
02
Acquisition dependence and balance-sheet leverage. With $14.93B of debt against $3.27B of cash, 0.64 debt-to-equity, a ~$400M CMR Surgical investment pending, and management explicitly prioritising M&A in a market where profitable targets sell for a median of about 12 times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation; Gabelli), Stryker is buying growth at rising prices. Each deal adds amortisation and integration cost, widens the GAAP-to-adjusted gap, and raises the bar for the next one.
03
Slower growth on a premium valuation. At 32.7x trailing GAAP earnings, above the S&P 500 figure in Section 04, SYK's price assumes growth stays above its markets. Organic growth guidance has already been cut to 6.5–7.0%, the hip and knee market is growing about 2.5%, orthopaedic pricing is slightly negative, and the stock fell 30.4% from its June 2025 month-end peak to its September 2026 close.
04
Leadership transition. Kevin Lobo hands the CEO role to Spencer Stiles on January 1, 2027, staying on as Executive Chair. Stiles ran the orthopaedics group that is now slowing, and he takes over as 2027 guidance is set.
  Industry Commentary
Robotic Orthopaedic Surgery Adoption
EMERGING TAILWIND
Robotics has moved from novelty to standard of care in joint replacement, and the installed-base race is effectively decided at the top: Mako ~3,000 systems versus ROSA 1,300+ and CORI 1,100+, with J&J's VELYS cleared for knees and launched in spine in 2025. At the end of 2025 Stryker ran over two-thirds of its U.S. knees and over a third of its U.S. hips robotically, and passed 2.5 million cumulative Mako procedures across 47 countries. The next phase is the ambulatory surgery center, where Smith+Nephew placed 40% of its 2025 CORI systems and where Stryker's handheld Mako RPS is launching, though management called its start slow. Stryker is also reaching into soft-tissue surgery, Intuitive Surgical's territory, through its October 2026 alliance with CMR Surgical (Versius). Because knee and hip prices fall about 1% a year, robotics is the only mechanism left for a manufacturer to defend price and win share simultaneously — which is why every player is spending here.
Elective Procedure Demand & Hospital Capital Budgets
ONGOING HEADWIND
The demand backdrop turned in Q3 2026. Lobo said “it is now clear that there is a market slowdown” in hips and knees, put this year's market growth at about 2.5% (against +5.6% for knees and hips in 2025, an $18.4B market), and tied it to affordability concerns from higher insurance premiums and deductibles, with Europe and the Middle East also soft. Hospital equipment is the other side: the order book is still “strong and elevated,” but Medical ($4.2B) and Mako capital sales depend on hospital capital budgets, which a squeeze on health-system margins hits first and with a lag. Management's counter is portfolio mix: a meaningful share of sales are high-acuity, medically necessary or emergent, which insulates against pure discretionary softness.
Medtech Consolidation — Bull vs Bear Implications
ONGOING HEADWIND
Orthopaedics is consolidating hard: Stryker bought Inari ($80/share, Feb 2025) and sold spine to VB Spine; Zimmer Biomet bought Paragon 28 (~$1.2B) and Monogram; Globus absorbed NuVasive and bought Nevro. Profitable targets have sold for a median of about 12 times EBITDA (Gabelli). The bull reading: Stryker is the best-capitalised, best-integrated acquirer in the sector, ROIC of 12.5% suggests the deals have earned their cost of capital, and $4.57B of free cash flow funds the pipeline without issuing shares; ZuriMED closed in October and the CMR Surgical option points to the next one. The bear reading: a company that needs acquisitions to sustain double-digit growth is a company whose organic engine is smaller than it looks — and $14.93B of debt, $175M of quarterly amortisation, a $293M Q3 impairment and a Vascular business that turned negative the moment Inari lapped are what that looks like on the way down. Tariffs cut across both: Stryker's Q2 2026 included a $158M reversal of 2025 tariffs that will not repeat.
