01 Company Overview
AT&T is the second-largest US wireless carrier and the largest fiber-to-the-home broadband builder in the country, selling mobile connectivity, fiber and fixed-wireless internet, and business networking across the US and Mexico. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $125.65B, up 2.71% year over year, with net income of $21.89B and EPS of $3.05; on a TTM basis revenue is $127.24B (+2.6%). At a $169.12B market capitalization the company ranks roughly #134 globally and #74 in the US, and is the fifth-largest listed telecom operator in the world behind China Mobile, SoftBank, Verizon and T-Mobile.
The business is now reported in three segments after a Q1 2026 reorganisation: Advanced Connectivity (wireless, fiber, fixed wireless, business advanced connectivity), Legacy (the copper-era voice and legacy data services being deliberately wound down), and Latin America (Mexican wireless). Management has re-pointed the whole company at a single metric — free cash flow — guiding to $18B+ in 2026, $19B+ in 2027 and $21B+ in 2028, funding a ~$10B buyback this year inside a $24B 2026–2028 repurchase plan.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
38.6M FIBER LOCATIONS LICENSED SPECTRUM PORTFOLIO SUNK CAPITAL / REPLACEMENT COST CONVERGENCE BUNDLING SCALE ECONOMICS REGULATORY BARRIERS SUBSCRIBER SWITCHING FRICTION
AT&T's moat is physical, not intellectual. Passing 38.6 million homes with fiber and holding a nationwide licensed spectrum portfolio — now deeper by ~50 MHz after the $23B EchoStar purchase — represents tens of billions of sunk capital that a new entrant cannot replicate at any sensible return. Once fiber is in the ground the incremental cost of adding a subscriber is small, which is why organic penetration of ~40% and rising is the key value driver rather than the passings number itself. The genuine strategic asset is convergence: 6.0 million customers now take both wireless and home internet (a 42.5% attach rate), and converged households churn materially less than single-product ones — visible in postpaid phone churn of 0.86%, a level that would have looked unattainable a decade ago. Regulatory barriers (spectrum licensing, rights-of-way, franchise agreements) and the sheer working-capital cost of handset subsidies keep the structure to three national carriers plus resellers.
Key Vulnerabilities

Balance sheet leverage. Total debt of $165.76B against $17.57B of cash leaves debt-to-equity at 1.29 and enterprise value at $317.30B — nearly twice the equity market cap. Net debt of $126.4B and 2.68x net-debt-to-adjusted-EBITDA leaves limited room for error, and management now expects to take roughly three years post-EchoStar to work leverage back toward ~2.5x. Interest coverage of 3.86x is adequate but not comfortable for a business with this much maturity roll.

Wireless competitive intensity. T-Mobile is still compounding revenue near 10% against AT&T's ~3% wireless service growth, and cable operators keep adding phone lines through wholesale MVNO agreements at prices that reset the low end of the market. Promotional handset economics — device upgrade subsidies — are the swing factor between reported net adds and actual profitability, and management explicitly flagged device cost effects on customer behaviour in Q2.

Capital intensity with a long payback. Capital investment of $23–24B a year through 2028 is being spent to pass locations whose penetration curve takes years to mature; the Lumen assets came in at only ~25% penetration versus AT&T's ~40% organic level. If penetration disappoints, the fiber build converts into stranded capital rather than free cash flow, and the entire $18B → $21B FCF ramp underpinning the equity story is at risk.

Spectrum deployment execution. Of the ~50 MHz bought from EchoStar, the 20 MHz of 600 MHz low-band is not currently supported in AT&T's radio portfolio and is likely more than a year from carrying traffic. That means $23B of capital is on the balance sheet earning a partial return while the depreciation and interest cost begins immediately — a real drag on 2026–2027 reported earnings.

Notable One-Off Items
AT&T's reported earnings history over this cycle is dominated by non-recurring charges, and comparing GAAP years without adjusting for them is actively misleading. FY2022 carried $27.50B of asset write-downs and restructuring — the media-era goodwill unwind — which is the entire reason the company reported a net loss of $8.06B on $120.74B of revenue that year. FY2024 carried a further $5.08B of write-downs and restructuring, holding net income to $10.75B and EPS to $1.49. FY2025's charge fell to $838M, and the trailing twelve months to $620M — which is why FY2025 EPS of $3.05 more than doubled FY2024's $1.49 on only 2.7% revenue growth. Roughly speaking, the entire optical EPS "doubling" is the absence of impairments, not operating improvement.
Two large 2026 transactions distort the balance sheet rather than the income statement: the $5.75B Lumen Mass Markets fiber purchase (closed Feb 2, 2026), whose network assets are held in a subsidiary earmarked for sale to an equity partner, and the $23B EchoStar spectrum acquisition (closed Jul 28, 2026), funded in part with a drawn term loan and accompanied by a $2.4B FCC-mandated trust. Both add capitalised assets and debt without proportionate near-term revenue. Asset sale proceeds are also running hot — $3.22B of property, plant and equipment sale proceeds in FY2025 and $3.93B on a TTM basis — which flatters cash flow relative to a pure operating run-rate. Buybacks stepped up sharply: $4.50B in FY2025 versus $7.99B TTM, against roughly $0.2–0.9B a year in 2021–2024. Prior-period comparisons involving the DirecTV stake and pension remeasurement should be verified directly in the 10-K before use; they were not confirmable from a live source at the time of writing.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying the largest fiber network in America at 8.2x trailing earnings and 10.35x forward, with a 4.50% dividend covered at a 36.7% payout ratio and a ~$10B buyback shrinking the share count. If management delivers the guided free-cash-flow ramp — $18B in 2026 to $21B+ in 2028 — the equity yields roughly 10.6% on today's $169B market cap in 2026, rising toward 12%, while leverage falls back toward 2.5x. Convergence is working: 6.0 million bundled households, 42.5% attach rate, and 0.86% postpaid churn are the operating proof.

Bear Case

This is a capital-destruction risk wearing a value-stock costume. ROIC of 8.89% is barely above the cost of capital while the company commits $23–24B a year of capex plus $23B for spectrum that is only partly deployable and $5.75B for fiber assets penetrated at 25%. Gross debt is $165.76B against $128.4B of equity, and enterprise value ($317.30B) is nearly double the market cap — so equity holders own the residual on a heavily levered, low-growth utility. Meanwhile T-Mobile grows revenue at 9.7% versus AT&T's 2.6%, and the FY2025 EPS "doubling" is entirely the absence of impairments.

Top 3 Risks
01
Fiber penetration disappoints. The whole thesis rests on converting 40M (soon 60M) passings into subscribers at ~40% penetration and reasonable ARPU. Fiber ARPU already fell 1.3% year over year on Lumen dilution, and the acquired base sits at ~25%. If penetration stalls, $23–24B of annual capital investment becomes stranded rather than compounding.
02
Leverage plus rates. $165.76B of gross debt, 2.68x net-debt-to-EBITDA and 3.86x interest coverage leave little cushion. Management now needs roughly three years post-EchoStar to get leverage back to ~2.5x. Any period of higher refinancing costs directly consumes the free cash flow earmarked for buybacks.
03
Wireless price war. T-Mobile's ~10% revenue growth, cable MVNO share gains and rising handset promotion costs can compress the 42.0% Advanced Connectivity margin quickly. Wireless service revenue is 55% of the company; a one-point margin loss there outweighs almost anything happening in fiber.
  Industry Commentary
Convergence and the Fiber Land Grab
EMERGING TAILWIND
Every US operator has concluded the same thing at once: owning both the pipe and the phone lowers churn and raises lifetime value. AT&T bought Lumen's Mass Markets fiber for $5.75B; Verizon closed a $20B acquisition of Frontier; T-Mobile has pursued fiber joint ventures. The land grab is finite — homes only get passed once — which is why AT&T's 38.6M locations (heading to 40M+ this year and 60M+ by 2030) and 6.0M converged customers at a 42.5% attach rate are the most valuable numbers in the release. Cable is the loser: Charter's revenue fell 1.5% and its market cap fell 46.9% over the year as fiber and fixed wireless took broadband share.
Wireless Competitive Intensity
ONGOING HEADWIND
US wireless is a mature ~$220B (est.) service-revenue pool growing at low single digits, so every net add is taken from someone. AT&T's 432,000 postpaid phone net adds beat the ~341,000 consensus and churn improved to 0.86% — but T-Mobile still grew total revenue 9.7% versus AT&T's 2.6%, and cable MVNOs keep adding lines on wholesale economics. The escalating cost of handset promotions is the mechanism by which share gains get paid for; management explicitly flagged device costs as shaping customer behaviour in Q2. Spectrum is the other front — the $23B EchoStar purchase buys capacity headroom, but 20 MHz of it needs new radios before it carries a single bit.
Rates, Debt and Who Gets the Cash Flow
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Telecom is a spread business between the cost of capital and the return on network assets, and AT&T's spread is thin: ROIC of 8.89% against $165.76B of gross debt, an enterprise value of $317.30B and 2.68x net leverage after spending $23B on spectrum and $5.75B on fiber inside seven months. The bull reading: the debt is largely long-dated and fixed, the dividend costs only ~$8B a year against $17.65B of TTM free cash flow, and every turn of deleveraging plus the ~$10B buyback transfers value to equity — at 8.2x earnings you are being paid to wait. The bear reading: a company earning ~8.9% on capital while borrowing at meaningfully positive real rates and reinvesting $23–24B annually is running a treadmill, and the 8.2x multiple is not a bargain but an accurate discount for that reality. Both readings depend entirely on whether fiber penetration curves deliver — which is why the 40M-locations checkpoint at year-end 2026 matters more than any quarterly EPS print.
