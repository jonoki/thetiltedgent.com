01 Company Overview
AT&T is one of the three national US wireless carriers and a leading builder of fiber-to-the-home broadband, with more than 39 million locations passed, selling mobile connectivity, fiber and fixed-wireless internet, and business networking across the US and, until a pending sale closes, Mexico. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $125.65B, up 2.71% year over year, with net income of $21.89B and EPS of $3.04; on a TTM basis to September 2026 revenue is $128.85B (+3.5%). At a $153.19B market capitalization the company ranks #81 in the S&P 500, and is the smallest of the three national carriers by market value, behind Verizon ($187.80B) and T-Mobile US ($170.33B) at the October 22, 2026 close (stockanalysis.com).
The business is now reported in three segments after a Q1 2026 reorganisation: Advanced Connectivity (wireless, fiber, fixed wireless, business advanced connectivity), Legacy (the copper-era voice and legacy data services being deliberately wound down), and Latin America (Mexican wireless), which AT&T agreed on October 13, 2026 to sell to Grupo Televisa and Fintech Holdings for $2B in cash, expected to close in the first quarter of 2027. Management has re-pointed the whole company at a single metric — free cash flow — guiding to $18B+ in 2026, $19B+ in 2027 and $21B+ in 2028, funding a ~$10B buyback this year inside a $24B+ 2026–2028 repurchase plan. At the Q3 print on October 21 it raised its 2026 adjusted EPS outlook to the $2.40 range (from $2.25–$2.35) and cut planned capital investment for 2027 and 2028 to $22–23B a year.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
39M+ FIBER LOCATIONS LICENSED SPECTRUM PORTFOLIO SUNK CAPITAL / REPLACEMENT COST CONVERGENCE BUNDLING SCALE ECONOMICS REGULATORY BARRIERS SUBSCRIBER SWITCHING FRICTION
AT&T's moat is physical, not intellectual. Passing more than 39 million homes and businesses with fiber and holding a nationwide licensed spectrum portfolio — now deeper by ~50 MHz after the $23B EchoStar purchase — represents tens of billions of sunk capital that a new entrant cannot replicate at any sensible return. Once fiber is in the ground the incremental cost of adding a subscriber is small, which is why penetration (the share of passed homes that sign up) is the key value driver rather than the passings number itself. The genuine strategic asset is convergence: 6.33 million customers now take both wireless and home internet (42.8% of home-internet households), and bundled households are harder to lose — postpaid phone churn was 0.92% a month in Q3. Regulatory barriers (spectrum licensing, rights-of-way, franchise agreements) and the sheer working-capital cost of handset subsidies keep the structure to three national carriers plus resellers — a structure SpaceX is now trying to break with satellite service backed by its own spectrum.
Key Vulnerabilities

Balance sheet leverage. AT&T's own figures put total debt at $159.6B and net debt at $149.8B on September 30, 2026 — 3.13 times annualised adjusted EBITDA after the EchoStar purchase, up from 2.59x a year earlier — and management expects to get back to its 2.5x range during 2029. On stockanalysis.com's basis, which counts lease liabilities, total debt is $180.61B, debt-to-equity 1.45 and enterprise value $323.03B — about 2.1 times the equity market cap. Interest coverage of 3.90x is adequate but not comfortable for a business with this much debt to refinance.

Wireless competitive intensity. T-Mobile is still compounding revenue near 10% against AT&T's 5.0% wireless service growth in Q3, cable operators keep adding phone lines by reselling other carriers' network capacity at prices that reset the low end of the market, and SpaceX's $8B spectrum purchase points to a satellite-plus-spectrum challenger. AT&T's own numbers softened in Q3: postpaid phone net adds slowed to 379,000 from 432,000 and churn rose to 0.92% from 0.86%. Promotional handset economics — device upgrade subsidies — are the swing factor between reported net adds and actual profitability.

Capital intensity with a long payback. Capital investment of $23–24B in 2026 and $22–23B a year in 2027–2028 is being spent to pass locations whose penetration curve takes years to mature; the Lumen assets came in at only ~25% penetration. If penetration disappoints, the fiber build converts into stranded capital rather than free cash flow, and the entire $18B → $21B FCF ramp underpinning the equity story is at risk.

Spectrum deployment execution. Of the ~50 MHz bought from EchoStar, the 20 MHz of 600 MHz low-band is not currently supported in AT&T's radio portfolio and is likely more than a year from carrying traffic. That means $23B of capital is on the balance sheet earning a partial return while the interest cost begins immediately — a real drag on 2026–2027 reported earnings.

Notable One-Off Items
AT&T's reported earnings history over this cycle is dominated by non-recurring items, and comparing GAAP years without adjusting for them is actively misleading. FY2022 carried $27.50B of asset write-downs and restructuring — the media-era goodwill unwind — which is the entire reason the company reported a net loss of $8.06B on $120.74B of revenue that year. FY2024 carried a further $5.08B of write-downs and restructuring, holding net income to $10.75B and EPS to $1.49. FY2025 GAAP EPS of $3.04 more than doubled that, but $0.80 of it was the gain on selling the DirecTV stake, a sale completed in Q3 2025; adjusted EPS rose from $1.95 to $2.12. Q3 2026 swings the other way: GAAP EPS from continuing operations was $0.13 against adjusted EPS of $0.65, after $0.52 a share of adjustments that include the $2.6B non-cash write-down of the Mexico business now held for sale, employee separation charges and other items. Those two items are why trailing GAAP EPS fell from $3.02 at the last edition to $1.87 while adjusted EPS grew.
Two large 2026 transactions distort the balance sheet rather than the income statement: the $5.75B Lumen Mass Markets fiber purchase (closed Feb 2, 2026), whose network assets sit in Forged Fiber 37, held for sale and reported as discontinued operations until the joint venture with GIP and CPP closes, and the $23B EchoStar spectrum acquisition (closed Jul 28, 2026), funded in part with a drawn term loan. Both add capitalised assets and debt without proportionate near-term revenue; net debt was $149.8B at September 30. Buybacks stepped up sharply: $4.50B in FY2025, then $7.47B in the first nine months of 2026 ($2.8B in Q3), against roughly $0.2–0.9B a year in 2021–2024.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying one of America's largest fiber networks at 12.2x trailing GAAP earnings (depressed by the Mexico write-down), about 9.5x the company's 2026 adjusted EPS guide and 8.95x analysts' forward estimate, with a 4.88% dividend that takes about 46% of guided adjusted EPS and a ~$10B buyback shrinking the share count. If management delivers the guided free-cash-flow ramp — $18B+ in 2026 to $21B+ in 2028 — the equity yields roughly 11.7% in free cash flow on today's $153B market cap in 2026, rising toward 14%, while leverage falls back to about 2.5x during 2029. The quarter backed the plan: a record 769,000 internet net adds, Advanced Connectivity EBITDA up 7.3%, 6.33 million bundled households, a raised EPS outlook and lower planned capital spending for 2027–2028.

Bear Case

This is a capital-destruction risk wearing a value-stock costume. ROIC of 8.25% is barely above the cost of capital while the company commits $22–24B a year of capex plus $23B for spectrum that is only partly deployable and $5.75B for fiber assets penetrated at 25%. Net debt is $149.8B, 3.13 times adjusted EBITDA, against $124.76B of equity, and enterprise value ($323.03B) is about 2.1 times the market cap — so shareholders own what is left of a heavily borrowed, low-growth business. Fiber ARPU fell 4%, postpaid phone adds slowed and churn rose in Q3, T-Mobile grows revenue at 9.7% versus AT&T's 3.5%, and SpaceX is buying spectrum to sell phone service from space.

Top 3 Risks
01
Fiber penetration disappoints. The whole thesis rests on converting 40M (soon 60M) passings into paying subscribers at reasonable ARPU. Fiber ARPU fell 4% year over year in Q3 (1.3% in Q2), and the acquired Lumen base sits at ~25% penetration. If penetration stalls, $22–24B of annual capital investment becomes stranded rather than compounding.
02
Leverage plus rates. $149.8B of net debt, 3.13x annualised adjusted EBITDA and 3.90x interest coverage leave little cushion. Management expects to be back in its 2.5x range only during 2029. Any period of higher refinancing costs directly consumes the free cash flow earmarked for buybacks.
03
Wireless price war. T-Mobile's ~10% revenue growth, cable companies reselling wireless service, SpaceX's satellite-plus-spectrum push and rising handset promotion costs can compress the 41.8% Advanced Connectivity margin quickly. Wireless service revenue is 55% of the company; a one-point margin loss there outweighs almost anything happening in fiber.
  Industry Commentary
Convergence and the Fiber Land Grab
EMERGING TAILWIND
Every US operator has concluded the same thing at once: owning both the pipe and the phone lowers churn and raises lifetime value. AT&T bought Lumen's Mass Markets fiber for $5.75B and on October 6 agreed to fold it, with Gigapower, into a joint venture with GIP and CPP Investments; Verizon closed a $20B acquisition of Frontier; T-Mobile has pursued fiber joint ventures. The land grab is finite — homes only get passed once — which is why AT&T's 39M+ locations (heading to 40M+ this year and 60M+ by 2030) and 6.33M converged customers (42.8% of its home-internet base) are the most valuable numbers in the release. Cable is the loser: Charter's revenue fell 1.5% over its last reported year as fiber and fixed wireless took broadband share.
Wireless Competitive Intensity
ONGOING HEADWIND
US wireless is a mature ~$220B (est.) service-revenue pool growing at low single digits, so every net add is taken from someone. AT&T's postpaid phone net adds slowed to 379,000 in Q3 from 432,000 in Q2 and churn rose to 0.92% — while T-Mobile still grew total revenue 9.7% versus AT&T's 3.5%, and cable companies keep adding lines on resold capacity. The escalating cost of handset promotions is the mechanism by which share gains get paid for. A new front opened on October 9: SpaceX announced a deal to buy about $8B of nationwide 800 MHz spectrum for Starlink Mobile, which Morgan Stanley analysts read as a sign SpaceX will be a more aggressive buyer of spectrum (Reuters). AT&T's answer on the Q3 call was that nearly all mobile traffic runs where it already has coverage and much of it starts indoors. The $23B EchoStar purchase buys AT&T capacity headroom, but 20 MHz of it needs new radios before it carries a single bit.
Rates, Debt and Who Gets the Cash Flow
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Telecom is a spread business between the cost of capital and the return on network assets, and AT&T's spread is thin: ROIC of 8.25% against $149.8B of net debt, an enterprise value of $323.03B and 3.13x net leverage after spending $23B on spectrum and $5.75B on fiber inside seven months. The bull reading: the dividend costs about $7.5B a year against $18.01B of TTM free cash flow, the Mexico sale brings in $2B, and every turn of deleveraging plus the ~$10B buyback transfers value to shareholders — at 8.95x forward earnings you are being paid to wait. The bear reading: a company earning ~8% on capital while borrowing at meaningfully positive real rates and reinvesting $22–24B a year is running a treadmill, and the low multiple is not a bargain but an accurate discount for that reality. Both readings depend entirely on whether fiber penetration curves deliver — which is why the 40M-locations checkpoint at year-end 2026 matters more than any quarterly EPS print.
