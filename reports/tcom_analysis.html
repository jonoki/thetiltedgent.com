01 Company Overview

Trip.com Group is, by CNBC's description, the largest online travel provider in Asia by market value, and in July 2026 China's market regulator found that it had abused a dominant market position in China. It runs four brands: Ctrip and Qunar in mainland China, Trip.com for travellers outside China, and Skyscanner, the flight-search site. It earns commissions and fees on hotel rooms, air and rail tickets, packaged tours and corporate travel. It reports in renminbi (RMB). FY2025 net revenue was RMB62.4B, up 17%, with accommodation 42% of revenue and transportation ticketing 36%. GAAP net income of RMB33.3B was more than half one-off: RMB19.9B of gains from investments, mostly the RMB15.2B gain on selling about US$3.0B of its MakeMyTrip stake back to the Indian travel company in July 2025.

2026 has been the year Beijing turned on the company. On January 14 China's market regulator, the SAMR, opened an anti-monopoly investigation; the ADS fell 17.05% that day. On July 25 the SAMR ruled that Trip.com had abused a dominant position by locking hotels into exclusive deals and imposing unreasonable terms. It fined the company RMB3,521M (7.5% of its 2025 China sales revenue), confiscated RMB1,658M of gains and ordered RMB122M of hotel deposits refunded. Growth slowed at the same time. Q2 2026 revenue rose just 6% after 17% in Q1, and the penalty turned the quarter into a GAAP loss. At $38.09 the ADS is down 49.3% in a year and closed October 2 at its lowest close of the past 52 weeks. The market value is $23.99B, against RMB100.5B (≈$15.0B) of cash, short-term investments and time deposits and RMB26.4B of debt at June 30. The question for the stock is whether the regulator has cut the business's earning power for good or charged it a one-time bill.

What an ADS holder actually owns

The New York share is an ADS of Trip.com Group Limited, a Cayman Islands company whose principal executive offices are in Singapore. Its main China websites run through VIEs: Ctrip Commerce for Ctrip and Qunar Beijing for Qunar (annual report). The accounts consolidate them, but an ADS holder's claim on the China business rests on those contracts. Since a March 2021 share subdivision, one ADS equals one ordinary share, so the Nasdaq and Hong Kong lines are the same share in different currencies. The Hong Kong listing is under Chapter 19C (a secondary-style listing for overseas-listed issuers). If 55% or more of worldwide trading moved to Hong Kong over a fiscal year, HKEX would treat Hong Kong as a dual primary listing.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Dominant China OTA (SAMR finding) Hotel supply network Four brands incl. Skyscanner Customer service scale (16,718 staff) Inbound-travel gateway RMB100.5B cash & investments

Trip.com's moat is a two-sided network: Chinese travellers start on Ctrip because the hotel and flight supply is deepest there, and hotels list there because that is where the travellers are. Service is a second layer. Of 43,574 employees at end-2025, 16,718 worked in customer service centres (annual report), which matters in travel because trips go wrong and the agency that fixes them keeps the customer. The SAMR ruling went after the strongest link, the hotel side. Exclusivity and deposit terms that kept hotels on Trip.com's terms are now illegal for this company, and it must adopt "rectification measures." The network remains; the company's power to set commission rates on it is what the ruling reduced.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The regulator now sets part of the business model. The SAMR found a dominant market position under Article 22 of the Anti-Monopoly Law. That finding stays after the fine is paid: future pricing, commission and exclusivity practices in China are judged against it. The company says it "sincerely accepts the Decision."
02
Growth outside the international platform has slowed sharply. Q2 revenue grew 6% overall while the international platform grew more than 50%. With the international platform above 50%, the rest of the business must have grown more slowly than the 6% total. Transportation, the second-largest line, shrank.
03
Margins are falling as marketing spend rises. Adjusted EBITDA margin fell from 33% to 29% in a year, and sales and marketing, which the release puts down to promotion activities, rose to 25% of revenue. Competing for international travellers against Booking and Expedia costs more per customer than serving a home market Trip.com already leads.
04
ADS structure and China exposure. The VIE contracts, US–China listing politics and the RMB rate all sit between the business and an ADS holder's return. The Hong Kong line gives some protection: the same share would still trade in Hong Kong if access to the US listing were ever cut.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort every trailing figure. First, the SAMR penalty: RMB5,180M ($773M) of fine and confiscated gains charged to G&A in Q2 2026, plus a contra-revenue item the SAMR imposed against accommodation revenue; separately, the decision ordered RMB122M of hotel deposits refunded. G&A without it would have been RMB1.2B, 7% of revenue. Second, the MakeMyTrip gain: Q3 2025 "other income" of RMB17,032M included the RMB15.2B gain on selling 34.4M MakeMyTrip shares back to that company for about US$3.0B. Immediately after the sale Trip.com remained MakeMyTrip's largest minority shareholder (annual report). Third, mark-to-market swings: fair-value changes on equity investments and exchangeable notes cost RMB1,454M in Q2 2026 and RMB876M in Q1, and share of affiliates' results swung from a RMB1,151M loss in Q1 to a RMB570M gain in Q2. Excluding share-based pay, the penalty and the fair-value changes (with their tax effects), but not the affiliates' results, Q2 non-GAAP net income was RMB4,798M against RMB5,011M a year earlier (−4.3%). Non-GAAP EPS of RMB7.27 still edged up from RMB7.20 because the share count shrank.

Buybacks. In August 2025 the board authorised a US$5B repurchase program. In 2025 the company bought 10.0M ADS/shares at an average $61.25, leaving about $5.07B of authorisation at year-end (annual report). The weighted basic share count fell from 659.9M in Q2 2025 to 632.3M in Q2 2026 (−4.2%). At today's price the remaining authorisation equals about a fifth of the market value.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $30.75 to the October 2, 2026 close of $38.09, the ADS gained 23.9% on price. The US$0.30 dividend of 2025 adds about one point. The path matters more than the total. The ADS bottomed at a $22.06 month-end in May 2022, then climbed through 2023–2025 to a $75.20 month-end in September 2025, 3.4 times the low. It has since given back almost half: the October 2 close is 49.3% below the October 2, 2025 close of $75.18 and 47.0% below the December 2025 close of $71.91. Five of the nine months of 2026 fell by 12% or more.

The 6-period monthly RSI reads 28.7, back in oversold territory after 23.4 at the June 2026 month-end. The 2022 low was deeper: 17.1 in March 2022, the lowest reading in the series. The 10-month moving average is about $47.41, about $9.30 above the price. The trend is down, and the July rebound after the penalty decision did not reach that average before rolling over. The same RSI peaked at 84.7 in January 2025.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The worst news is known and booked. The SAMR penalty came to RMB5.3B in total, about 3% of today's market value, and the company has accepted it. What is left is a platform the regulator itself found dominant in China, at about book value (P/B 1.01), 10.2x consensus 2026 adjusted earnings and 9.2x 2027. RMB100.5B of cash and investments sits against RMB26.4B of debt, and FY2025 free cash flow was 8.4% of the market value. Outside China, platform revenue grew more than 50% and inbound travel at a high double-digit rate, so the business that will matter in five years is growing fast and outside the SAMR's reach. About $5.07B of unused buyback authorisation at end-2025, including the August 2025 US$5B program, gives management room to shrink the share count at half the price it paid in late 2025. The lowest consensus target, $41.99, is above the price.

Bear Case

The fine is not the expensive part; the finding of market dominance is. Trip.com's China margins rested on exclusive hotel deals and terms the regulator has now ruled illegal for this company, and revenue growth fell from 17% to 6% in one quarter as "operational adjustments" began. Q2 adjusted EBITDA margin fell to 29% from 33% as sales and marketing rose 15%; abroad it competes with Booking and Expedia without a home advantage. The cash pile is real but not all spare: customer advances and payables are funded from it, and US$1.5B of converts can be put in June 2027. Two co-founders left the board in February. The October 2 close was the lowest of the past 52 weeks, the 10-month average is about $9 above the price, and the sell side cut targets after the print.

Top 3 Risks

01
A permanently lower China take rate. With exclusivity gone, hotels can list more widely and push commissions down. If accommodation growth stays near Q2's 6%, down from 21% in 2025, the market will read it as a structural cut in earning power rather than a one-time adjustment.
02
Further regulatory action. The Q1 release said Trip.com "is and has been the subject of investigations or inquiries by national authorities regarding competition law matters, consumer protection issues, and other areas." The anti-monopoly case is closed; the others are not described as closed.
03
Macro and travel demand. Management named elevated energy prices and geopolitical volatility as Q2 headwinds, and transportation revenue fell 12% from Q1. Oil prices and flight disruption feed straight into ticket volumes, and a softer Chinese consumer hits both domestic and outbound travel.
Industry Commentary
China's platform rulebook, applied to travel
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The Trip.com case follows a pattern China's regulator set with e-commerce. CNBC recalled that the SAMR fined Alibaba a record RMB18.2B in 2021 for monopolistic practices, and an analyst it quoted pointed to Trip.com's 2017 fine for forced bundling of value-added services. The 2026 decision names the conduct: exclusive arrangements with hotels "without justifiable cause" and "unreasonable terms on transactions." Trip.com's RMB3,521M fine is 7.5% of its 2025 China sales revenue. The company's annual report notes that the Anti-Monopoly Law allows a fine of up to 10% of the preceding year's sales revenue, and two to five times that in extremely serious cases. The fine therefore sits at three-quarters of the ordinary ceiling, and the conduct finding, which governs how Trip.com may deal with hotels from here, matters more than the cash.

Inbound China and "world-to-world" travel
EMERGING TAILWIND

Trip.com's growth now comes from travel that does not start in China. Inbound travel means foreigners visiting China. In the Q2 release CEO Jane Sun named inbound and "world-to-world" travel as "structural growth drivers." The company served about 20 million inbound travellers in 2025; inbound bookings rose about 90% in Q1 2026 and inbound revenue at a high double-digit rate in Q2. James Liang's Q1 framing was that inbound travel is "contributing to local economic development." The international platform's 50%-plus growth is the part of the story the domestic rulebook does not touch.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
DEBATE

At $23.99B, Trip.com is valued at about a fifth of Booking Holdings ($119.48B) and below Expedia ($31.81B) on market value. FY2025 net revenue was RMB62.4B, about $9.3B at today's rate. The bull framing: a business at book value, 10x forward earnings, with roughly RMB74B of cash and investments net of debt (about $11B, close to half the market value), is pricing a China discount on top of a regulatory discount, and both are already in the numbers. The bear framing: a dominant platform told to stop acting dominant is worth less, and the cash is partly customers' money and partly the 2027 convertible put. Two questions decide it: does China accommodation growth stabilise once the rectification is in place, and does the buyback get used at these prices? The Q3 print in November answers the first, and the share count answers the second.
