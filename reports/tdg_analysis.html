01 Company Overview

TransDigm designs and manufactures highly engineered aircraft components — actuators, ignition systems, pumps, valves, motors, generators, batteries, cargo handling systems, cockpit controls, fastening systems and a long tail of niche parts — sold across three reportable segments: Power & Control, Airframe and a very small Non-aviation business. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $8.83B; fiscal 2026, which ended September 30, 2026, came in at $10.56B, up 19.5% (11.3% organic, meaning excluding acquired businesses), and management has guided fiscal 2027 to $11.95–12.15B. It ranks #190 in the S&P 500 at a $66.45B market capitalisation.

This report leads with the capital structure, not the earnings, because the capital structure is the story. Almost every other aerospace supplier is a manufacturing business that happens to carry debt. TransDigm is a financial machine that happens to make parts. Management states that about 90% of net sales come from unique proprietary products — parts for which TransDigm holds the design approval and is, in practice, the only qualified supplier of a component already certified on an aircraft that is flying today. That position is priced accordingly, and the pricing is converted into EBITDA As Defined margin of 53.0% in the September 2026 quarter (52.7% for fiscal 2026). The cash that margin throws off is then borrowed against — net debt/EBITDA of 5.9× at the end of fiscal 2026 (the CFO’s figure on the November 10 call; 5.8× on our calculation from the release) against a stated target band of 5–7× — and returned to shareholders in irregular, enormous special dividends rather than a regular quarterly payout. The arithmetic consequence is a stockholders' deficit of $10.0B: book equity is not small, it is deeply negative. Everything below follows from that one design choice, including the fact that two of the most commonly quoted ratios in equity analysis cannot honestly be printed for this company at all.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOLE-SOURCE POSITIONS ~90% PROPRIETARY SALES CERTIFICATION LOCK-IN AFTERMARKET RAZOR-BLADE DECADES-LONG PLATFORM LIFE ACQUISITION PLAYBOOK PRICING AUTHORITY

The moat is not technology and it is not scale. It is regulatory switching cost. When a commercial aircraft is certified, every part on it is certified as part of that configuration. Replacing an approved component with a functionally identical one from a different supplier requires qualification, testing, documentation and airworthiness approval — an engineering and regulatory project that can cost far more than a lifetime of buying the incumbent part, and which no airline will undertake for a PMA saving of a few hundred dollars on a $2,000 item. So the buyer's real choice is not "TransDigm or a competitor." It is "pay TransDigm's price, or ground the aeroplane."

Management states that approximately 90% of net sales are generated by unique proprietary products. The commercial consequence is visible in a single number: EBITDA As Defined margin of 53.0% in the September 2026 quarter and 52.7% across fiscal 2026 — for a company that manufactures physical metal parts in factories. Compare it to the 43.6% margin in the Non-aviation segment, where the same management, the same operating discipline and the same acquisition model are applied without the certification barrier. Roughly nine points of margin sit on the barrier itself. And because installed aircraft fly for two to four decades, the aftermarket annuity attached to a design win in 1998 is still paying in 2026 — the razor was sold a generation ago and the blades are still being invoiced.

The second half of the moat is the acquisition machine, which is really a mechanism for buying more of the first half. TransDigm identifies businesses with proprietary, sole-source, aftermarket-heavy content, pays a full price, and applies its pricing and cost model. In fiscal 2026 alone it closed Jet Parts Engineering and Victor Sierra for $2.2B (April 2026) and Prince & Izant for about $1.07B (announced July 2026, completed September 28, 2026); acquisitions in fiscal 2026 totalled about $4.4B, and management says it enters fiscal 2027 with "significant liquidity and the financial flexibility to pursue additional M&A" (Q4 FY2026 release). That firepower is funded with debt, which is where the vulnerabilities begin.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Government pricing scrutiny — the named, documented risk. The Department of Defense Inspector General has examined TransDigm's pricing on sole-source military spare parts twice. A 2019 report led to a $16.1M refund demand. A second report was the subject of a January 19, 2022 House Oversight Committee hearing titled "Price Gouging in Military Contracts," which found approximately $20.8M of excess profit obtained by acquiring companies holding sole-source military contracts and raising spare-part prices. Then-Chairwoman Carolyn Maloney said "all $20.8 million should be repaid"; a DoD official testified at that hearing that the refund had not been received. Then-CEO Kevin Stein said the company would "gladly pay money back" if it found it had made a mistake. In April 2022 Maloney and Senator Grassley again called on the company to repay. The dollar amounts are trivial against $10.6B of revenue — the precedent is not. The entire model rests on charging what a monopolist can charge; a legislated pricing constraint on defense sole-source parts would attack the mechanism rather than the margin.
▸
Leverage is a policy, not an accident. Total debt of $34.76B (long-term $33.74B, current portion $0.15B and $0.87B drawn on the receivables facility) against $2.28B of cash gives net debt near $32.5B. Gross debt/EBITDA As Defined is 6.25× and net debt/EBITDA about 5.8× on our calculation from the release (5.9× as the CFO reported it on the November 10 call), against the 5–7× range management says it is comfortable operating in. Management now expects the ratio to fall below 5× by the end of fiscal 2027; its record is to borrow again whenever the ratio drifts low, and pay out the difference. It works while EBITDA keeps growing and credit markets stay open. It is a very different instrument if either stops being true.
▸
The single-customer concentration nobody names: the flying fleet. Aftermarket revenue is a function of flight hours. It is not a function of aircraft ownership, orders or backlog — it is a function of aeroplanes actually taking off. Any shock that grounds fleets converts a high-fixed-charge balance sheet from an advantage into a problem in a single quarter. The 2020 experience is the template, and the leverage today is higher than it was then.
▸
Acquisition dependence and margin dilution. Fiscal 2026 net sales growth of 19.5% was 11.3% organic; acquired businesses added $725M (8.2%) of it. Management flagged on the Q3 call over 200 basis points of EBITDA margin dilution from recent acquisitions (a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point). The model requires a continuous supply of proprietary aerospace businesses at prices that still work after the fact. As TransDigm gets larger, each deal has to be larger to matter, and the pool of targets with genuine sole-source content is finite.
04 Key Financial Metrics

Before the table: what "EBITDA As Defined" actually is. TransDigm leads with its own profit metric, and it is not a standard one. Per the company's definition in its earnings releases, EBITDA As Defined is EBITDA plus certain non-operating items recorded as corporate expenses, including non-cash compensation charges from stock option and deferred compensation plans, foreign currency gains and losses, acquisition-integration costs, acquisition transaction-related expenses, and refinancing costs. Read that list carefully: for a company that acquires businesses continuously and refinances debt continuously, "acquisition transaction expenses" and "refinancing costs" are not unusual events — they are the operating rhythm of the business. Adding them back is defensible for covenant purposes (this is the credit-agreement metric) and generous for valuation purposes. The 52.7% headline margin for fiscal 2026 should be read as the number the debt documents care about, not as an owner's economic margin. The GAAP operating margin for fiscal 2026 was 46.5% (stockanalysis.com) — still extraordinary, and about six points lower.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the December 2021 close of $636.28 to the November 13, 2026 close of $1,217.28, TDG is up +91.3% on price alone — roughly a 14.3% compound annual rate over 4.87 years. But the price return understates the outcome badly, because this company returns capital in lumps that the unadjusted line simply loses. Add the four special dividends paid across the window — $18.50 + $35.00 + $75.00 + $90.00 = $218.50 per share — and the crude total return is roughly +126%, or about 18.2% annualised (est., ignoring reinvestment timing). That gap of about 34 percentage points over five years is the single best illustration on this page of why looking at a TransDigm price chart without the dividends is analytically wrong.

The recent stretch has been poor, then partly recovered. 08 Summary & Key Risks

The machine, in order. Start with the part. TransDigm holds the design approval on a component certified onto an aircraft that will fly for thirty years. About 90% of net sales come from such proprietary products. Because the customer's alternative is a re-certification project rather than a phone call to a competitor, the price is set by the switching cost, not by the manufacturing cost. That produces a 59.67% gross margin and a 52.7% EBITDA As Defined margin on physical hardware. The margin produces cash — $1.91B of free cash flow in fiscal 2026 (operating cash flow less capital spending). The cash supports debt: $34.76B of it, held deliberately at about 5.8× net debt to EBITDA within a stated 5–7× band. And the debt capacity is converted into shareholder returns two ways: buybacks (a record $2.8B in fiscal 2026, $943M in the September quarter alone at an average $1,150 per share) and irregular special dividends ($218.50 per share across four payments since August 2022). Then the whole loop is fed more raw material by acquisition — $2.2B closed in April 2026, $1.07B closed on September 28, 2026, and about $4.4B across fiscal 2026.

Why ROE and debt-to-equity are printed as "n/m" and not as numbers. This is worth stating plainly because it is where most screens break. Total stockholders' equity is negative $10.0B; book value per share is −$183. That is not distress — it is the accumulated arithmetic of paying out and buying back more than the company has retained. But it means the denominator of return on equity and of debt-to-equity is a negative number. Divide $2.11B of net income by −$10.0B and you get "−21.1%", which a screen will read as a company losing money at a 20% rate on its capital when in fact it earned more than two billion dollars. Divide $34.76B of debt by −$10.0B and you get "−3.5×", which is meaningless in both sign and magnitude. Printing either figure would be worse than printing nothing. The correct substitutes are on the table: ROIC of 15.12%, ROA of 12.06%, and net debt/EBITDA of 5.8×. Any analysis of this company that quotes an ROE has not understood what it is looking at.

Where the leverage actually shows up. Not in the operating line — the fiscal 2026 GAAP operating margin was 46.5%. It shows up between there and net income: that operating margin becomes a net margin of 19.97%, and about 70% of the 26-point compression is net interest expense ($2.01B) on $34.8B of debt, the rest tax. Put differently, roughly 40% of TransDigm's operating profit currently goes to lenders rather than owners. The bull reading is that this is a deliberate, low-cost financing of a monopoly annuity, and that the lenders take a fixed claim while the owners keep all the upside on a business growing revenue at 19.5%. The bear reading is that a company carrying net debt of 5.8× EBITDA in a cyclical industry has a much shorter distance between "guidance raised" and "covenant conversation" than the current 0.95 beta implies.

Valuation, on the bases that mean something here. Against trailing GAAP earnings the stock is 34.3×. Against FY2027 guided adjusted EPS of $47.09–47.94 it is 25.4–25.9×; against FY2027 consensus of $48.09 it is 25.3×. On enterprise value — the only frame that respects the balance sheet — $98.9B of EV (our calculation: $66.45B market cap plus $34.76B of debt less $2.28B of cash) against $6.20–6.35B of guided FY2027 EBITDA As Defined is 15.6–16.0×, and 17.8× fiscal 2026's $5.56B. Against fiscal 2026 free cash flow of $1.91B the equity trades at roughly 34.7×. Note what the EV framing does that the P/E does not: it charges the buyer for the $32.5B of net debt up front, which is the honest way to price a company whose shareholders are paid only after a very large debt. At 16–18× EV/EBITDA for a business with 52%+ margins, mid-teens growth and a certification moat, the multiple is demanding but not absurd — which is why 23 analysts rate it Buy overall, yet 11 of them say Hold and their targets run from $1,100 to $1,754.

Bull Case

A genuine monopoly annuity, priced at about 17.8× EV/EBITDA after a drawdown of up to 32.7% (July 2025 to September 2026 monthly closes) that no fundamental development justified. About 90% of sales are proprietary; the certification barrier is worth roughly nine margin points on the company's own Non-aviation control group; all three channels grew double digits organically in fiscal 2026 (commercial OEM about +14%, aftermarket about +13%, defense about +10%) and the fourth quarter beat the top of company guidance. ROIC of 15.12% says the operating business earns its capital before any leverage. Management bought back a record $2.8B of stock in fiscal 2026 ($943M in the September quarter at an average $1,150), says it has "significant liquidity" for more M&A, and holds an unused option to declare another special dividend inside its 5–7× leverage band. The average analyst target of $1,441 implies 18% upside (read November 16).

Bear Case

$34.76B of debt, a $10.0B stockholders' deficit, and net leverage of about 5.8× on a business whose revenue is ultimately a function of aeroplanes flying. Roughly 40% of operating profit already goes to interest. Fiscal 2026 reported growth of 19.5% was 11.3% organic — the rest was purchased, and the purchases diluted EBITDA margin by over 200 basis points: the margin fell to 52.7% from 53.9%, and FY2027 guidance implies about 52.0%. The 7.5% "yield" some data sites still print is a one-off borrowed distribution from September 2025, not income. The pricing model has already drawn two DoD Inspector General reports and a congressional hearing on price gouging; the amounts were small but the precedent is not. And a 0.95 beta for a company carrying net debt of 5.8× EBITDA is a market judgement that has never been tested by a real demand shock at this debt level.

Top 3 Risks

01
Flight hours, not orders. The aftermarket annuity that produces the margin depends on aircraft actually flying, and the balance sheet has no tolerance for an extended interruption. A demand shock hits revenue immediately while $34.8B of debt and its interest coupon do not move at all. This is the risk that changes the sign of the equity, not its multiple.
02
Regulatory constraint on sole-source pricing. Two DoD Inspector General reports found excess profit ($16.1M in 2019, $20.8M examined at the January 19, 2022 House Oversight hearing) and Congress has repeatedly demanded repayment. The refunds are immaterial; a statutory or contractual pricing rule on sole-source defense spares would not be. Defense is a minority of sales, but the same commercial logic that draws scrutiny there is the logic underneath the commercial aftermarket book.
03
The acquisition engine stalling or misfiring. Acquired businesses supplied $725M of the $1,726M rise in fiscal 2026 sales (42%), and the buying diluted margin by more than 200 basis points. The model requires an ongoing supply of proprietary, sole-source aerospace targets at prices that still work — with each deal needing to be bigger to matter at a $10.6B revenue base. Overpay once at 5.8× leverage and the deleveraging is not optional.
  Industry Commentary
The Aftermarket Cycle Is Still Running Hot
EMERGING TAILWIND
Commercial aftermarket revenue grew about 13% organically in fiscal 2026 (10-K), and about 55% of the year's net sales came from the aftermarket. Aftermarket demand is a function of flight hours and of fleet age: when new-aircraft deliveries run behind demand, operators fly older aeroplanes harder and longer, and older aeroplanes consume more spares. That is a direct transfer of economics from the airframers to the component aftermarket, and TransDigm sits at the highest-margin end of it. The double-digit aftermarket growth is the evidence, and it is the segment of TransDigm's revenue that carries the least OEM price pressure and the most pricing authority.
OEM Build Rates: The Other Half of the Engine
ONGOING HEADWIND / RECOVERING
TransDigm's commercial OEM channel grew about 14% organically in fiscal 2026 (10-K); on the Q3 call co-COO Patrick Murphy reported commercial transport OEM revenue up 25% and said the teams were positioned to support rising build rates, and the fiscal 2027 outlook assumes OEM growth in the high single digits to low double digits. This is the leading indicator for the next decade of aftermarket: every shipset installed today becomes a thirty-year spares annuity. But OEM is also the lower-margin channel — parts sold into a new-build programme face an assembler with real negotiating leverage, unlike an airline holding a grounded aircraft. Rising OEM mix therefore lifts revenue growth and pressures margin at the same time, which is part of why the EBITDA As Defined margin fell to 52.7% in fiscal 2026 from 53.9% a year earlier (acquisitions also weighed).
Monopoly Pricing Is a Political Asset Class
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
TransDigm is the clearest listed expression of a strategy — buy the sole-source position, then price it — that is commercially unimpeachable and politically radioactive. Two DoD Inspector General reports and a January 2022 House Oversight hearing explicitly titled "Price Gouging in Military Contracts" put the company's name on the practice, with $16.1M and then $20.8M of identified excess profit and bipartisan demands for repayment. Bull framing: a decade of scrutiny has produced demands for roughly $37M in total against a company now earning $5.6B of EBITDA a year — the political risk has been repeatedly tested and repeatedly priced at near zero, and the commercial aftermarket, where most of the money is made, is not subject to government pricing rules at all. Bear framing: every one of those hearings is a rehearsal. A future defense authorisation that mandates cost data or caps profit on sole-source spares would not dent earnings — it would establish that the price of a certified monopoly part is negotiable, and that is the one assumption the entire 52.7% margin rests on.
