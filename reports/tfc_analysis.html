01 Company Overview

Truist Financial Corporation is a top-10 US commercial bank with $556 billion in total assets (June 30, 2026), $409.4 billion of deposits and $329.2 billion of held-for-investment loans, operating principally across the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic. The company was created by the 2019 merger of equals between BB&T and SunTrust — the legal entity traces to 1872 — and it books its business in two reporting segments, Consumer & Small Business Banking and Wholesale Banking. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $18.43 billion, and trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $19.03 billion, up 4.75% year over year. Reported revenue growth across 2024–2025 is severely distorted by one-off items and should not be read as underlying growth — see Section 04. Truist ranks #420 globally by market capitalisation.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SOUTHEAST DEPOSIT FRANCHISE $409B DEPOSIT BASE TOP-10 US BANK SCALE 54-YEAR DIVIDEND RECORD REGULATORY CHARTER BARRIER SWITCHING COSTS MIDDLE-MARKET RELATIONSHIPS

A bank's moat is its funding cost, and Truist's is a $409 billion deposit base assembled across the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic — a footprint in the part of the United States that has been gaining population and business formation while the Northeast and Midwest have not. That is a genuine structural advantage: deposit growth and loan demand are demographically supported rather than share-shifted, and Truist competes there as a top-10 US commercial bank against mostly larger national players and mostly smaller regionals. Retail deposits are sticky — the average cost of total deposits was 1.56% in Q2 2026 against a Fed funds environment far above that, which is the moat expressed as a number. Layer on charter and capital requirements that make de novo entry effectively impossible, treasury-management and payments relationships that are painful to move, and a dividend maintained for 54 consecutive years, and the franchise is durable. What it is not, any longer, is differentiated. The insurance brokerage that gave Truist a genuinely distinct, capital-light, high-multiple earnings stream is gone. What remains is a very good conventional spread lender with a growing but conventional capital-markets and wealth business.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. The franchise is now a commodity spread lender. Having sold Truist Insurance Holdings, the company's earnings depend far more on NIM and credit than they did in 2023. NIM-TE was 2.98% in Q2 2026, down 4 basis points sequentially from 3.02%, and management cut full-year net interest income growth guidance from +2%–3% to +1%–1.5%. Fee income is growing well, but it is now the ordinary banking fee stack — investment banking, wealth, payments — that every peer also owns.

2. Deposit mix is deteriorating. Noninterest-bearing deposits fell from 27% to roughly 25% of total deposits, which management named directly as the reason for the NII guidance cut. Cumulative interest-bearing deposit beta is 45% and total deposit beta 30% — both improved by one point sequentially, but the mix shift is doing more damage than the beta improvement is repairing.

3. Commercial real estate concentration. CRE loans were $24.4 billion and commercial construction a further $7.6 billion at March 31, 2026 — together 9.7% of the $329.2 billion held-for-investment book. Management described "continued improvement in sectors like office and multifamily" in Q2 2026. Office-specific balances and the reserve held against office were not separately disclosed in any source retrieved for this report and are therefore not stated. That is itself a disclosure gap a reader should weigh, and it is the single most important number missing from this page.

4. Unrealised securities losses sitting in equity. Accumulated other comprehensive loss was $(6,337) million at March 31, 2026 against tangible common equity of roughly $41 billion — about 15% of the tangible equity base. It does not touch earnings, but it is real, and Silicon Valley Bank demonstrated the mechanism by which it stops being theoretical.

Notable One-Off Items — read this before using any trailing per-share number

Truist's reported financials from 2023 through 2025 contain distortions large enough to invert the sign of the numbers. Anyone comparing TFC's trailing earnings, revenue growth or margins against peers without adjusting for these would reach the wrong conclusion.

The insurance sale (May 6–7, 2024). Truist completed the sale of Truist Insurance Holdings, its insurance brokerage, generating approximately $10.1 billion in after-tax cash proceeds and an after-tax gain of approximately $4.7 billion. Including $4.6 billion released from deconsolidating TIH's intangibles, the transaction added $9.4 billion to CET1 capital — a pro-forma increase of 230 basis points to 12.4%. This was the single largest event in the company's post-merger history and it is entirely non-recurring.

The securities repositioning (same announcement). Truist used the proceeds to sell $27.7 billion of lower-yielding securities carrying a book value of $34.4 billion — a weighted average book yield of just 2.80% — crystallising an after-tax loss of $5.1 billion. It reinvested $18.7 billion into shorter-duration securities at 5.27% (5.22% blended including hedges) and retained $20.7 billion in cash. The repositioning cut CET1 by 107 basis points to 11.4% pro forma but raised tangible book value per share by $7.16, or 33%, to $28.80, and added $160 million of net interest income in Q2 2024 and roughly $710 million across full-year 2024.

What that does to the reported series. The $5.1 billion securities loss ran through revenue. FY2024 reported revenue was $11,408 million — down 36.31% — while the insurance gain sat in discontinued operations, producing a reported net income of $4,469 million and a nonsensical 39.17% "profit margin." FY2025 then reported revenue of $18,425 million, up 61.51%. Neither figure describes anything happening in the business: the collapse and the rebound are the same transaction, seen twice. Truist's revenue did not fall by a third in 2024 and did not grow by 62% in 2025.

And 2023. Fiscal 2023 produced a net loss of $1,452 million, diluted EPS of −$1.09, against $5,927 million of net income in 2022 — a year driven by non-recurring charges. The specific composition of those 2023 charges was not verified from a primary filing within this report's source budget and is therefore not itemised here; the loss itself is confirmed. A reader building a five-year EPS trend for TFC will find −$1.09 (2023), $3.35 (2024), $3.82 (2025) and $4.35 (TTM) and should treat only the last two as describing the operating business.

The good news. The trailing twelve months to June 2026 — the basis for the 11.58x P/E on this page — is clean of these items. TFC's current trailing multiple is comparable to peers. Its 2023–2025 reported history is not.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The simplification is done and it worked. ROTCE has climbed from 12.3% a year ago to 15.4%, the efficiency ratio from 59.3% to 58.0%, and the net charge-off rate from 0.60% to 0.50% — all three moving the right way simultaneously. Wholesale Banking is compounding fees hard (investment banking and trading up 72% in Q2 2026), CET1 of 10.9% against a 10% target funds a $5 billion buyback — roughly 8% of the company annually — and the Southeast footprint sits in the fastest-growing region in the country. At 11.58x trailing earnings, 1.51x tangible book and a 4.13% yield backed by 54 consecutive years of dividends, a rerating toward the 16%–18% long-term ROTCE target is not priced in.

Bear Case

Truist sold the one business that made it different. What remains is a conventional spread lender whose spread is compressing: NIM fell to 2.98%, noninterest-bearing deposits slid from 27% to ~25% of the base, and management cut full-year NII guidance from +2%–3% to +1%–1.5% within a single quarter. The 15.4% ROTCE still sits below the company's own 16%–18% target, and 11 of 21 analysts rate it Hold with three at sell-equivalent. Meanwhile a $(6.3) billion AOCI deficit — about 15% of tangible common equity — sits in the securities book, and a new CEO takes over on September 1, 2026 with no track record in the seat. Five-year total return is roughly +8%: the franchise has not yet proved it can compound.

  Industry Commentary
Deposit Mix Is Beating Deposit Beta
ONGOING HEADWIND

Truist's cumulative interest-bearing deposit beta improved from 46% to 45% and total deposit beta from 31% to 30% in Q2 2026 — genuinely good news on pricing discipline. It did not matter. Noninterest-bearing deposits fell from 27% to roughly 25% of total deposits, and management named that mix shift, not beta, as the reason NII guidance was cut to +1%–1.5%. The average cost of total deposits is 1.56%, up 1bp sequentially from 1.55%. When customers move money from checking into interest-bearing accounts, the bank pays more regardless of how well it holds the line on rates offered. This is the defining industry dynamic for spread lenders right now and it is not within Truist's control.

The Securities Book and the SVB Mechanism
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Truist holds $65.4 billion of available-for-sale securities at fair value and $46.4 billion of held-to-maturity securities at amortised cost, with an accumulated other comprehensive loss of $(6,337) million at March 31, 2026. Here is the mechanism that matters: AFS securities are marked to market through equity (AOCI), not earnings — so a bank can carry billions in unrealised losses while reporting perfectly healthy profits. HTM securities are not marked at all. The losses become real only if the bank is forced to sell, which happens when deposits flee. That is exactly what happened at Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023, and it is why TFC fell 27.4% that month despite having nothing wrong with its earnings. Truist has already demonstrated the earnings-versus-capital trade explicitly: in 2024 it chose to realise $5.1 billion of after-tax losses to reinvest at 5.27% instead of 2.80% — voluntarily, funded by the insurance sale, on its own timetable. That is the good version of this event. The involuntary version is what the $(6.3) billion still represents.

Southeast Demographics vs. a Commoditised Franchise
EMERGING TAILWIND

Truist banks the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic — the region gaining population and business formation while much of the country is flat or shrinking. For a deposit-funded lender, that is the closest thing to a structural growth tailwind available: loan demand and low-cost deposits arrive without having to be won from a competitor. Middle-market deposits grew 12% in Q2 2026. Bull framing: a franchise with demographic wind behind it, 15.4% ROTCE rising toward a 16%–18% target, 10.9% CET1 funding an ~8%-of-shares annual buyback, priced at 11.58x earnings and 1.51x tangible book — the geography does the compounding and the buyback does the rest. Bear framing: geography is not a moat if every competitor wants the same footprint, and Truist has just sold the asset that made it structurally different. A conventional spread lender earning 15.4% on tangible equity in a good credit environment, with NIM falling and deposit mix worsening, is worth roughly what it trades for. On this reading the five-year +8% total return is not an aberration to be corrected — it is the base rate.
