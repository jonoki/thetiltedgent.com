01 Company Overview
The TJX Companies is the world's largest off-price apparel and home-fashions retailer, selling branded and designer merchandise at a discount to full-price channels through 5,262 stores across the United States, Canada, Europe and Australia under the TJ Maxx, Marshalls, HomeGoods, HomeSense, Sierra, Winners and TK Maxx banners. Fiscal 2026 (ended January 31, 2026) revenue reached $60.37 billion, up 7.1% YoY, on consolidated comparable-store sales growth of 5% and diluted EPS of $4.87 (+14%). On a trailing-twelve-month basis revenue is $61.58 billion (+8.1%) with net income of $5.79 billion. At a $166.64 billion market capitalization TJX is the 133rd most valuable listed company globally and roughly the 76th largest in the United States — bigger than Ross Stores ($75.8B) and Burlington ($21.0B) combined, and worth more than several of the department-store chains whose inventory mistakes it monetizes.
The business model is deliberately un-glamorous: a large, decentralised organisation of buyers purchases opportunistically — close-outs, cancelled orders, packaway, mid-season overruns — rather than committing months ahead to a planned assortment. That merchandise is flowed to stores in small, rapidly rotating lots, creating the "treasure hunt" that drives repeat traffic without the advertising and markdown machinery full-price retail requires. The structural result shows up in the returns line: ROE of 61.25% and ROIC of 31.25% on a gross margin of only 31.35%. TJX does not earn its returns on price; it earns them on turns.

Vendor Relationship Depth
The moat is a buying network, not a brand. Off-price economics rest on one asymmetry: TJX can absorb, in size and without notice, inventory that a vendor or a full-price retailer needs to move. Doing that requires three things simultaneously — a very large buying organisation with standing relationships across thousands of vendors, a store network big enough to place an odd-lot purchase without wrecking any single store's assortment, and a logistics system that can flow small quantities of many SKUs rather than large quantities of few. TJX has all three at a scale no one else does: its $61.58 billion of trailing revenue is 63.3% of the combined revenue of the three scaled U.S. off-price players (TJX, Ross at $23.78B, Burlington at $11.91B). A vendor that wants to clear a problem in one phone call has essentially one address.
Two consequences follow. First, the model is counter-cyclical on the supply side: when full-price retailers over-order, when a season turns warm, when a department-store chain shrinks or liquidates, TJX's input costs improve. Herrman's Q1 FY27 framing — "excellent buying opportunities," availability "outstanding" — is the model describing its own tailwind. Second, the model is counter-cyclical on the demand side too: a consumer trading down from full price arrives at TJX rather than leaving the category. Very few retailers are levered positively to both the supply and demand consequences of a weak retail environment. That combination is why the market pays a P/E of 29.3 for a business with a 31.4% gross margin — the multiple is being paid for the durability and the returns profile, not the margin. Advertising intensity stays low because the merchandise is the marketing; SG&A ran 19.5% of sales in Q1 FY27, essentially flat year-on-year even as comps rose 6% — operating leverage in its purest form.
Key Vulnerabilities

1. Inventory availability is not contractual. The entire model depends on other people's mistakes. If the supply chain tightens structurally — vendors producing closer to demand, better demand-planning software, fewer over-orders, fewer distressed retailers to buy from — the buying advantage compresses. TJX has no long-term supply agreements to fall back on; merchandise margin is re-earned every season. Inventory per store was up 6–7% in Q1 FY27, which is a positive read on availability today but says nothing about next year.

2. Tariff and sourcing-cost exposure is real and two-sided. TJX imports a large share of its merchandise, and tariffs raise landed cost on goods it buys directly. Management's own FY27 guidance explicitly assumes "a higher cost of fuel will be in place for the remainder of the year," a negative to margin versus prior expectations. The offset — that tariffs squeeze full-price retailers harder and generate more distressed inventory — is a second-order benefit with uncertain timing, while the cost hit is first-order and immediate.

3. Valuation leaves no room for a comp miss. At 29.3x trailing and 28.6x forward earnings with a PEG of 3.21, TJX trades at a meaningful premium to the apparel-retail group. FY27 guidance implies EPS of $5.08–$5.15 on 3–4% comps; consensus FY27 EPS is $5.22. A single quarter of flat comps would compress both the earnings estimate and the multiple simultaneously — and the Q2 print lands the day after this snapshot, with company guidance of $1.15–$1.17 sitting slightly below the $1.18 street estimate.

4. Digital and ultra-low-price competition. The treasure-hunt format does not translate cleanly online, and TJX's e-commerce presence is small relative to its store base. Meanwhile Shein and Temu attack the same value-seeking customer at a lower price point with a direct-from-factory cost structure, and Amazon continues to expand branded apparel. None of these replicates the branded-close-out proposition, but they compete for the same discretionary dollar and can cap pricing.

Notable One-Off Items

Credit-card interchange litigation settlement (FY2026). TJX recorded a $470 million gain from a credit-card interchange litigation settlement, partially offset by $249 million of related expenses, for a net $221 million pretax benefit. This is the single largest distortion in the trailing numbers: GAAP FY26 EPS of $4.87 compares with adjusted EPS of $4.73, and GAAP pretax margin of 12.1% compares with an adjusted 11.7%. Any year-on-year comparison that uses GAAP FY26 as the base — including parts of the TTM P/E and the 20.9% TTM EPS growth figure — is flattered by roughly 14 cents of non-recurring earnings.

Currency translation and hedging. With 22.7% of revenue outside the U.S. (TJX Canada plus TJX International), reported growth carries a translation component that management routinely strips out via constant-currency comps. Q1 FY27 gross margin expansion of 180 basis points was attributed partly to favourable hedges — a genuine benefit, but a mark-to-market one that does not repeat automatically. Inventory per store was up 7% reported versus 6% constant currency, quantifying the FX drag/benefit at roughly one point in that period.

Fuel and freight cost assumption. FY27 guidance explicitly embeds an assumption of higher fuel costs for the remainder of the year, a negative revision to the margin outlook versus prior expectations. This is a genuine cost headwind rather than an accounting artefact, and it is one of the few items management has pre-flagged against its own raised guidance. Beyond these three items, there are no material write-downs, litigation charges, acquisitions or unusual tax items in the trailing period.

Five-year trajectory. TJX closed at $61.57 in September 2021 and at $150.85 on August 18, 2026 — a price return of +145.0%, or roughly 19.6% compounded annually, before the ~1.3% dividend yield collected along the way. The path was not linear. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

TJX is the rare retailer that benefits from both sides of a difficult retail environment — consumers trading down arrive as customers, and struggling full-price retailers arrive as suppliers. That shows up in a 61.25% ROE, a 31.25% ROIC and 8.1% revenue growth on a business already doing $61.6 billion. FY27 guidance was raised to $5.08–$5.15 EPS on 3–4% comps after a Q1 that beat by 18%, HomeGoods comps ran +9% and international segment profit grew 32% off a ~7% margin that is half Marmaxx's — a multi-year convergence lever. At $150.85 the stock is 11.3% off its high, below both key moving averages, and 17.6% below a $177.40 street target set by 20 of 22 analysts rating it a buy.

Bear Case

You are paying 29.3x trailing and 28.6x forward earnings, a 3.21 PEG, for a retailer growing high-single-digit revenue and low-double-digit EPS — and roughly 14 cents of trailing EPS is a non-recurring interchange-litigation gain. The market has already told you what it thinks: the stock fell on a large Q1 beat and has drifted lower through six of the last seven months despite raised guidance. The buying advantage depends on other retailers' mistakes continuing, which is neither contractual nor observable in advance. Tariffs and a company-flagged fuel-cost headwind pressure landed costs now, while the offsetting inventory windfall is speculative. Q2 guidance of $1.15–$1.17 sits below the $1.18 street number, and the print lands tomorrow.

Top 3 Risks
01
Multiple compression on a decelerating comp. The entire premium — 29.3x versus an estimated ~22x apparel-retail average — rests on comp consistency. FY27 guidance calls for 3–4% comps against a Q1 print of 6%; the implied back half is materially slower. A quarter of flat or negative comps would hit the estimate and the multiple at the same time, and at a 3.21 PEG there is no valuation floor to catch it.
02
Merchandise availability normalising. Off-price margin is bought, not manufactured. If vendors and full-price retailers tighten production discipline, or if the wave of department-store liquidations exhausts itself, the "outstanding availability" management describes today reverses into a merchandise-margin headwind with a one-to-two-season lag — and there is no contract or backlog to cushion it.
03
Tariff and input-cost pass-through failing. Higher import duties and the company's own assumed higher fuel costs raise landed cost immediately. TJX's value proposition is a price gap versus full-price retail; if it passes costs through it narrows that gap, and if it absorbs them the 31.35% gross margin — already thin by design — compresses. The theoretical offset (more distressed inventory from tariff-squeezed competitors) is real but arrives later and is impossible to size in advance.
  Industry Commentary
Consumer Trade-Down as an Off-Price Tailwind
EMERGING TAILWIND
The demand-side driver of the entire cycle. TJX delivered 5% consolidated comps in FY26 and accelerated to 6% in Q1 FY27 — against a plan of 2–3% — with every segment positive: Marmaxx +6%, HomeGoods +9%, Canada +7%, International +4%. Ross Stores grew revenue 11.9% and Burlington 10.6% over the same trailing period, so this is a channel phenomenon, not a share shift among the three. Management explicitly stated it saw "no signs of consumer weakness." The read-through: a value-seeking shopper is not a weak shopper for off-price, and the customer acquired during a trade-down cycle is historically sticky.
Tariffs, Sourcing Costs and Freight
ONGOING HEADWIND
The genuine two-sided variable, and the one most often argued badly. Cost side: TJX imports heavily, so duties raise landed cost on directly-sourced goods, and FY27 guidance explicitly assumes higher fuel costs for the balance of the year — a pre-flagged margin negative against otherwise-raised guidance. Benefit side: tariffs compress full-price retailers with longer lead times and firmer pre-commitments, producing cancelled orders and excess stock that flows to the off-price channel at attractive prices. Q1 FY27 gross margin of 31.3% was up 180bps on better merchandise margin and favourable hedges, which suggests the benefit side is currently winning — but hedges expire and duties do not.
Department-Store Decline as Inventory Supply — Bull vs Bear
STRUCTURAL — DOUBLE-EDGED
Bull framing: every closed department store removes competing capacity and releases branded inventory into the off-price channel. TJX added 129 net stores in FY26 and 48 more in Q1 FY27 to reach 5,262, taking real estate and share while the full-price channel shrinks. Herrman's "excellent buying opportunities" is this dynamic in management language, and TJX's $61.58B of revenue — 63.3% of the scaled U.S. off-price pool — means it gets first call on the biggest lots.

Bear framing: a shrinking pool of distressed sellers is a depleting resource. Once the legacy department stores are gone, the remaining vendor base is more disciplined, more direct-to-consumer, and better at demand planning — precisely the conditions that shrink the supply of mistakes TJX monetises. The bull case and the bear case are the same trend at different points on its curve; the debate is only about where on the curve we currently sit, and a 29.3x multiple assumes it is early.
