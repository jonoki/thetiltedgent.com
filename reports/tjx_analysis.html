01 Company Overview
The TJX Companies is the world's largest off-price apparel and home-fashions retailer, selling branded and designer merchandise at a discount to full-price channels through 5,349 stores across the United States, Canada, Europe and Australia under the TJ Maxx, Marshalls, HomeGoods, Homesense, Sierra, Winners and TK Maxx banners. Fiscal 2026 (ended January 31, 2026) revenue reached $60.37 billion, up 7.1% YoY, on consolidated comparable-store sales growth of 5% and diluted EPS of $4.87. On a trailing-twelve-month basis through the quarter ended October 31, 2026, revenue is $63.20 billion (+7.2%) with net income of $6.17 billion. At the November 19 close the company is worth about $153 billion — some $9 billion more than at the previous edition's September 21 close, after a quarter in which its largest division began to recover.
The business model is deliberately un-glamorous: a large, decentralised organisation of buyers purchases opportunistically — close-outs, cancelled orders, mid-season overruns, goods bought now and stored for a later season — rather than committing months ahead to a planned assortment. That merchandise is flowed to stores in small, rapidly rotating lots, creating the "treasure hunt" that drives repeat traffic without the advertising and markdown machinery full-price retail requires. The structural result shows up in the returns line: ROE of 61.40% and ROIC of 29.70% on a gross margin of only 31.34%. TJX does not earn its returns on price; it earns them on speed — buying cheap and selling through its stock fast.
What the Q3 FY27 print changed. The November 18 report answered the question the August quarter raised. Marmaxx — TJ Maxx, Marshalls and Sierra, about 60% of sales — comped +2% after +1% in Q2, a little ahead of the 1.6% FactSet estimate cited by CNBC, and CFO John Klinger said on the call, as CNBC reported it, that comps were stronger in the back half of the quarter and came mainly from bigger baskets, with transactions roughly flat. The other three divisions kept running: HomeGoods +5%, TJX Canada +6%, TJX International +8%. In the release Herrman said: “Importantly, we expect further improvement at Marmaxx in the fourth quarter.” The stock rose 8.05% on the day. Two things temper the result. Excluding tariff refunds, margins slipped — adjusted pretax margin fell 0.2 points to 12.5% on higher freight and fuel costs — and the fourth-quarter guide (comps +2–3%, adjusted EPS $1.42–$1.45) came in below the consensus CNBC cited. Tariff refunds again lifted the reported figures, this time $132 million, worth $0.05 of EPS.

Vendor Relationship Depth
The moat is a buying network, not a brand. Off-price economics rest on one asymmetry: TJX can absorb, in size and without notice, inventory that a vendor or a full-price retailer needs to move. Doing that requires three things simultaneously — a very large buying organisation with standing relationships across thousands of vendors, a store network big enough to place an odd-lot purchase without wrecking any single store's assortment, and a logistics system that can move small quantities of many different items rather than large quantities of a few. TJX has all three at a scale no one else does: its $63.20 billion of trailing revenue is 62.8% of the combined revenue of the three scaled U.S. off-price players (TJX, Ross at $25.17B, Burlington at $12.21B). A vendor that wants to clear a problem in one phone call has essentially one address.
Two consequences follow. First, the model is counter-cyclical on the supply side: when full-price retailers over-order, when a season turns warm, when a department-store chain shrinks or liquidates, TJX's input costs improve. The Q1 FY27 release's wording — "excellent buying opportunities," availability of branded merchandise "outstanding" — is the model describing its own tailwind. Second, the model is counter-cyclical on the demand side too: a consumer trading down from full price arrives at TJX rather than leaving the category. Very few retailers gain from both the supply side and the demand side of a weak retail environment. That combination is why the market paid a P/E of 29.3 in August, before the Marmaxx stumble, for a business with a ~31% gross margin — and why it still pays 25.3 now: investors pay up for durability, one soft quarter at the largest division took about five points off the multiple by September, and the Q3 recovery has won back about one. Advertising intensity stays low because the merchandise is the marketing; SG&A ran 19.5% of sales in Q1 FY27 and 20.3% in Q2, inflated by bonus accruals tied to the tariff refunds; in Q3 it was 20.1%, flat on a year earlier, and 19.8% excluding refund-related items, helped by lower incentive pay and by lapping last year's TJX Foundation contribution. The pressure has moved to the cost of goods instead: Q3 gross margin excluding refunds fell 0.5 points to 32.1% on freight and fuel.
Key Vulnerabilities

1. Inventory availability is not contractual. The entire model depends on other people's mistakes. If the supply chain tightens structurally — vendors producing closer to demand, better demand-planning software, fewer over-orders, fewer distressed retailers to buy from — the buying advantage compresses. TJX has no long-term supply agreements to fall back on; merchandise margin is re-earned every season. Inventory per store was up 6–7% in Q1 FY27, 2–3% in Q2 and flat in Q3 (total inventory $9.84 billion against $9.35 billion a year earlier) — a sign of discipline that says nothing about next year's supply. Q2 added a second, internal version of the same risk: Marmaxx comped +1% while the other three divisions comped +6–7% from the same buying network. Q3's +2% is better, but still well behind the other divisions' +5–8% and behind the 8% comp Ross Stores reported for the same quarter on November 19.

2. Tariff and sourcing-cost exposure is real and two-sided. TJX imports a large share of its merchandise, and tariffs raise the landed cost (the all-in cost of getting goods into its stores) of what it buys directly. Management's FY27 guidance at Q1 explicitly assumed "a higher cost of fuel will be in place for the remainder of the year." The Q2 and Q3 prints showed the other side of the ledger too: $331 million and then $132 million of refunds of previously paid IEEPA tariffs (duties imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act). These are one-offs, and reading the reported margins without stripping them out overstates what TJX can earn year in, year out: excluding them, Q3 gross margin fell 0.5 points on freight and fuel, and Q4 guidance now includes additional fuel costs.

3. Valuation still assumes the comp comes back. At 25.3x trailing earnings and 24.3x the FY2028 (year to January 2028) consensus EPS of $5.74 (StockAnalysis), with a PEG of 2.99, TJX trades at a premium to the apparel-retail group (est.). The Q3 beat came with a softer fourth-quarter guide — comps of 2–3% and adjusted EPS of $1.42–$1.45, against the $1.49 LSEG consensus CNBC cited — and the full-year adjusted range moved up only at the bottom, to $5.17–$5.20 from $5.15–$5.20. If Marmaxx stalls at low-single-digit comps, there is room for the multiple to fall again.

4. Digital and ultra-low-price competition. The treasure-hunt format does not translate cleanly online, and TJX's e-commerce presence is small relative to its store base. Meanwhile Shein and Temu attack the same value-seeking customer at a lower price point with a direct-from-factory cost structure, and Amazon continues to expand branded apparel. None of these replicates the branded-close-out proposition, but they compete for the same discretionary dollar and can cap pricing.

Notable One-Off Items

IEEPA tariff refunds (Q2 and Q3 FY2027). TJX received $331 million of refunds for a portion of the IEEPA tariffs it had previously paid in Q2 and accrued $112 million of incremental incentive compensation and bonuses against it — a net $219 million pretax benefit, $0.14 of EPS. In Q3 it received a further $132 million, worth $0.05 of EPS net of related incentive accruals: Q3 gross margin of 32.9% compares with 32.1% excluding the refunds, pretax margin of 13.0% with 12.5%, and GAAP EPS of $1.39 with adjusted $1.34. Both sit inside the $5.51 TTM EPS and the $5.33–$5.36 GAAP guide, so the 25.3x trailing P/E is a little flattering.

Credit-card interchange litigation settlement (FY2026). TJX recorded a $470 million gain from a credit-card interchange litigation settlement, partially offset by $249 million of related expenses, for a net $221 million pretax benefit. This is the single largest distortion in the trailing numbers: GAAP FY26 EPS of $4.87 compares with adjusted EPS of $4.73, and GAAP pretax margin of 12.1% compares with an adjusted 11.7%. Any year-on-year comparison that uses GAAP FY26 as the base is flattered by roughly 14 cents of non-recurring earnings. TJX booked it in the fourth quarter of FY2026 (Q4 release, February 25, 2026), so it sits inside the trailing-twelve-month window.

Currency translation and hedging. With 23.1% of trailing revenue outside the U.S. (TJX Canada plus TJX International), reported growth carries a translation component that management routinely strips out via constant-currency comps. Q1 FY27 gross margin expansion of 1.8 points was attributed partly to favourable hedges — a genuine benefit, but one that depends on market prices at the time and does not repeat automatically. Inventory per store was up 7% reported versus 6% constant currency, quantifying the FX drag/benefit at roughly one point in that period.

Fuel and freight cost assumption. FY27 guidance has embedded higher fuel costs since Q1, and the Q4 guide given on November 18 includes additional fuel costs; freight and fuel took 0.5 points off Q3 gross margin excluding refunds. This is a genuine cost headwind rather than an accounting artefact. The Q3 release calls out no other one-off items.

2H FY27
Tariffs, landed costs and the fuel assumption
TJX embedded higher fuel costs into its FY27 guidance, a pre-flagged margin headwind that showed up in Q3 (adjusted gross margin −0.5 points on freight and fuel), and Q2 and Q3 brought $331 million and $132 million of IEEPA tariff refunds — one-offs, not a trend. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

TJX is the rare retailer that benefits from both sides of a difficult retail environment — consumers trading down arrive as customers, and struggling full-price retailers arrive as suppliers. That shows up in a 61.40% ROE, a 29.70% ROIC and 7.2% revenue growth on a business already doing $63.2 billion. Q3 beat the company's own plan on comps, margin and EPS; Marmaxx comps improved from +1% to +2%, with management expecting more in Q4; the other three divisions comped +5–8%; full-year comp guidance went up again, to about 4%; and the long-term store target is 7,500 with openings accelerating to 4% a year. At $139.41 the stock trades at 25.3x trailing earnings, 18% below its 52-week high, with a $165.55 average street target implying +18.8%.

Bear Case

Marmaxx is about 60% of sales and earned nearly 70% of FY26 segment profit (our calculation from the FY26 segment figures), and its +2% Q3 comp still trails the other divisions' +5–8% and the 8% comp Ross Stores reported for the same quarter. Its margin excluding tariff refunds fell from 14.9% to 13.8%, and Wells Fargo flagged price competition in the sector. Freight and fuel are now pushing margins down: adjusted gross margin fell 0.5 points and adjusted pretax margin 0.2 points in Q3, the Q4 guide sits below consensus, and the full-year adjusted EPS range rose only at the bottom. Reported EPS still leans on tariff refunds ($0.19 in Q2 and Q3). At 25.3x trailing and a 2.99 PEG the stock is not cheap.

Top 3 Risks
01
Marmaxx does not keep recovering. The multiple fell from 29.3x in August to 24.3x in September on one division's +1% comp, and has recovered only to 25.3x on its +2%. It is still a premium to an estimated ~22x apparel-retail average. Q4 guidance of +2–3% consolidated comps leaves little room; another quarter of low-single-digit Marmaxx comps while Ross comps +8% would hit estimates as well as the multiple, and would support Wells Fargo's point about price competition in the sector and Jefferies' about Ross out-executing TJX's largest division.
02
Merchandise availability normalising. Off-price margin is bought, not manufactured. If vendors and full-price retailers tighten production discipline, or if the wave of department-store liquidations exhausts itself, the availability that management called "outstanding" in May reverses into a merchandise-margin headwind with a one-to-two-season lag — and there is no contract or backlog to cushion it.
03
Tariff and input-cost pass-through failing. Higher import duties and the company's own assumed higher fuel costs raise landed cost immediately. TJX's value proposition is a price gap versus full-price retail; if it passes costs through it narrows that gap, and if it absorbs them the ~31% underlying gross margin — already thin by design — compresses. The $331 million (Q2) and $132 million (Q3) tariff refunds are the reverse of this risk and will not repeat on schedule; Q3 already showed freight and fuel taking 0.5 points off gross margin excluding refunds. The theoretical offset (more distressed inventory from tariff-squeezed competitors) is real but arrives later and is impossible to size in advance.
  Industry Commentary
Consumer Trade-Down as an Off-Price Tailwind
EMERGING TAILWIND
The demand-side driver of the entire cycle. TJX delivered 5% consolidated comps in FY26, 6% in Q1 FY27 and 4% in each of Q2 and Q3. Ross Stores grew TTM revenue 14.3% and Burlington 10.9% against TJX's 7.2%, so the channel is still growing — but the last two quarters suggest some of the growth is a share shift among the three rather than pure channel expansion, at least at Marmaxx: Ross reported an 8% comp for the quarter to October 31 (after the close on November 19), against Marmaxx's 2%. The read-through: a value-seeking shopper is not a weak shopper for off-price, but the trade-down tailwind is not TJX's alone to keep.
Tariffs, Sourcing Costs and Freight
ONGOING HEADWIND
The genuine two-sided variable, and the one most often argued badly. Cost side: TJX imports heavily, so duties raise landed cost on directly-sourced goods, and FY27 guidance explicitly assumes higher fuel costs for the balance of the year — a pre-flagged margin negative against otherwise-raised guidance. Benefit side: tariffs compress full-price retailers with longer lead times and firmer pre-commitments, producing cancelled orders and excess stock that flows to the off-price channel at attractive prices. Q1 FY27 gross margin of 31.3% was up 1.8 points on better merchandise margin and favourable hedges; Q2 gross margin was 33.4%, or 31.4% (+0.7 points) after stripping out $331 million of IEEPA tariff refunds; Q3 was 32.9%, or 32.1% (−0.5 points) after stripping out $132 million, as freight and fuel costs rose. The benefit side was winning in the first half; in Q3 the cost side took over — hedges expire, refunds are one-offs, and fuel bills and duties do not.
Department-Store Decline as Inventory Supply — Bull vs Bear
STRUCTURAL — DOUBLE-EDGED
Bull framing: every closed department store removes competing capacity and releases branded inventory into the off-price channel. TJX added 129 net stores in FY26 and 135 more in the first nine months of FY27 to reach 5,349, and targets 7,500, taking real estate while the full-price channel shrinks. The "excellent buying opportunities" TJX cited in its Q1 release are this dynamic in management language, and TJX's $63.20B of revenue — 62.8% of the scaled U.S. off-price pool — means it gets first call on the biggest lots.

Bear framing: a shrinking pool of distressed sellers is a depleting resource. Once the legacy department stores are gone, the remaining vendor base is more disciplined, more direct-to-consumer, and better at demand planning — precisely the conditions that shrink the supply of mistakes TJX profits from. The bull case and the bear case are the same trend at different points on its curve; the debate is only about where on the curve we currently sit, and even after the fall in the multiple, 25.3x earnings assumes it is early.
