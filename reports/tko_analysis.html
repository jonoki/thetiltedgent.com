01 Company Overview

TKO Group Holdings is the holding company for the UFC and WWE, and — since the February 28, 2025 acquisition of IMG, On Location and PBR from Endeavor for $3.25 billion in stock — for a third reported segment, IMG. It is not really a sports company in the operating sense; it is a rights owner. TKO owns the leagues it broadcasts, so the intellectual property, the athletes' contracts, the event calendar and the media inventory all sit inside one P&L. Full-year 2025 revenue was $4.735 billion, down 3% from $4.884 billion in 2024 — a decline that is almost entirely an IMG artifact (the 2024 comparative carried Paris Olympics hospitality volume through On Location) — while full-year adjusted EBITDA rose 47% to $1.585 billion. TKO joined the S&P 500 on March 24, 2025.

The central debate is whether the 2026 step-change is a re-rating or a one-time reset. Two enormous media contracts switched on within eight months of each other — ESPN for WWE premium live events (September 2025) and Paramount for the UFC (January 2026) — and they did most of the work in a quarter where consolidated revenue grew 18% and adjusted EBITDA grew 23%. Rights fees of that kind reprice once and then escalate on a contractual schedule. The bull case says TKO has only started monetizing an under-priced library of live content; the bear case says the market has already paid for the step-up, at 66.5x trailing earnings and roughly 17.6x guided 2026 adjusted EBITDA, and that organic growth from here is ticketing, sponsorship and site fees — good businesses, but slower ones.

Control and share classes. TKO is an "Up-C" structure: publicly traded Class A shares (NYSE: TKO) sit above an operating partnership, and Class B shares held by Endeavor/WME Group — itself controlled by Silver Lake following its take-private of Endeavor — carry voting power without a matching claim on the Class A share count. Endeavor's stake was reported at roughly 59% after the February 2025 IMG transaction. The practical consequences show up in the numbers: total shares outstanding are ~189.3M but the public float is ~59.8M (~32%), and weighted-average basic Class A shares in Q2 2026 were only 74.8 million — which is why consolidated net income of $303.9M translated into just $1.34 of Class A EPS. Minority holders own the economics of a fraction of the enterprise and control essentially none of the votes.

The core advantage is ownership. Nearly every other property that sells live sports rights is a league that rents its teams, negotiates with a players' association and shares revenue with franchise owners. TKO owns the UFC and WWE outright — the brands, the title belts, the tape libraries, the athlete contracts and the production trucks. That means when a rights fee steps up, essentially all of the increment falls to segment EBITDA rather than being redistributed: WWE's FY2025 adjusted EBITDA margin of 52.5% and the UFC's 56.7% are numbers no franchise league can print. It also means TKO can manufacture inventory at will — add a Fight Night, add a Saudi site-fee event, move a premium live event to a new market — because there is no schedule committee to ask. The scarcity works in TKO's favour on the other side of the table too: streaming platforms buying appointment-viewing content in an on-demand world have a short list of sellers, and TKO holds two of the slots.

Rights-cycle cliff risk
The business just repriced. The Paramount UFC deal runs seven years from January 2026 and the ESPN WWE deal began September 2025. Between now and those renewals, domestic media growth is contractual escalators, not negotiation. If the streaming bidding environment cools — and 2026 has been a consolidating year for distributors — the next cycle could be flat.
Key-person and talent concentration
TKO's value is unusually tied to individuals: Ariel Emanuel as dealmaker, Dana White as the UFC's public face, and a small number of drawing stars in WWE. Unlike a franchise league, TKO cannot fall back on civic loyalty to a city — fans follow performers. Athlete-classification litigation and pressure to unionize UFC fighters remain live tail risks to the margin structure.
Controlled-company governance
Endeavor/Silver Lake held roughly 59% after the February 2025 IMG deal, with a Class A/Class B structure and a public float of only ~59.8M of ~189.3M shares. Minority holders bear the economics of related-party transactions — the $3.25B IMG purchase from the controlling shareholder being the obvious example — with limited ability to vote against them.
PPV revenue model abandoned
Moving UFC numbered events inside a Paramount+ subscription removes the variable upside that a McGregor- or Jones-scale event used to deliver. Revenue becomes more predictable and less convex; a genuine cultural moment now enriches Paramount's subscriber file rather than TKO's quarter.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

TKO owns two of the very few properties on earth that reliably deliver live, unscripted, global appointment viewing — and it owns them outright, with no union, no franchises and no revenue share. FY2025 adjusted EBITDA rose 47% to $1.585B and management has guided 2026 to $2.275–2.305B, a further ~45% increase. At $189.64 the stock trades at roughly 17.6x the midpoint of that guidance on an enterprise value of $40.38B, and 36x forward earnings against consensus EPS growth above 100% — yet it is down 3% over 52 weeks. Free cash flow of $1.67B funds a growing dividend and a buyback that has retired stock at a 13.6% annualized rate.

Bear Case

The 2026 growth is a repricing, not a compounding rate. The Paramount and ESPN agreements are signed for years; from January 2027 domestic media revenue grows by contractual escalator only, and the UFC has surrendered pay-per-view upside in exchange. Returns on capital are poor — ROIC 7.84%, ROE 7.06% — because the company was assembled from two large mergers, and one of them was a $3.25B purchase from the controlling shareholder. With Endeavor/Silver Lake holding ~59% through a Class A/Class B structure and a float of only ~32% of shares, minority investors price governance risk into every multiple. At 66.5x trailing Class A earnings there is little room for a soft renewal cycle.

01
Rights-cycle concentration. A very large share of consolidated EBITDA now depends on two counterparties — Paramount and ESPN — on multi-year contracts. A distributor's financial distress, a strategic retreat from live sports, or a weak next negotiation would hit the highest-margin revenue in the company with no operational offset available.
02
Controlled-company and related-party risk. Endeavor/Silver Lake's ~59% stake and the dual-class structure mean minority holders cannot block transactions with the controller. The IMG/On Location/PBR purchase demonstrates the pattern; the persistent valuation discount relative to the earnings step-up is plausibly the market pricing exactly this.
03
Talent, labour and regulatory exposure. UFC fighter-classification and antitrust litigation, any move toward athlete collective bargaining, and reliance on a handful of drawing stars in both promotions all threaten the 50%+ segment margins that justify the multiple. Reputational risk attaches unusually tightly to individuals at this company.

Industry Commentary

Streaming's pivot to live sport
EMERGING TAILWIND
The structural bid for TKO's inventory is that subscription video has run out of cheap ways to reduce churn, and live sport is the remaining one. The evidence is in TKO's own P&L: a single new WWE agreement with ESPN added $80.8M of year-over-year revenue in Q2 2026 alone, and the UFC's Paramount deal drove segment revenue up 29% in a quarter that had fewer numbered events than the prior year. Ariel Emanuel framed it directly on the Q2 release — "premium live content and experiences are heating up in an increasingly AI-driven world." Content that cannot be generated, spoiled or time-shifted is the scarce category.
Distributor consolidation on the other side of the table
ONGOING HEADWIND
TKO's leverage in a negotiation is the number of credible bidders. That number is shrinking: Paramount and Skydance have combined, ESPN has launched a direct-to-consumer service and is buying rather than bidding against itself, and Netflix already holds WWE's international and Raw rights. With both flagship US deals now signed for multi-year terms, TKO has taken the money at what looks like a cyclical high for rights inflation and will renegotiate into a more concentrated buyer set. The seven-year Paramount term is defensive as much as it is bullish.
Bull vs bear: what the 36x actually buys
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The bull owns a business guided to $2.29B of adjusted EBITDA at ~17.6x EV/EBITDA, with 50%+ margins in two segments, $1.67B of free cash flow, a 0.64 beta and no sell ratings among 22 analysts — trading 15% below its February high. The bear owns a controlled company at 66.5x trailing Class A earnings, earning 7.8% on invested capital, whose 2026 growth is the one-time recognition of contracts signed in 2025, with a ~32% float and short interest equal to roughly 13% of it. Both are looking at the same numbers. The disagreement is entirely about what happens after the escalators start — and the market, by holding the stock flat through an 18% revenue quarter, has voted for the bear so far.
