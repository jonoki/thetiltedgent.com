01 Company Overview

TKO Group Holdings is the holding company for the UFC and WWE, and — since the February 28, 2025 acquisition of IMG, On Location and PBR from Endeavor for $3.25 billion in stock — for a third reported segment, IMG. It is not really a sports company in the operating sense; it is a rights owner. TKO owns the leagues it broadcasts, so the intellectual property, the athletes' contracts, the event calendar and the media inventory all sit inside one set of accounts. Full-year 2025 revenue was $4.735 billion, down 3% from $4.884 billion in 2024 — a decline that comes entirely from IMG (2024 included Paris Olympics hospitality sales through On Location) — while full-year adjusted EBITDA rose 47% to $1.585 billion. TKO joined the S&P 500 on March 24, 2025. In the third quarter of 2026, reported November 4, revenue rose 21% to $1.360 billion and adjusted EBITDA rose 54% to $555.7 million, and management raised full-year guidance for the second quarter running.

The central debate is whether the 2026 jump in profit is the start of something or a one-time reset. Two enormous media contracts switched on about four months apart — ESPN for WWE premium live events (September 2025) and Paramount, now part of Skydance, for the UFC (January 2026). Through nine months of 2026, revenue is up 22% ($4.504B against $3.697B) and adjusted EBITDA up 35% ($1.755B against $1.304B). Q3 shows both halves of the argument: with the Skydance deal still new, UFC revenue rose 60%, while WWE, with the ESPN deal reaching its first anniversary and three fewer main-roster premium live events, slipped 1%. Rights fees of that kind jump once and then rise on a fixed contractual schedule. The bull case says TKO has only started charging full price for a library of live content; the bear case says the market has already paid for the step-up, at 51.5x trailing earnings and roughly 17.5x guided 2026 adjusted EBITDA, and that growth from here comes from ticketing, sponsorship and the fees host cities pay — good businesses, but slower ones.

Control and share classes. TKO is an "Up-C" structure: publicly traded Class A shares (NYSE: TKO) sit above an operating partnership, and Class B shares held by Endeavor/WME Group — itself controlled by Silver Lake following its buyout of Endeavor — carry voting power without a matching claim on the Class A share count. TKO's 2026 proxy put Endeavor's combined voting power at 63.9% as of April 16, 2026 (122.1M shares). The practical consequences show up in the numbers: total shares outstanding were 188.6M at October 30, 2026 (72.4M Class A and 116.2M Class B) but the public float is ~59.1M (~31%), and weighted-average basic Class A shares in Q3 2026 were only 73.1 million — which is why consolidated net income of $277.9M translated into just $1.33 of diluted Class A EPS. Minority holders own the economics of a fraction of the enterprise and control a minority of the votes.

The core advantage is ownership. Nearly every other property that sells live sports rights is a league that rents its teams, negotiates with a players' association and shares revenue with franchise owners. TKO owns the UFC and WWE outright — the brands, the title belts, the tape libraries, the athlete contracts and the production trucks. That means when a rights fee steps up, a large share of the increase falls to segment EBITDA rather than being shared out (in Q3 2026 UFC revenue rose $194.0M and adjusted EBITDA $119.7M): WWE's FY2025 adjusted EBITDA margin of 52.4% and the UFC's 56.7% are numbers a league that shares its revenue with teams and players would struggle to reach. It also means TKO can manufacture inventory at will — add a Fight Night, add a Saudi site-fee event, move a premium live event to a new market — because there is no schedule committee to ask. The scarcity works in TKO's favour on the other side of the table too: streaming platforms buying appointment-viewing content in an on-demand world have a short list of sellers, and TKO holds two of the slots.

Risk when the contracts come up for renewal
TKO's big US media contracts have just been reset at higher prices. The Paramount (now Skydance) UFC deal runs seven years from January 2026 and the ESPN WWE deal began September 2025. Between now and those renewals, growth in US media revenue comes from increases already written into the contracts, not from new negotiation. If the streaming bidding environment cools — and 2026 has been a consolidating year for distributors — the next cycle could be flat.
Key-person and talent concentration
TKO's value is unusually tied to individuals: Ariel Emanuel as dealmaker, Dana White as the UFC's public face, and a small number of drawing stars in WWE. Unlike a franchise league, TKO cannot fall back on civic loyalty to a city — fans follow performers. TKO's 10-K flags state laws that could challenge treating athletes as independent contractors, and efforts to organize them; either would threaten profit margins.
Controlled-company governance
Endeavor/Silver Lake held 63.9% of the votes at the April 2026 proxy date, with a Class A/Class B structure and a public float of only ~59.1M of ~188.6M shares. The proxy also shows 83.1M of Endeavor's units pledged as security for a margin loan. Outside shareholders share the cost of deals struck with the controlling owner — the $3.25B IMG purchase from Endeavor being the obvious example — with limited ability to vote against them.
PPV revenue model abandoned
Moving UFC numbered events inside a Paramount+ subscription removes the extra income that a McGregor- or Jones-scale event used to deliver. Revenue becomes more predictable, with less upside from a single huge night; a genuine cultural moment now brings Paramount+ new subscribers rather than TKO extra revenue.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

TKO owns two of the very few properties on earth that reliably deliver live, unscripted, global appointment viewing — and it owns them outright, with no union, no franchises and no revenue share. FY2025 adjusted EBITDA rose 47% to $1.585B and management has guided 2026 to $2.295–2.315B, a further ~45% increase at the midpoint. At $191.00 the stock trades at roughly 17.5x that midpoint on an enterprise value of about $40.34B, and 25.1x Nasdaq's 2027 consensus EPS (40.8x on StockAnalysis's lower figure) — yet it is up only 5.5% over 52 weeks. Free cash flow of $1.72B funds the dividend and, with new debt, a buyback that has helped return more than $1.6B to holders so far in 2026.

Bear Case

The 2026 growth is a one-time price reset, not a rate that repeats. The Skydance (ex-Paramount) and ESPN agreements are signed for years; from January 2027 domestic media revenue grows by contractual increases only, and the UFC has given up pay-per-view upside in exchange. Returns on capital are modest — ROIC 9.16%, ROE 9.39% — because the company was assembled from two large mergers, and one of them was a $3.25B purchase from the controlling shareholder. Endeavor/Silver Lake held 63.9% of the votes at the April 2026 proxy date through a Class A/Class B structure, the float is only ~31% of shares, and buybacks are set to be funded partly with new debt at 6.5%. At 51.5x trailing Class A earnings there is little room for a soft round of contract renewals.

01
Reliance on a few media partners. TKO's 10-K says its distribution partners are expected to remain a significant percentage of revenue, and the contracts with Skydance (formerly Paramount), ESPN and Netflix run for years. A distributor's financial distress, a retreat from live sports or a weak next negotiation would hit the highest-margin revenue in the company, with nothing else in the business able to fill the gap quickly.
02
Controlled-company and related-party risk. Endeavor/Silver Lake's 63.9% voting stake (April 2026 proxy) and the dual-class structure mean minority holders cannot block transactions with the controller. The IMG/On Location/PBR purchase demonstrates the pattern.
03
Talent, labour and regulatory exposure. State laws that could challenge treating athletes as independent contractors and efforts to organize athletes (both noted in TKO's 10-K), antitrust claims over UFC fighter pay, and reliance on a handful of drawing stars in both promotions all threaten the 50%+ segment margins that justify the share price. Reputational risk attaches unusually tightly to individuals at this company.
04
Debt-funded buybacks. Net debt was about 2x trailing adjusted EBITDA at September 30, 2026, before the $1.0B of 6.500% notes priced on November 5. Management said it intends to use cash on hand along with new debt to finish the remaining authorization, which raises fixed interest costs just as the big contract step-ups stop adding to growth.
05
Event execution in the Middle East. The raised 2026 guide assumes all live events proceed as planned, including those in the region; the CFO said the outlook would be affected if they could not be staged.

Industry Commentary

Streaming's pivot to live sport
EMERGING TAILWIND
Buyers keep paying up for TKO's events because subscription video has run out of cheap ways to stop customers cancelling, and live sport is the one left. The evidence is in TKO's own results: a single new WWE agreement with ESPN added $80.8M of year-over-year revenue in Q2 2026 alone, and UFC revenue rose 29% that quarter on fewer numbered events, then 60% in Q3 with one more numbered event and one more Fight Night. Ariel Emanuel framed it directly in the Q2 release — "Premium live content and experiences are heating up in an increasingly AI-driven world…" Content that cannot be generated, spoiled or time-shifted is the scarce category.
Distributor consolidation on the other side of the table
ONGOING HEADWIND
TKO's bargaining power in a negotiation depends on the number of credible bidders. That number is shrinking: Paramount Skydance bought Warner Bros. Discovery on October 6, 2026 and now trades as Skydance, ESPN has launched a direct-to-consumer service and is buying rather than bidding against itself, and Netflix already holds WWE's international and Raw rights. Shapiro argues the new Skydance helps TKO, with TNT Sports and HBO adding reach. With both flagship US deals now signed for multi-year terms, TKO has taken the money at what looks like a high point for rights prices and will renegotiate into a more concentrated group of buyers. The seven-year Paramount term is defensive as much as it is bullish.
Bull vs bear: what today's price actually buys
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The bull owns a business guided to about $2.305B of adjusted EBITDA at ~17.5x enterprise value to EBITDA, with 50%+ margins in two segments, $1.72B of free cash flow, a 0.65 beta and no sell ratings among 22 analysts — trading 15% below its February high month-end close. The bear owns a controlled company at 51.5x trailing Class A earnings, earning 9.2% on invested capital, whose 2026 growth is the one-time recognition of contracts signed in 2025, with a ~31% float, short interest equal to about 13% of it and rising debt. Both are looking at the same numbers. The disagreement is entirely about what happens after the contracted increases start — and the market, by leaving the stock up only 5.5% in a year with an 18% and a 21% revenue quarter, has so far leaned toward the bear.
