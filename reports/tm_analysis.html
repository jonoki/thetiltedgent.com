01 Company Overview

Toyota is the world's second-most-valuable automaker — $257.4B against Tesla's $1.49T at the November 9 closes, per companiesmarketcap's automaker ranking — and the most valuable company in Japan (ahead of Mitsubishi UFJ, per companiesmarketcap) — its home listing is the Tokyo Stock Exchange Prime Market (code 7203). In the fiscal year to March 2026 (FY2026) it booked ¥50.68 trillion of revenue, up 5.5%, but operating income fell to ¥3,766.2 billion from ¥4,795.5 billion — a 7.4% operating margin, down from 10.0% the year before and 11.9% in FY2024. The current year has turned: on November 5 Toyota raised its FY2027 operating-income forecast by ¥500 billion to ¥3.9 trillion, which would be its first increase in three years, after July–September operating income rose 34.3% to ¥1,127.6 billion. Toyota sells about 10.5 million Toyota- and Lexus-brand vehicles a year; the US-listed security is an ADS representing 10 ordinary shares.

The story is a bet that the rest of the industry took the other side of. While competitors spent the early 2020s racing to all-battery fleets, Toyota kept building hybrids. That bet is now the volume engine: FY2027 guidance calls for 5.01 million hybrids against 463,000 battery EVs — and the BEV figure has been cut twice since May (598,000 → 533,000 in August → 463,000 in November), after a provision in the first quarter for reviewing BEV development projects. But the margin line tells a second story the hybrid bet cannot fix. Two external forces decide this company's earnings: the yen, which Toyota's own Q1 bridge shows adding roughly ¥13bn of quarterly operating profit per ¥1 of weakness against the dollar (about ¥50bn+ a year — derived below), and US tariffs, which the company says cost ¥1,380.0 billion of operating income in FY2026 — 37% of what it actually earned — and which it now expects to cost about ¥1.1 trillion in FY2027. FX exposure in one line: Toyota reports in yen, earns it everywhere — FY2026 regional revenue was ¥22.07T booked in Japan (much of it exports) and ¥21.08T in North America before eliminations — so a weaker yen inflates reported profit while a stronger yen drains it, independent of how many cars it sells.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hybrid scale (5M+/yr) Toyota Production System Quality, durability, reliability brand Captive finance arm Global dealer network ¥11T net liquid assets Supplier group (DENSO, AISIN)

The moat is manufacturing optionality. Toyota can build a petrol car, a hybrid, a plug-in, a battery EV or a fuel-cell car off the same organisation, and it has let customers — not regulators' timetables — set the mix. With US demand for hybrids strong enough that management describes low inventory days and lower incentives than competitors, the constraint on the hybrid business is Toyota's own battery capacity, not demand. Behind it sits a fortress balance sheet: even after spending more than ¥3 trillion on the Toyota Industries tender, management said in August that it retains roughly ¥11 trillion of net liquid assets (cash and investments, net of debt, outside the finance business). The vulnerability is that none of this protects the margin from forces it doesn't control — tariffs, the yen and materials prices took operating margin from 11.9% in FY2024 to 7.4% in FY2026 while volumes held, and even the raised FY2027 guide (¥3.9T on ¥53.5T of revenue) is a 7.3% margin.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The hybrid bet has a clock on it. Hybrids win while charging infrastructure is thin, battery costs are high and regulators tolerate a petrol engine. Toyota has cut its FY2027 BEV sales plan twice (598k in May → 533k in August → 463k in November) and booked a provision in Q1 for reviewing BEV projects. In China, the market was running at about 80% of last year's level in the summer, conventional vehicles at ~66%, and Toyota-brand sales fell ~28% in Q1; in November Miyazaki said of China, "we are still struggling, and we are in a tough situation." In Southeast Asia management reports dealers switching to Chinese brands.
02
Materials inflation it can only half pass through. In August management put the FY2027 materials-price and supplier-support impact at about ¥1.3 trillion (¥1.2T from materials alone — memory chips and aluminium are named) and aimed to recover "roughly half" via price increases; the November forecast factors in about ¥1 trillion of higher materials prices. What price increases don't recover is margin.
03
Exports from Japan are a structural FX and tariff position. Japan-booked revenue (¥22.07T in FY2026) includes vehicles built at home and sold abroad. A stronger yen shrinks their yen value; a US tariff raises their landed cost. Mexico-built trucks face both logistics and tariff costs — the reason Tacoma production (150,000/yr) moves to a second Texas plant, but not until 2030.
04
Event risk in the supply chain. The Middle East disruption cut regional exports (normally ~500,000–600,000 vehicles a year) roughly in half before rerouting restored capacity to ~75% from September. In November Azuma described production disruption from earthquakes and floods during the half, and the revised forecast factors in flooding in Thailand. Toyota's lean, just-in-time model is efficient in calm years and fragile in shocks.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported numbers. First, the Toyota Industries unwind: Q1 FY2027 other income of ¥900.3B includes ~¥600B from selling Toyota Industries shares and ~¥300B of FX gains on dollar-denominated assets, which is why Q1 net income rose 75.6% to ¥1,477.0B while operating income fell 8.8%. The pattern reversed in Q2: operating income rose 34.3% while net income fell 18.5% to ¥759.4B. Earnings-based valuation on a trailing basis is still flattered by the Q1 gains. Second, Hino left the consolidated group in Q1 (its deconsolidation and the gain on selling ARCHION shares roughly offset, per management), so FY2027 volume and revenue comparisons exclude ~104,000 Hino vehicles. Third, a ¥250B unrealised-profit adjustment in Q1 — profit on group inventory in transit, inflated by the weak yen — was deducted from operating income as a consolidation adjustment; it is timing, not lost profit. The undisclosed BEV-project-review provision and IEEPA tariff refunds sit inside a −¥60B "other expenses" line.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 9, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $177.85 to the November 9, 2026 settled close of $218.51, the ADS gained 22.9% on price in five years — before dividends, and in dollars. That dollar framing matters: over the same window the yen weakened sharply (Toyota's own average-rate table runs from ¥145/$ in Q1 FY2026 to ¥160/$ in Q1 FY2027), which lifted Toyota's yen profits while shrinking what those yen are worth to a US holder. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 11.5x Toyota's own FY2027 earnings guide and about 1.1x book for the world's top-selling automaker (No. 1 for a sixth straight year in 2025, Reuters), which has raised its operating-income forecast twice since May (¥3.0T → ¥3.4T → ¥3.9T, which would be its first increase in three years) and lifted its buyback to ¥1.3T. Strip out the ¥1.38T tariff hit and FY2026 operating income would have exceeded FY2025, and the tariff bill is now guided down to about ¥1.1T. The hybrid bet is being validated in real time — electrified share at 55.6% of first-half sales, hybrids up 8.3%, North America back in profit — while BEV-first rivals write down programs. The Toyota Industries deal shrank the share count ~9% and erased a large cross-holding, and the 20% ROE target puts capital efficiency on the agenda of a company long criticised for hoarding it.

Bear Case

Even after the raise, the guide is a 7.3% operating margin against 11.9% in FY2024, and the ADS has already climbed 29.7% from its June 2026 month-end close. The forces holding the margin down — tariffs (~¥1.1T), materials (~¥1T) and the yen — are outside management's control, and the November raise leans on a weak yen (the half's exchange-rate gain was ¥685bn) that can reverse. The trailing P/E of 10.0 is inflated by one-off gains on Toyota Industries shares and FX. And the hybrid moat may be a bridge rather than a destination: China's market is shrinking under Toyota, Chinese brands are expanding across Asia, Europe and Latin America, Toyota cut its full-year sales plan to 10.3 million, and it has cut its BEV plan twice since May.

01
Yen strength. By TTG's derivation from Toyota's own bridge, each ¥1 move against the dollar is worth ~¥13bn of operating profit a quarter (~¥53bn a year) through US-dollar exports alone. 02
US trade policy. ¥1.38T of FY2026 operating income lost to tariffs; about ¥1.1T expected in FY2027, some of it offset by expected US refunds. The USMCA review and Mexico-built trucks were raised on the Q1 call; the Texas Tacoma plant that mitigates it arrives in 2030.
03
The EV transition arrives faster in the markets that matter. Toyota sold ~28% fewer vehicles in China in Q1, cut its full-year sales plan in November mainly because of China, and is fighting Chinese BEVs across Asia. If hybrid demand in the US fades before next-generation BEVs are competitive, the capacity being built for hybrids becomes the stranded asset.
Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the North American production map
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For Toyota, US tariffs are a line item with a number on it: ¥1,380.0B of FY2026 operating income by the company's own count, and about ¥1.1T expected for FY2027 after supplier cost work and expected US refunds. North America went from profit to a ¥192.5B operating loss in FY2026 and back to ¥166.2B of operating profit in the first half of FY2027 (a ¥67.9B loss a year earlier), which Azuma put down mainly to price revisions and lower US tariffs. The structural answer is "local production, local procurement, local sales" — a second Texas plant for Tacoma, moving the truck away from Mexico, where management says logistics and tariffs compound. That plant builds from 2030. Until then, tariff policy is a direct input to Toyota's earnings, sized in trillions of yen.

Hybrid versus full EV
ONGOING DEBATE

Toyota has bet on hybrids over full EVs, and the company's own plan shows it: FY2027 guidance is 5,008k hybrids, 269k plug-ins and 463k BEVs out of 10.3M Toyota/Lexus sales (November 5), with the BEV number cut from 598k in May and 533k in August and a Q1 provision for reviewing BEV projects. The nuance: BEV sales are growing fast from a small base (231k in the first half, +127.4%), and Toyota is building a wholly owned Lexus BEV plant in Shanghai. The market that punishes the hybrid bet is China, where conventional-vehicle demand is ~66% of last year's level; the market that rewards it is the US, where — as one analyst put it on the August call — higher crude prices have accelerated hybrid demand, and Toyota reports low inventory days and lower incentives than rivals. Which of those two describes 2030 is the whole question.

Unwinding the cross-shareholding web
EMERGING TAILWIND

Japan's governance push against cross-shareholdings is visible in Toyota's list of major shareholders. The Toyota Industries take-private — completed, with Toyota Industries delisting notice issued May 29, 2026 — let Toyota buy back 1.19B of its own shares (9.15% of the voting base at March 31) and sell its stake in Toyota Industries for a ~¥600bn Q1 gain. Management said in August it has "substantially reduced" policy shareholdings (shares companies hold in each other to cement business ties) and will recycle sale proceeds into buybacks, sizing the ¥1T program on exactly that; in November it added ¥300bn, and a tender offer for its own shares, in response to shareholders asking to sell. Group companies DENSO, Toyota Fudosan and AISIN still hold roughly 7% of Toyota between them (TTG estimate on the post-tender share base, assuming their holdings unchanged since March 31). The bull framing: fewer shares, a higher ROE and a Price-to-Book back above 1.0 (1.09 at November 9). The bear framing: buybacks near book value are a sensible use of surplus capital, but they don't fix a margin being set in Washington and on the currency market.
