01 Company Overview

Toyota is the world's second-most-valuable automaker — $227.66B against Tesla's $1.48T, per companiesmarketcap's automaker ranking — and the #3 company in Japan by market capitalisation (behind Mitsubishi UFJ and SoftBank Group, per companiesmarketcap) — its home listing is the Tokyo Stock Exchange Prime Market (code 7203). In the fiscal year to March 2026 (FY2026) it booked ¥50.68 trillion of revenue, up 5.5%, but operating income fell to ¥3,766.2 billion from ¥4,795.5 billion — a 7.4% operating margin, down from 10.0% the year before and 11.9% in FY2024. Toyota sells about 10.5 million Toyota- and Lexus-brand vehicles a year; the US-listed security is an ADS representing 10 ordinary shares.

The story is a bet that the rest of the industry took the other side of. While competitors spent the early 2020s racing to all-battery fleets, Toyota kept building hybrids. That bet is now the volume engine: FY2027 guidance calls for 5.09 million hybrids against 533,000 battery EVs — and the BEV figure was just cut 10.9%, alongside a provision for "the review of BEV development projects." But the margin line tells a second story the hybrid bet cannot fix. Two external forces decide this company's earnings: the yen, which Toyota's own Q1 bridge shows adding roughly ¥13bn of quarterly operating profit per ¥1 of weakness against the dollar (about ¥50bn+ a year — derived below), and US tariffs, which the company says cost ¥1,380.0 billion of operating income in FY2026 — 37% of what it actually earned. FX exposure in one line: Toyota reports in yen, earns it everywhere — FY2026 regional revenue was ¥22.07T booked in Japan (much of it exports) and ¥21.08T in North America before eliminations — so a weaker yen inflates reported profit while a stronger yen drains it, independent of how many cars it sells.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Hybrid scale (5M+/yr) Toyota Production System Quality, durability, reliability brand Captive finance arm Global dealer network ¥11T net liquid assets Supplier keiretsu (DENSO, AISIN)

The moat is manufacturing optionality. Toyota can build a petrol car, a hybrid, a plug-in, a battery EV or a fuel-cell car off the same organisation, and it has let customers — not regulators' timetables — set the mix. With US demand for hybrids strong enough that management describes low inventory days and lower incentives than competitors, the constraint on the hybrid business is Toyota's own battery capacity, not demand. Behind it sits a fortress balance sheet: even after spending more than ¥3 trillion on the Toyota Industries tender, management says it retains roughly ¥11 trillion of net liquid assets. The vulnerability is that none of this protects the margin from forces it doesn't control — tariffs, the yen and materials prices took operating margin from 11.9% to a guided 6.3% in three years while volumes held.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The hybrid bet has a clock on it. Hybrids win while charging infrastructure is thin, battery costs are high and regulators tolerate a petrol engine. Toyota just cut its FY2027 BEV sales plan 10.9% (598k → 533k) and booked a provision for reviewing BEV projects. In China, the market is running at about 80% of last year's level, conventional vehicles at ~66%, and Toyota-brand sales fell ~28% in Q1; management concedes it needs to make BEVs competitive there, not just hybrids. In Southeast Asia it reports dealers switching to Chinese brands.
02
Materials inflation it can only half pass through. Management puts the FY2027 materials-price and supplier-support impact at about ¥1.3 trillion (¥1.2T from materials alone — memory chips and aluminium are named) and aims to recover "roughly half" via price increases. The other half is margin.
03
Exports from Japan are a structural FX and tariff position. Japan-booked revenue (¥22.07T in FY2026) includes vehicles built at home and sold abroad. A stronger yen shrinks their yen value; a US tariff raises their landed cost. Mexico-built trucks face both logistics and tariff costs — the reason Tacoma production (150,000/yr) moves to a second Texas plant, but not until 2030.
04
Event risk in the supply chain. The Middle East disruption cut regional exports (normally ~500,000–600,000 vehicles a year) roughly in half before rerouting restored capacity to ~75% from September. The 2026 Kumamoto earthquake's impact is not in the forecast. Toyota's lean, just-in-time model is efficient in calm years and fragile in shocks.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported numbers. First, the Toyota Industries unwind: Q1 FY2027 other income of ¥900.3B includes ~¥600B from selling Toyota Industries shares and ~¥300B of FX gains on dollar-denominated assets, which is why Q1 net income rose 75.6% to ¥1,477.0B while operating income fell 8.8%. Earnings-based valuation on a trailing basis is therefore flattered. Second, Hino left the consolidated group in Q1 (its deconsolidation and the gain on selling ARCHION shares roughly offset, per management), so FY2027 volume and revenue comparisons exclude ~104,000 Hino vehicles. Third, a ¥250B unrealised-profit adjustment in Q1 — profit on group inventory in transit, inflated by the weak yen — was deducted from operating income as a consolidation adjustment; it is timing, not lost profit. The undisclosed BEV-project-review provision and IEEPA tariff refunds sit inside a −¥60B "other expenses" line.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You pay 11x Toyota's own FY2027 earnings guide and 0.97x book for the world's largest automaker, which just raised its operating-income forecast by ¥400bn and authorised a ¥1T buyback. Strip out the ¥1.38T tariff hit and FY2026 operating income would have exceeded FY2025; management says underlying earning power is about ¥5T against a ¥3.4T guide. The hybrid bet is being validated in real time — electrified share at 55.3% of Q1 sales, 5M+ hybrids in 2026, demand ahead of capacity, low US incentives — while BEV-first rivals write down programs. The Toyota Industries deal shrank the share count ~9% and erased a large cross-holding, and the 20% ROE target puts capital efficiency on the agenda of a company long criticised for hoarding it.

Bear Case

Operating margin has fallen from 11.9% to a guided 6.3% in three years and the company itself expects a third consecutive decline. The forces doing it — tariffs (~¥1.31T), materials (~¥1.2T, half passed through), and a yen already ¥2.7 stronger than the ¥160 guidance premise — are outside management's control, and the tailwind behind the FY2024 record, a weak yen, is the one that can reverse. The trailing P/E of 8.6 is inflated by one-off gains on Toyota Industries shares and FX. And the hybrid moat may be a bridge rather than a destination: China's market is shrinking under Toyota, Chinese brands are poaching Southeast Asian dealers, and Toyota just cut its BEV plan again.

Top 3 Risks

01
Yen strength. By TTG's derivation from Toyota's own bridge, each ¥1 move against the dollar is worth ~¥13bn of operating profit a quarter (~¥53bn a year) through US-dollar exports alone. The guide assumes ¥160; the ECB rate on September 21 was ¥157.27.
02
US trade policy. ¥1.38T of FY2026 operating income lost to tariffs; ~¥1.31T expected in FY2027. The USMCA review and Mexico-built trucks were raised on the Q1 call; the Texas Tacoma plant that mitigates it arrives in 2030.
03
The EV transition arrives faster in the markets that matter. Toyota sold ~28% fewer vehicles in China in Q1 and is fighting Chinese BEVs across Asia. If hybrid demand in the US fades before next-generation BEVs are competitive, the capacity being built for hybrids becomes the stranded asset.
Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the North American production map
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

For Toyota, US tariffs are a line item with a number on it: ¥1,380.0B of FY2026 operating income by the company's own count, and ~¥1.31T expected for FY2027 after ¥70bn of relief including IEEPA-tariff refunds. North America went from profit to a ¥192.5B operating loss in FY2026 and back to ¥185.4B of profit in Q1 FY2027 on price increases, FX and lower tariffs. The structural answer is "local production, local procurement, local sales" — a second Texas plant for Tacoma, moving the truck away from Mexico, where management says logistics and tariffs compound. That plant builds from 2030. Until then, tariff policy is a direct input to Toyota's earnings, sized in trillions of yen.

Hybrid versus full EV
ONGOING DEBATE

The premise of this report — that Toyota has bet on hybrids over full EVs — checks out against the company's own plan: FY2027 guidance is 5,088k hybrids, 284k plug-ins and 533k BEVs out of 10.5M Toyota/Lexus sales, with the BEV number cut from 598k in May and a Q1 provision for reviewing BEV projects. The nuance: BEV sales are growing fast from a small base (Q1 114k, more than double the prior year), and Toyota is building a wholly owned Lexus BEV plant in Shanghai. The market that punishes the hybrid bet is China, where conventional-vehicle demand is ~66% of last year's level; the market that rewards it is the US, where — as one analyst put it on the Q1 call — higher crude prices have accelerated hybrid demand, and Toyota reports low inventory days and lower incentives than rivals. Which of those two describes 2030 is the whole question.

Unwinding the cross-shareholding web
EMERGING TAILWIND

Japan's governance push against cross-shareholdings is visible in Toyota's register. The Toyota Industries take-private — completed, with Toyota Industries delisting notice issued May 29, 2026 — let Toyota buy back 1.19B of its own shares (9.15% of the voting base at March 31) and sell its stake in Toyota Industries for a ~¥600bn Q1 gain. Management says it has "substantially reduced" policy shareholdings and will recycle sale proceeds into buybacks, sizing the new ¥1T program on exactly that. Group companies DENSO, Toyota Fudosan and AISIN still hold roughly 7% of Toyota between them (TTG estimate on the post-tender share base, assuming their holdings unchanged since March 31). The bull framing: fewer shares, a higher ROE and a Price-to-Book heading back above 1.0. The bear framing: buybacks at 0.97x book are a sensible use of surplus capital, but they don't fix a margin being set in Washington and on the currency market.
