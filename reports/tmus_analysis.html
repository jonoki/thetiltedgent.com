01 Company Overview

T-Mobile US is the second-largest U.S. wireless carrier by market capitalization and, by its own service revenue growth, the fastest-growing of the big three. It sells postpaid (billed monthly after use, on credit) and prepaid (paid in advance, no credit check) wireless plans, sells and finances handsets, and increasingly sells home broadband — both FWA (fixed wireless access: home internet delivered over the cellular network rather than a wire) and, through joint ventures, fiber. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $88.31B, up 8.49% year over year, and the trailing twelve months through June 30, 2026 reached $92.19B, up 9.68%. At $195.89B of market value it is the 60th-largest U.S. company and 98th-largest globally — and the largest company in the Telecom Services industry, ahead of Verizon ($180.01B) and AT&T ($149.67B).

The point worth making up front: a wireless carrier is not an EPS story. It is a free cash flow and capital-return story, and T-Mobile is the clearest example in the sector. Trailing FCF is $18.40B against $10.56B of GAAP net income, because the company's largest expense — the 5G network — was capitalized years ago and is now depreciating rather than consuming new cash. Management guides 2026 adjusted FCF to $18.4B–$18.8B on roughly $10B of cash capex. That is a ~25% FCF margin on service revenue, a rate neither Verizon nor AT&T currently matches.

The engine. Q2 2026 postpaid service revenue was $15.9B, up 13% YoY, against total service revenue of $18.98B (+8.9%). T-Mobile ended the quarter with 34.7 million postpaid accounts, added 277,000 postpaid account net additions (down 13% from 318,000 a year earlier), and reported postpaid account churn of 0.99% with postpaid phone churn of 0.85%. ARPA (average revenue per account — total postpaid service revenue divided by accounts, not by lines) was $152.91, up 2%, or +3.7% excluding merger effects. Full-year ARPA guidance is +2.5% to +3.0%.

Two caveats matter more than the headline. First, the 13% postpaid revenue growth is not organic: the UScellular acquisition closed in Q3 2025 and added 1.45 million accounts, and Metronet added 633,000. Strip acquisitions out and the underlying rate is materially lower — management's own ex-merger ARPA figure (3.7% vs 2.0% reported) shows the M&A drag on the per-account metric even as it inflates the aggregate. Second, T-Mobile no longer publishes postpaid phone net additions as a headline metric; it guides and reports on accounts. Because an account only deactivates when its last line leaves, account churn of 0.99% can sit on top of meaningfully worse line-level churn without ever surfacing. Q3 2026 account adds are guided to roughly 250,000 on "rate plan modernization" — management's phrase for repricing existing customers, which reliably lifts ARPA and reliably lifts churn.

Inside this line sits the genuine incremental growth story: FWA home internet. Total broadband net additions in Q2 2026 were in the upper 400,000s, and T-Mobile has raised its 2030 FWA target to 15 million customers (from 12 million) using what it calls a "fallow capacity" model — selling excess 2.5GHz capacity that would otherwise sit idle, at close to zero incremental network cost. For context, cable lost customers in the same quarter: Comcast −167,000 domestic residential broadband, Charter −172,000.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
2.5GHz MID-BAND DEPTH LOWEST COST PER BIT ~25% FCF MARGIN SPECTRUM PORTFOLIO BRAND / NPS 46 FALLOW-CAPACITY FWA DEUTSCHE TELEKOM BACKING

The moat has one honest source and several derivative ones. The honest source is spectrum: the 2020 Sprint merger handed T-Mobile a deep, contiguous 2.5GHz mid-band position at a moment when neither rival had one, and mid-band is where 5G capacity and coverage actually reconcile. That advantage produced a structurally lower cost per bit, which funded a decade of share-taking on price without margin destruction, which in turn produced the sector's best FCF conversion — trailing FCF of $18.40B on $92.19B of revenue, versus GAAP net income of only $10.56B. Everything else — the brand, the record NPS of 46, the "fallow capacity" FWA business that sells idle 2.5GHz capacity at near-zero marginal cost — is downstream of that one asset.

The problem is that this is 2026, not 2021. Verizon and AT&T have now largely finished their own C-Band and mid-band deployments. The network gap that justified T-Mobile's premium has narrowed, and the market has noticed: TMUS is down 27.5% over the past 52 weeks and trades at $182.62 against a 52-week high of $258.66. The stock is no longer paying for a structural network advantage; it is paying roughly 14.6× forward earnings and about 10.6× trailing FCF for a mature, cash-generative utility with a maturing growth algorithm. That is the whole debate. The 2027–2028 spectrum auctions (C-Band 2.0 and 2.7GHz) will decide whether the spectrum edge is renewed or competed away — and management has explicitly said it is reserving capital for them, which is a tacit admission that the current position is not permanent.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Decelerating net adds and a disclosure blackout. Postpaid account net additions fell 13% YoY to 277,000, and Q3 2026 is guided to roughly 250,000. More troubling for analysis: T-Mobile stopped reporting postpaid phone net additions entirely at the end of 2025, replacing them with account counts. Recon Analytics notes an account only deactivates when its last line leaves, so account churn of 0.99% can mask materially worse line-level attrition. Verizon and AT&T made parallel changes. Investors are now being asked to trust a growth story with the key growth metric removed.
▸
Cable MVNOs are the underpriced threat. Comcast added 448,000 wireless lines in Q2 2026 (a record quarter, 10.2M total) and Charter added 406,000 — roughly 854,000 combined against T-Mobile's 277,000 account adds. Cable offloads about 90% of traffic to Wi-Fi and prices wireless as a broadband retention tool rather than a profit centre, economics no standalone carrier can match. T-Mobile hosts part of this traffic as a wholesaler, earning thin margins on lines that compete directly with its own retail postpaid base.
▸
Growth is increasingly bought, not earned. The 13% postpaid revenue growth rests substantially on UScellular (1.45M accounts, closed Q3 2025) and Metronet (633K accounts). Q2 2026 postpaid adds were explicitly depressed by higher UScellular deactivations. Reported ARPA growth of 2.0% versus 3.7% ex-merger shows acquisitions diluting per-account economics. Integration costs are real — $476M net of tax in Q1 2026 alone — and the FY2025 outcome was revenue +8.5% with net income down 3.1%.
▸
Leverage, governance and float. Total debt is $120.43B against $2.83B of cash; debt-to-equity is 2.14 and the current ratio is 0.92, with a 2.5× target leverage ratio. Separately, Deutsche Telekom's majority control (Yahoo shows 58.46% insider-held) means minority holders do not control the board, DT's own balance-sheet needs can influence capital allocation, and the true public float is roughly 40% of shares — thinner liquidity and a permanent overhang if DT ever monetizes.

Notable One-Off Items

Several non-recurring items materially distort the reported numbers and should be adjusted for.

UScellular acquisition and integration costs. The deal closed in Q3 2025 and added 1.45 million accounts and roughly 24% household share in rural markets. Q1 2026 merger costs were $476M net of tax, including accelerated depreciation and restructuring, with integration targeted for completion by end-2026. This is the single largest reason FY2025 revenue rose 8.49% while net income fell 3.06% to $10.99B. It also mechanically inflates postpaid revenue growth (+13% reported) and depresses per-account metrics (ARPA +2.0% reported vs +3.7% ex-merger).

Metronet account repurchase. Q2 2026 postpaid accounts were reduced by 16,000 from a Metronet account repurchase — a small item, but it means the reported 277,000 net add figure is not clean either.

Prior-year Lumos comparison. The Q2 2025 base included 85,000 accounts acquired via Lumos, which flatters the year-ago comparison and makes the −13% YoY decline in account adds look slightly worse than the underlying trend. Both directions of distortion are present; neither is disclosed in a way that permits a clean organic figure.

Reporting-definition change. Not an accounting one-off, but analytically equivalent: the retirement of postpaid phone net additions as a reported metric at year-end 2025 breaks the historical time series. Comparisons to pre-2026 net-add data are no longer like-for-like.

RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It says nothing about value — only about the pace of the move.

The five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 monthly close of $127.76 to $182.62 on August 24, 2026, TMUS returned +42.9% on price over five years — roughly 7.4% annualized, plus a dividend that now runs $4.08 per share. That headline understates how violent the path was. The stock bottomed at a monthly close of $108.17 in January 2022, then compounded almost without interruption for three years to a monthly-close peak of $269.69 in February 2025 — a +149% run as the Sprint synergies landed, the 2.5GHz network advantage translated into share gains, and free cash flow inflected. Everything since has been give-back: the stock is down 27.5% over the last 52 weeks and sits 29.4% below its 52-week high of $258.66, below both its 50-day ($181.67) and 200-day ($195.84) moving averages.

The shape of that reversal matters for the thesis. The de-rating did not begin with bad earnings — it began in October 2025 (−12.4% in a month, $239.38 → $210.05), roughly when Verizon and AT&T's mid-band builds reached parity and the "best network" premium started to look contestable. The July 2026 earnings drop (−10.7% in a session, on a quarter that beat on EPS and raised FCF guidance but missed on revenue and net adds) is the confirming datapoint: the market has stopped paying for cash flow and started demanding subscriber growth. That is the central tension in owning this stock today.

Relative performance. Over the trailing year TMUS's −27.5% has badly lagged the NASDAQ-100, which the stock's low 0.33 beta did nothing to protect against — a reminder that beta measures co-movement, not downside. Within its own sector the picture is more flattering: TMUS at $195.89B remains the largest company in Telecom Services, ahead of Verizon ($180.01B) and AT&T ($149.67B), and it is the only one of the three growing revenue near double digits. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 14.6× forward earnings and roughly 10.6× trailing free cash flow for the fastest-growing large carrier in America, with $18.4–18.8B of guided 2026 FCF, a ~25% FCF margin on service revenue, and an up-to-$14.6B buyback authorization retiring stock at a 27% discount to last year's price. The 2027 targets set in February — $40–41B EBITDA and $19.5–20.5B FCF — imply roughly 10% FCF compounding, and two genuine incremental businesses are still early: FWA on a 15-million-customer 2030 target using fallow 2.5GHz capacity at near-zero marginal cost, and fiber JVs already reaching ~20% penetration within a year of each market build. A sub-1.0 PEG and 23 of 27 analysts at Buy against a $243 average target say the sell side thinks the sell-off overshot.

Bear Case

The growth algorithm is visibly maturing and the company has removed the metric that would prove it. Postpaid account adds fell 13% to 277,000 with Q3 guided to ~250,000; postpaid phone net additions are no longer reported at all; and the 13% postpaid revenue growth is acquisition-fed (UScellular's 1.45M accounts, Metronet's 633K) with ARPA growing just 2.0% as reported. Meanwhile Comcast and Charter added roughly 854,000 wireless lines in the same quarter — three times T-Mobile's account adds — using Wi-Fi-offload economics no carrier can match, and the 2.5GHz advantage that justified the premium has been substantially matched by C-Band. ROIC of 8.93% is a utility's return, debt-to-equity is 2.14, and the 2027–2028 spectrum auctions are a large unquantified claim on the FCF that funds the buyback. Wells Fargo's $169 target is below the current price for a reason.

Top 3 Risks

01
Subscriber deceleration hidden by a metric change. Account adds are decelerating (−13% YoY, Q3 guided lower), and the retirement of postpaid phone net additions means line-level churn is now unobservable — account churn of 0.99% only registers when an account's last line leaves. If the underlying line trend is worse than the account trend suggests, the market will find out through revenue rather than through disclosure, which is the slower and more painful path.
02
Convergence competition from cable and fiber. Cable MVNOs added ~854,000 lines in Q2 2026 against T-Mobile's 277,000 account adds, and AT&T is executing a fiber-first convergence strategy with owned infrastructure. T-Mobile's answer is 50%-owned fiber JVs and FWA — capital-efficient but structurally thinner. If bundled broadband-plus-wireless becomes the default purchase, T-Mobile competes with half an economic interest in the fixed half of the bundle.
03
Spectrum reinvestment versus capital return. The entire equity story rests on FCF funding buybacks and dividends. C-Band 2.0 and 2.7GHz auctions in 2027–2028 are an unquantified call on that cash, arriving on top of $120.43B of debt, a 2.14 debt-to-equity ratio and a 0.92 current ratio. Management has said it is reserving capital — which protects the network and squeezes the returns that currently justify the multiple.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters for T-Mobile Right Now
The Metric Blackout: The Industry Stopped Reporting Phone Net Adds
ONGOING HEADWIND
At year-end 2025 the US wireless industry abandoned standardized customer reporting — simultaneously, and in mutually incompatible directions. T-Mobile now reports accounts only, eliminating the line count; because an account deactivates only when its last line leaves, its 0.99% account churn is a floor that can conceal much worse line-level attrition. Verizon removed the Consumer/Business phone split from its tables. AT&T moved postpaid phone metrics inside a convergence-focused segment. The residual comparable data for Q2 2026: Verizon 184,000 postpaid phone adds at 0.92% churn, AT&T 432,000 at 0.86%, T-Mobile 277,000 accounts at 0.99% account churn and 0.85% phone churn. Recon Analytics' read is that each carrier executed its own strategy well, but that the loss of comparable metrics "prevents market transparency on whether these strategies represent genuine competitive advantage or accounting concealment." For an investor, the practical effect is that the single most important operating variable in a wireless carrier is now partly unobservable — and multiples typically compress when disclosure quality falls.
Convergence: Cable and Fiber Are Closing the Bundle Gap
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The competitive centre of gravity in US telecom has moved from network quality to bundle economics. In Q2 2026 Comcast added 448,000 wireless lines — its best quarter on record, up from 378,000 a year earlier — crossing 10.2 million lines, while Charter added 406,000. Combined that is roughly 854,000 lines against T-Mobile's 277,000 account adds. Cable is losing broadband (Comcast −167,000, Charter −172,000) and using cheap bundled wireless to slow the bleed, funded by offloading roughly 90% of mobile traffic to its own Wi-Fi. The revenue per line is lower but the retention is better, and neither Comcast nor Charter needs wireless to be profitable standalone. T-Mobile sits on both sides of this: it hosts Comcast and Charter business-mobile traffic under a July 2025 MVNO agreement — a $2.81B wholesale line that is declining 1.1% — while those same lines compete with its retail postpaid base. Meanwhile AT&T's CEO has stated the company can serve 98%+ of traffic through converged fiber and wireless. T-Mobile's convergence answer is 50%-owned fiber JVs plus FWA; capital-light, but a half-share of the fixed economics.
Post-5G Capex, FWA Economics, and the 2027–28 Spectrum Bill — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND (WITH A CATCH)
The tailwind. T-Mobile's 5G build is behind it. Cash capex is held at roughly $10B annually against $28.4–28.8B of guided operating cash flow, producing $18.4–18.8B of adjusted FCF in 2026 and a targeted $19.5–20.5B in 2027 — roughly 10% FCF compounding from 2023, with FCF margin "well over 20%" of service revenue. The FWA business monetizes fallow 2.5GHz capacity at close to zero incremental network cost, which is why the 2030 target was raised from 12 million to 15 million customers; broadband net adds ran in the upper 400,000s in Q2 2026 while cable lost subscribers. Add $3B of incremental EBITDA management attributes to digitalization and AI by end-2027, and the cash algorithm looks durable.

The catch. On the Q2 2026 call management confirmed it is reserving capital for the C-Band 2.0 and 2.7GHz auctions in 2027 and 2028. Spectrum is the asset that created this company's advantage, and it is bought at auction against two rivals with the same need and, in AT&T's case, better convergence economics to defend. The bull case says $19–20B of annual FCF absorbs an auction bill and still funds a $14.6B buyback. The bear case says an $80B capital envelope through 2027, $120.43B of existing debt, a 2.5× leverage target and an unbounded auction obligation cannot all coexist — and that the buyback is the line item that gives. Which of those is right will determine whether 14.6× forward earnings turns out to have been the bargain the sell side thinks it is, or a value trap in a maturing industry.
