01 Company Overview

T-Mobile US is the second-largest U.S. wireless carrier by market capitalization and, by its own service revenue growth, the fastest-growing of the big three. It sells postpaid (billed monthly after use, on credit) and prepaid (paid in advance, no credit check) wireless plans, sells and finances handsets, and increasingly sells home broadband — both FWA (fixed wireless access: home internet delivered over the cellular network rather than a wire) and, through joint ventures, fiber. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $88.31B, up 8.49% year over year, and the trailing twelve months through September 30, 2026 reached $93.82B; third-quarter revenue alone grew 7.4%. At $165.71B of market value (1,059,272,571 shares on the 10-Q cover × the October 30 close) it ranks #57 in the S&P 500 — second in StockAnalysis's Telecom Services industry table at the October 30 close, behind Verizon ($185.41B) and ahead of AT&T ($144.23B).

The point worth making up front: a wireless carrier is not an EPS story. It is a free cash flow and capital-return story, and T-Mobile is the clearest example in the sector. Trailing FCF is $18.65B against $11.30B of GAAP net income, because the company's largest expense — the 5G network — was capitalized years ago and is now depreciating rather than consuming new cash. In October management narrowed its 2026 adjusted FCF guidance to $18.7B–$18.8B (from $18.4B–$18.8B) on roughly $10B of cash capex. That is about a quarter of trailing service revenue ($18.65B of FCF on $75.89B), and with the share price down, the stock now trades at under 9× trailing FCF.

The engine. Q3 2026 postpaid service revenue was $16.27B, up 9.3% YoY, against total service revenue of $19.38B (+6.2%). T-Mobile ended the quarter with 34.97 million postpaid accounts (+3%), added 274,000 postpaid net accounts (down from 396,000 a year earlier, but ahead of the roughly 250,000 management had guided), and reported postpaid account churn of 1.09%, up from 0.97% a year earlier. ARPA (average revenue per account — total postpaid service revenue divided by accounts, not by lines) was $155.73, up 4% from $149.44, double the 2% reported growth of Q2.

That pairing is the quarter in one line. In July management had guided Q3 adds lower around "rate plan modernization" — its phrase for moving existing customers onto newer, pricier plans; in Q3 revenue per account grew faster, and churn rose. Two older caveats still apply. First, postpaid revenue growth has leaned on acquisitions: UScellular closed in Q3 2025 and added 1.45 million accounts, and Metronet and other deals added 633,000 in the same quarter. With both now in the year-ago base, the Q3 growth rate of 9.3% is closer to organic than Q2's 13%. Second, T-Mobile no longer publishes postpaid phone net additions as a headline metric; it guides and reports on accounts. Because an account only deactivates when its last line leaves, account churn can sit on top of meaningfully worse line-level churn without ever surfacing. Full-year account additions are now guided to about 1.0 million (from 950,000–1,050,000).

Inside this line sits the genuine incremental growth story: FWA home internet. Total broadband net additions in Q2 2026 were in the upper 400,000s (the Q3 release gives no broadband count), and in February T-Mobile raised its FWA target to 15 million customers by 2030 (from a previous target of 12 million by the end of 2028), using what it calls a "fallow capacity" model — selling excess 2.5GHz capacity that would otherwise sit idle, at close to zero extra network cost. For context, cable lost customers in the same quarter: Comcast −167,000 domestic residential broadband, Charter −172,000.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
2.5GHz MID-BAND DEPTH LOW COST PER UNIT OF DATA ~25% FCF MARGIN SPECTRUM PORTFOLIO BRAND / NPS 46 FALLOW-CAPACITY FWA DEUTSCHE TELEKOM BACKING

The moat has one honest source and several derivative ones. The honest source is spectrum: the 2020 Sprint merger handed T-Mobile a deep, contiguous 2.5GHz mid-band position at a moment when neither rival had one, and mid-band is where 5G capacity and coverage actually reconcile. That advantage produced a lower cost for every unit of data carried, which funded a decade of share-taking on price without margin destruction, which in turn produced large free cash flow — trailing FCF of $18.65B on $93.82B of revenue, versus GAAP net income of $11.30B. Everything else — the brand, the record Net Promoter Score of 46 (a survey of how likely customers are to recommend the carrier; Q2 2026 release), the "fallow capacity" FWA business that sells idle 2.5GHz capacity at near-zero extra cost — is downstream of that one asset.

The problem is that this is 2026, not 2021. Verizon and AT&T have now largely finished their own C-Band and mid-band deployments, and in October a fourth contender showed up: on October 8 SpaceX agreed to buy a nationwide block of 800MHz spectrum (up to 14MHz paired) from Grain Management to take its Starlink service into mobile, a deal that still needs FCC approval (CNBC). TMUS fell 13.3% the next day, its worst session since 2013, and the market has kept marking the moat down: the stock is down 25.7% over the past year and trades at $156.44 against a 52-week high of $224.75. It is no longer priced for a lasting network advantage; it is priced at roughly 11× 2027 consensus earnings and under 9× trailing FCF, like a mature, cash-generative utility. That is the whole debate. The 2027–2028 spectrum auctions (C-Band 2.0 and 2.7GHz) will decide whether the spectrum edge is renewed or competed away — and management has explicitly said it is reserving capital for them, which is a tacit admission that the current position is not permanent.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Slowing net adds, rising churn and a disclosure blackout. Postpaid account net additions were 274,000 in Q3 2026, down from 396,000 a year earlier, and account churn rose to 1.09% from 0.97%. More troubling for analysis: T-Mobile stopped reporting postpaid phone net additions entirely at the end of 2025, replacing them with account counts. Recon Analytics notes an account only deactivates when its last line leaves, so account churn can mask materially worse line-level attrition. Verizon and AT&T made parallel changes. Investors are now being asked to trust a growth story with the key growth metric removed.
▸
Cable MVNOs are the underpriced threat. Comcast added 448,000 wireless lines in Q2 2026 (a record quarter, 10.2M total) and Charter added 406,000 — roughly 854,000 combined against T-Mobile's 277,000 account adds. Cable offloads about 90% of traffic to Wi-Fi and prices wireless as a broadband retention tool rather than a profit centre, economics no standalone carrier can match. T-Mobile hosts part of this traffic as a wholesaler, earning thin margins on lines that compete directly with its own retail postpaid base.
▸
A satellite company wants to be a carrier. SpaceX's October 8 agreement to buy nationwide 800MHz spectrum from Grain Management, and a Bloomberg report, summarised by 24/7 Wall St. on October 23, that it was talking to at least one major tower owner about access to a large U.S. cell-site network, came with TMUS closes of −13.3% on October 9 and −7.7% on October 23. The threat is years away and expensive — BNP Paribas put the bill at $100B or more of spending before revenue, excluding spectrum, while Recon Analytics' Roger Entner sketched a $10–15B rural-first network (both as reported by 24/7 Wall St., Oct 30) — but a fourth bidder for spectrum and customers is exactly what a mature three-player market prices badly. T-Mobile already works with Starlink on direct-to-phone satellite service (T-Satellite); on the Q3 call Gopalan ruled out letting SpaceX rent T-Mobile's network as an MVNO, per 24/7 Wall St.
▸
Growth has leaned on acquisitions. Postpaid revenue growth rested substantially on UScellular (1.45M accounts, closed Q3 2025) and Metronet (633K accounts); with both now in the year-ago base, Q3 2026 postpaid revenue growth slowed to 9.3% from 13% in Q2. Integration costs are real — UScellular merger-related costs were $129M in Q3 2026 and $717M over the first nine months (Q3 release) — and the FY2025 outcome was revenue +8.5% with net income down 3.1%.
▸
Leverage and control. Total debt is $118.99B including leases (StockAnalysis) against $5.62B of cash; debt-to-equity is 2.11 and the current ratio is 0.91, with a 2.5× target leverage ratio. Deutsche Telekom's majority control (nearly 53% per LSEG; Yahoo shows 56.26% insider-held) means minority holders do not control the board, and DT's own plans shape T-Mobile's: Bloomberg reported in April 2026 that DT was weighing a full combination through a new holding company offering stock for both companies' shares (Reuters), and on October 28 that DT had shelved the plan after resistance from investors including Elliott, which has built a DT stake (TelecomTV, Oct 29).

Notable One-Off Items

Several non-recurring items materially distort the reported numbers and should be adjusted for.

UScellular acquisition and integration costs. The deal closed in Q3 2025 and added 1.45 million accounts. Q1 2026 merger costs were $476M net of tax, including accelerated depreciation; in September 2025 T-Mobile said it expected to finish the integration in about two years. The Q3 2026 release lists $129M of UScellular merger-related costs for the quarter and $717M for the first nine months, against $73M and $120M a year earlier, plus $38M of network restructuring costs and $50M of store-closure costs in Q3. This is the single largest reason FY2025 revenue rose 8.49% while net income fell 3.06% to $10.99B. It also mechanically inflates postpaid revenue growth (+13% reported) and depresses per-account metrics (Q2 2026 ARPA grew 2% as reported).

Metronet account repurchase. Q2 2026 postpaid accounts were reduced by 16,000 from a Metronet account repurchase — recorded as an adjustment to the account base, so it did not change the reported 277,000 net additions (Q2 2026 release).

Prior-year Lumos comparison. The Q2 2025 base included 85,000 accounts acquired via Lumos, which flatters the year-ago comparison and makes the −13% YoY decline in account adds look slightly worse than the underlying trend. Both directions of distortion are present; neither is disclosed in a way that permits a clean organic figure.

Reporting-definition change. Not an accounting one-off, but analytically equivalent: the retirement of postpaid phone net additions as a reported metric at year-end 2025 breaks the historical time series. Comparisons to pre-2026 net-add data are no longer like-for-like.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 11× 2027 consensus earnings and under 9× trailing free cash flow for a carrier that just grew service revenue 6%, ARPA 4% and Core Adjusted EBITDA 11%, with $18.7–18.8B of guided 2026 adjusted FCF and a 2026 stockholder return program of up to $18.2B (buybacks plus dividends, raised from $14.6B in April) retiring stock about 26% below last year's price. The diluted share count is already 4.9% lower than a year ago, and the dividend just rose 15% to a 3.0% yield. The 2027 targets set in February — $40–41B EBITDA and $19.5–20.5B FCF — imply roughly 10% FCF growth a year, and two genuine new businesses are still early: FWA on a 15-million-customer 2030 target using spare 2.5GHz capacity at near-zero extra cost, and fiber JVs (i3 closed on October 1). SpaceX still needs FCC approval for its spectrum, plus towers and a costly build-out, before it can sell a full mobile service. A sub-1.0 PEG and 24 of 28 analysts at Buy against a $227 average target say Wall Street analysts think the sell-off overshot.

Bear Case

The growth formula is visibly maturing and the company has removed the metric that would prove it. Postpaid account adds were 274,000 against 396,000 a year earlier, account churn rose to 1.09% from 0.97% in the same quarter that ARPA rose 4%, and postpaid phone net additions are no longer reported at all. Postpaid revenue growth has slowed to 9.3% as UScellular moved into the year-ago base. Comcast and Charter added roughly 854,000 wireless lines in Q2 2026 — three times T-Mobile's account adds — using Wi-Fi-offload economics no carrier can match, and now SpaceX is buying spectrum and, per Bloomberg, talking to tower owners. The 2.5GHz advantage that justified the premium has been substantially matched by C-Band. ROIC of 9.45% is a utility's return, debt-to-equity is 2.11, and the 2027–2028 spectrum auctions are a large unquantified claim on the FCF that funds the buyback. Citi cut its target to $165 the day after the print.

Top 3 Risks

01
Subscriber slowdown hidden by a metric change. Account adds are slowing (274,000 in Q3 2026 against 396,000 a year earlier) and account churn rose to 1.09% from 0.97%, while the retirement of postpaid phone net additions means line-level churn is now unobservable — account churn only registers when an account's last line leaves. If the underlying line trend is worse than the account trend suggests, the market will find out through revenue rather than through disclosure, which is the slower and more painful path.
02
Competition from cable, fiber and now SpaceX. SpaceX's October deal for nationwide 800MHz spectrum, pending FCC approval, opens the prospect of a fourth national player, and the stock fell 13.3% the day after it was announced. Cable MVNOs added ~854,000 lines in Q2 2026 against T-Mobile's 277,000 account adds, and AT&T is executing a fiber-first convergence strategy with owned infrastructure. T-Mobile's answer is 50%-owned fiber JVs and FWA — capital-efficient but structurally thinner. If bundled broadband-plus-wireless becomes the default purchase, T-Mobile competes with half an economic interest in the fixed half of the bundle.
03
Spectrum reinvestment versus capital return. The entire equity story rests on FCF funding buybacks and dividends. C-Band 2.0 and 2.7GHz auctions in 2027–2028 are an unquantified call on that cash, arriving on top of $118.99B of debt including leases, a 2.11 debt-to-equity ratio and a 0.91 current ratio. Management has said it is reserving capital — which protects the network and squeezes the returns that currently support the share price.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters for T-Mobile Right Now
A Fourth Carrier From Orbit? SpaceX Buys Mobile Spectrum
NEW THREAT
On October 8, 2026 SpaceX agreed to buy a nationwide spectrum portfolio — up to 14MHz of paired 800MHz airwaves — from Grain Management to take Starlink into mobile service; the deal needs FCC approval, and FCC Chair Brendan Carr called more competition in spectrum "really good news for the American consumers" (CNBC). The next day Verizon closed down 8.75%, its worst day since 2002, AT&T fell 9.81% and TMUS 13.27%, its worst since 2013 (CNBC). On October 23, 24/7 Wall St. summarised a Bloomberg report that SpaceX was in talks with at least one major tower owner for access to a large U.S. cell-site network, and TMUS closed down another 7.7%. The cost of competing is the open question: BNP Paribas projected at least $100B of spending before revenue, excluding spectrum, while Recon Analytics' Roger Entner said a rural-first network could cost $10–15B (as reported by 24/7 Wall St., Oct 30). T-Mobile already works with Starlink on direct-to-phone satellite service (its T-Satellite product), and on the Q3 call Gopalan ruled out letting SpaceX rent its network as an MVNO, per 24/7 Wall St. For the stock, the point is that a three-player market that priced like a utility now has to price a possible fourth player.
The Metric Blackout: The Industry Stopped Reporting Phone Net Adds
ONGOING HEADWIND
At year-end 2025 the US wireless industry abandoned standardized customer reporting — simultaneously, and in mutually incompatible directions. T-Mobile now reports accounts only, eliminating the line count; because an account deactivates only when its last line leaves, its account churn (1.09% in Q3 2026) is a floor that can conceal much worse line-level attrition. Verizon removed the Consumer/Business phone split from its tables. AT&T moved postpaid phone metrics inside a convergence-focused segment. The residual comparable data for Q2 2026: Verizon 184,000 postpaid phone adds at 0.92% churn, AT&T 432,000 at 0.86%, T-Mobile 277,000 accounts at 0.99% account churn and 0.85% phone churn. T-Mobile's Q3 release added 274,000 accounts at 1.09% account churn and gave no phone churn figure. Recon Analytics' read is that each carrier executed its own strategy well, but that without comparable metrics investors cannot tell whether those strategies reflect real competitive gains or reporting choices. For an investor, the practical effect is that the single most important operating variable in a wireless carrier is now partly unobservable — and investors tend to pay less for earnings they can see less clearly.
Convergence: Cable and Fiber Are Closing the Bundle Gap
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
The competitive centre of gravity in US telecom has moved from network quality to bundle economics. In Q2 2026 Comcast added 448,000 wireless lines — its best quarter on record, up from 378,000 a year earlier — crossing 10.2 million lines, while Charter added 406,000. Combined that is roughly 854,000 lines against T-Mobile's 277,000 account adds. Cable is losing broadband (Comcast −167,000, Charter −172,000) and using cheap bundled wireless to slow the bleed, funded by offloading roughly 90% of mobile traffic to its own Wi-Fi. The revenue per line is lower but the retention is better, and neither Comcast nor Charter needs wireless to be profitable standalone. T-Mobile sits on both sides of this: it hosts Comcast and Charter business-mobile traffic under a July 2025 MVNO agreement — a $2.76B wholesale line that is declining (−7.5% in Q3 2026) — while those same lines compete with its retail postpaid base. T-Mobile's convergence answer is 50%-owned fiber JVs plus FWA; capital-light, but a half-share of the fixed economics.
Post-5G Capex, FWA Economics, and the 2027–28 Spectrum Bill — Bull vs Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND (WITH A CATCH)
The tailwind. T-Mobile's 5G build is behind it. Cash capex is held at roughly $10B annually against $28.7–28.8B of guided operating cash flow (narrowed up in October), producing $18.7–18.8B of adjusted FCF in 2026 and a targeted $19.5–20.5B in 2027 — roughly 10% FCF growth a year from 2023, with FCF margin "well over 20%" of service revenue. The FWA business monetizes fallow 2.5GHz capacity at close to zero incremental network cost, which is why the target was raised to 15 million customers by 2030 (from 12 million by the end of 2028); broadband net adds ran in the upper 400,000s in Q2 2026 while cable lost subscribers. Add the nearly $3B of extra Core Adjusted EBITDA management expects from digitalization and AI by end-2027 (against 2025), and the cash machine looks durable.

The catch. On the Q2 2026 call management confirmed it is reserving capital for the C-Band 2.0 and 2.7GHz auctions in 2027 and 2028. Spectrum is the asset that created this company's advantage, and it is bought at auction against two rivals with the same need and, in AT&T's case, better convergence economics to defend. The bull case says $19–20B of annual FCF absorbs an auction bill and still funds buybacks on the scale of 2026's up-to-$18.2B return program. The bear case says an $80B capital envelope through 2027, $118.99B of existing debt including leases, a 2.5× leverage target and an unbounded auction obligation cannot all coexist — and that the buyback is the line item that gives. Which of those is right will determine whether about 11× 2027 earnings turns out to have been the bargain analysts think it is, or a cheap price that stays cheap in a maturing industry.
