01 Company Overview

Texas Pacific Land was born out of a railroad bankruptcy. In 1888, after the Texas and Pacific Railway failed, its land grant was put into a trust so the trustees could sell the acreage and pay back bondholders — with no deadline for finishing the job. They never finished. The trust became a corporation in 2021, and today TPL owns about 882,000 acres (per its FY2025 results release) and a reported 224,000 net royalty acres in West Texas, most of it in the Permian Basin. It drills nothing. It owns the ground, collects a royalty on every barrel other companies pump from it, sells them the water they need to frack, charges them for the pipes and roads they lay across it, and gets paid again to take away the salty water that comes back up.

That makes for unusually rich economics: $897.54M of trailing-twelve-month revenue at a 93.3% gross margin and a 60.3% net margin, run by 99 employees, with almost no debt. The catch is the price the market charges for it. At the September 21, 2026 settled close of $366.80 the stock trades at 46.8x trailing earnings — a multiple you'd expect on a fast-growing software company, not an oil royalty. In 2026 the market added a second story to the first: West Texas land, water and power for AI data centers. The stock rose 50.5% in February 2026 alone, then gave back roughly 30% of the February month-end level by September. The question underneath the price is how much of $25.3B belongs to barrels, and how much to server farms that have not been built yet.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

~882,000 surface acres ~224,000 net royalty acres (reported) Cost-free royalty on others' capex Surface control = easement toll Water sourcing + disposal network Debt-free balance sheet (D/E 0.01) 99-person cost base

The moat is land that can't be replicated. Nobody is making more Permian acreage, and TPL got its acreage in 1888. The surface acres matter as much as the mineral rights: an operator drilling nearby needs to cross TPL land with pipelines, roads and power lines, needs water to frack, and needs somewhere to put the produced water afterwards. Each of those is a fee. Gross margins above 93% for five straight years (FY2021–FY2025, per StockAnalysis) show what the business looks like when someone else carries the cost of production. The weakness is just as structural: TPL controls none of the volume. Drilling pace, commodity prices and where the operators choose to drill are all decided by others.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Pure commodity exposure with no cost cushion. Oil and gas royalties were 59.2% of Q2 2026 revenue. A royalty owner's revenue is volume × price with nothing in between. The Q2 realized price jump from $37.06 to $42.17/Boe did much of the work in the record quarter, and the same lever works in reverse.
02
The valuation assumes a second business. At 46.8x trailing earnings, 28.2x sales and 33.8x EV/EBITDA, the price reaches well past a royalty stream on production growing about 19% a year. Some of the premium is paying for data-center land and water deals (Bolt, Project Kilby) that have not yet produced revenue anyone can separate out in the release.
03
Water sales are volume-sensitive. Sourcing-water revenue fell $7.1M quarter-on-quarter in Q2 2026 on lower volumes. Water sales follow completion activity directly, so a slowdown in Permian fracking shows up here before it reaches royalty production.
04
Lumpy land sales. Q1 2026 included $20.9M of land sales and Q2 2026 had none. Land sales are one-off. Comparisons that include or exclude them can move revenue growth by several points.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — an expensive stock can stay overbought for a long time.

On a split-adjusted basis, TPL went from a September 2021 month-end of $134.37 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $366.80, a +173.0% price return over five years before dividends (dividends were not summed for this report). The path was anything but smooth. The stock doubled into late 2022 as oil spiked, halved into mid-2023, then more than tripled from the January 2024 month-end ($162.37) to the November 2024 month-end ($533.36), the month it joined the S&P 500. It then fell 30.9% the following month. After a year-long slide to $287.22 in December 2025, the data-center story took it to $524.29 by February 2026. It has drifted lower since.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 96.6 in November 2024, the most overbought reading in the series and the month before a 30.9% fall. It reached 80.2 in February 2026 and has since cooled to 43.5, just below neutral. The 10-month moving average is about $405.25, above the current price, so the medium-term trend has turned down: every month-end since February 2026 except June has been lower than the one before. Momentum has left, but the stock is not oversold.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You own an irreplaceable piece of the most productive oil basin in America, and other companies pay to develop it. Royalty production grew 19% year-on-year in H1 2026 with zero capital from TPL. Margins run 93% gross and 60% net, cash covers debt more than thirteen times over, and 99 employees run the whole thing. Now the same land, water and easements are being sought for power plants and data centers: a Chevron-built power facility on TPL land for Project Kilby, a reported right of first refusal on Bolt's 10 GW ambition, and a desalination plant in commissioning. If even part of that turns into long-dated lease and water income, TPL stops being an oil royalty and becomes a West Texas infrastructure landlord. Landlords earn premium multiples.

Bear Case

At 46.8x trailing earnings and a ~2.1% FCF yield, the price already assumes the second business works. Today's revenue is still 59% oil and gas royalties priced at whatever the commodity fetches. Q2's record owed a good deal to a realized price that jumped from $37.06 to $42.17/Boe. The two times momentum ran this hot, RSI 96.6 in November 2024 and 80.2 in February 2026, the stock fell about 30% afterwards. A 7.87% short float and a $247 Strong Sell target say part of the market sees an excellent business at the wrong price. Data-center "ambitions" are not contracts, and the release disclosed no financial terms for Kilby.

Top 3 Risks

01
Multiple compression. The stock trades at 46.8x trailing and 33.8x EV/EBITDA. With no leverage and no operating risk to speak of, the main way to lose money is the market deciding to pay less per dollar of earnings. That happened after both prior momentum peaks, each time costing about 30%.
02
Commodity prices and Permian activity. Royalty revenue is volume × realized price, and water sales follow completion activity. A drop in oil prices or a slowdown in Permian drilling hits both segments at once, and TPL has no costs to cut against it.
03
Data-center optionality that doesn't convert. Bolt's reported 10 GW is an ambition, and Kilby's economics to TPL were not disclosed. If these projects are delayed, shrink, or pay TPL less than hoped, the premium that built up from December 2025 to February 2026 has nothing underneath it.
Industry Commentary
West Texas as a data-center and power site
EMERGING TAILWIND

The Motley Fool describes West Texas as prime territory for data centers because of "cheap land, lower regulatory burden, and access to abundant energy." For TPL this is a new buyer for the same assets: surface acres for plants and campuses, easements for transmission, and brackish or treated produced water for cooling and power generation. Two concrete pieces are in the public record: the $50M Bolt investment (Q4 2025, per TPL) with a reported right of first refusal on power and water, and the Chevron Project Kilby agreement for land and brackish water in Reeves County (disclosed with Q2 2026). TPL also bought $110.2M of land in Shackelford and Jones Counties, which the CEO tied to expanding data-center and power efforts beyond the Permian. None of this is broken out as revenue yet.

Permian activity and the commodity cycle
CYCLICAL DRIVER

The base business still depends on how much gets drilled in the Delaware and Midland Basins and what it sells for. TPL's realized price per Boe rose from $37.06 in Q1 2026 to $42.17 in Q2, and production reached a record 39.7k Boe/d. The financial history shows how sensitive this is: revenue grew 48.0% in FY2022, fell 5.4% in FY2023 as prices normalised, then grew 11.8% and 13.1% in FY2024–25 on volume. A royalty owner doesn't carry the operator's costs, but it doesn't escape the price cycle either.

Produced water: from disposal problem to resource
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Permian wells bring up large volumes of salty produced water that has to be handled. TPL earns a royalty on produced water on its land ($37.1M in Q2 2026, a record in volume). Midstream companies such as Western Midstream also gather and dispose of it. The Orla Phase 2B desalination plant (10,000 inlet barrels/day anticipated) is TPL's attempt to turn waste into supply. If treated produced water can serve power generation or be discharged, the constraint on disposal eases and the water becomes a product. The plant is in commissioning, and its economics have not been reported.
