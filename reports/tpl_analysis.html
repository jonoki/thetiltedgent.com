01 Company Overview

Texas Pacific Land was born out of a railroad bankruptcy. In 1888, after the Texas and Pacific Railway failed, its land grant was put into a trust so the trustees could sell the acreage and pay back bondholders — with no deadline for finishing the job. They never finished. The trust became a corporation in 2021, and at September 30, 2026 TPL owned about 896,000 surface acres and about 224,000 net royalty acres (its royalty interests converted to a standard unit) in West Texas, per its Q3 10-Q, most of it in the Permian Basin. It drills nothing. It owns the ground, collects a royalty on every barrel other companies pump from it, sells them the water they need to frack, charges them for the pipes and roads they lay across it, and gets paid again to take away the salty water that comes back up.

That makes for unusually rich economics: $967.85M of trailing-twelve-month revenue at a 94.1% gross margin and a 60.7% net margin, run by 114 full-time employees, with no borrowings. In the third quarter of 2026 it set records for revenue, net income and free cash flow. The stock went the other way. At the November 6, 2026 settled close of $272.36 it is down 25.8% from the previous edition's $366.80 and 48.1% below its February 2026 month-end. It still trades at 32.0x trailing earnings (the share price divided by the last twelve months' earnings per share), a high price for an oil royalty. In 2026 the market added a second story to the first: West Texas land, water and power for AI data centers. The stock rose 50.5% in February 2026 alone. Then Texas's governor ordered a halt to data-center permits in September, and the day after the November 3 elections the shares fell 13.4%. That rally is now gone: the stock sits 5.2% below its December 2025 month-end, where it began. The question underneath the price is how much of $18.8B belongs to barrels, and how much to server farms that have not been built yet.

Fracking uses a lot of water, and every producing well sends back up a lot of salty "produced water" that has to go somewhere. TPL makes money at both ends. It sells sourcing water (Q3 2026: $28.4M, down $11.3M from Q2 even though volumes were roughly flat at 672,000 barrels a day against 663,000, which works out to about 30% less revenue per barrel), and it earns a royalty on produced water handled on its land (Q3 2026: $41.4M on a record 5.5 million barrels a day, up 14% from Q2). The newer bet is desalination. TPL has finished building its Phase 2B produced-water desalination facility in Orla, Texas and is commissioning it, with an anticipated capacity of 10,000 inlet barrels per day. Robert Crain, who runs the water business, said on the Q3 call: "by the end of the year, we pivot from synthetic brines over to actual produced water and let the system start to run." The same land and water are now being offered to power and data-center developers (see Catalysts).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

~896,000 surface acres ~224,000 net royalty acres Cost-free royalty on others' drilling Surface control = easement toll Water sourcing + disposal network No borrowings; $370M cash 114-person cost base

The moat is land that can't be replicated. Nobody is making more Permian acreage, and TPL got its acreage in 1888. The surface acres matter as much as the mineral rights: an operator drilling nearby needs to cross TPL land with pipelines, roads and power lines, needs water to frack, and needs somewhere to put the produced water afterwards. Each of those is a fee. Gross margins above 93% for five straight years (FY2021–FY2025, per StockAnalysis) show what the business looks like when someone else carries the cost of production. The weakness is just as structural: TPL controls none of the volume. Drilling pace, commodity prices and where the operators choose to drill are all decided by others.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Pure commodity exposure with no cost cushion. Oil and gas royalties were 61.2% of Q3 2026 revenue. A royalty owner's revenue is volume × price with nothing in between, and TPL does not hedge. The realized price climbed from $37.06/Boe in Q1 2026 to $42.17 in Q2 and $44.97 in Q3, helping the record quarters, and falling prices would work the same way in reverse.
02
The valuation assumes a second business. At 32.0x trailing earnings, 19.4x sales and 22.5x EV/EBITDA, the price reaches well past a royalty stream whose production grew 18% in the first nine months of 2026. Some of that premium is paying for data-center land and water deals (Bolt, Project Kilby) whose timing now depends on a Texas permit halt. Apart from the $20.9M TPL booked when the Kilby land sale closed, they have not produced revenue anyone can separate out in the release.
03
Water sales swing with drilling crews and pricing. Sourcing-water revenue fell $11.3M from Q2 2026 and $16.1M from a year earlier, with volumes roughly flat against Q2, so revenue per barrel dropped. Management said completion activity has been slow to ramp. Water sales follow completion activity directly, so a slowdown in Permian fracking shows up here before it reaches royalty production.
04
Lumpy land sales. Q1 2026 included $20.9M of land sales, the amount TPL booked when the Chevron Project Kilby land sale closed. Q2 had none and Q3 had $0.1M. Land sales are one-off. Comparisons that include or exclude them can move revenue growth by several points.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the per-share and period comparisons. First, two 3-for-1 stock splits: one effective for trading on March 27, 2024 (announced March 7, 2024) and a second effective for trading on December 23, 2025 (record date December 12, distribution December 22, 2025). A share held since before March 2024 is now nine shares. Every per-share figure in this report is on the post-December-2025 basis. Second, land sales: $20.9M in Q1 2026 and $0.1M across Q2 and Q3, which flatters or depresses quarter-to-quarter revenue. Third, easement income is lumpy: $36.2M in Q3 2026 against $23.7M in Q2, yet the nine-month total of $77.2M is up only 8.4% from $71.2M a year earlier.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 6, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — an expensive stock can stay overbought for a long time.

On a split-adjusted basis, TPL went from a November 2021 month-end of $134.31 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $272.36, a +102.8% price return over about five years before dividends, against +61.4% for SPY and +66.6% for QQQ from the same month-end. The path was anything but smooth. The stock doubled into late 2022 as oil spiked, halved into mid-2023, then more than tripled from the January 2024 month-end ($162.37) to the November 2024 month-end ($533.36), the month it joined the S&P 500. It then fell 30.9% the following month. After a year-long slide to $287.22 in December 2025, the data-center story took it to $524.29 by February 2026. All of that has been given back: the stock is 48.1% below February's month-end and 5.2% below December 2025's, its lowest point on this chart since June 2024.

The 6-period monthly RSI hit 96.6 in November 2024, the most overbought reading in the series and the month before a 30.9% fall. It reached 80.2 in February 2026 and has since dropped to 29.3, under the 30 line that marks "oversold" and the lowest reading in the series, just below May 2023's 29.7. The 10-month moving average is about $398.00, well above the current price, so the medium-term trend is down: every month-end since February 2026 except June and October has been lower than the one before. Oversold says the selling has been fast, not that the stock is cheap.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You own an irreplaceable piece of the most productive oil basin in America, and other companies pay to develop it. Royalty production grew 18% year-on-year over the first nine months of 2026 with zero capital from TPL. Margins run 94% gross and 61% net, the company holds $370M of cash and no borrowings, and 114 employees run the whole thing. Now the same land, water and easements are being sought for power plants and data centers: a Chevron-built power facility on land TPL sold for Project Kilby, a right of first refusal to supply water to Bolt's projects, and a desalination plant in commissioning. Management says its data-center backlog is robust and the Texas permit halt is not a blanket ban. At $272.36 the stock has given back the whole of its data-center rally, so a buyer today pays much less for that hope than in February. If even part of it turns into long-dated lease and water income, TPL stops being an oil royalty and becomes a West Texas infrastructure landlord, and investors usually pay more per dollar of earnings for a landlord with long leases.

Bear Case

At 32.0x trailing earnings and a 3.1% free-cash-flow yield (the cash the business throws off, as a share of its market value), the price still assumes the second business works. Today's revenue is still 61% oil and gas royalties priced at whatever the commodity fetches, and TPL does not hedge. Q3's record owed part of its gain to a realized price up from $42.17 to $44.97/Boe. The three times momentum peaked this high, RSI 89.0 in November 2022, 96.6 in November 2024 and 80.2 in February 2026, the stock fell hard afterwards: 49.7% by May 2023, 30.9% the next month, and 48.1% by this date. With 7.19% of the tradable shares sold short and a $247 Strong Sell target, part of the market says it sees an excellent business at the wrong price. Texas has halted data-center permits, ambitions are not contracts, and the disclosed value of the Kilby deal so far is a $42.5M land sale paid out through 2046.

Top 3 Risks

01
The market paying less per dollar of earnings. The stock trades at 32.0x trailing earnings, down from 46.8x at the previous edition, and 22.5x EV/EBITDA. With no borrowings, the main way to lose money outside an oil-price fall is the market deciding to pay less for each dollar TPL earns. That has happened after all three momentum peaks on the chart: 49.7% in the six months after November 2022, 30.9% in the month after November 2024, and 48.1% so far since February 2026.
02
Commodity prices and Permian activity. Royalty revenue is volume × realized price, and water sales follow completion activity. A drop in oil prices or a slowdown in Permian drilling hits both segments at once, and TPL has no costs to cut against it.
03
Data-center hopes that don't convert. Bolt's reported 10 GW is an ambition, Texas's governor has ordered a halt to data-center permits until the state grid audit is done, and the disclosed Kilby terms so far are a $42.5M land sale paid through 2046. If these projects are delayed, shrink, or pay TPL less than hoped, what remains of the premium in the stock has little underneath it.
Industry Commentary
West Texas as a data-center and power site
EMERGING, PERMITS HALTED

The Motley Fool describes West Texas as prime territory for data centers because of "cheap land, lower regulatory burden, and access to abundant energy." For TPL this is a new buyer for the same assets: surface acres for plants and campuses, easements for transmission, and brackish or treated produced water for cooling and power generation. Two concrete pieces are in the public record: the $50M Bolt investment (Q4 2025, per TPL) with a right of first refusal to supply water to Bolt's projects, and the Chevron Project Kilby agreement for land and brackish water in Reeves County ($42.5M land sale, per TPL's 10-Q). TPL also bought land in Shackelford and Jones Counties for its data-center and power plans, part of $122.7M of land purchases in the first nine months of 2026. Then on September 21, 2026 Governor Abbott directed the state environmental regulator to "halt all permits sought by data centers" until the state grid operator completes its audit. CEO Tyler Glover told investors the actions "add to the diligence checklist" and "are not viewed as a blanket ban," and called the political change "a net positive for the long-term value proposition for TPL and West Texas more broadly." It could still delay near-term projects, he said. Beyond the Kilby land sale, none of this is broken out as revenue yet.

Permian activity and the commodity cycle
CYCLICAL DRIVER

The base business still depends on how much gets drilled in the Delaware and Midland Basins and what it sells for. TPL's realized price per Boe rose from $37.06 in Q1 2026 to $42.17 in Q2 and $44.97 in Q3, and production reached a record 42.3k Boe/d. On the Q3 call the CFO said: "the Permian oil machine and by extension, TPL is not even close to slowing down." The financial history shows how sensitive this is: revenue grew 48.0% in FY2022, fell 5.4% in FY2023 as prices normalised, then grew 11.8% and 13.1% in FY2024–25 on volume. A royalty owner doesn't carry the operator's costs, but it doesn't escape the price cycle either.

Produced water: from disposal problem to resource
STRUCTURAL SHIFT

Permian wells bring up large volumes of salty produced water that has to be handled. TPL earns a royalty on produced water on its land ($41.4M in Q3 2026, a record in volume). Midstream companies such as Western Midstream also gather and dispose of it. The Orla Phase 2B desalination plant (10,000 inlet barrels/day anticipated) is TPL's attempt to turn waste into supply. If treated produced water can serve power generation or be discharged, the constraint on disposal eases and the water becomes a product. The plant is in commissioning and management expects to feed it real produced water by year-end; its economics have not been reported.
