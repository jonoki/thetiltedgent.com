01 Company Overview

Tapestry is the handbag company that used to be called Coach. The leather workshop founded in New York in 1941 listed as Coach, Inc. in October 2000, bought Kate Spade in 2017 in a $2.4 billion deal, and renamed itself Tapestry — ticker COH became TPR — on October 31, 2017, to sound like a house of brands rather than one brand. Nine years later it is, in practice, one brand again: in fiscal 2026 (year ended June 27, 2026) Coach produced $6.91B of $8.00B in revenue — 86% — and grew 24%, while Kate Spade shrank 10% and Stuart Weitzman was sold on August 4, 2025. Total revenue rose 14% reported, 17% on a pro forma constant-currency basis, with non-GAAP EPS up 38% to $7.05 and GAAP EPS of $7.27.

The detour that explains the last five years is the one that did not happen. In August 2023 Tapestry agreed to buy Capri Holdings — Michael Kors, Versace, Jimmy Choo — for $8.5 billion. The FTC sued to block it in April 2024, a court blocked it on October 24, 2024, and the companies called it off the following month. The market's verdict on the deal was written in the stock: a 22.8% fall the month it was announced, a 31.3% rise the month it died. Freed of the integration, management spent the cash on buybacks instead — $2.06B in FY2025 and $1.64B in FY2026 per the cash-flow statement — and hit its September 2025 Investor Day revenue, margin and EPS targets two years early. And then, on August 13, 2026, the stock fell 16.5% in a day on a beat, because the FY2027 guide called for mid-single-digit growth after a 17% year. That is the whole debate: is Coach a structurally re-rated brand compounding at a lower speed, or a fashion cycle that has just peaked?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

85-year Coach brand heritage Pricing power (AUR up mid-teens) Direct-to-consumer control Gen Z customer acquisition 76.6% gross margin Greater China scale $1.8B free cash flow

A handbag company's moat is the distance between what the bag costs to make and what someone will pay for the logo. Tapestry's non-GAAP gross margin of 76.6% says that distance is wide, and the direction matters more than the level: rising average unit prices alongside rising units is the signature of a brand gaining heat rather than buying volume with discounts. The operating model — mostly own stores and own websites, not department stores — means Tapestry sees the customer data and sets the price. The vulnerability is that a brand moat is rented from the customer's taste, and taste can be withdrawn faster than a factory can be closed.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Single-brand concentration. Coach is 86% of revenue and nearly all of the growth. A portfolio built to diversify fashion risk has ended up with the opposite: one brand, one core category (handbags were $4.0B of Coach's $6.9B), and a customer base increasingly weighted to young buyers whose loyalty is untested through a downturn.
02
Kate Spade keeps shrinking. −10% in FY2026, −7% in Q4, $855M of impairments already taken in FY2025. Each year it shrinks, fixed store and marketing costs are spread over less revenue.
03
Tariffs. Tariff and duty costs took 130bps off FY2026 gross margin, and the FY2027 guide embeds a mid-20% tariff rate on U.S. inventory receipts. FY2026 also benefited from one-off IEEPA tariff refunds, which are excluded from non-GAAP figures.
04
A thin equity base. Buybacks have left book equity at $692.1M against $3.96B of total debt (including leases), so debt-to-equity reads 5.72x and ROE 197%. Neither number is a useful signal on its own; the company's own leverage measure — gross debt to adjusted EBITDA — was 1.1x at fiscal year-end.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported series. First, the Kate Spade impairment: $855M of goodwill and brand-intangible charges in Q4 FY2025 produced a quarterly GAAP loss of $2.49 per share and make every FY2026-vs-FY2025 GAAP growth rate meaningless (StockAnalysis shows net income +734%). Second, FY2026 items that ran the other way: IEEPA tariff refunds, distribution-network optimisation, organisation-efficiency costs and the Stuart Weitzman divestiture together added $49M to operating income and $0.22 to EPS — which is why GAAP EPS ($7.27) is above non-GAAP ($7.05), and why this report's trailing P/E uses the lower-quality GAAP figure only because it is the reported one. Third, the Capri financing: debt on the balance sheet was $8.77B at the end of FY2024 (with $7.20B of cash) because the acquisition was pre-funded; it fell back to $3.90B after the deal was abandoned. Looking ahead, FY2027 has a 53rd week, which management says adds about a point to revenue growth and is excluded from its guidance.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $37.02 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $113.07, TPR roughly tripled — +205% on price — before dividends of roughly $7 a share over the period (an approximation from the fiscal-year dividend series). Almost all of that came in one leg: the stock went nowhere for three years, bottoming at $27.56 in October 2023 while the Capri deal hung over it, then rose more than fivefold to a $155.47 month-end peak in February 2026. It is now 27% below that peak.

The 6-period monthly RSI spent most of 2025 and the first half of 2026 above 70 — peaking at 92.3 in February 2025 and 89.3 in February 2026 — the reading of a stock that had run a long way on a re-rating. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 15.6x trailing GAAP earnings and about 14x forward for a brand that grew 17% (pro forma, constant currency), expanded operating margin 340bps, raised prices on its core product by a mid-teens rate while selling more units, and added about 11 million customers, a third of them Gen Z. It converts 23% of revenue to free cash flow, yields 8% on FCF, and hands back $1.7B a year. The August sell-off punished a guide that management has beaten repeatedly — it hit Investor Day targets two years early — and every named analyst kept a Buy or Hold after the drop, with the lowest target of $96 only 15% below the price.

Bear Case

This is one brand, one category, riding a fashion cycle, priced as if the cycle were structural. FY2026 was flattered by a 60–80bps divestiture benefit and one-off tariff refunds; FY2027 growth is guided to mid-single digits with a mid-20% tariff rate embedded. Kate Spade is still shrinking after an $855M write-down, Japan is going backwards, and a customer base tilted to Gen Z has never been tested through a consumer downturn. The 10-month moving average has rolled over, the stock is 27% off its peak, and a guide that merely matches consensus was enough for a 16.5% one-day fall.

Top 3 Risks

01
Coach demand normalises. 86% of revenue and nearly all growth sit in one brand whose momentum depends on staying fashionable with young buyers. A slowdown in Coach alone would take group growth below the guide, with no second engine to offset it.
02
Tariffs and trade policy. 130bps of FY2026 gross margin lost to tariffs and duties; the FY2027 guide assumes a mid-20% rate on U.S. receipts holds. Changes in either direction, or in the treatment of the IEEPA refunds, move the margin line directly.
03
China and consumer cyclicality. Greater China grew 35% to $1.40B and is now the second-largest region. It is also the region where luxury demand has historically been most volatile, and TPR's 1.43 beta says the stock amplifies consumer cycles.
Industry Commentary
Accessible luxury is winning the middle
EMERGING TAILWIND

A WSJ piece this summer put it plainly: "Coach and Ralph Lauren are still growing because they understand what middle-class shoppers can afford." Tapestry's own numbers agree — handbag prices up mid-teens, units up too, and a new-customer base that is about 35% Gen Z. The accessible-luxury tier sits between mass fashion and the European houses, and when the top end raises prices faster than incomes, the middle collects the shoppers who trade down. The risk is symmetrical: the same shopper trades back out when budgets tighten.

Tariffs on imported leather goods
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Handbags are made overseas and sold in America, so import duties go straight to gross margin. Tapestry absorbed a 130bps tariff and duty hit in FY2026 and still expanded non-GAAP gross margin by 120bps — pricing power doing its job. Its FY2027 outlook embeds a mid-20% tariff rate on U.S. inventory receipts and assumes a neutral net year-over-year impact. The FY2025 Kate Spade impairment was itself partly driven, per coverage of the company's FY2025 results (ouispeakfashion.com), by the expected impact of tariff and trade-policy cost increases.

Consolidation that regulators would not allow
STRUCTURAL FACT

The FTC's successful challenge to the Tapestry–Capri deal defined "affordable luxury handbags" as a market in its own right and blocked Coach's owner from combining with Michael Kors's. The practical effect for investors: Tapestry's growth now has to come organically or from smaller deals, and its capital goes to buybacks — $1.64B in FY2026 per the cash-flow statement — rather than acquisitions. For Capri, now valued at $1.63B, the outcome was far worse.
