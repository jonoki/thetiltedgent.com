01 Company Overview

Tapestry is the handbag company that used to be called Coach. The leather workshop founded in New York in 1941 listed as Coach, Inc. in October 2000, bought Kate Spade in 2017 in a $2.4 billion deal, and renamed itself Tapestry — ticker COH became TPR — on October 31, 2017, to sound like a house of brands rather than one brand. Nine years later it is, in practice, one brand again: in fiscal 2026 (year ended June 27, 2026) Coach produced $6.91B of $8.00B in revenue — 86% — and grew 24%, while Kate Spade shrank 10% and Stuart Weitzman was sold on August 4, 2025. Total revenue rose 14% reported, 17% leaving out the sold Stuart Weitzman and the effect of exchange rates, with non-GAAP EPS up 38% to $7.05 and GAAP EPS of $7.27. The first quarter of fiscal 2027, to September 26, 2026, kept going: revenue rose 11% to $1.89B, Coach rose 16% and Kate Spade fell 10%.

The detour that explains the last five years is the one that did not happen. In August 2023 Tapestry agreed to buy Capri Holdings — Michael Kors, Versace, Jimmy Choo — for $8.5 billion. The FTC sued to block it in April 2024, a court blocked it on October 24, 2024, and the companies called it off the following month. The market's verdict on the deal was written in the stock: a 22.8% fall the month it was announced, a 31.3% rise the month it died. Freed of the integration, management spent the cash on buybacks instead — $2.06B in FY2025 on the cash-flow statement and $1.35B in FY2026 by the company's count — and hit its September 2025 Investor Day revenue, margin and EPS targets two years early. And then, on August 13, 2026, the stock fell 16.5% in a day on a beat, because the FY2027 guide called for mid-single-digit growth after a 17% year. Twelve weeks later, on November 5, management raised that guide to $8.5–$8.6B of revenue and $8.25–$8.35 of non-GAAP EPS, and the stock rose 7.07% that day. That is the whole debate: has Coach permanently raised what shoppers will pay for it, with growth now slowing to a steadier rate, or is this a fashion cycle near its peak?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

85-year Coach brand heritage Pricing power (AUR up low double digits) Direct-to-consumer control Gen Z customer acquisition 78.7% gross margin (Q1) Greater China scale $1.9B free cash flow (TTM)

A handbag company's moat is the distance between what the bag costs to make and what someone will pay for the logo. Tapestry's non-GAAP gross margin of 78.7% in the latest quarter (76.6% for FY2026) says that distance is wide, and the direction matters more than the level: rising average unit prices alongside rising units is the signature of a brand gaining heat rather than buying volume with discounts. The operating model — mostly own stores and own websites, not department stores — means Tapestry sees the customer data and sets the price. The vulnerability is that a brand moat is rented from the customer's taste, and taste can be withdrawn faster than a factory can be closed.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Single-brand concentration. Coach was 88% of Q1 FY2027 revenue and all of the growth. A portfolio built to diversify fashion risk has ended up with the opposite: one brand, one core category (handbags were $4.0B of Coach's $6.9B), and a customer base increasingly weighted to young buyers whose loyalty is untested through a downturn.
02
Kate Spade keeps shrinking. −10% in FY2026, −10% again in Q1 FY2027, $855M of impairments already taken in FY2025, and a non-GAAP operating loss of $6M in the latest quarter. Each year it shrinks, fixed store and marketing costs are spread over less revenue.
03
Tariffs. Tariff and duty costs took 130bps off FY2026 gross margin, then gave back a 70bps net benefit in Q1 FY2027. The raised FY2027 guide embeds a low-to-mid-20% tariff rate on U.S. inventory receipts and a modest net benefit for the year. FY2026 also benefited from one-off refunds of tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which are excluded from non-GAAP figures; collecting them lifted reported Q1 cash flow, and the company strips that out of adjusted free cash flow.
04
A thin equity base. Buybacks have left book equity at $344M against $4.23B of borrowings and lease liabilities, so debt-to-equity reads 12.29x and ROE 434%. Neither number is a useful signal on its own; the company's own leverage measure — gross debt divided by adjusted EBITDA, a rough gauge of cash operating profit — was 1.1x at September 26, 2026.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the reported series. First, the Kate Spade impairment: $855M of goodwill and brand-intangible charges in Q4 FY2025 produced a quarterly GAAP loss of $2.49 per share and make every FY2026-vs-FY2025 GAAP growth rate meaningless (StockAnalysis shows net income +734%). Second, FY2026 items that ran the other way: IEEPA tariff refunds, distribution-network optimisation, organisation-efficiency costs and the Stuart Weitzman divestiture together added $49M to operating income and $0.22 to EPS — which is why GAAP EPS ($7.27) is above non-GAAP ($7.05), and why this report's trailing P/E uses the lower-quality GAAP figure only because it is the reported one. Third, the Capri financing: debt including lease liabilities was $8.77B at the end of FY2024 (with $7.20B of cash) because the acquisition was pre-funded; it fell back to $3.90B after the deal was abandoned. In Q1 FY2027 the non-GAAP figures exclude $15M of costs ($0.06 a share) for moving distribution from Ohio to a third-party site in Pennsylvania. Looking ahead, FY2027 has a 53rd week, which management says adds about a point to revenue growth and is excluded from its guidance.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying 17.1x trailing GAAP earnings and about 16x this year's EPS guidance for a brand that grew 16% in the latest quarter while its average handbag price rose at a low-double-digit rate and it still sold more units, and for a company that added about 2.6 million customers in three months. Q1 non-GAAP operating margin rose 230 basis points to 23.2%. Over the past twelve months it converted 23% of revenue to free cash flow, a 7.2% yield on its market value, and it plans to hand back about $2.0B this year. The August sell-off punished a guide that management raised one quarter later, lifting the EPS midpoint 5.7%. All eight firms on the post-print ratings table kept a Buy, and the average target is 23% above the price.

Bear Case

This is one brand, one category, riding a fashion cycle, priced as if the boom will last. North America, the largest region, grew only 4% in Q1; Coach handbag price gains slowed from mid-teens to low double digits; and management's Q2 guide of 5%–6% constant-currency growth is about half the Q1 pace. Greater China, up 39%, is modelled to slow to the mid-teens. Kate Spade is still shrinking after an $855M write-down and is expected to lose money this year. A customer base tilted to Gen Z has never been tested through a consumer downturn. Book equity is down to $344M after buybacks. The stock is still 14.6% off its month-end peak and just showed that one soft guide can cost 16.5% in a day.

Top 3 Risks

01
Coach demand normalises. 88% of Q1 revenue and all of the growth sit in one brand whose momentum depends on staying fashionable with young buyers. A slowdown in Coach alone would take group growth below the guide, with no second engine to offset it.
02
Tariffs and trade policy. 130bps of FY2026 gross margin lost to tariffs and duties; the raised FY2027 guide assumes a low-to-mid-20% rate on U.S. receipts holds and yields a modest net benefit. Changes in either direction, or in the treatment of the IEEPA refunds, move the margin line directly.
03
China and consumer cyclicality. Greater China grew 39% in constant currency to $385M in Q1 and is the second-largest region. It is also the region where luxury demand has historically been most volatile, and TPR's 1.46 beta says the stock amplifies consumer cycles.
Industry Commentary
Accessible luxury is winning the middle
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bulk of Coach handbags sell for $300 to $700, Fortune reported in May 2026, well below top luxury rivals. Tapestry's fiscal 2026 numbers show what that price point has been worth — handbag prices up at a mid-teens rate, units up too, and a new-customer base that is about 35% Gen Z. The accessible-luxury tier sits between mass fashion and the European houses, and when the top end raises prices faster than incomes, the middle collects the shoppers who trade down. The risk is symmetrical: the same shopper trades back out when budgets tighten.

Tariffs on imported leather goods
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Handbags are made overseas and sold in America, so import duties go straight to gross margin. Tapestry absorbed a 130bps tariff and duty hit in FY2026 and still expanded non-GAAP gross margin by 120bps — pricing power doing its job. Its raised FY2027 outlook embeds a low-to-mid-20% tariff rate on U.S. inventory receipts and a modest net benefit year over year, and Q1 gross margin got a 70bps net benefit from tariffs and duties. Tariffs also figured in the FY2025 Kate Spade impairment: on the August 2025 earnings call, CEO Joanne Crevoiserat said they fall disproportionately on that brand (Inside Retail).

Consolidation that regulators would not allow
STRUCTURAL FACT

The FTC's successful challenge to the Tapestry–Capri deal defined "accessible luxury" handbags as a market in its own right and blocked Coach's owner from combining with Michael Kors's. The practical effect for investors: Tapestry's growth now has to come organically or from smaller deals, and its capital goes to buybacks — $1.35B in FY2026 — rather than acquisitions. For Capri, now valued at $2.12B, the outcome was far worse.
