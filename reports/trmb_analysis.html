01 Company Overview

Trimble began in November 1978 as Trimble Navigation, a start-up founded by Charles Trimble and two Hewlett-Packard engineers that bought a navigation technology Hewlett-Packard had stopped developing — for $50,000. It rode GPS into surveying, construction machine control and farm guidance, and then spent the last decade trying to become something the market pays more for: a recurring-revenue software company for the people who design, build and move physical things. The tape measure became a data platform. Today it reports three segments — AECO (architects, engineers, construction and owners: SketchUp, Tekla, Viewpoint), Field Systems (the GNSS receivers, total stations, scanners and machine-control kits) and Transportation & Logistics (Transporeon and the freight software stack). Trailing-twelve-month revenue is $3.85B, up 6.9%; ARR reached a record $2.56B at the end of Q3 2026, up 11% (10% organic, meaning excluding acquisitions).

The portfolio was rebuilt in plain sight. Trimble contributed its agriculture business to a joint venture with AGCO (PTx Trimble, closed April 1, 2024 — Trimble kept 15%), booking a $1.69B gain on divestitures that pushed FY2024 GAAP net income to $1.50B, or $6.09 a share, against $2.85 non-GAAP. It then sold the Mobility telematics business to Platform Science for an equity stake, closing February 8, 2025 — which is why FY2025 revenue fell 2.6% to $3.59B while the business that remained grew. Then came 2026. For two quarters the operating numbers kept beating — Q1 revenue up 12% organic, Q2 up 10% organic, guidance raised in August — while the stock fell from a $78.35 December close to $51.18 at the end of June. In Q2 the gap between the two stories became an accounting entry: a $562.0M goodwill impairment (a write-down of the premium paid for past acquisitions) on Transportation & Logistics, which the company attributed to its own falling market capitalisation and "lower market multiples for software businesses." That charge is why trailing EPS is −$0.70, and why a trailing P/E does not exist for this stock right now. On the same day it reported the charge, management put T&L itself up for a strategic review.

Q3 is where the operating story slipped. Revenue and adjusted EPS landed inside guidance, but on November 4 management cut its full-year ARR growth forecast to about 10%, from 12–14%, naming slower demand for satellite-positioning services in farming (South America especially), fewer construction customers renewing the positioning subscriptions bundled with their hardware, a soft European freight market and the planned exit from a low-margin resold product. The stock had climbed back to $65.61 the day before; it closed at $54.25, down 17.3%. The question the price is asking: is Trimble a hardware business that rises and falls with the economy but is valued like software, or a steadily growing software business being priced like a hardware one?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Installed base of survey & machine-control hardware Caterpillar JV (since 2002) Construction ERP switching cost (Viewpoint) SketchUp user base Connected field-to-office data Freight network effects (Transporeon) 71.6% gross margin

The moat is the job site's workflow. A surveyor's receiver, the grade-control kit on the dozer, the model the engineer built in Tekla and the contractor's accounting in Viewpoint all speak to each other — and the bet behind "Connect & Scale" is that each connection makes the next piece harder to replace. The evidence it is working sits in the gross margin, which climbed from 58.0% in FY2021 to 71.6% on a trailing basis (StockAnalysis figures) as low-margin hardware lines left and software grew. The vulnerability is that connection is not the same as lock-in: construction software is contested by well-funded pure plays, the hardware side still sells into equipment budgets that rise and fall with the economy, and Q3 showed that bundled subscriptions do not all renew.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The growth number the stock is priced on just fell. Full-year ARR growth went from a 12–14% forecast in August to about 10% in November. Part of that is a chosen product exit; the rest is market softness in farm positioning, weaker renewals and European freight, which management cannot simply decide away.
02
Software valuations are lower — and Trimble said so itself. The company's own explanation of the $562.0M impairment was a "sustained decline in market capitalization and stock price reflecting heightened macroeconomic uncertainty and lower market multiples for software businesses." When a company writes the market's lower valuation of software into its accounts, the market is not imagining it.
03
The biggest segment still sells mostly hardware. Field Systems is 45.1% of Q3 revenue but only about 15% of ARR. Bookings strength rests on data-centre, factory and energy work; if that build-out slows, the biggest segment has little subscription cushion under it.
04
AI could shrink the number of paid seats. Much of AECO software is priced per user. If AI tools let a smaller design team do the same work, seat counts fall. Management's answer is hybrid licence-plus-usage pricing, but on its own account the near-term priority is adoption, not revenue.
05
A current ratio below 1. Current ratio 0.93 (short-term assets against short-term bills), quick ratio 0.57 (the same, leaving out inventory), net debt (borrowings minus cash) $1.32B. Not alarming for a business with $798M of trailing free cash flow and a leverage ratio the CFO puts at 1.2x, but there is less slack than the margin profile suggests.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items make Trimble's GAAP history nearly unreadable without adjustment. FY2024: a $1,687.9M gain on divestitures, primarily the agriculture contribution to the AGCO joint venture, lifted GAAP EPS to $6.09 against $2.85 non-GAAP. FY2025: the Mobility sale (closed February 8, 2025) removed revenue, so reported revenue fell 2.6% while the continuing business grew. Q2 2026: the $562.0M non-cash goodwill impairment on Transportation & Logistics turned a quarter with $200.3M of non-GAAP net income into a $471.7M GAAP loss; the November guidance now puts the full-year GAAP tax rate between 372.6 and 618.3 percent. None of these touch cash. The GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap outside those items is also large — Q3 GAAP operating margin 14.7% versus 27.2% adjusted — so the reader should decide how much of the adjustments they are willing to accept. Coming in fiscal 2027: the fiscal calendar has no January 1, which moves term-licence revenue into fiscal 2028, a headwind of about $90M to reported revenue and operating income, with no effect on ARR or free cash flow (CFO, Q3 call).

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $85.87 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $54.11, TRMB lost 37.0% of its price; it pays no dividend, so that is also the total return. Over the same period SPY rose 61.4% and QQQ 66.6% on price. In those years the company sold its agriculture and telematics businesses, bought Transporeon, lifted gross margin from 58% to 72% and grew ARR to $2.56B. The shape is two full round trips: down from $87.19 (December 2021) to $46.40 (November 2023), back up to $83.89 (July 2025), and down again to $51.18 (June 2026), followed by a rebound to $64.94 at the end of October that the November 4 report mostly erased. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 14.7x this year's consensus earnings and 15.5x trailing free cash flow for a business whose recurring revenue still grew 10% organically in Q3, whose gross margin has climbed more than 13 points since FY2021, and which kept its full-year revenue midpoint even as it cut the ARR forecast. The $562.0M impairment is a non-cash reflection of the share price, not a hole in the business. Part of the ARR cut is a deliberate exit from a low-margin product. A T&L sale at a decent price would turn the segment the market worries about into cash, leave a simpler AECO-plus-field company, and fund more of the buyback that has already cut the share count 3.81% in a year. Every one of the 13 analysts' targets on StockAnalysis sits above the price.

Bear Case

Trimble cut its ARR growth forecast from 12–14% to about 10% within three months, and only part of that was its own choice: farm positioning, subscription renewals and European freight all came in short. Software peers rallied while the stock fell. Trimble told you itself why the stock was already down: investors were paying less for software businesses. If AI puts pressure on per-seat design software, the valuation can fall further without earnings missing. Nearly half the revenue is Field Systems, sold largely as hardware into construction and infrastructure budgets; the current ratio is below 1 and net debt is $1.32B. Fiscal 2027 carries a calendar headwind of about $90M to reported revenue and operating income. The GAAP record is a string of one-offs — a $1.69B gain one year, a $562.0M write-down two years later — which makes the "real" earnings power a matter of whose adjustments you accept. And a strategic review with "no predetermined outcome" can end in no sale at all.

Top 3 Risks

01
Slower recurring growth. ARR growth is now guided at about 10%, down from 12–14%. The CFO said he does not expect a further slowdown next year; a miss on that would land directly on the number the stock is valued on.
02
Hardware cycle in Field Systems. 45.1% of Q3 revenue but only about 15% of ARR. Bookings strength leans on data-centre, factory and energy demand, which can rise and fall and is concentrated in a few sectors, and the segment's margin fell to 29.5% from 33.4% a year earlier.
03
Execution on the T&L review and the product exit. A failed or low-priced sale, or a longer-than-flagged ARR drag from replacing the resold product, would both land in the metrics the bull case depends on.
Industry Commentary
The reset in software valuations
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Through the first half, Trimble's 2026 was a case study in the market marking software down as a category. Organic revenue growth of 12% and then 10%, record ARR and a rising EBITDA margin did not stop the stock losing a third of its value between December and June, and the accounting rules then forced the company to write $562.0M off T&L goodwill because its market value no longer supported the value carried on its balance sheet. The market's working thesis, as the impairment note puts it, is simply "lower market multiples for software businesses." Q3 added a company-specific problem: ARR growth guided down while Autodesk, Bentley and Procore all rose between the September and November closes used in this report. For Trimble the question is sharper than for a pure software name: about a third of Q3 revenue came from product sales such as hardware ($324.3M of $966.8M) and the largest segment sells mostly hardware, so part of what it was paid for was the promise of becoming more of a software company. If software itself earns a lower valuation, that promise is worth less.

Freight: four years down, "initial green shoots"
ONGOING HEADWIND

The T&L segment has lived through what Painter called "four years of a freight recession." In August he said Trimble was "seeing initial green shoots," pointing to rising spot rates (the price of a one-off truck shipment) and more carriers turning down contracted loads, known as tender rejections (FreightWaves, Aug 12, 2026). By November the wording had changed to "the European freight market remains somewhat mixed," with higher diesel prices and weak European growth in the background (FreightWaves, Nov 4, 2026). T&L ARR growth slowed to 5% from 7%, though its margin still widened about half a point. The timing of the strategic review matters: selling a freight-software business near the bottom of the freight cycle hands the upside to the buyer; waiting risks the inbound interest going cold.

Building the physical economy: data centres and infrastructure
EMERGING TAILWIND

On the Q3 call management again pointed to bookings strength from data-centre, factory-reshoring and energy projects — the physical side of the AI build-out. That is the counterweight to the AI risk in design software: the same spending that could reduce per-seat pricing is pouring concrete and laying cable, and every one of those sites needs positioning, surveying and machine control. The bull framing: Trimble is paid on both sides of AI — in the tools and in the build. The bear framing: the hardware side depends on one spending cycle, and the software side is exposed to the technology driving that cycle. Field Systems growth and AECO seat counts over the next four quarters will show which framing holds.
