01 Company Overview

Tractor Supply calls itself the largest rural lifestyle retailer in the United States. It sells the things a small farm, a horse barn, a backyard flock and a dog on a long driveway go through every week — livestock and equine feed, companion-animal products, seasonal goods, truck and hardware items, and workwear. At the end of fiscal 2025 it ran 2,602 stores in 49 states (2,395 Tractor Supply plus 207 Petsense by Tractor Supply). FY2025 net sales were $15.52B, up 4.3%, with comparable store sales up 1.2% and diluted EPS of $2.06, up 0.9%.

The stock is where the story turns. TSCO closed August 2025 at $61.76, the highest month-end in this report's five-year window. It closed Monday, September 21, 2026 at $32.33 — down 47.7% from that month-end and 35.4% since December 31, 2025. Nothing blew up. What happened is slower and, for a retailer valued on dependable compounding, worse: Q1 2026 missed on earnings (the stock fell 22.5% in April), and on July 23, 2026 management cut full-year guidance — comparable sales from +1% to +3% down to −1% to flat, GAAP diluted EPS from $2.13–$2.23 to $1.78–$1.88 — and withdrew its long-term financial framework, citing "current market softening in farm, ranch, and rural economies." The bet the market is now pricing is simple: is rural America's wallet in a cyclical dip, or has the growth runway for this store format shortened for good?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Rural store footprint (2,602 stores, 49 states) Needs-based, repeat-purchase feed & pet Owned & exclusive brands (29.0% of Q2 sales) 7.8M sq ft distribution network In-store vet clinics (VIP Petcare) Heavy, bulky goods favour local stores

The moat is geography plus weight. A 50-pound bag of feed is expensive to ship to a farm at the end of a gravel road, and the customer needs it again next week. Tractor Supply's stores sit in the towns where that customer lives, which is why feed and pet — 51% of sales — held up even as the rest softened. Owned and exclusive brands rose to 29.0% of Q2 2026 sales from 27.6% a year earlier, which supports gross margin (37.1% in Q2, up from 36.9%). The vulnerability is the other half of the bar: seasonal, tools and clothing ride on rural discretionary income, and that is what fell.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Costs grow faster than sales. In Q2 2026 SG&A rose 14.4% to 26.8% of sales (adjusted: +7.3%, 25.1%) against net sales up 2.3%. Operating income fell 19.2% (adjusted −5.1%). The TTM operating margin is 9.03%, down from 10.26% in FY2021, and the updated guide is 8.0–8.3%.
02
The rural economy is the whole customer base. Management withdrew its long-term framework citing "current market softening in farm, ranch, and rural economies." A retailer this concentrated in one kind of customer has no urban or international business to offset it.
03
Traffic is falling. Comparable transactions fell 1.0% in Q1 and 1.7% in Q2 2026. Ticket gains of 1.6% and 0.2% are no longer covering the lost visits.
04
Thin free cash flow after heavy capex. TTM operating cash flow of $1.29B funded $978.8M of capital spending, leaving $307M of free cash flow — less than the ~$500M a year the company pays in dividends. Debt (including leases, per StockAnalysis) is $6.54B against $231.6M of cash.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $40.52 (split-adjusted) to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $32.33, TSCO lost 20.2% of its price over five years. For four of those years it looked like a textbook compounder: a 2022 dip to $37.03, then a steady climb to a September 2024 month-end of $58.19, a 5-for-1 split in December 2024, and a five-year high month-end of $61.76 in August 2025. Everything since has been one direction. The stock ended 2025 at $50.01 after a disappointing Q4, held around $51 into February 2026, then fell 12.6% in March, 22.5% in April and another 10.2% in May. The July 2026 month-end of $30.77 was the lowest of the window.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how unusual that was. It sat below 30 from March through July 2026, bottoming at 15.1 in July — more oversold than any reading in the prior four years (the 2023 low was 29.5). August's 13.4% bounce lifted it to 33.0; September's slide has taken it back to 28.5. The 10-month moving average is about $39.43, 22% above the price: the long-term trend is still down, and the bounce did not get close to it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Half of what Tractor Supply sells — feed and pet — is bought because an animal has to eat, and those "CUE" categories kept growing through the worst of 2026. Gross margin is still rising (36.5% TTM, owned brands 29.0% of sales), the company still earns a 12.8% ROIC, and the damage is concentrated in weather- and fuel-sensitive discretionary categories that have recovered before. At $32.33 the stock trades at about 16.8x trailing and FY2026 consensus EPS — a large discount to where it traded as a compounder — with a 2.97% yield. The company itself was buying stock at an average $40.86 in the first half. If comps return to positive once May 2026 laps and VIP Petcare lifts pet traffic, the 30-analyst average target of $35.48 is not a stretch.

Bear Case

This is not one bad month. Transactions fell in both Q1 and Q2 2026, operating margin has slid from 10.26% in FY2021 to 9.03% TTM and is guided to 8.0–8.3%, and management withdrew its long-term framework because the farm, ranch and rural economy is softening. Growth now depends on new stores (3.6% of Q2 sales) at the same time as the company cuts 2027 openings to 85–90 and closes 75 Petsense stores. Free cash flow of $307M TTM does not cover the dividend, debt including leases is $6.54B, and 8.33% of shares are sold short. At ~17x earnings that are falling, the stock is not obviously cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
A rural downturn, not a weather blip. If farm and ranch incomes keep softening, discretionary categories (roughly half of sales) stay weak beyond 2026, and the new long-term framework due with Q4 results resets growth expectations lower.
02
Margin deleverage. With comps negative, fixed store costs grow faster than sales: Q2 2026 SG&A rose 14.4% (7.3% adjusted) on 2.3% sales growth. Each further guidance step-down comes from this line.
03
Capital allocation under strain. TTM free cash flow of $307M against roughly $500M of annual dividends plus buybacks means the gap is funded by the balance sheet. Capex cuts or a slower buyback would be the first signal; a dividend change would be a large one.
Industry Commentary
The rural economy softens
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Tractor Supply's own words carry the diagnosis. In Q2 2026 it pointed to "discrete headwinds impacting the majority of our end markets," while stressing that "needs-based businesses continue to perform well." The earnings-call summary names May's high fuel prices and a Southeast drought, and the long-term framework was withdrawn because of "current market softening in farm, ranch, and rural economies." For a retailer whose customer is defined by where they live, that is not a sector headwind; it is the entire market.

From store rollout to ecosystem
STRATEGY IN TRANSITION

For decades the growth formula was simple: open about a hundred stores a year (99 in FY2025) and let comps add a point or two. Management is now trimming the store pipeline (85–90 openings in 2027), closing about 75 Petsense stores, and putting capital into vet services (VIP Petcare), the Allivet pharmacy, Final Mile delivery and Project Fusion remodels. The logic is to earn more from each existing customer rather than chase new towns. It is a sensible response to slower growth, and it also admits that the old formula has run into limits.

What the price already assumes
VALUATION RESET

At $32.33, TSCO trades at 16.8x trailing EPS, 12.0x EV/EBITDA and 1.07x sales, with a 2.97% dividend yield. The bull reading: the market has priced in a permanent slowdown after a weather-driven quarter, while gross margins, ROIC and the needs-based half of the business are intact. The bear reading: earnings are still being revised down (FY2026 consensus $1.93 vs $2.06 in FY2025), so today's multiple is on a shrinking base. Watch comparable transactions: two more quarters of declines settle the argument for the bears; a return to positive traffic settles it the other way.
