01 Company Overview

Tractor Supply calls itself the largest rural lifestyle retailer in the United States. It sells the things a small farm, a horse barn, a backyard flock and a dog on a long driveway go through every week — livestock and equine feed, companion-animal products, seasonal goods, truck and hardware items, and workwear. At the end of fiscal 2025 it ran 2,602 stores in 49 states (2,395 Tractor Supply plus 207 Petsense by Tractor Supply). FY2025 net sales were $15.52B, up 4.3%, with comparable store sales up 1.2% and diluted EPS of $2.06, up 0.9%.

The stock is where the story turns. TSCO closed August 2025 at $61.76, the highest month-end in this report's five-year window. It closed Friday, October 23, 2026 at $33.42 — down 45.9% from that month-end and 33.2% since December 31, 2025. Nothing blew up. What happened is slower and, for a retailer valued on dependable compounding, worse: Q1 2026 missed on earnings (the stock fell 22.5% in April), and on July 23, 2026 management cut full-year guidance — comparable sales from +1% to +3% down to −1% to flat, GAAP diluted EPS from $2.13–$2.23 to $1.78–$1.88 — and withdrew its long-term financial framework "given the revised 2026 outlook," as the Q2 release put it. On the call CEO Hal Lawton said about 40% of the company's addressable market is tied to farm, ranch and rural economies, where customers face high fuel costs, persistent drought and more cautious spending (MarketBeat transcript). The bet the market is now pricing is simple: is rural America's wallet in a cyclical dip, or has the growth runway for this store format shortened for good?

The third quarter, reported on October 22, gave the first evidence for the dip reading. Net sales rose 5.1% to $3.91B and comparable store sales turned positive again (+0.4%), improving month by month from negative in July to stronger in September, per the release. Diluted EPS of $0.43 was down from $0.49 a year earlier but above the $0.40 Nasdaq consensus, and management held, rather than raised, its reduced full-year guidance. The cost was margin: gross margin fell to 36.4% from 37.4%, which the company put down to higher transportation and fuel costs and spending to keep its prices competitive. Traffic was still slightly negative (transactions −0.3%). The stock rose 6.24% on results day and gave back 3.77% the next.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Rural store footprint (2,621 stores, 49 states) Needs-based, repeat-purchase feed & pet Owned & exclusive brands (29.0% of Q2 sales) 7.8M sq ft distribution network (FY2025) In-store vet clinics (VIP Petcare) Heavy, bulky goods favour local stores

The moat is geography plus weight. A 50-pound bag of feed is expensive to ship to a farm at the end of a gravel road, and the customer needs it again next week. Tractor Supply's stores sit in the towns where that customer lives, which is why feed and pet — 51% of sales — held up even as the rest softened. Owned and exclusive brands rose to 29.0% of Q2 2026 sales from 27.6% a year earlier, and Q2 gross margin rose to 37.1% from 36.9%, which the release credits to product cost management and tariff-related benefits. Q3 showed the limit: gross margin fell to 36.4% from 37.4% as transportation and fuel costs rose and the company spent on keeping prices competitive. The vulnerability is the other half of the bar: seasonal, tools and clothing ride on rural discretionary income, and that is what fell.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Costs grow faster than sales. In Q3 2026 SG&A rose 6.8% to 28.6% of sales against net sales up 5.1%, and operating income fell 10.2% to $307.7M; the release names smaller comparable-sales gains and start-up costs at a new distribution center. In Q2 SG&A had risen 14.4% (adjusted 7.3%) on 2.3% sales growth. The TTM operating margin is 8.59%, down from 10.26% in FY2021, and the guide is 8.0–8.3%.
02
The rural economy is the whole customer base. On the Q2 call CEO Hal Lawton said about 40% of the company's addressable market is tied to farm, ranch and rural economies, where customers face high fuel costs, persistent drought and more cautious spending (MarketBeat transcript). A retailer this concentrated in one kind of customer has no urban or international business to offset it.
03
Traffic is still falling. Comparable transactions fell 1.0% in Q1, 1.7% in Q2 and 0.3% in Q3 2026. Ticket gains of 1.6%, 0.2% and 0.7% only just covered the lost visits in Q3, and did not in Q2.
04
Thin free cash flow after heavy capex. TTM operating cash flow of $1.21B funded $917.2M of capital spending, leaving $290M of free cash flow (StockAnalysis TTM) — less than the ~$500M a year the company pays in dividends ($124.9M in Q3). Debt (including leases, per StockAnalysis) is $6.72B against $198.9M of cash.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $43.43 (split-adjusted) to the October 23, 2026 settled close of $33.42, TSCO lost 23.1% of its price over five years. For four of those years it looked like a textbook compounder: a 2022 dip to $37.03, then a steady climb to a September 2024 month-end of $58.19, a 5-for-1 split in December 2024, and a five-year high month-end of $61.76 in August 2025. Everything since has been one direction. The stock ended 2025 at $50.01, held around $51 into February 2026, then fell 12.6% in March, 22.5% in April and another 10.2% in May. The July 2026 month-end of $30.77 was the lowest of the window.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how unusual that was. It sat below 30 from March through July 2026, bottoming at 15.1 in July — more oversold than any reading in the prior four years (the 2023 low was 29.2). August's 13.4% bounce lifted it to 33.0, September's 10.3% slide took it back to 27.0, and the post-earnings rise has it at 35.3 on the October 23 close. The 10-month moving average is about $37.66, 12.7% above the price: the long-term trend is still down, and neither bounce has reached it.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Half of what Tractor Supply sells — feed and pet — is bought because an animal has to eat, and those "CUE" categories ran above the company average again in Q3 2026. Q3 also showed the discretionary half can turn: comparable sales went from negative in July to positive in September, sales rose 5.1%, EPS beat, and CEO Hal Lawton told the call the momentum had continued into October (Investing.com transcript). The company still earns a 12.0% ROIC. At $33.42 the stock trades at about 18.0x trailing EPS and 17.1x FY2026 consensus — a large discount to where it traded as a compounder — with a 2.87% yield, and the company kept buying its own stock (2.7M shares in Q3). If comps stay positive as May 2026 laps and VIP Petcare lifts pet traffic, the 30-analyst average target of $37.33 is not a stretch.

Bear Case

One positive quarter is not a turnaround. Transactions fell in each of the first three quarters of 2026, gross margin fell a full point in Q3 on freight, fuel and price investment, operating margin has slid from 10.26% in FY2021 to 8.59% TTM and is guided to 8.0–8.3%, and the long-term framework is still withdrawn. Management held its guidance after the beat rather than raising it. The company is cutting 2027 openings to 85–90 and closed 74 Petsense stores in Q3. Free cash flow of $290M TTM does not cover the dividend, debt including leases is $6.72B, and 8.30% of shares were sold short at the September 30 settlement. At 18x earnings that fell 10% over the last year, the stock is not obviously cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
A rural downturn, not a weather blip. If farm and ranch incomes keep softening, discretionary categories (roughly half of sales) stay weak beyond 2026, and the updated long-term framework management has promised resets growth expectations lower.
02
Margin deleverage. With comps negative, fixed store costs grow faster than sales: Q2 2026 SG&A rose 14.4% (7.3% adjusted) on 2.3% sales growth, and Q3 SG&A rose 6.8% on 5.1%. Each further guidance step-down comes from this line.
03
Capital allocation under strain. TTM free cash flow of $290M against roughly $500M of annual dividends, before buybacks ($344.9M in the first nine months of 2026), means the gap is funded by the balance sheet. Capex cuts or a slower buyback would be the first signal; a dividend change would be a large one.
Industry Commentary
The rural economy softens
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Tractor Supply's own words carry the diagnosis. In Q2 2026 it pointed to "discrete headwinds impacting the majority of our end markets," while stressing that "needs-based businesses continue to perform well." On the Q2 call CEO Hal Lawton named fuel prices that peaked in the spring selling season and drought in key southeastern markets, and the company withdrew its long-term framework "given the revised 2026 outlook." In Q3 the softness eased — comparable sales turned positive — but higher transportation and fuel costs were the main drag on gross margin, per the release. For a retailer whose customer is defined by where they live, that is not a sector headwind; it is the entire market.

From store rollout to ecosystem
STRATEGY IN TRANSITION

For decades the growth formula was simple: open about a hundred stores a year (99 in FY2025) and let comps add a point or two. Management is now trimming the store pipeline (85–90 openings in 2027), closing Petsense stores (74 in Q3 2026), and putting capital into vet services (VIP Petcare), the Allivet pharmacy, Final Mile delivery and Project Fusion remodels. The logic is to earn more from each existing customer rather than chase new towns. It is a sensible response to slower growth, and it also admits that the old formula has run into limits.

What the price already assumes
VALUATION RESET

At $33.42, TSCO trades at 18.0x trailing EPS, 12.7x EV/EBITDA and 1.09x sales (StockAnalysis for the last two), with a 2.87% dividend yield. The bull reading: the market has priced in a permanent slowdown after a weather-driven quarter, while gross margins, ROIC and the needs-based half of the business are intact. The bear reading: earnings are still falling (TTM EPS −10.1%; FY2026 consensus $1.95, inside the adjusted guidance range, vs $2.06 in FY2025), so today's multiple is on a shrinking base. Watch comparable transactions: down in each of the first three quarters of 2026 (−1.0%, −1.7%, −0.3%). Two more quarters of declines settle the argument for the bears; a return to positive traffic settles it the other way.
