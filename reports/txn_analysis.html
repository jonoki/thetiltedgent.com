01 Company Overview

Texas Instruments designs, manufactures and sells analog and embedded processing semiconductors — the power-management, signal-chain and microcontroller chips that sit inside industrial equipment, cars, phones, data-center racks and appliances. It is the world's largest analog chipmaker by revenue, with an estimated ~19% share of the global analog market, and it owns and operates the overwhelming majority of its own fabrication and assembly/test capacity — an unusually vertically-integrated model in an industry that has largely gone fab-lite.

FY2025 revenue was $17.68B, up 13.1% YoY, the first growth year after a brutal two-year analog inventory correction (revenue fell −12.5% in 2023 and a further −10.7% in 2024, from a $20.0B peak in 2022). The recovery has kept accelerating through 2026: trailing-twelve-month revenue is now $20.77B (+20.3%), and Q3 FY26 revenue of $6.06B grew 28% YoY (11% on the quarter before), with gross margin at 64.2% and diluted EPS of $2.62, up 77%. CEO Haviv Ilan credited "continued broad growth in industrial, automotive and data center" in the Q3 release.

The biggest change to the company itself is a purchase. On February 4, 2026 TI agreed to buy Silicon Labs, a maker of wireless-connectivity chips, for $231 a share in cash, an enterprise value (price of the shares plus net debt) of about $7.5B. Silicon Labs' shareholders approved the deal on April 30 and the US antitrust waiting period expired on May 22; TI's Q3 10-Q (filed Oct 23, 2026) still expects closing in the first half of 2027, subject to the remaining regulatory approvals, funded with cash on hand and new debt.

Analog splits into two product families: Power (DC/DC converters, power-management ICs, battery chargers, gate drivers, e-fuses) and Signal Chain (amplifiers, data converters, interface, sensing, clocks). These are the chips that convert, condition and move real-world electrical signals — they do not shrink onto leading-edge nodes, they carry 20-year product lifetimes, and a typical car or factory-automation cabinet contains hundreds of them from dozens of vendors. TI sells more than 80,000 catalogue parts, roughly 80% of them through its own direct/online channel, giving it an unusually granular real-time read on demand. Analog is the margin engine: mature, largely depreciated 200mm capacity plus new 300mm output supports corporate gross margins that reached 64.2% in Q3 FY26 versus a 55.9% trough in Q4 FY25; Analog's own operating margin was 51.0% in Q3 ($2.51B of operating profit on $4.91B). 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
80,000+ PART CATALOGUE OWNED 300MM FABS LOWEST COST/DIE DIRECT SALES ~80% 20-YEAR DESIGN CYCLES US-BASED SUPPLY CHAIN CHIPS ACT SUBSIDIES

TI's moat is manufacturing cost, not technology. Analog chips are designed on trailing-edge nodes where performance is set by circuit design and process maturity rather than transistor density, so the durable advantage goes to whoever produces the most die per dollar. TI's answer has been to move analog production to 300mm wafers — which yield roughly 2.3× the die of a 200mm wafer for well under 2× the cost, a ~40% unpackaged cost-per-chip advantage — while nearly every peer either stayed on 200mm or outsourced. Layer on internal assembly and test (most peers outsource), a catalogue of over 80,000 parts sold ~80% direct through ti.com with same-day shipping, and design wins that stay in production for 15–20 years in a car platform or factory controller, and switching costs become high in a market where each individual part is cheap but requalification is expensive. The 2022–2026 capex program (Sherman, Texas SM1/SM2; Richardson RFAB2; Lehi, Utah) plus CHIPS Act support — up to ~$1.6B in direct federal funding alongside a 25%-plus federal investment tax credit on qualifying US fab spend — is designed to extend that cost gap through 2030. It also makes TI one of the few analog vendors that can credibly promise a geopolitically-insulated, US-based supply chain to defense, aerospace and automotive customers.

Chinese domestic analog competition. China consumes roughly a third of global analog output and has poured state capital into domestic suppliers — SG Micro, 3Peak, Silergy, Will Semiconductor and dozens of others — explicitly targeting TI's catalogue. These entrants compete on price in commodity power-management and standard signal-chain parts, and Beijing's procurement guidance actively steers Chinese OEMs toward local sourcing. TI itself cut prices aggressively in 2023–24 to defend share, which is a meaningful part of why gross margin fell from 68.8% in 2022 to 55.9% at the Q4 FY25 trough. Even in the current upcycle, this is a permanent structural cap on pricing power in the low end.

Cyclicality and inventory whiplash. TI has no subscription revenue and no backlog visibility worth the name — it sells catalogue parts into distribution and direct accounts whose order patterns amplify end-demand swings. Revenue fell 23% peak-to-trough (FY2022 $20.03B → FY2024 $15.64B) and diluted EPS fell 45% ($9.41 → $5.20) over the same span, while the company kept spending heavily on fabs ($5.07B of capex in FY2023 and $4.82B in FY2024). The current recovery is real, but a business whose factory costs are mostly fixed sees profits, and usually the share price, fall hard if the industrial/auto restock proves to be orders pulled forward rather than a genuine demand cycle.

Capital intensity and the depreciation overhang. The 300mm buildout has left TI with a fixed-asset base that must be depreciated whether or not it is loaded. Annual capex peaked at $5.07B in FY2023, ran $4.82B in FY2024 and $4.55B in FY2025, then fell to $2.58B over the twelve months to September 2026. Depreciation continues to step up for several years after the spending stops, so gross margin only benefits from the new capacity if utilization stays high. If the cycle rolls over before the fabs fill, TI carries the worst of both worlds — high depreciation and low volume.

Automotive concentration and content risk. Roughly a third of revenue is automotive, a market simultaneously exposed to global vehicle-unit softness, tariff regimes on cross-border vehicle and component flows, and an EV transition whose pace keeps being revised. Chinese EV makers — the fastest-growing part of the market — are also the most aggressive adopters of domestic Chinese analog. A slower Western EV ramp combined with Chinese OEMs designing out Western silicon compresses TI's single best content-growth story.

Notable One-Off Items

Four distortions matter when reading TI's reported numbers. First, CHIPS Act investment tax credits: TI qualifies for the US Section 48D advanced manufacturing investment credit on qualifying domestic fab spend and uses it to reduce the income tax it pays (a line of its own in the Q3 10-Q cash-flow statement), so its cash taxes run below what an unsubsidized manufacturer would pay; the direct CHIPS grant award (up to ~$1.6B) is separate and flows in as milestones are achieved. Second, elevated capital expenditure and the resulting depreciation step-up: capex of $5.07B at its FY2023 peak ($4.82B in FY2024) fell to $2.58B over the twelve months to September 2026, but depreciation continues to rise for several years afterward — so FY2026 gross margin expansion is being achieved despite a growing fixed-cost base, and TI's free cash flow ($8.42B over twelve months) also includes $1.10B of CHIPS Act incentive cash that will not recur at that pace forever. Third, the payout ratio (dividends per share over EPS, our calculation) is 73.7% on trailing figures ($5.68 ÷ $7.71); on the new $6.08 annual rate and FY26 EPS at the midpoint of TI's Q4 range ($6.44 for nine months plus $2.62, so $9.06) it is about 67%. Fourth, Q3 FY26 carried $7M of acquisition charges for the pending Silicon Labs purchase ($41M over the first nine months of 2026, per the 10-Q), and the release says EPS included a 1-cent reduction that was not in TI's original guidance. ⚠ TI has periodically taken restructuring and site-closure charges (notably in Embedded and in legacy 150mm/200mm facilities). Five-year trajectory. TXN closed October 2021 at $187.48 and closed October 23, 2026 at $284.69 — a +51.9% price gain over five years (closing prices, dividends not included). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The analog cycle has bottomed and TI is the highest-quality company tied to it, with three straight quarters of faster revenue growth (+18.6%, +22.8%, then +27.7%) and gross margin up 830bps from the Q4 FY25 trough to 64.2%. The 300mm buildout that crushed free cash flow for four years is past its peak — twelve-month capex is down to $2.58B from the $5.07B peak in FY2023 — and the free-cash-flow inflection has arrived: $8.42B over twelve months on TI's definition, about $9.22 a share. Data-center power chips add a growth line the old cycle never had. At 24.9× forward earnings with a 0.89 PEG, 30.1% ROIC and a 2.0% dividend just raised for the 23rd straight year, an investor is paying a reasonable price for the lowest-cost producer in a $90B market with rising chip content per car, per factory and per server rack.

Bear Case

Everything good is already in the price. The stock nearly doubled in six months, trades at 36.9× trailing earnings, and fell after each of its last two beats (−3.1% in July, −1.8% in October): buyers who wanted in are already in. Fifteen of 36 analysts are on Hold, and Morgan Stanley's Underweight target of $278 sits below the price. Free cash flow is flattered by $1.10B of CHIPS Act incentive cash that will not last, $7.5B of cash is about to go to Silicon Labs, partly borrowed, and the depreciation from the new fabs keeps stepping up for years after the capex stops, so margins depend on the factories staying busy. Meanwhile state-backed Chinese analog vendors are permanently capping pricing in the low end, roughly a third of revenue sits in an automotive market facing tariffs and an uncertain EV ramp, and the current recovery may be a channel restock rather than genuine end demand — in which case the 2027 comparison is brutal against a fixed-cost base built for a much larger company.

01
The restock proves to be a pull-forward. TI sells catalogue parts into distribution, where order patterns amplify end demand in both directions. If industrial and automotive customers have been rebuilding safety stock rather than consuming, 2027 revenue faces a comparison against inflated 2026 shipments — and because TI's factory costs are mostly fixed, a modest revenue miss turns into a large earnings miss, exactly as it did from 2022 to 2025.
02
Free cash flow fails to inflect. The bull case depends on capex staying near the current $2.6B a year while depreciation is still rising and revenue compounds. Any decision to accelerate Sherman SM3/SM4, any change to CHIPS Act funding or the federal investment tax credit, or any utilization shortfall breaks the FCF-per-share math — and with a trailing payout ratio of 74% of earnings and new acquisition debt to service, the dividend growth streak that anchors the shareholder base becomes the pressure point.
03
Chinese share loss becomes structural rather than cyclical. SG Micro, 3Peak, Silergy and peers are moving up from commodity power management into signal chain with state capital and domestic procurement mandates behind them. TI already cut prices hard in 2023–24 to defend share. If China permanently designs out Western analog in its industrial and EV supply chains, TI loses both the volume and the pricing umbrella that makes the 300mm cost advantage worth having.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Drives This Stock
The Analog Cycle Has Bottomed — Probably
EMERGING TAILWIND

The analog semiconductor cycle that began rolling over in mid-2022 was the longest and deepest in twenty years: TI's revenue fell 23% peak-to-trough ($20.03B FY2022 → $15.64B FY2024) and gross margin fell 1,287bps (68.76% FY2022 → 55.89% in Q4 FY25). The evidence that it has ended is now in the numbers rather than the commentary — three straight quarters of sequential revenue growth ($4.42B in Q4 FY25, then $4.83B, $5.46B and $6.06B), YoY growth of +18.6%, +22.8% and +27.7% in those three quarters, and gross margin recovering 830bps over the same span. Peers pointed the same way at the last edition (StockAnalysis, Aug 13, 2026: ADI's TTM revenue +29.8%, NXP +8.8%, STMicro +10.5%). The important caveat is that analog upcycles historically run three to four years from trough, but they also historically overshoot on the way up as distributors rebuild inventory — meaning 2026 growth rates are almost certainly not the sustainable run-rate. The right way to model this is not "28% growth continues" but "the through-cycle revenue base has reset higher, and margins settle in the low-to-mid 60s."

Chinese Domestic Analog: A Permanent Ceiling on Pricing
ONGOING HEADWIND

China is roughly a third of global analog consumption and has spent a decade building a domestic alternative. SG Micro, 3Peak, Silergy, Will Semiconductor and dozens of smaller firms now offer functional substitutes for large parts of TI's commodity power-management catalogue at materially lower prices, backed by state capital, preferential financing and explicit procurement guidance toward local sourcing. The effect on TI is already visible in the historical record: TI cut prices aggressively across 2023–24 to hold share, and that pricing action is part of why gross margin fell to 55.9%. Even with the cycle recovering, this is best understood as a structural change to the shape of the analog market — the low end is being commoditized and the returns will increasingly concentrate in high-performance signal chain, automotive-qualified parts and applications where a 20-year supply guarantee matters more than a 30% price delta. TI's 300mm cost advantage is the correct strategic response, but it is a defensive one.

Capex → Free Cash Flow: The Bet TI Made, Now Being Settled
DEFINING VARIABLE — BULL vs BEAR

Between 2023 and 2025 TI spent $14.4B ($4.55–5.07B a year) building 300mm analog capacity — Sherman TX (SM1/SM2, with SM3/SM4 shells), Richardson RFAB2 and Lehi, Utah — through the worst analog downturn in two decades, supported by up to ~$1.6B in CHIPS Act direct funding and a federal investment tax credit on qualifying US fab spend. The result was that free cash flow per share, the measure TI says it manages to, collapsed while the dividend kept rising. That bet is now being settled, and the Q3 FY26 numbers favour TI. The bull reading: twelve-month capex is down to $2.58B from a $5.07B peak (FY2023), revenue is compounding into the new capacity, gross margin printed 64.2% in Q3, and twelve-month free cash flow on TI's definition reached $8.42B, about $9.22 a share, comfortably above the $6.08 annual dividend at the new rate, while the cost gap versus 200mm peers widens. The bear reading: depreciation from those fabs steps up for several more years regardless of demand; $1.10B of that free cash flow was CHIPS Act incentive cash, and without it the figure is about $8.01 a share; the $7.5B Silicon Labs purchase will absorb cash and add debt; and if utilization slips even modestly, TI is left carrying a fixed-cost base sized for a larger company than its $20.8B of twelve-month revenue. At 36.9× trailing earnings the market has already voted for the bull reading, which is why a stumble would cost more than another beat would earn.
