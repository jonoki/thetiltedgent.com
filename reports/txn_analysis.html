01 Company Overview

Texas Instruments designs, manufactures and sells analog and embedded processing semiconductors — the power-management, signal-chain and microcontroller chips that sit inside industrial equipment, cars, phones, data-center racks and appliances. It is the world's largest analog chipmaker by revenue, with an estimated ~19% share of the global analog market, and it owns and operates the overwhelming majority of its own fabrication and assembly/test capacity — an unusually vertically-integrated model in an industry that has largely gone fab-lite.

FY2025 revenue was $17.68B, up 13.1% YoY, the first growth year after a brutal two-year analog inventory correction (revenue fell −12.5% in 2023 and a further −10.7% in 2024, from a $20.0B peak in 2022). The recovery has accelerated sharply in 2026: trailing-twelve-month revenue is now $19.45B (+16.7%), and Q2 FY26 revenue of $5.46B grew 22.8% YoY with gross margin back above 61%. TXN is the 48th-largest US public company and roughly the 69th-largest in the world by market capitalization.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
80,000+ PART CATALOGUE OWNED 300MM FABS LOWEST COST/DIE DIRECT SALES ~80% 20-YEAR DESIGN CYCLES US-BASED SUPPLY CHAIN CHIPS ACT SUBSIDIES

TI's moat is manufacturing cost, not technology. Analog chips are designed on trailing-edge nodes where performance is set by circuit design and process maturity rather than transistor density, so the durable advantage goes to whoever produces the most die per dollar. TI's answer has been to move analog production to 300mm wafers — which yield roughly 2.3× the die of a 200mm wafer for well under 2× the cost, a ~40% unpackaged cost-per-chip advantage — while nearly every peer either stayed on 200mm or outsourced. Layer on internal assembly and test (most peers outsource), a catalogue of over 80,000 parts sold ~80% direct through ti.com with same-day shipping, and design wins that stay in production for 15–20 years in a car platform or factory controller, and switching costs become high in a market where each individual part is cheap but requalification is expensive. The 2022–2026 capex program (Sherman, Texas SM1/SM2; Richardson RFAB2; Lehi, Utah) plus CHIPS Act support — up to ~$1.6B in direct federal funding awarded in late 2024 alongside a 25%-plus federal investment tax credit on qualifying US fab spend — is designed to extend that cost gap through 2030. It also makes TI one of the few analog vendors that can credibly promise a geopolitically-insulated, US-based supply chain to defense, aerospace and automotive customers.

Chinese domestic analog competition. China consumes roughly a third of global analog output and has poured state capital into domestic suppliers — SG Micro, 3Peak, Silergy, Will Semiconductor and dozens of others — explicitly targeting TI's catalogue. These entrants compete on price in commodity power-management and standard signal-chain parts, and Beijing's procurement guidance actively steers Chinese OEMs toward local sourcing. TI itself cut prices aggressively in 2023–24 to defend share, which is a meaningful part of why gross margin fell from 68.8% in 2022 to 55.9% at the Q4 FY25 trough. Even in the current upcycle, this is a permanent structural cap on pricing power in the low end.

Cyclicality and inventory whiplash. TI has no subscription revenue and no backlog visibility worth the name — it sells catalogue parts into distribution and direct accounts whose order patterns amplify end-demand swings. Revenue fell 23% peak-to-trough (FY2022 $20.03B → FY2024 $15.64B) and diluted EPS fell 45% ($9.41 → $5.20) over the same span, while the company kept spending ~$5B a year on fabs. The current recovery is real, but a business with 65%+ fixed-cost absorption exposure will de-rate violently if the industrial/auto restock proves to be a pull-forward rather than a genuine demand cycle.

Capital intensity and the depreciation overhang. The 300mm buildout has left TI with a fixed-asset base that must be depreciated whether or not it is loaded. Annual capex ran near $5.1B in FY2024 and remained elevated through FY2025 before moderating to $3.31B on a trailing-twelve-month basis. Depreciation continues to step up for several years after the spending stops, so gross margin only benefits from the new capacity if utilization stays high. If the cycle rolls over before the fabs fill, TI carries the worst of both worlds — high depreciation and low volume.

Automotive concentration and content risk. Roughly a third of revenue is automotive, a market simultaneously exposed to global vehicle-unit softness, tariff regimes on cross-border vehicle and component flows, and an EV transition whose pace keeps being revised. Chinese EV makers — the fastest-growing part of the market — are also the most aggressive adopters of domestic Chinese analog. A slower Western EV ramp combined with Chinese OEMs designing out Western silicon compresses TI's single best content-growth story.

Notable One-Off Items

Three distortions matter when reading TI's reported numbers. First, CHIPS Act investment tax credits: TI qualifies for the US Section 48D advanced manufacturing investment credit on qualifying domestic fab spend, and has been recognizing material amounts of credit each year against the carrying value of assets and against tax expense. This has the effect of suppressing reported D&A and boosting cash taxes relative to what an unsubsidized manufacturer would report; the direct CHIPS grant award (up to ~$1.6B, announced in late 2024) is separate and flows in as milestones are achieved. Second, elevated capital expenditure and the resulting depreciation step-up: capex of roughly $5.1B in FY2024 fell to a $3.31B trailing-twelve-month run-rate, but depreciation continues to rise for several years afterward — so FY2026 gross margin expansion is being achieved despite a growing fixed-cost base, and the FCF inflection will look larger than the earnings inflection. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The analog cycle has bottomed and TI is the highest-quality asset levered to it, with two consecutive quarters of accelerating revenue (+18.6%, then +22.8%) and gross margin recovering 548bps from the Q4 FY25 trough to 61.4%. The $60B+ 300mm buildout that crushed free cash flow for four years is now behind the peak — capex has already fallen to a $3.31B TTM run-rate from ~$5.1B — so revenue growth and capex decline compound into an FCF inflection: consensus models FY26 FCF of ~$8.19B against $5.36B TTM. At 28.6× forward earnings with a 1.21 PEG, 25.5% ROIC and a 2.05% dividend backed by 22 straight annual increases, an investor is paying a reasonable price for the structurally lowest-cost producer in a $90B market with rising content per car and per factory.

Bear Case

Everything good is already in the price. The stock nearly doubled in six months, trades at 42× trailing earnings, and fell 3% on a quarter that beat on every line — the classic signature of exhausted positioning. Half the analyst panel is on Hold. Trailing FCF per share of $5.86 barely covers the $5.68 dividend, and the depreciation from the new fabs keeps stepping up for years after the capex stops, so margin expansion depends entirely on utilization staying high. Meanwhile state-backed Chinese analog vendors are permanently capping pricing in the low end, roughly a third of revenue sits in an automotive market facing tariffs and an uncertain EV ramp, and the current recovery may be a channel restock rather than genuine end demand — in which case the 2027 comparison is brutal against a fixed-cost base built for a much larger company.

01
The restock proves to be a pull-forward. TI sells catalogue parts into distribution, where order patterns amplify end demand in both directions. If industrial and automotive customers have been rebuilding safety stock rather than consuming, 2027 revenue faces a comparison against inflated 2026 shipments — and TI's 65%+ fixed-cost absorption exposure turns a modest revenue miss into a large earnings miss, exactly as it did from 2022 to 2025.
02
Free cash flow fails to inflect. The bull case is arithmetically dependent on capex staying near $3.3B while depreciation is still rising and revenue compounds. Any decision to accelerate Sherman SM3/SM4, any change to CHIPS Act funding or the federal investment tax credit, or any utilization shortfall breaks the FCF-per-share math — and with a trailing payout ratio of 86%, the dividend growth streak that anchors the shareholder base becomes the pressure point.
03
Chinese share loss becomes structural rather than cyclical. SG Micro, 3Peak, Silergy and peers are moving up from commodity power management into signal chain with state capital and domestic procurement mandates behind them. TI already cut prices hard in 2023–24 to defend share. If China permanently designs out Western analog in its industrial and EV supply chains, TI loses both the volume and the pricing umbrella that makes the 300mm cost advantage worth having.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Drives This Stock
The Analog Cycle Has Bottomed — Probably
EMERGING TAILWIND

The analog semiconductor cycle that began rolling over in mid-2022 was the longest and deepest in twenty years: TI's revenue fell 23% peak-to-trough ($20.03B FY2022 → $15.64B FY2024) and gross margin fell 1,287bps (68.76% FY2022 → 55.89% in Q4 FY25). The evidence that it has ended is now quantitative rather than anecdotal — four consecutive quarters of sequential revenue improvement, +18.6% and +22.8% YoY growth in the last two, and gross margin recovering 548bps in two quarters. Peers corroborate: ADI's TTM revenue is +29.8%, NXP +8.8% with Q2 at +19%, STMicro +10.5%. The important caveat is that analog upcycles historically run three to four years from trough, but they also historically overshoot on the way up as distributors rebuild inventory — meaning 2026 growth rates are almost certainly not the sustainable run-rate. The right way to model this is not "23% growth continues" but "the through-cycle revenue base has reset higher and margins normalize toward 62–65%."

Chinese Domestic Analog: A Permanent Ceiling on Pricing
ONGOING HEADWIND

China is roughly a third of global analog consumption and has spent a decade building a domestic alternative. SG Micro, 3Peak, Silergy, Will Semiconductor and dozens of smaller firms now offer functional substitutes for large parts of TI's commodity power-management catalogue at materially lower prices, backed by state capital, preferential financing and explicit procurement guidance toward local sourcing. The effect on TI is already visible in the historical record: TI cut prices aggressively across 2023–24 to hold share, and that pricing action is a substantial part of why gross margin fell to 55.9% rather than the ~62% that volume decline alone would imply. Even with the cycle recovering, this is best understood as a structural change to the shape of the analog market — the low end is being commoditized and the returns will increasingly concentrate in high-performance signal chain, automotive-qualified parts and applications where a 20-year supply guarantee matters more than a 30% price delta. TI's 300mm cost advantage is the correct strategic response, but it is a defensive one.

Capex → Free Cash Flow: The Bet TI Made, Now Being Settled
DEFINING VARIABLE — BULL vs BEAR

Between 2022 and 2025 TI spent roughly $5B a year building 300mm analog capacity — Sherman TX (SM1/SM2, with SM3/SM4 shells), Richardson RFAB2 and Lehi, Utah — through the worst analog downturn in two decades, supported by up to ~$1.6B in CHIPS Act direct funding and a federal investment tax credit on qualifying US fab spend. The result was that free cash flow per share, TI's own stated north-star metric, collapsed while the dividend kept rising. That bet is now being settled. The bull reading: capex has already fallen to $3.31B TTM from a ~$5.1B peak, revenue is compounding into depreciated capacity, gross margin printed 61.4% in Q2, and consensus FY26 free cash flow of ~$8.19B versus $5.36B TTM would take FCF per share from $5.86 to roughly $9 — restoring dividend coverage and buyback capacity while the cost gap versus 200mm peers widens permanently. The bear reading: depreciation from those fabs steps up for several more years regardless of demand; TTM FCF per share of $5.86 currently sits below the $5.68 dividend plus any buyback; and if utilization slips even modestly, TI is left carrying a fixed-cost base sized for a $25B revenue company while generating $19B. At 42× trailing earnings, the market has already voted for the bull reading — which is precisely why the risk is asymmetric to the downside from here.
