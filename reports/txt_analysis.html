01 Company Overview

Textron is the company behind the Cessna and Beechcraft business aircraft and Bell helicopters and tiltrotors, with a defense electronics and vehicles arm (Textron Systems) and — for now — an Industrial segment that makes automotive fuel systems (Kautex) and E-Z-GO golf cars. It is an S&P 500 constituent, NYSE-listed under TXT, and not a NASDAQ-100 member. FY2025 revenue was $14.80B, up 8.01% from $13.70B, with net income of $921M; trailing-twelve-month revenue is $15.45B and GAAP (standard accounting rules) EPS $5.56 (the fourth quarter of 2025 plus the first nine months of 2026). At $85.70 the market values the whole thing at about $14.52B (169.40M shares × the October 30 close).

2026 is the year Textron decided to stop being a conglomerate. On January 4, 2026 Lisa Atherton, who ran Bell, took over as CEO from Scott Donnelly, who became Executive Chairman. On April 30, 2026 the company announced it would separate the Industrial segment — by sale or tax-free spin — leaving a "pure-play aerospace and defense" New Textron with over $12B of expected 2026 revenue and $19B of backlog. By July the headline bullet on the Q2 release read "Initiated sale process for the Industrial Segment." The stock marked its five-year month-end high of $98.65 in February 2026, then fell for five straight months to $76.20 at the end of September, while the Army's money for Bell's MV-75 Cheyenne waited on Congress. The October 29 third-quarter release reported the wait over: the Army had received congressional approval for the funding transfer, Textron raised its full-year outlook, and the stock rose 11.67% that day. At $85.70 it is still 13.1% below the February peak. The debate moves from funding to delivery: jet deliveries recovered to 51 from 42, but Bell's revenue fell 9%, mostly on lower MV-75 volume, and the Q3 release gives no update on the Industrial sale.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Cessna & Beechcraft brands FAA type certificates Installed fleet → aftermarket MV-75 program of record $19.1B segment backlog Captive aircraft finance

The moat in aviation is certification plus installed base. A new light jet or turboprop takes years and very large sums to certify, and every Cessna and Beechcraft already flying needs parts, inspections and service for decades — the aftermarket line grew again in Q3, on volume and pricing. At Bell the moat is the MV-75 itself: an Army development program already producing long-lead parts, which competitors cannot easily displace once the aircraft is funded and fielded. The vulnerability is that both moats sit behind execution. A brand does not deliver jets that the factory can't build, and a program of record — one the Army has formally committed to — still needs Congress to fund it each year.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
MV-75 funding — settled for this year, voted every year. The Q3 release says the Army received congressional approval for its Above Threshold Reprogramming request — a mid-year transfer of Pentagon money that needs the defense committees' sign-off — and Textron raised its outlook on it. Aviation Week reported on October 12, 2026 that a September 28 Pentagon notice reprogrammed $200.246M into the Army's development account for the aircraft. The program still depends on Congress each budget year, and Bell's Q3 military revenue fell $59M on lower MV-75 volume.
02
Factory throughput at Aviation. Jet deliveries recovered to 51 from 42 in Q3, after falling to 40 from 49 in Q2. One good quarter after a bad one is not yet a trend.
03
Bell margin during the ramp. Bell's margin was 8.1% in Q3 against 9.0% a year earlier, on lower volume and mix, after 7.0% in Q2. Development-heavy military programs often dilute margins before production pays; the risk is how long "before" lasts.
04
Separation execution. The Industrial exit is targeted for 12–18 months from April 30, 2026, with "no assurance regarding the ultimate timing or structure." The head-office costs left behind once Industrial is gone and the sale price are both unknown.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the headline figures. First, the GAAP–adjusted gap: Textron's adjusted EPS adds back the LIFO inventory provision (an accounting charge that books rising material costs into cost of sales) and intangible amortisation — $0.20 per share in Q3 ($1.56 GAAP vs $1.76 adjusted: LIFO $0.21 and amortisation $0.04, less $0.05 of tax) — so the raised $6.55–$6.65 adjusted guide and the $5.64–$5.74 GAAP guide describe the same year. Second, pension income: non-service pension and postretirement income added $70M to Q3 pre-tax income ($210M over nine months) — real accounting income, but not from selling aircraft. Third, the Finance segment: its Q3 profit fell to $5M from $18M because the year-earlier quarter included $11M of gains on asset sales. The segment realignment (eAviation folded in from January 4, 2026) means pre-2026 segment history has been recast.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 15x trailing GAAP earnings and 13x this year's adjusted consensus for a company with $19.1B of segment backlog, nine-month revenue up 6%, and a share count shrinking almost 4% a year. The MV-75 funding gap that hung over the guide is closed, and management raised the year on it. Jet deliveries recovered to 51 in Q3, which makes Q2's shortfall look like a bad quarter rather than a broken factory. The Industrial exit turns a conglomerate into a pure aerospace and defense business, and a sale would add cash for more buybacks.

Bear Case

The quality is still thin. Q3 segment profit slipped to $355M from $360M, Bell's revenue fell 9% and its profit 17%, and adjusted EPS grew 13.5% partly because the share count fell 3.7%. Manufacturing cash flow for the first nine months is $344M against $459M a year ago, so the raised $800–$900M guide needs at least $456M in the fourth quarter. Most of the analyst panel still rates it Hold, and two firms cut targets to $86 in October. MV-75 money still has to be voted every year. Separations take longer and cost more than planned, and until it closes the Industrial sale hangs over the stock.

Top 3 Risks

01
MV-75 execution and annual funding. This year's money is approved, but Bell's Q3 military revenue fell $59M on lower MV-75 volume and its margin fell to 8.1%. The program anchors Bell's future and needs Congress to fund it every budget year.
02
Aviation production. Demand is there ($8.0B backlog); Q3 deliveries recovered to 51 jets from 42 after Q2's 40. If output slips again and pricing stops offsetting lower volume, Aviation — 45% of revenue — stops growing profit.
03
Separation execution. Timing, structure and price for Industrial are all undisclosed. A weak sale price or a slow process would dent the "New Textron" story the bull case rests on.
Industry Commentary
Defense funding runs on Washington's calendar
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A defense contractor's revenue is appropriated one fiscal year at a time, and moving money between accounts mid-year needs the defense committees' sign-off. The MV-75 case shows the mechanics: the Army asked to shift about $350M ($230M of FY26 and $120M of FY25 money) into the program, inside a ~$1.5B package sent to Congress in July (Breaking Defense, September 9); Textron self-funded some of the work to keep the schedule while it waited (CEO Lisa Atherton, July 28 call, per Aviation Week); and by its October 29 release the approval had come through and the downside case was out of the guide. Winning the competition was the hard part; getting paid on the government's timetable is the slow part.

Business aviation: the factory is the bottleneck
ONGOING HEADWIND

Textron's last two quarters show both sides. In Q2 aircraft revenue rose on pricing while jet deliveries fell to 40 from 49, with management citing strong orders on one side and manufacturing inefficiencies on the other; in Q3 jet deliveries rose to 51 from 42 and Aviation revenue rose 14%. When the order book is full and the factory sets the pace, every quarter of lost output is revenue deferred rather than lost — until the backlog starts to age.

The conglomerate discount — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: investors have long paid less for mixed bags of businesses than for focused ones, and Textron is removing the bag — auto fuel tanks and golf cars out, aircraft and defense in. Management says New Textron will have a higher growth profile and higher margins, and a sale would add cash to a buyback that cut the diluted share count 3.7% in a year. The bear framing: Industrial earned a 5.0% segment margin in Q3, against 8.1% at Bell and 10.7% at Aviation, so removing it lifts the group's margin a little, not a lot. The separation fixes the label; the jet line and Bell's MV-75 work decide what the rest is worth.
