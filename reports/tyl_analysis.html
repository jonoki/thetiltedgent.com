01 Company Overview

Tyler Technologies started life as a pipe company. Founded in 1966 as Saturn Industries, it bought Tyler Pipe in 1968 — an iron-pipe maker that became its main source of revenue — entered government software in 1998 and renamed itself Tyler Technologies in 1999. Today it is the software behind the counter at thousands of county courthouses, tax assessors, city finance offices and police dispatch centres: more than 16,000 client locations, per the company's own Q2 2026 release boilerplate. FY2025 revenue was $2.33B, up 9.1%, of which 87.1% was recurring, with GAAP EPS of $7.20 (+20.0%) and free cash flow of $620.8M. The $2.3B all-cash purchase of NIC in 2021 added a large government-payments business, which is why 2021 revenue jumped 42.6%.

Here is the odd part. Nothing in the reported numbers broke, and the stock still halved. From a month-end high of $629.17 in November 2024 the shares fell to $292.46 at the end of June 2026 and settled at $332.46 on September 21, 2026 — 38.3% below the 52-week high. The market's worry is not this year's earnings but whether they last: if AI lets government agencies, or cheaper rivals, build the software that Tyler rents to them, what is a 20-year customer relationship worth? Management's answer is on the record — SaaS revenue grew 20.6% in FY2025 and 21.7% in Q2 2026, Q2 2026 set a record for SaaS bookings, and 1.62 million shares were bought back in one quarter. At issue: is Tyler a utility-like franchise the market has mistakenly thrown out with the rest of software, or does a 43.8x trailing multiple still assume too much of a future AI may shorten?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Mission-critical government systems ~2% annual client turnover 16,000+ client locations 87% recurring revenue Payments rails embedded in portals Procurement cycles slow rivals too Cross-sell (avg ~3 products/client)

The moat is procedural, not technical. A county court system is not replaced because a better product exists; it is replaced after a budget cycle, a public procurement, a data conversion of decades of case records, and retraining of clerks who cannot afford a day of downtime. The Rational Walk puts it plainly: once a customer "goes live" it typically stays with the same vendor "for a very long period of time." That is why Tyler can promise ~10% annual recurring growth to 2030 with a straight face. The same inertia cuts the other way: it slows Tyler's own flips, and it means any AI-driven change in how governments buy software would show up slowly — first in new deals, years before it reaches the installed base.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The AI substitution argument. The bear thesis described by The Motley Fool (July 5, 2026) is that large language models could let agencies, or lighter-weight rivals, build custom alternatives and erode Tyler's pricing power. The bull counter is that governments are unlikely to hand-build regulated, mission-critical systems, and management's plan is to embed AI in Tyler's own products — monetised through subscription uplifts or outcome-based pricing, with meaningful revenue expected from the second half of 2027. Until then the argument can't be settled either way from reported numbers.
02
GAAP earnings are thin relative to the multiple. TTM GAAP EPS is $7.59 against 2026 non-GAAP guidance of $12.95–$13.20. The gap is mostly amortisation of acquired intangibles and share-based compensation ($151.3M in FY2025). On GAAP numbers the stock is 43.8x trailing; on consensus non-GAAP it is ~25x forward. Which one you believe is the valuation debate.
03
Transaction revenue is lumpy at the contract level. The Texas payments contract termination cut 2026 transaction growth roughly in half (5–7% guided vs 10–12% ex-Texas). State-level payment portals are large, individually re-bid contracts — the one place in Tyler's model where a single loss moves the group number.
04
Leverage has crept back. Tyler issued $1.4B of 0.50% convertible notes due 2031 in Q2 2026 while buying back ~$505M of stock in the quarter. Total debt is $1.46B against $970M of cash (StockAnalysis) — manageable against $714M of TTM free cash flow, but it is a balance sheet now funding buybacks, not only acquisitions.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items distort the 2026 picture. First, capital structure: in Q2 2026 Tyler issued $1.4B of 0.50% convertible notes due 2031 (about $1.2B net proceeds), opened a new $1.0B five-year revolver, repaid the $600M of convertibles due 2026, and bought back 1,622,762 shares for about $505M. On the Q2 call management said repurchases exceeded 5.5% of shares outstanding year-to-date. Share count is falling fast, which flatters per-share growth. Second, the Texas payments contract ended, cutting 2026 transaction growth by roughly five points versus the ex-Texas rate. Third, acquisitions: For The Record (~$212.7M cash) closed in Q2 2026 and Tyler has since acquired CODY Systems (public safety), per StockAnalysis news; both add revenue that is not organic growth. Per Investing.com's summary of the Q2 slides, management expects about 100bps of margin expansion through the rest of 2026.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over the five years from the September 2021 close of $458.65 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $332.46, TYL lost 27.5% of its price. There are no dividends to add back. The path was a full round trip: a 2022 rate-shock slide to the low $320s, a steady two-year climb to a $629.17 month-end peak in November 2024, and then a 19-month decline to $292.46 in June 2026 — a 53.5% peak-to-trough fall in month-end closes, while revenue, SaaS and free cash flow all kept growing.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how one-sided that slide was. It fell below 30 in October 2025 and stayed there for nine consecutive months, bottoming at 7.3 in June 2026 — the most oversold reading in the five-year series by a wide margin (the 2022 low was 29.3). The August rally took it to 48.1, and it now sits at 37.8. The 10-month moving average, at roughly $348, still sits above the price: momentum has come back from the extreme, but the long-term trend has not turned.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 25x consensus non-GAAP earnings — against a five-year history in the mid-40s — for a business with 87% recurring revenue, ~2% annual client turnover, SaaS growth above 20% (21.7% in Q2 2026), a 29% free-cash-flow margin and a $1.745B buyback capacity equal to about 13% of the company. Its customers are county clerks and state agencies, and the bull view is that they will not hand-build a court case-management system with an AI model. If AI turns out to be a feature Tyler sells (Resident AI Assistant, outcome-based pricing from H2 2027) rather than a substitute for it, the 2030 targets of $3.35B ARR and $1.15B FCF imply the stock is priced as a broken growth story when it is a steady compounder.

Bear Case

On GAAP earnings the stock is still 43.8x trailing, with ROIC of 8.6% and a growing debt load raised partly to fund buybacks. ARR growth is 8.2%, transaction growth has slowed to 3.5% with the loss of Texas, and Q2 revenue missed consensus. On StockAnalysis's 22-analyst panel not one rates it a Sell, yet 9.4% of shares are sold short — the short sellers are betting that the sell side is anchored to a pre-AI view of software. If AI compresses what governments will pay per seat or per module, the slow procurement cycle that protects Tyler today also hides the damage until it's in the renewal numbers years from now.

Top 3 Risks

01
AI substitution and price compression. This is the thesis that took the stock from $629 to $292 in month-end terms without a broken quarter. Management's monetisation plan does not report meaningful revenue until H2 2027, so the market has at least a year with no hard evidence either way.
02
Flip execution and SaaS growth deceleration. The whole growth algorithm assumes 20%+ SaaS growth funded by moving the maintenance base to the cloud. A slowdown in flips, or SaaS growth dropping below 20%, would weaken the core bull argument at the same time.
03
Contract-level concentration in payments. The Texas termination showed that a single state payments contract can halve a revenue line's growth rate. Transaction-based revenue is 35% of the total; another large state re-bid lost would hit growth and sentiment together.
Industry Commentary
AI vs. the software subscription
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Through 2026, per The Motley Fool, software stocks were sold on concerns that AI spending would cannibalise the enterprise budget devoted to software, and that large language models would let customers build their own tools. Tyler was caught in that selloff despite reporting record SaaS bookings and record free cash flow. The public-sector twist matters: management said on the Q2 call that government budgets remain "stable" and that it has seen no meaningful AI-related delay in buying decisions, and that a "shrinking labor force" in government is driving demand for labour-saving software. For now the damage is in the multiple, not the numbers.

The cloud migration as the real growth engine
MULTI-YEAR TRANSITION

Tyler's plan to 2030 is essentially a migration project: move 85% of its 2023 maintenance base to SaaS, earning roughly 1.7x more revenue per migrated client and cutting the cost of supporting old software versions. The courts business is nearly done (7% on legacy); public safety now sells almost entirely cloud. Making some AI features cloud-only is a deliberate push to speed this up. The trade-off is that the cleaner the migration runs, the faster maintenance revenue shrinks, so reported growth depends on SaaS outrunning maintenance every quarter.

The valuation reset — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

At $332.46 TYL trades at 43.8x trailing GAAP earnings, ~25x consensus non-GAAP 2026 earnings, 5.9x EV/sales and a ~5.25% free-cash-flow yield (StockAnalysis inputs; FCF yield recomputed at the settled close). The bull framing: this is the cheapest the franchise has been in five years on cash flow, and the company is using that price to buy back more than 5.5% of its shares in a single year. The bear framing: a mid-40s multiple was the anomaly, and a regulated-customer software business growing ARR at 8% may deserve something close to today's multiple even before AI risk is priced. What settles it is the SaaS growth rate and the flip count every quarter to 2027, not the P/E.
