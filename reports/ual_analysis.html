01 Company Overview

United Airlines Holdings runs the second-largest US network carrier by revenue, flying mainline and regional passenger and cargo services across the United States, Canada, the Atlantic, the Pacific and Latin America from hubs at Chicago O'Hare, Newark, Denver, Houston, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Washington Dulles. FY2025 revenue was $59.07B, up 3.52% from $57.06B in FY2024, with GAAP net income of $3.35B and diluted EPS of $10.20. The TTM figure through June 2026 is $62.90B, up 8.5%. At $34.6B the company ranks roughly #720 globally by market capitalisation — second among US carriers behind Delta ($52.1B), ahead of Southwest ($19.2B) and American ($8.6B).

The central debate on this page is not whether United is a good airline. It is whether the market is pricing a fuel spike as a cycle or as a regime. Jet fuel averaged $4.19 a gallon in Q2 2026, up 79.4% year on year, adding roughly $6 billion to United's expected full-year fuel bill versus what management assumed in January. The stock is down from $135.99 at the end of June to $106.49 — a 21.7% drawdown in ten weeks — while the company simultaneously raised full-year adjusted EPS guidance to $9.00–$11.00 and posted +16% revenue growth. Either the recapture math holds and 8.7x forward earnings is a gift, or the market is correctly telling you that a carrier levered to a commodity it cannot hedge does not deserve a multiple at all. Sections 04 and the airline-metrics table below take that apart.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Slot-constrained hubs Largest US global network Star Alliance + 3 JVs MileagePlus / Chase Premium cabin density Widebody fleet scale Corporate contract base

The durable advantage is geographic, not operational. Newark, O'Hare, San Francisco and Dulles are physically constrained assets that no competitor can replicate at any price — you cannot build a second Newark. That constraint is what lets United run a global long-haul map (the largest transatlantic network of any US carrier, the largest trans-Pacific franchise, a growing India presence) that feeds off domestic connections a point-to-point carrier cannot generate. The fleet follows the same logic: 1,552 aircraft as of Q2 2026, up 5.4% year on year, weighted toward larger-gauge narrowbodies and widebodies, which lowers unit cost per seat and creates more premium inventory to sell. Premium cabin revenue grew +16% in Q2 and Basic Economy +11% — the barbell working at both ends. Starlink connectivity, installed on 450 aircraft with roughly 1,000 targeted by year-end, is the current version of the same product-differentiation play. None of this makes United a wide-moat business in the Buffett sense. It makes it the structurally advantaged member of a structurally terrible industry.

01
Fuel is unhedged and unbounded. United, unlike most European carriers, does not run a systematic hedging programme. Q2 fuel averaged $4.19/gal against $2.33 a year earlier, and the full-year bill is running roughly $6B above the January plan. Every cent of recovery depends on an industry-wide willingness to raise fares in lockstep; if one large competitor decides to buy share instead, the recapture math fails immediately.
02
The balance sheet has no slack at the bottom. Total debt of $33.67B against $16.64B of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 2.02, a current ratio of 0.78 and an Altman Z-score of 1.31, which is in the statistical distress zone. The company is comfortably solvent today on $19.6B of liquidity and 2.2x net leverage. But an airline's capital structure is engineered for the good half of the cycle, and the Z-score is the reminder that the margin for error is thinner than a 9.96x P/E suggests.
03
Loyalty economics are the second-best in the industry. Delta's AmEx relationship throws off roughly $10B a year of fuel-insensitive, high-margin cash and is disclosed as its own line. United's Chase agreement is not disclosed at that granularity and is generally understood to be smaller. In a fuel spike that difference is not cosmetic — it is the difference between earnings that compress and earnings that collapse, and it is precisely why the market pays Delta a 30% multiple premium.
04
Labour and airport costs ratchet up and never down. CASM-ex rose 6.1% in Q2 on 3.5% capacity growth. Headcount grew 5.6% to 117,500. Pilot and flight-attendant contracts, gate leases and landing fees are all sticky in one direction. If fuel normalises but CASM-ex has permanently stepped up, the trough margin on the next downturn is lower than the last one.

Notable one-off items. Three distortions worth naming. First, Q2 2026 GAAP diluted EPS of $2.46 came in above adjusted EPS of $1.99 — the reverse of the usual direction, meaning GAAP earnings were flattered by roughly $0.47 of items management excludes. Anyone anchoring on the trailing $10.69 TTM EPS and the 9.96x multiple is using a number that includes those gains. Second, the entire +16% Q2 revenue growth rate is a fuel pass-through: capacity grew 3.5%, fares did the rest. It is not organic demand growth and should not be extrapolated. Third, the FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance range of $9.00–$11.00 is unusually wide — a $2.00 spread on a ~$10 midpoint, or ±20% — which is management telling you directly that the fuel-recapture outcome is genuinely uncertain. The consensus FY2026 EPS of $9.92 sits just below the midpoint.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen hard and fast enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 means the opposite. It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a stock can stay "overbought" for months in a genuine re-rating, and "oversold" all the way down.

From the October 2021 close of $46.14 to the September 10, 2026 close of $106.49, UAL returned +130.8% over five years with no dividend — a compound annual rate of roughly 18.2%. That headline number badly misrepresents the experience of holding it. The path ran $46 → $32.53 (September 2022, a 29.5% drawdown from the start) → $105.84 (January 2025, a 3.25x gain off the low in 28 months) → $68.82 (April 2025, −35% in three months) → $135.99 (June 2026, a fresh high) → $106.49. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

United raised prices into an 79% fuel spike, grew capacity 3.5%, and still filled more seats — load factor up 30bp with yield up 12.1%. CASM-ex grew 6.1% against PRASM's 12.5%, so the unit spread is positive through the worst quarter of the cycle. ROIC is 10.29% at a fuel-depressed trough. If management's 80–90% Q3 recapture and full Q4 recovery hold, FY2027's consensus $14.98 EPS puts today's $106.49 at 7.1x two-years-out earnings on a business earning above its cost of capital — with 23 buy ratings, an average target 49.5% above spot, and 6.36% short interest to squeeze.

Bear Case

The entire thesis rests on one unhedged commodity and one assumption: that every large US carrier raises fares in lockstep and none defects to buy share. United has no dividend, an Altman Z-score of 1.31, debt-to-equity of 2.02 and a current ratio of 0.78 — there is no cushion if the recapture stalls. The loyalty income that makes Delta's trough shallow is materially smaller here and not even disclosed. GAAP EPS exceeded adjusted EPS last quarter, so the 9.96x multiple rests on a flattered denominator. And the 52-week chart already tells you the market's verdict: 64% of range, +2.72% of return.

01
The fuel recapture does not happen. Management guided to 80–90% recovery in Q3 and 100% by Q4. This is a specific claim that gets tested on October 14. If the TRASM/CASM-ex spread narrows instead of widening, the $9.00–$11.00 full-year guide breaks at the low end, FY2027's +51% consensus EPS growth becomes unsupportable, and an 8.68x forward multiple turns out to have been 13x on the real number.
02
Demand cracks under the higher fares. Basic Economy grew 11% and load factor rose in Q2 — but United raised fares aggressively into a consumer that has now absorbed two years of them. A leisure air-pocket with 5.4% more aircraft and 3.5% more seats in the system is the classic airline value trap: costs are fixed, fares collapse, and the operating leverage that makes 5.6% margins possible works in reverse at speed.
03
The balance sheet meets the cycle. $33.67B of total debt, an Altman Z-score of 1.31, no dividend to cut as a first line of defence, and $3.0B of first-half capex committed to the fleet plan. United is comfortably financed at 2.2x EBITDAR on peak earnings. Leverage ratios are denominators as much as numerators, and a 30% EBITDAR decline takes 2.2x to 3.1x without a dollar of new borrowing.
Industry Commentary
The 2026 jet fuel shock
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Jet fuel at $4.19 a gallon in Q2 2026 against $2.33 a year earlier — up 79.4% — is the dominant fact in US aviation this year. United's Q2 fuel expense rose $2.3B, or 84%, and the full-year bill is running roughly $6B above what management planned for in January. Delta's comparable adjusted fuel price was $3.93 and it described the quarter as its "highest quarterly fuel expense in history." Fuel is typically 20–30% of a US carrier's cost base, and neither United nor most US majors run a systematic hedging programme — the industry abandoned hedging after the 2014–2016 collapse made it look expensive. The entire sector's 2026 earnings now depend on an industry-wide pricing response that no individual carrier controls. What makes this a constraint rather than a disaster is the demand side: United recovered roughly half the increase in Q2 while raising load factor, which is not what a broken pricing environment looks like. Delta guides its September quarter on an assumed step down to ~$3.15/gal. If that step down happens, 2027 estimates are conservative. If it does not, they are fiction.

Consolidation held — capacity discipline is the new normal
ONGOING HEADWIND

The structural argument for owning US airlines at all is that four carriers now control the overwhelming majority of domestic capacity, and that after two decades of bankruptcies they behave like an oligopoly rather than a commodity market. The 2026 fuel spike is the first real stress test of that behaviour: United grew ASMs 3.5% while Delta held at +1%, and both raised fares double digits. Nobody defected. That is the evidence for. The evidence against is inside the cost line — United's CASM-ex rose 6.1% and headcount 5.6% to 117,500, so the controllable cost base is ratcheting up in the good years in a way that lowers the floor in the bad ones. Discipline on capacity plus indiscipline on cost is how airlines historically converted a good decade into a mediocre one. Watch CASM-ex, not ASMs.

The premium and loyalty divide — where the bull and bear cases actually separate
EMERGING TAILWIND

The most important structural shift in US aviation is that the front of the aircraft and the credit card have become the earnings engine, and the back of the aircraft has become the cost of feeding them. Delta has crossed over — premium ticket revenue reached parity with main cabin at $6.9B each, and AmEx remuneration runs near $10B annualised. United is one step behind: premium revenue grew +16%, exactly in line with total passenger revenue, and its co-brand remuneration is not disclosed at all. For the bull, that is the opportunity — United has the network (largest US transatlantic, largest trans-Pacific, growing India) to build the same mix, is installing Starlink across 1,000 aircraft to win the traveller who pays for it, and trades at a 30% discount to the company that already did it. For the bear, it is the reason the discount exists and should persist: in a fuel shock, disclosed fuel-insensitive cash flow is the only thing that makes trough earnings shallow, and United has less of it. The next four quarters of TRASM-minus-PRASM spread will settle which reading is right.
