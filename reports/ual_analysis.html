01 Company Overview

United Airlines Holdings runs the second-largest US network carrier by revenue, flying mainline and regional passenger and cargo services across the United States, Canada, the Atlantic, the Pacific and Latin America from hubs at Chicago O'Hare, Newark, Denver, Houston, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Washington Dulles. FY2025 revenue was $59.07B, up 3.52% from $57.06B in FY2024, with GAAP net income of $3.35B and diluted EPS of $10.20. The TTM figure through September 2026 is $65.55B, up 12.31%. At $32.7B the company ranks #306 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation — second among US carriers behind Delta ($51.8B), ahead of Southwest ($21.0B) and American ($8.1B); peer caps are our calculation, the Oct 26 close times shares outstanding.

The central debate on this page is still fuel, but the October print changed what is being argued about. In July United raised its full-year adjusted EPS guidance to $9.00–$11.00 on the promise to win back 80–90% of the extra fuel cost through higher fares in Q3 and all of it by Q4. On October 20 it reported Q3 adjusted EPS of $2.54 on record revenue of $17.9B (+17.4%), said on the call that it had recaptured 72% of the fuel increase in the quarter, and guided Q4 adjusted EPS to $0.50–$1.50 on fuel of about $4.80 a gallon. The investor update gave no full-year range. Adding the first three quarters' adjusted EPS ($1.19 + $1.99 + $2.54 = $5.72) to the Q4 range gives roughly $6.22–$7.22 for the year (our arithmetic) against July's $9–$11, and the consensus FY2026 estimate on stockanalysis.com is $7.16. United's fuel averaged $3.90 a gallon in Q3, up 60.3% from $2.43, and fuel expense rose $1.8B to $4,843M. The stock is down from $135.99 at the end of June to $100.69 — a 26.0% drawdown in four months — and rose 4.0% close to close on October 21, the first session after the release. Either the recapture math still holds with a lag and 11.7x forward earnings is cheap, or the market is correctly telling you that a carrier this exposed to fuel does not deserve a high multiple. Section 04 and the airline-metrics table take that apart.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Slot-constrained hubs Global hub network Star Alliance + joint ventures MileagePlus / Chase Premium cabin density Widebody fleet scale Corporate contract base

The durable advantage is geographic, not operational. Newark, O'Hare, San Francisco and Dulles are physically constrained assets that no competitor can replicate at any price — you cannot build a second Newark. That constraint is what lets United run a global long-haul map (a large transatlantic network, a trans-Pacific franchise, a growing India presence) that feeds off domestic connections a point-to-point carrier cannot generate. The fleet follows the same logic: 1,575 aircraft at the Q3 2026 release, weighted toward larger-gauge narrowbodies and widebodies, which lowers unit cost per seat and creates more premium inventory to sell. Premium cabin revenue grew +17% in Q3 and Basic Economy +16% — the barbell (premium cabins at one end, the cheapest fares at the other) working at both ends. Starlink connectivity, on more than 640 aircraft and about half of flights, is the current version of the same product-differentiation play; the release targets 61% availability by the end of 2026 and the full fleet by the end of 2027. None of this makes United a wide-moat business in the Buffett sense. It makes it the structurally advantaged member of a structurally difficult industry.

01
Fuel is the swing factor. Q3 fuel averaged $3.90 a gallon against $2.43 a year earlier, and fuel expense rose $1.8B to $4,843M — 29.5% of Q3 operating expenses (our calculation: $4,843M of $16,429M total operating expenses, Q3 release). For Q4 United assumes about $4.80 a gallon, and says every $0.01 move in the price is worth roughly 2.5 cents of quarterly EPS. Every cent of recovery depends on an industry-wide willingness to raise fares; if a large competitor decides to buy share instead, the recapture math fails.
02
The balance sheet has little slack at the bottom. Total debt of $34.29B against $14.65B of cash (stockanalysis.com), a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.93, a current ratio of 0.75 and an Altman Z-score of 1.36, which is in the statistical distress zone. The company is solvent today on $17.7B of liquidity and 2.4x net leverage (Q3 release), but liquidity is down from $19.6B and leverage up from 2.2x at the Q2 report. An airline's capital structure is engineered for the good half of the cycle, and the Z-score is the reminder that the margin for error is thinner than a 9.22x P/E suggests.
03
Loyalty economics are less visible than Delta's. Delta discloses its American Express remuneration as its own line ($2.4B in Q2 2026, per its report). United's Chase agreement is not disclosed at that granularity, and management says it will wait for the new contract before adding detail. In a fuel spike, visible fuel-insensitive cash flow is what makes trough earnings shallow, and investors cannot size United's.
04
Labour and airport costs ratchet up and never down. CASM-ex rose 7.8% in Q3 on 1.3% capacity growth, after 6.1% in Q2; salaries and related costs rose 8.9% to $4,961M and headcount stands at 117,900. Pilot and flight-attendant contracts, gate leases and landing fees are all sticky in one direction. If fuel normalises but CASM-ex has permanently stepped up, the trough margin on the next downturn is lower than the last one.

Notable one-off items. Three distortions worth naming. First, Q3 2026 GAAP diluted EPS of $3.14 came in above adjusted EPS of $2.54 — the reverse of the usual direction for the second quarter running, meaning GAAP earnings were flattered by roughly $0.60 of items management excludes. Anyone anchoring on the trailing $10.92 EPS and the 9.22x multiple is using a number that includes those gains. Second, the +17.4% Q3 revenue growth is mostly a fuel pass-through: capacity grew 1.3%, fares did the rest. It is not organic demand growth and should not be extrapolated. Third, the guidance itself moved. The July full-year range of $9.00–$11.00 was not repeated in the October investor update; the only range given was Q4 adjusted EPS of $0.50–$1.50, a $1.00 spread on a $1.00 midpoint, assuming fuel of about $4.80 a gallon. The consensus FY2026 estimate of $7.16 sits inside the $6.22–$7.22 that the first three quarters plus that Q4 range imply (our arithmetic).

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 means the stock has risen hard and fast enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 means the opposite. It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a stock can stay "overbought" for months in a genuine re-rating, and "oversold" all the way down.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

United raised fares into a 60% fuel spike, grew capacity only 1.3%, and still grew revenue 17.4% — yield up 16.8% with load factor down only half a point. The unit spread is positive: total revenue per seat mile of 20.19¢ against total cost of 18.56¢. Basic Economy revenue grew 16%, so the price-sensitive customer is still buying. If recapture catches up with fuel and the Q4 guide of $0.50–$1.50 proves the trough, FY2027's consensus $11.96 EPS puts today's $100.69 at 8.4x next year's (FY2027) earnings, with 23 buy ratings and an average target 49.1% above spot.

Bear Case

The thesis rests on one commodity and one assumption: that fares rise as fast as fuel. In Q3 they did not quite — 72% of the increase recaptured against an 80–90% target — and the Q4 guide of $0.50–$1.50 at $4.80 fuel leaves the year at roughly $6.22–$7.22 against July's $9–$11. United has no dividend, an Altman Z-score of 1.36, debt-to-equity of 1.93 and a current ratio of 0.75, and its leverage rose to 2.4x. GAAP EPS exceeded adjusted EPS again, so the 9.22x multiple rests on a flattered denominator. Adjusted pre-tax margin fell to 6.2% from 8.0% on 17% more revenue.

01
The fuel recapture falls short again. Management said Q4 recapture should be similar to Q3's 72%, with fuel at about $4.80 a gallon. The company says each cent on the fuel price is worth about 2.5 cents of quarterly EPS, so a $0.50 move in the price is roughly $1.25 a share (our arithmetic), more than the whole Q4 range. If the TRASM/CASM-ex spread narrows instead of widening, even the low end of the Q4 range is at risk and FY2027's +66.9% consensus EPS growth becomes unsupportable.
02
Demand cracks under the higher fares. Basic Economy grew 16% and yield rose 16.8% in Q3, but load factor fell half a point, with traffic up 0.7% against capacity up 1.3%. A leisure air-pocket is the classic airline value trap: costs are fixed, fares fall, and the operating leverage (high fixed costs, so a small change in revenue moves profit a lot) that makes 5.5% margins possible works in reverse at speed.
03
The balance sheet meets the cycle. $34.29B of total debt, an Altman Z-score of 1.36, no dividend to cut as a first line of defence, and $5.2B of nine-month capital spending committed to the fleet plan. United is financed at 2.4x adjusted net debt to EBITDAR, up from 2.2x. Leverage ratios are denominators as much as numerators: a 30% EBITDAR decline takes 2.4x to 3.4x without a dollar of new borrowing (our arithmetic).
Industry Commentary
The 2026 jet fuel shock
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Jet fuel averaged $3.90 a gallon in Q3 2026 against $2.43 a year earlier — up 60.3% — after $4.19 in Q2, and United assumes about $4.80 for Q4 (forward curve as of October 16). Fuel expense rose $1.8B to $4,843M in Q3, 29.5% of operating expenses (our calculation). It is the dominant fact in US aviation this year, and the sector's earnings depend on an industry-wide pricing response that no individual carrier controls. What makes it a constraint rather than a disaster is the demand side: United's yield rose 16.8% with load factor down only half a point, which is not what a broken pricing environment looks like. But the timing matters. United recaptured 72% of the increase in Q3 and expects the same in Q4 while fuel is still rising, so the recapture runs behind the price. If fuel steps back, 2027 estimates (FY2027 consensus EPS $11.96 against $7.16 for FY2026) look conservative. If it does not, they are at risk.

Capacity discipline, and the cost line
ONGOING HEADWIND

The structural argument for owning US airlines at all is that a handful of carriers now control most domestic capacity and, after two decades of bankruptcies, behave more like an oligopoly than a commodity market. The 2026 fuel spike is the first real stress test of that behaviour. United grew capacity only 1.3% in Q3 and its revenue per seat mile rose 15.9%: fares did the work, and its Q4 outlook expects unit revenue growth to accelerate. The evidence against is inside the cost line — CASM-ex rose 7.8% and salaries and related costs 8.9%, so the controllable cost base is ratcheting up in good years in a way that lowers the floor in bad ones. Discipline on capacity plus indiscipline on cost is how airlines historically converted a good decade into a mediocre one. Watch CASM-ex, not ASMs.

The premium and loyalty divide — where the bull and bear cases actually separate
EMERGING TAILWIND

The most important structural shift in US aviation is that the front of the aircraft and the credit card have become the earnings engine, and the back of the aircraft has become the cost of feeding them. United's premium revenue grew +17% in Q3 against passenger revenue growth of 17.6%, and loyalty revenue +17%; its co-brand remuneration is not disclosed at all, while Delta reports American Express remuneration separately ($2.4B in Q2 2026). For the bull, the opportunity is that United has the network to build the same mix, is putting Starlink across its fleet to win the traveller who pays for it, and trades at a lower multiple than the company that already did it (9.22x against Delta's 15.80x trailing). For the bear, the discount exists for a reason and should persist: in a fuel shock, disclosed fuel-insensitive cash flow is what makes trough earnings shallow, and United has said it will wait for its new Chase contract before disclosing more. The next four quarters of the TRASM-minus-PRASM spread will settle which reading is right.
