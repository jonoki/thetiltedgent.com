01 Company Overview

Uber Technologies runs a global marketplace for rides and local delivery, connecting a record 215 million monthly active platform consumers with 10.8 million drivers and couriers across three reportable segments: Mobility (ride-hailing), Delivery (Uber Eats, groceries and retail) and Freight (digital truckload brokerage). Fiscal 2025 delivered revenue of $52.0B, up 18.3% YoY, on $193.5B of gross bookings (the total value of rides, meals and freight booked, before payouts to drivers and restaurants; +19%), with 13.6 billion trips completed. In Q3 2026 gross bookings rose 20% to $59.8B, the fifth straight quarter of 20%-plus growth, and free cash flow over the trailing twelve months reached a record $10.4 billion.

The stock has gone the other way: shares are −25.5% over the past year (SPY +9.7%, QQQ +7.3%, price returns) and about 28% below the $95.18 52-week high. Three things are in the debate now. Autonomy: Waymo ended its Phoenix service on Uber in May 2026 and has told Uber it will enter Austin and Atlanta on its own from January 2028, which feeds the fear that robotaxi operators bypass Uber's network. Deals: Uber is offering €41.50 a share in cash for Germany's Delivery Hero, which values the whole company at $14.8B, and has agreed to pay $2.3B in cash for catering marketplace ezCater, with debt rising to fund it. Earnings quality: the low trailing P/E the last edition showed is gone. A year-ago quarter of $3.11 a share, swollen by a one-time tax benefit and investment gains, dropped out of the trailing twelve months, leaving GAAP EPS of $1.68 and a trailing P/E of about 41.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Riders Attract Drivers, Drivers Attract Riders Short Waits in Busy Cities Uber One Cross-Segment Lock-In Rides, Food and Freight in One Network Global Regulatory Scale Owns Almost No Vehicles 30+ AV Partnership Portfolio

Uber's moat is driver supply, not technology. In any given city, the marketplace with the shortest average wait wins the next rider, which attracts more drivers, which shortens waits further, a loop that has proven very hard to attack head-on. Q3 2026 showed the loop's weak point: where drivers ran short abroad, trips were left unserved, and Uber is now spending to recruit and win back drivers. Uber One deepens the moat by tying Mobility and Delivery into one subscription: about 55M members now generate more than half of combined bookings. The business needs little capital: Uber owns essentially no vehicles and spent $336M of capex in FY2025 against $52.0B of revenue, and it produced $10.4B of trailing free cash flow. That cash is now being redirected: $4.28B of buybacks over nine months sits beside a $14.8B takeover offer, the $2.3B ezCater deal and AV stakes. Finally, the AV strategy is itself a moat claim: with partnerships across 30+ autonomy companies (Nvidia, Nuro/Lucid, WeRide, Wayve, Pony.ai, Rivian, Momenta, May Mobility, Baidu, Zoox, VW/MOIA, Avride, Aurora, Waabi and more), Uber is positioning itself as the neutral marketplace that brings riders to fleets without a consumer app of their own. Management says it runs about 30% more trips per vehicle per day for its US AV partners than operators running their own services do nationwide.

Key Vulnerabilities

A
Robotaxi operators going direct. The moat assumes AV operators need Uber's demand. Waymo does not: it runs its own consumer app, its Phoenix service on Uber ended in May 2026, and in July Uber confirmed Waymo's notice that it will enter Austin and Atlanta on its own from January 2028, with the existing contract running to May 2028. The Financial Times has reported that Waymo explored leaving the partnership entirely. Tesla is building its own robotaxi network. If the two most capable operators both go direct in dense, high-value city centers, Uber is left with less-capable fleets in less-profitable places.
B
Driver classification and gig-labor regulation. Uber's profit on each trip depends on classifying drivers and couriers as independent contractors. A ruling that reclassifies them in any large market turns pay that rises and falls with trips into fixed employment cost with benefits, payroll taxes and minimum-earnings floors. Legal settlements and regulatory rulings have recurred across US states, the UK and the EU, and remain a rare but costly risk that plays out one jurisdiction at a time.
C
Insurance cost inflation. US commercial auto insurance costs — driven by larger claims and lawsuits funded by outside investors — have long weighed on Mobility margins and directly cut how much of each fare Uber keeps. Insurance reserve re-estimates can also move reported results in either direction without any change in the business, though management credited insurance reform for supporting US growth in Q3 2026.
D
Delivery trails at home. Against DoorDash's 60.7% of US food delivery at the end of 2024 (Earnest Analytics), Uber Eats' 26.1% makes Uber the challenger in its fastest-growing large segment, and DoorDash's agreed purchase of Instacart would add grocery scale. If Uber must buy share with promotions, Delivery's 30% segment operating income growth is not durable.

Notable One-Off Items — these are material and must be adjusted for:

1. Investment revaluations swing GAAP net income. Q3 2026 GAAP net income of $512M included a $476 million net pre-tax loss from revaluing Uber's equity stakes, marked at $11.5B including equity-method holdings. Q2 2026 went the other way, with a $1.6B gain inside $2.4B of net income, and Q3 2025's $6.6B included a $4.9B tax benefit plus a $1.5B gain. "Our GAAP Net Income may continue to see swings from quarter-to-quarter due to equity stakes on our balance sheet," CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy said. Adjusted EBITDA, non-GAAP EPS and free cash flow are the better lens: a trailing P/E of about 41 on $1.68 of GAAP EPS overstates the price just as the 15 of the last edition understated it.

2. UK business-model change. Q3 2026 Mobility revenue fell 2% against 18% bookings growth. The change moved driver payments out of cost of revenue and netted them against revenue, cutting reported revenue by $1.1B in the quarter, about 8 percentage points of total growth, and Mobility's revenue as a share of bookings by about 3.5 points. Uber expects a similar effect in Q4. Year-on-year revenue and take-rate comparisons across this change are not like for like.

3. Insurance reserve re-estimates. Uber periodically re-estimates auto-insurance reserves; these adjustments flow through cost of revenue and can move segment margins by a few tenths of a percentage point in either direction without any change in the economics of a trip.

4. UK VAT payment and legal settlements. Uber expects to pay about $1.5B in cash in Q4 2026 on a previously disclosed UK VAT matter, which will reduce that quarter's free cash flow. Driver-classification settlements and regulatory penalties have also recurred across US states and abroad, so they are better treated as a cost of doing business than as true one-offs.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case — The Marketplace Every Robotaxi Needs

Uber is a toll booth on local transport that needs little capital, generating $10.4B of trailing free cash flow on about $314M of trailing capex, with bookings growing 20% and 215M monthly users. At 15.7x consensus FY2027 adjusted earnings and a PEG of 0.53, investors are paying less per dollar of expected earnings than for the S&P 500 as a whole, for a business growing faster. The robotaxi argument is that driverless cars lower the cost of supply while making the app that gathers riders more valuable: 30+ partnerships mean almost every AV developer without a consumer brand needs Uber's 215M users, its dispatch and routing, and its fleet operations. Management's evidence so far: about 30% more trips per vehicle per day for US AV partners than operators' own fleets nationwide, faster trip growth in US cities with AVs, and a record 8.0% Mobility margin. Delivery Hero would add scale abroad, and if Uber becomes the default marketplace for other companies' robotaxis, taking driver pay out of its costs is the largest margin opportunity in the company's history.

Bear Case — Robotaxi Owners Go Direct

The marketplace idea fails if the AV winners have their own apps, and the two most credible do. Waymo's Uber partnership is shrinking: Phoenix ended in May 2026 and Waymo will enter Austin and Atlanta on its own from January 2028, while Tesla is building its own network with no reason to pay a toll. In an AV world, the "network effect" Uber sells is really having drivers nearby, and a fleet operator with 5,000 owned vehicles in one city needs no outside drivers at all. Uber would be left offering weaker fleets in less dense markets while the highest-margin city centers are taken directly. Meanwhile Uber's business is becoming heavier: a $14.8B offer for Delivery Hero funded partly with new debt, long-term debt up from $10.5B to $15.7B in nine months, $2.3B for ezCater and AV stakes and vehicle commitments. Mobility growth also slowed in Q3 for a reason autonomy cannot fix yet: not enough drivers abroad. Delivery, the offsetting growth engine, is a distant #2 to DoorDash at home, and DoorDash is buying Instacart.

The honest synthesis: both cases rest on the same facts and differ on one unknown, whether autonomous supply consolidates into a few integrated networks or fragments across dozens of developers. Q3 did not settle it. Uber has spread its bets widely and still generates more than $10B a year in free cash flow, but it has moved from paying that cash back toward spending it on large deals. The Waymo relationship's direction is real evidence, and it points the wrong way.

Top 3 Risks

1
Robotaxis taking the best city markets. If Waymo, Tesla and other operators running their own services capture the busiest, most profitable city markets directly, Uber loses its best trips first. Waymo's Phoenix exit and its plan to enter Austin and Atlanta on its own from January 2028 make this a demonstrated risk rather than a hypothetical.
2
Deal execution and a heavier balance sheet. Delivery Hero (expected to complete in the second half of 2027) and ezCater bring integration, regulatory and financing risk at the same time, and the debt taken on since last year raises fixed costs even with leverage guided below two times. GAAP earnings add noise: investment revaluations swung from a $1.6B gain in Q2 2026 to a $476M loss in Q3.
3
Regulatory, insurance and driver-supply shocks. An adverse driver-classification ruling in a large jurisdiction would turn variable driver pay into fixed employment cost with benefits and minimum-earnings floors. US commercial auto insurance inflation can return, and Q3 showed that fuel costs, labor markets and rival platforms can tighten driver supply abroad. These arrive on regulators' and markets' timelines rather than management's.
  Industry Commentary — Macro Themes
Autonomy: Existential Risk or the Largest Margin Unlock in Company History
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

This is the theme that decides what the business is worth over the long run. The bear evidence is concrete: Waymo's Phoenix service on Uber ended in May 2026, Waymo has told Uber it will enter Austin and Atlanta on its own from January 2028, the Financial Times has reported it explored leaving altogether, and Tesla is building its own robotaxi network. The bull evidence is also concrete: Uber has assembled 30+ AV partnerships, including Nvidia (Stellantis committed to 5,000+ Drive-powered vehicles), Nuro/Lucid (Bay Area commercial launch expected Q1 2027; Lucid order raised to at least 35,000 vehicles in April 2026), Rivian (10,000 exclusive R2 robotaxis; Uber to invest up to $1.25B), WeRide, Baidu and Pony.ai (fully driverless abroad), Wayve (supervised rides in London), Zoox, VW/MOIA, Momenta, May Mobility, Avride and Waabi (Uber's $250M of milestone-based funding, January 2026, is tied to 25,000+ robotaxis), plus Aurora and Volvo in trucking. Management targets about 700 fully driverless vehicles on Uber by year-end and over 2,000 by the end of 2027, with a much larger ramp from 2028, and says Uber's trip growth in US AV markets exceeds its US average. The market still leans toward the bear case: about 22 times trailing adjusted earnings is not what an unthreatened marketplace growing bookings 20% usually fetches.

Gig-Worker Regulation and Insurance Cost Inflation
ONGOING HEADWIND

Uber's model depends on independent-contractor classification for millions of drivers and couriers across dozens of jurisdictions, each with its own legislature and courts. Reclassification turns a fully variable cost into fixed employment cost, and legal settlements have recurred. Alongside it, US commercial auto insurance costs have risen on larger claims and lawsuits funded by outside investors, a direct drag on Mobility that helps explain why Uber's 41.44% gross margin sits well below software-peer levels, although management credited insurance reform for supporting US growth in Q3. The ironic hedge: autonomy eliminates both problems at once. No driver means no classification exposure and a very different insurance risk. Uber's AV urgency is therefore partly defensive: it is the exit from its two most persistent cost headwinds.

Consumer Resilience, Membership and Advertising — Framing Bull vs. Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND

Ride-hailing and food delivery are discretionary purchases, yet Uber grew monthly users 14% to a record 215M and trips 16% to 4.1B in Q3 2026, with monthly trips per user also at a record. The clearest sign is Uber One: about 55M members accounting for more than half of combined Mobility and Delivery bookings, plus merchant advertising and offers above a $10B annualized run rate. Bull implication: membership and advertising are earnings streams that do not depend on who supplies the vehicle, and 215M engaged consumers is not something a fleet operator can replicate cheaply. Bear implication: membership only matters if Uber remains the place transportation is bought. If Waymo and Tesla apps become the default in the busiest cities, Uber One becomes a loyalty program for the second-best supply pool, and a consumer downturn would hit a discretionary business already fighting for its distribution role.
