01 Company Overview

UDR is a Denver-area apartment landlord. As of September 30, 2026 it owned or had an ownership position in 60,617 apartment homes, including 634 under development, spread across coastal and Sun Belt metros. It is a REIT, so the number that matters is not GAAP earnings (standard accounting profit) but FFO. On that basis the business earned $2.54 of FFO as Adjusted (FFOA) per share in 2025, up from $2.48 in 2024, and management now guides 2026 to $2.54–$2.56 (midpoint $2.55), a range it has raised twice this year. Three years of nearly the same number.

The earnings stream is still barely moving; what changed this autumn is the balance sheet. On October 21, UDR announced a $2.0 billion joint venture with Apollo, an investment firm, and the Q3 release says it closed (the filing lists the closing as October 30). Apollo put in $1.34 billion of equity for roughly two-thirds of 19 communities (7,059 homes) valued at about $2.0 billion, a price that implies a 6.0% cap rate (a building's first-year income as a percentage of its price). UDR keeps about one-third, runs the buildings for a management fee, and holds an option to buy Apollo out at a 7.0% target return, usable for a limited period starting at the fifth anniversary. The release says 2026 net income, FFO and FFOA are unaffected. It is the same trade as the asset sales and buybacks of the past year, on a larger scale: sell part of the portfolio to private money and put the cash to work. On the November 3 call, CFO Dave Bragg said about one-third of the $1.3 billion had gone to reducing debt and the remaining two-thirds will fund wholly owned acquisitions and share buybacks. About 16.7 million shares have been repurchased since the restart in September 2025, for roughly $603.2 million at an average $36.06.

The market noticed. UDR closed the prior edition (September 21) at $33.89 and closed November 6 at $36.58, up 7.9%, while the 2026 FFOA guidance midpoint rose only $0.02 (from $2.53 to $2.55, +0.8%). The stock rose 5.1% over the two sessions after the Apollo announcement ($34.41 on October 20 to $36.18 on October 22). The valuation multiple (the price paid per dollar of earnings) is the story: at $36.58 the stock trades at 14.3x the 2026 guidance midpoint (our calculation), against 13.4x at the prior edition and about 17.5x at the December 2024 close. Occupancy is in the mid-96% range, same-store revenue grows low-single digits, and the December 2026 dividend will be the 220th paid in a row. The surrounding landscape has also shifted: AvalonBay and Equity Residential closed an all-stock merger of equals on August 17, 2026, with Equity Residential as the surviving company, renamed Vivmark Residential (NYSE: VMRK). The question for an investor is whether the Sun Belt supply wave is truly passing, and whether the multiple, not the business, is what was cheap.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Coastal land scarcity Operating platform Resident retention Low-cost fixed-rate debt (3.45%) Geographic diversification Unbroken dividend record

Apartments are a commodity; nobody has a monopoly on a two-bedroom. The moat, such as it is, has two parts. The first is location: in coastal California and the Northeast, zoning and construction cost limit new supply, so existing buildings keep pricing power when demand rises — Q3 2026's regional numbers show exactly that, with the West and Northeast growing NOI 3.9% and 3.0%. The second is operating cost and retention: on the Q3 call, per UDR's published transcript, Lacy said fewer move-outs supported lease growth and occupancy and kept same-store expense growth to 2.2%, and that other income (revenue beyond base rent) was growing about 7% in the quarter. Lower turnover means fewer vacant days and less cost to ready a home for the next tenant. Neither of these protects rental income from a flood of new buildings down the street — which is precisely what the Sun Belt has been.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Earnings are flat, not growing. FFOA per share of $2.48 (2024), $2.54 (2025) and a $2.55 midpoint guide for 2026; nine-month 2026 FFOA of $1.90 equals the nine months of 2025. Buybacks help per-share math, but asset sales shrink the earnings base at the same time: total Q3 revenue fell $3.8M (0.9%) YoY because sold properties' revenue outweighed same-store growth.
02
Sun Belt rents are still falling. Southwest and Southeast (21.5% of same-store NOI) posted revenue of −0.4% and −0.9% in Q3 2026. Until those markets absorb the supply, about a fifth of the portfolio subtracts from growth.
03
Refinancing drift. The 3.45% average debt cost is a legacy of the low-rate years. Average maturity has shortened from 4.6 to 3.7 years in twelve months; each refinancing at today's rates is a small, permanent drag on FFO.
04
Scale gap after the Vivmark merger. AvalonBay and Equity Residential combined on August 17, 2026 into Vivmark Residential, a roughly $50.7B company at the November 6 close (our calc.) — about four times UDR's market value. In a business where the cost of running each apartment matters, UDR is now clearly mid-sized.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported numbers. First, GAAP earnings swing on property sales: net income per share was $0.26 in 2024 and $1.13 in 2025 (+335%), while FFOA grew 2%. In Q3 2026 net income ($0.67) exceeded FFO ($0.62) — which can only happen when gains on property sales outweigh depreciation, since FFO adds depreciation back and strips those gains out. Full-year 2026 net income guidance was raised to $1.58–$1.60 per share from $1.03–$1.11 (midpoint +$0.52), while FFO guidance was trimmed to $2.48–$2.50 (midpoint −$0.02) and FFOA guidance raised to $2.54–$2.56 (midpoint +$0.02). That is why the trailing P/E of 17.3 says little about the business. Second, FFO versus FFOA: the "as adjusted" figure excludes items management treats as non-recurring, so it runs ahead of FFO ($0.65 against $0.62 in Q3). Third, the balance sheet is being reshaped: the Apollo venture, the $183.0M of Seattle and Nashville sales, a pending $69.0M Washington, D.C. sale and the LaSalle venture expansion all move buildings off UDR's books or into partnerships, while its Debt and Preferred Equity lending book is being wound down: the Q3 release lists two acquisitions that came out of it (a 483-home Inland Empire community for $160.3M of incremental cash and a 286-home Portland community for $74.7M) and a $29.5M preferred-equity repayment, and expects about $250.0M of that book at year-end (excluding a 237-home Inland Empire acquisition agreed for the first half of 2027). The release says the Apollo transaction does not change 2026 net income, FFO or FFOA.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the November 2021 month-end close of $56.73 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $36.58, UDR lost 35.5% of its price (price only, closing prices; dividends not included). Dividends — $1.45 in 2021, rising to $1.72 in 2025 (UDR's 2021 results release; StockAnalysis) — recover part of that, but not most of it. FFOA per share did not fall over the years reported here ($2.48 in 2024, $2.54 in 2025, $2.55 guided for 2026), so the decline came from investors paying less per dollar of FFOA, not from lower earnings. The shape: a peak of $59.99 in December 2021, a rate-driven slide through 2022, a trough of $31.81 in October 2023, a recovery to $45.86 in November 2024 and $45.18 in February 2025, and then a grind lower as Sun Belt supply hit rents. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 14.3x FFOA and 16.6x AFFO for a 60,600-home portfolio that is 96.5% occupied, with a 4.76% dividend covered by AFFO at a 79% payout, a 3.45% average debt cost and leverage of 5.5x EBITDAre. Management has now sold two-thirds of 19 communities to Apollo at a roughly 6.0% cap rate and kept the option to buy them back, and has raised 2026 guidance twice. The supply wave that crushed Sun Belt rents is fading: Sun Belt blended lease rates improved to about −1.5% in Q3 from about −2% in Q2, per the Q3 call transcript, and management expects a 2027 revenue earn-in of 0.8–1.0 percentage points. If FFOA simply starts growing again, a return to the ~17.5x multiple of late 2024 on $2.55 would be worth about $45 a share (our arithmetic, not a forecast) — above the $39.69 average target.

Bear Case

Flat earnings for three years is not a supply story; it is a business that cannot grow per-share cash flow. Same-store NOI guidance of 0.8%–1.2% is modest, the Southeast still posts falling NOI (−2.2%), and the Mid-Atlantic lost 0.8 percentage points of occupancy with NOI down 1.0%. Every disposition shrinks the earnings base: UDR now keeps about one-third of 19 communities, and the release says 2026 is untouched, so the first full year without all of that income is 2027, which the February guidance will show. Goldman Sachs maintained a Sell with a $35 target on November 3, below the $36.58 price, and the stock has already risen 7.9% since the prior edition on a $0.02 higher guidance midpoint. A price of 14.3 times FFOA is no floor if interest rates stay high — the stock traded at $31.81 in 2023.

Top 3 Risks

01
The supply recovery arrives later than promised. Management cited "waning supply" in its July release. If Sun Belt blended lease rates stay negative into 2027, the 0.8–1.0 percentage-point earn-in cited on the Q3 call (which depends on results through the end of 2026) may be all the help revenue gets, and FFOA could stay flat for a fourth year.
02
Interest rates. REIT prices move with the return investors demand from property income. If rates stay high for longer, investors pay less per dollar of earnings and refinancing costs more on the $333.6M maturing through 2027 and the debt behind it — average maturity is only 3.7 years.
03
Shrinking to grow per share. With the Apollo venture, $183.0M of further sales and buybacks funded partly from them, UDR is getting smaller. That works while private buyers pay more for buildings than the stock market implies. If private cap rates rise (private buyers pay less for the same income), that gap closes and the company is left smaller without being cheaper.
Industry Commentary
The Sun Belt supply wave
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The post-2021 building boom put large numbers of new apartments into Texas, Florida, Nashville and similar metros just as rent growth normalised. New buildings lease up with concessions, and the existing landlords nearby have to match. UDR's Q3 2026 numbers show the split plainly: the West +3.9% and Northeast +2.5% revenue, the Southwest −0.4% and Southeast −0.9%. Management's language has moved from defence to cautious optimism — in July Toomey cited "the resiliency of the economy, waning supply, and attractive relative affordability of apartments" — and on the November 3 call, per UDR's published transcript, Lacy put Sun Belt blended lease rates at about −1.5% in Q3 against about −2% in Q2: still negative, but less so.

Consolidation and the public–private gap
ONGOING HEADWIND

The AvalonBay–Equity Residential merger, closed on August 17, 2026 (Equity Residential survived and was renamed), created Vivmark Residential at roughly $50.7B of market value at the November 6 close (our calc.). Scale matters in apartments mainly through operating cost — technology, staffing, procurement — and a bigger platform can compete away a smaller landlord's edge. At the same time, when private buyers pay more for buildings than the stock market implies, selling assets and buying back stock beats building new ones. On the Q3 call, per UDR's published transcript, CFO Dave Bragg said apartment REIT stocks continue to trade at an unusually wide discount to private-market apartment pricing, and UDR's answer is the Apollo venture, its sales of older assets and its buybacks. The wind-down of its Debt and Preferred Equity lending business signals a narrowing to the core landlord role.

Affordability and retention — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: high home prices and mortgage rates keep renters renting. UDR's resident retention hit a seasonally adjusted all-time high of 60% in Q2 2026 (Q2 release), and on the Q3 call, per the transcript, Lacy put Q3 retention at 52%, an all-time third-quarter high, and said fewer move-outs supported lease growth and occupancy. Longer tenancy means lower turnover cost and steadier occupancy, and "other income" (revenue beyond base rent) was growing about 7% in Q3, faster than same-store revenue. The bear framing: retention that high can also mean landlords are buying occupancy with modest renewals rather than pushing rent, and affordability cuts both ways — a renter who cannot afford to buy may also not be able to absorb rent increases. The test is the 2027 same-store revenue guidance, due with Q4 results.
