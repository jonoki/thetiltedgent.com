01 Company Overview

UDR is a Denver-area apartment landlord. As of June 30, 2026 it owned or had an ownership position in 60,259 apartment homes, including 685 under development, spread across coastal and Sun Belt metros. It is a REIT, so the number that matters is not GAAP earnings but FFO. On that basis the business earned $2.54 of FFO as Adjusted (FFOA) per share in 2025, up from $2.48 in 2024, and management guides 2026 to $2.49–$2.57. Three years of the same number.

And that is the story. The earnings stream has barely moved; the price the market will pay for it has. UDR closed December 2024 at $43.41 — about 17.5x that year's FFOA — and closed on September 21, 2026 at $33.89, roughly 13.4x the 2026 guidance midpoint (both our calculations). Nothing broke in between. Occupancy has stayed in the mid-96% range, same-store revenue keeps growing low-single digits, and the dividend has been paid 216 times in a row, with the 217th due September 30. What changed is the table around it: a wave of new apartment supply in the Sun Belt, higher interest rates raising the discount rate on every property, and a competitor landscape that just consolidated — AvalonBay and Equity Residential closed an all-stock merger of equals on August 17, 2026, with Equity Residential as the surviving company, renamed Vivmark Residential (NYSE: VMRK). Management's response is the classic landlord's arbitrage: sell old buildings to private buyers and use the cash to buy back its own stock — about 11.5 million shares repurchased since September 2025. The bet for an investor is whether the supply wave is truly passing and the multiple, not the business, is what's cheap.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Coastal land scarcity Operating platform (43 homes per associate) Resident retention Low-cost fixed-rate debt (3.4%) Geographic diversification Unbroken dividend record

Apartments are a commodity; nobody has a monopoly on a two-bedroom. The moat, such as it is, has two parts. The first is location: in coastal California and the Northeast, zoning and construction cost limit new supply, so existing buildings keep pricing power when demand rises — Q2 2026's regional numbers show exactly that. The second is operating cost: per Investing.com's summary of the September BofA conference, UDR runs about 43 homes per associate, which it describes as roughly 20% better than the peer average, and resident retention about 500 basis points above peers. Lower turnover means fewer vacant days and less make-ready spend. Neither of these protects the rent roll from a flood of new buildings down the street — which is precisely what the Sun Belt has been.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Earnings are flat, not growing. FFOA per share of $2.48 (2024), $2.54 (2025) and a $2.53 midpoint guide for 2026. Buybacks help per-share math, but asset sales shrink the earnings base at the same time: total Q2 revenue was flat YoY because disposed properties' revenue offset same-store growth.
02
Sun Belt rents are still falling. Southeast and Southwest (23.4% of same-store NOI) both posted −1.0% revenue in Q2 2026. Until those markets absorb the supply, a quarter of the portfolio subtracts from growth.
03
Refinancing drift. The 3.4% average debt cost is a legacy of the low-rate years. Average maturity has shortened from 4.7 to 3.9 years in twelve months; each refinancing at today's rates is a small, permanent drag on FFO.
04
Scale gap after the Vivmark merger. AvalonBay and Equity Residential combined on August 17, 2026 into Vivmark Residential, a roughly $47.5B company at the September 21 close (our calc.) — about four times UDR's market value. In a business where platform cost per unit matters, UDR is now clearly mid-sized.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the reported numbers. First, GAAP earnings swing on property sales: net income per share was $0.26 in 2024 and $1.13 in 2025 (+335%), while FFOA grew 2%. In Q4 2025 net income ($0.67) exceeded FFO ($0.62) — which can only happen when gains on property sales outweigh depreciation, since FFO adds depreciation back and strips those gains out. That is why the trailing P/E of 21.4 says little about the business. Second, FFO versus FFOA: the "as adjusted" figure excludes items management treats as non-recurring — in H1 2026, $13.6M of legal and other costs and $8.8M of casualty charges, partly offset by a $14.2M gain on real-estate technology investments. Third, the balance sheet is being reshaped: with the pending sales, 2026 dispositions would total about $656.0M, and, per Multifamily Dive's call coverage, management is letting its Debt and Preferred Equity book "run off in the coming years." Both shrink near-term FFO even as buybacks shrink the share count — the 2026 FFO guidance midpoint was cut by $0.02 in July while FFOA was raised by $0.01. StockAnalysis's consensus 2026 EPS estimate of $0.55 also conflicts with company GAAP guidance of $1.03–$1.11 per diluted share (Q2 2026 release); the reason for the gap is not stated on either source, and the forward P/E it produces is not used here.

Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 13.4x FFOA and 15.3x AFFO for a 60,000-home portfolio that is 96.6% occupied, with a 5.13% dividend covered by AFFO at a 79% payout, 3.4% average debt cost and leverage of 5.6x EBITDAre. Management is selling old buildings to private buyers at mid-5% cap rates and buying back stock that trades 7% below where it bought in Q2. The supply wave that crushed Sun Belt rents is fading — management said so in both the February and July releases, raised 2026 same-store guidance in July, and told the September conference it expects positive rent earn-in into 2027. If FFOA simply starts growing again, a return to the ~17.5x multiple of late 2024 on $2.53 would be worth about $44 a share (our arithmetic, not a forecast) — close to the $41.45 average target.

Bear Case

Flat earnings for three years is not a supply story; it is a business that cannot grow per-share cash flow. Same-store NOI guidance of 0.00%–1.25% is barely positive, the Sun Belt still posts negative revenue, the Mid-Atlantic is exposed to federal job cuts, and market rents fell about 50bps in the 30 days before the September conference. Every disposition shrinks the earnings base, and each debt refinancing resets a 3.4% coupon toward today's rates. Three brokers trimmed targets between September 15 and 21, and a 13.4x multiple is no floor in a higher-rate world — the stock traded at $31.81 in 2023.

Top 3 Risks

01
The supply recovery arrives later than promised. Management has pointed to easing supply since February. If Sun Belt blended lease rates stay negative into 2027, the 60–100bps earn-in cited in September does not materialise and FFOA stays flat for a fourth year.
02
Interest rates. REIT prices move with the rate at which investors discount property income. A higher-for-longer path compresses the multiple further and raises refinancing cost on the $328.4M maturing through 2026 and the debt behind it — average maturity is only 3.9 years.
03
Shrinking to grow per share. With ~$656M of 2026 sales and buybacks funded from them, UDR is getting smaller. That works while private buyers pay more than the public market. If private cap rates rise — i.e. the private bid weakens — the arbitrage closes and the company is left smaller without being cheaper.
Industry Commentary
The Sun Belt supply wave
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The post-2021 building boom put large numbers of new apartments into Texas, Florida, Nashville and similar metros just as rent growth normalised. New buildings lease up with concessions, and the existing landlords nearby have to match. UDR's Q2 2026 numbers show the split plainly: the West +3.7% and Northeast +3.0% revenue, the Southeast and Southwest −1.0% each. Management's language has moved from defence to cautious optimism — Toomey in July cited "the resiliency of the economy, waning supply, and attractive relative affordability of apartments" — but the September conference, as summarised by Investing.com, still had Sun Belt blended lease rates negative and expected to improve only slightly in Q3.

Consolidation and the public–private gap
ONGOING HEADWIND

The AvalonBay–Equity Residential merger, closed on August 17, 2026 (Equity Residential survived and was renamed), created Vivmark Residential at roughly $47.5B of market value at the September 21 close (our calc.). Scale matters in apartments mainly through operating cost — technology, staffing, procurement — and UDR's claimed edge (43 homes per associate) is exactly the kind of advantage a bigger platform can compete away. At the same time, when private buyers pay more for buildings than the stock market implies, selling assets and buying back stock beats building new ones; UDR's program of selling older, lower-rent assets and buying back stock is the textbook response, and its decision to wind down the Debt and Preferred Equity lending business signals a narrowing to the core landlord role.

Affordability and retention — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

The bull framing: high home prices and mortgage rates keep renters renting. UDR's resident retention hit a seasonally adjusted all-time high of 60% in Q2 2026, and per Investing.com's conference summary its average resident is 37 years old. Longer tenancy means lower turnover cost and steadier occupancy, and "other income" (parking, fees, services), about 12% of revenue per the same summary, is expected to grow 5–7%, faster than same-store revenue. The bear framing: retention that high can also mean landlords are buying occupancy with modest renewals rather than pushing rent, and affordability cuts both ways — a renter who cannot afford to buy may also not be able to absorb rent increases. The test is the 2027 same-store revenue guidance, due with Q4 results.
