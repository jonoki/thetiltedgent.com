01 Company Overview

Universal Health Services runs two very different hospital businesses under one roof. It operates 30 inpatient acute care facilities (general hospitals: emergency rooms, surgery, obstetrics, cardiology) and more than 380 inpatient behavioral health facilities (psychiatric hospitals and residential treatment), plus about 170 outpatient and other sites across 40 US states, Washington D.C., Puerto Rico, Ireland and the United Kingdom, according to the company's own August 2026 description. FY2025 revenue was $17.37B, up 9.71%, with net income of $1.49B and EPS of $23.10, up 37.3%. At the September 21, 2026 close the whole company is valued at about $10.3B — roughly seven times trailing earnings.

The interesting thing about UHS is where the money comes from. The acute hospitals bring in more revenue, but the psychiatric hospitals earn most of the profit: in Q2 2026 behavioral health produced $410.8M of pre-tax income on $2.03B of revenue (20.3%), while acute care produced $227.7M on $2.61B (8.7%). The second thing is who is in charge. UHS has a dual-class structure: the shares you can buy on the NYSE are Class B, while founder Alan B. Miller alone holds 88.9% of the general voting power through Class A and Class C shares, and directors and executive officers as a group hold 91.8% (April 2026 proxy). Founder Alan B. Miller is Executive Chairman; Marc D. Miller is CEO. Public shareholders get the cash flows and very little say. The third thing is the stock itself. It closed November 2025 at $243.63 and May 2026 at $146.11 — down 40.0% in six months — a slide that ran alongside federal Medicaid changes set to shrink the supplemental payments UHS relies on (the sources fetched do not pin the move on a single cause). It has since recovered to $174.74. The question for a buyer at seven times earnings: is the Medicaid cliff already priced in, or is it the reason the multiple is seven?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

380+ inpatient behavioral facilities $7.14B net property & equipment Acute + behavioral in one system Talkspace virtual front door Founder control, long horizon Operations in 40 states + D.C., PR, UK, Ireland

The moat is physical and slow to copy. A licensed, staffed psychiatric hospital takes years to plan, build and fill, and UHS operates more than 380 inpatient behavioral facilities. The balance sheet shows $7.14B of net property and equipment at June 30, 2026. That footprint arguably gives it pricing weight with payers in behavioral health, which is where the 20% margins come from (our reading, not a sourced claim). The weakness is that a big share of that price is set by governments, not negotiated with private insurers. When a state or Washington changes the formula, the moat does not help. Management's answer is breadth: Investing.com's write-up of the CFO's September 2026 remarks describes UHS as "the only scaled provider with an end-to-end behavioral continuum" — virtual therapy, outpatient, inpatient. That is the company's framing, not an independently checked fact.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The Medicaid supplemental cliff. About $1.5B a year of revenue comes from supplemental Medicaid programs (per the CFO, as reported by Investing.com). The July 4, 2025 legislation limits the provider taxes that fund them; UHS's own release warns this "may be expected to reduce our revenues and likely increase the level of uncompensated care." The CFO's figure is about $500M of cuts over five years from 2028. Supplemental payments likely carry little direct cost (our inference), so the EBITDA hit could be close to dollar-for-dollar.
02
Exchange coverage losses. The same legislation ended some ACA exchange premium tax credits after 2025. The CFO put the 2026 headwind at about $85M (up from a $75M original estimate) and described it as "an almost one-for-one swap" into uncompensated care (Investing.com).
03
Behavioral volume is flat. Behavioral adjusted admissions grew 0.5% in Q2 2026. A segment growing almost entirely through price is exposed when the biggest payer, government, stops raising price.
04
Governance and legal history. Class B holders elect only two of seven directors; the founder alone holds 88.9% of the vote. The behavioral business has also drawn regulatory scrutiny: in July 2020 UHS and related entities agreed to pay a combined $122M to settle False Claims Act allegations about medically unnecessary inpatient behavioral admissions (the US Department of Justice announcement, as quoted on Wikipedia). In Q2 2026 the company added $28M to its professional and general liability reserve.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 7.1x trailing earnings, 5.7x EV/EBITDA and an 8.2% free-cash-flow yield, you are buying the largest part of the profit, the behavioral segment with 20% pre-tax margins, cheaply. The Medicaid cuts are real but known, dated (from 2028) and sized: the CFO puts them at about $500M over five years, against a company earning $2.7B of EBITDA a year. In the meantime the business is growing revenue about 10% a year, acute volumes are rising (+2.9% adjusted admissions in Q2), the D.C. hospital should stop losing money, and management is shrinking the share count about 6–7% a year. Talkspace gives the behavioral franchise a way to reach patients outside the hospital. If the cliff turns out to be a slope, a 7x multiple is too low.

Bear Case

The low multiple is a warning, not an oversight. Behavioral revenue is growing on price (+6.1% per patient day) with flat volume (+0.5% admissions), and much of that price comes from governments that have just legislated limits on the programs funding it. Supplemental payments of ~$1.5B a year likely carry little direct cost (our inference), so cuts could fall almost straight to EBITDA. Exchange coverage losses are turning into uncompensated care, profit guidance was cut in July, and consensus expects 2026 EPS below the trailing figure. Class B holders cannot change any of this: the founder alone holds 88.9% of the vote. Cheap can stay cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
Government pricing. Roughly $1.5B a year of supplemental Medicaid revenue faces statutory limits from 2028, about $500M of cuts over five years per the CFO. Timing and size depend on state programs and CMS approvals, as the pending Florida approvals show.
02
Behavioral volume stagnation. Adjusted admissions up 0.5% in Q2 2026. If price growth slows and volume does not pick up, the segment that earns 64% of pre-tax income stops growing.
03
Governance, legal and liability exposure. 88.9% founder voting control (91.8% for insiders as a group), a $122M False Claims Act settlement in 2020, and a $28M liability-reserve increase in Q2 2026. Minority holders carry these risks with no vote to change course.
Industry Commentary
Federal Medicaid limits and the supplemental-payment economy
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

US hospitals that treat many Medicaid patients have increasingly been paid through state supplemental and "directed payment" programs, funded largely by taxes on the providers themselves that draw matching federal money. For UHS these programs are worth about $1.5B a year (CFO, via Investing.com). Legislation adopted July 4, 2025 adds work and community-service requirements to Medicaid eligibility, limits the provider fees used to raise federal matching money, and ended some exchange premium tax credits after 2025. UHS's release says these provisions "may be expected to reduce our revenues and likely increase the level of uncompensated care." The timing risk is visible even before 2028: Q2 2026 benefited from a $100M Florida catch-up for a period that ended in September 2025, and guidance assumes nothing further until CMS acts.

Behavioral health: price without volume
ONGOING HEADWIND

The question for behavioral health is converting demand into paid inpatient days. UHS's Q2 2026 behavioral same-facility admissions grew 0.5% while revenue per patient day grew 6.1%. The Talkspace purchase is the industry's direction in miniature: reach patients earlier, virtually and in outpatient settings, and move them up or down the levels of care. The CFO said UHS currently captures only 3–5% of step-down patients (those leaving inpatient care for lower-intensity treatment) and aims for double digits (Investing.com summary). Whether that lifts profit or only volume in a lower-margin setting is not yet known.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

UHS trades at about 7.1x trailing earnings and 5.7x EV/EBITDA, with debt at 1.84x EBITDA and a buyback that has shrunk the share count 6.69% in a year. The bull framing: the market has capitalised a phased, partial cut to one revenue line as if it were permanent damage to the whole company, and management is using the discount to retire stock (it bought 2.565M shares in the first half at about $174, almost exactly today's price). The bear framing: a hospital company whose best segment grows on government-set price, controlled by a family that cannot be outvoted, deserves a discount to HCA and has one. To judge between them, watch three reported numbers each quarter: behavioral adjusted admissions, the supplemental-payment line, and adjusted EBITDA against the $2.610–$2.717B guidance range.
