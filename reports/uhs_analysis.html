01 Company Overview

Universal Health Services runs two very different hospital businesses under one roof. It operates 30 inpatient acute care facilities (general hospitals: emergency rooms, surgery, obstetrics, cardiology) and more than 380 inpatient behavioral health facilities (psychiatric hospitals and residential treatment), plus about 170 outpatient and other sites across 40 US states, Washington D.C., Puerto Rico, Ireland and the United Kingdom, according to the company's own August 2026 description. FY2025 revenue was $17.37B, up 9.71%, with net income of $1.49B and EPS of $23.10, up 37.3%. At the October 30, 2026 close the whole company is valued at about $10.4B — roughly seven times trailing earnings.

The interesting thing about UHS is where the money comes from. The acute hospitals bring in more revenue, but the psychiatric hospitals earn most of the profit: in Q2 2026 behavioral health produced $410.8M of pre-tax income on $2.03B of revenue (20.3%), while acute care produced $227.7M on $2.61B (8.7%). The second thing is who is in charge. UHS has a dual-class structure: the shares you can buy on the NYSE are Class B, while founder Alan B. Miller alone holds 88.9% of the general voting power through Class A and Class C shares, and directors and executive officers as a group hold 91.8% (April 2026 proxy). Founder Alan B. Miller is Executive Chairman; Marc D. Miller is CEO. Public shareholders get the cash flows and very little say. The third thing is the stock itself. It closed November 2025 at $243.63 and May 2026 at $146.11 — down 40.0% in six months — a slide that ran alongside federal Medicaid changes set to shrink the supplemental payments UHS relies on. It has since recovered to $176.05, even after a 2.9% fall the day after its third-quarter report. The question for a buyer at seven times earnings: is the Medicaid cliff already priced in, or is it the reason the multiple is seven?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

380+ inpatient behavioral facilities $7.14B net property & equipment Acute + behavioral in one system Talkspace virtual front door Founder control, long horizon Operations in 40 states + D.C., PR, UK, Ireland

The moat is physical and slow to copy. A licensed, staffed psychiatric hospital takes years to plan, build and fill, and UHS operates more than 380 inpatient behavioral facilities. The balance sheet shows $7.14B of net property and equipment at June 30, 2026. That footprint arguably gives it pricing weight with payers in behavioral health, which is where the 20% margins come from. The weakness is that a big share of that price is set by governments, not negotiated with private insurers. When a state or Washington changes the formula, the moat does not help. Management's answer is breadth: at a September 2026 conference the CFO said Talkspace makes UHS "the only viable end-to-end continuum provider", from the lightest care (virtual therapy) up to inpatient hospital care (Investing.com transcript write-up, September 15, 2026). That is the company's framing.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The Medicaid supplemental cliff. About $1.5B a year of revenue comes from supplemental Medicaid programs (per the CFO, as reported by Investing.com). The July 4, 2025 legislation limits the provider taxes that fund them; UHS's own release warns this "may be expected to reduce our revenues and likely increase the level of uncompensated care." The CFO's figure is about $500M of cuts over five years from 2028; for 2027 he estimated a headwind of about $175M–$200M (October 28 call).
02
Exchange coverage losses. The same legislation ended some ACA exchange premium tax credits after 2025. The CFO put the 2026 headwind at about $85M (up from a $75M original estimate) and described it as "an almost one-for-one swap" into uncompensated care (Investing.com). On the October call he said the second-half run rate of about $50M should be treated as the minimum going into 2027.
03
Behavioral growth depends on price. Behavioral adjusted admissions grew 0.5% in Q2 2026 and 2.5% in Q3, while revenue per patient day growth slowed from 6.1% to 2.1%. A segment that grew mainly through price is exposed when the biggest payer, government, stops raising price.
04
Governance and legal history. Class B holders elect only two of seven directors; the founder alone holds 88.9% of the vote. The behavioral business has also drawn regulatory scrutiny: in July 2020 UHS and related entities agreed to pay a combined $122M to settle False Claims Act allegations about medically unnecessary inpatient behavioral admissions (US Department of Justice, July 10, 2020). In Q2 2026 the company added $28M to its professional and general liability reserve, and $26M in Q3, a quarter in which it also reached a tentative settlement of the Cumberland Hospital litigation (terms confidential; the company says a substantial portion is covered by commercial excess insurance and existing reserves).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

At 7.3x trailing earnings, 6.2x EV/EBITDA and a 10.8% free-cash-flow yield, you are buying the largest part of the profit, the behavioral segment with 20% pre-tax margins, cheaply. The Medicaid cuts are real but known, dated and sized: the CFO puts the 2028-onward cuts at about $500M over five years and the 2027 supplemental-payment headwind at about $175M–$200M, against a company earning $2.65B of EBITDA a year. In the meantime the business is still growing revenue (+5.3% in Q3), acute volumes are rising (+4.9% adjusted admissions in Q3), behavioral admissions picked up (+2.5%), the D.C. hospital is expected to stop losing money in the first half of 2027, and buybacks have cut the share count 6.83% in a year ($689.6M spent in the first nine months). Talkspace gives the behavioral franchise a way to reach patients outside the hospital. If the cliff turns out to be a slope, a 7x multiple is too low.

Bear Case

The low multiple is a warning, not an oversight. Profit is falling while revenue grows: Q3 adjusted EBITDA fell 6.9% and adjusted EPS fell 8% ($5.22 against $5.69), and management expects 2026 toward the lower end of its July ranges. Behavioral revenue grew mostly on price in the first half (+6.1% per patient day in Q2), much of it from governments that have just legislated limits on the programs funding it, and price growth slowed to +2.1% in Q3. Exchange coverage losses are turning into uncompensated care. Class B holders cannot change any of this: the founder alone holds 88.9% of the vote. Cheap can stay cheap.

Top 3 Risks

01
Government pricing. Roughly $1.5B a year of supplemental Medicaid revenue faces statutory limits from 2028, about $500M of cuts over five years per the CFO. Timing and size depend on state programs and CMS approvals, as the pending Florida approvals show.
02
Behavioral growth. Adjusted admissions rose 0.5% in Q2 2026 and 2.5% in Q3, while revenue per patient day growth slowed from 6.1% to 2.1%. If price growth keeps slowing and volume does not pick up faster, the segment that earned 64% of pre-tax income in Q2 stops growing.
03
Governance, legal and liability exposure. 88.9% founder voting control (91.8% for insiders as a group), a $122M False Claims Act settlement in 2020, and liability-reserve increases of $28M in Q2 2026 and $26M in Q3. Minority holders carry these risks with no vote to change course.
Industry Commentary
Federal Medicaid limits and the supplemental-payment economy
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

US hospitals that treat many Medicaid patients have increasingly been paid through state supplemental and "directed payment" programs, funded largely by taxes on the providers themselves that draw matching federal money. For UHS these programs are worth about $1.5B a year (CFO, via Investing.com). Legislation adopted July 4, 2025 adds work and community-service requirements to Medicaid eligibility, limits the provider fees used to raise federal matching money, and ended some exchange premium tax credits after 2025. UHS's release says these provisions "may be expected to reduce our revenues and likely increase the level of uncompensated care." The timing risk is visible even before 2028, and the CFO put the 2027 headwind at about $175M–$200M on the October 28 call, mostly earlier-period amounts booked in 2026: Q2 2026 benefited from a $100M Florida catch-up for a period that ended in September 2025, and guidance assumes nothing further until CMS acts.

Behavioral health: price without volume
ONGOING HEADWIND

The question for behavioral health is converting demand into paid inpatient days. UHS's behavioral same-facility admissions grew 0.5% in Q2 2026 and 2.5% in Q3, while revenue per patient day grew 6.1% and then 2.1%. The Talkspace purchase is the industry's direction in miniature: reach patients earlier, virtually and in outpatient settings, and move them up or down the levels of care. The CFO said in September that UHS captures only about 3–5% of step-down patients (those leaving inpatient care for lower-intensity treatment) and that this could eventually reach double digits (Investing.com transcript write-up, September 15, 2026). Whether that lifts profit or only volume in a lower-margin setting is not yet known.

The valuation gap — bull and bear framing
EMERGING TAILWIND

UHS trades at about 7.3x trailing earnings and 6.2x EV/EBITDA, with debt at 2.20x EBITDA and a buyback that has shrunk the share count 6.83% in a year. The bull framing: the market has capitalised a phased, partial cut to one revenue line as if it were permanent damage to the whole company, and management is using the discount to retire stock (it bought 3.915M shares in the first nine months at an average of about $176, almost exactly the October 30 price). The bear framing: a hospital company whose best segment grows on government-set price, controlled by a family that cannot be outvoted, deserves a discount to HCA and has one. To judge between them, watch three reported numbers each quarter: behavioral adjusted admissions, the supplemental-payment line, and adjusted EBITDA against the $2.610–$2.717B guidance range, where management now expects the lower end.
