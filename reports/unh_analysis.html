Section 01

Company Overview

UnitedHealth Group is two businesses bolted together: UnitedHealthcare, the largest health insurer in the United States, covering 48.5 million people; and Optum, a health-services conglomerate that owns the country's largest pharmacy benefit manager, its largest employed-physician network, and the plumbing that processes roughly one in three American medical claims. FY2025 revenue was $447.6B, up 11.8% year over year, with TTM revenue of $450.5B through June 2026 — the third-largest revenue base of any company in the world, behind only Walmart and Amazon.

The story of the last two years, though, is not growth — it is the collapse and partial repair of profitability. Net income fell from $22.4B in FY2023 to $12.1B in FY2025 as medical costs ran far ahead of premium pricing, Medicare Advantage risk-model changes bit, and the company absorbed the Change Healthcare cyberattack, a DOJ investigation, and a CEO change. FY2025 net margin bottomed at 2.86%. The Q2 2026 print — adjusted EPS of $6.38 versus a $4.90 consensus, and a medical care ratio of 86.7% against 89.4% a year earlier — was the clearest evidence yet that repricing is working. Management raised FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance to $19.50–$20.00.

Section 03

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

Scale & Purchasing Power Vertical Integration Claims Data Asset Regulatory Barriers Provider Network Density Employer Switching Costs Actuarial & Risk-Coding Capability Insurance Float

The moat is that UnitedHealth sits on both sides of most of its own transactions. A UnitedHealthcare member can see an Optum Health physician, fill a prescription through an Optum Rx mail pharmacy, and have the claim adjudicated on Change Healthcare rails — with the margin captured at every step and the underlying data feeding back into pricing and risk-coding models nobody else can replicate. Scale compounds this: at ~$450B of revenue, UNH negotiates hospital and drug rates that a regional plan simply cannot match, and a one-point improvement in medical cost ratio is worth roughly $3B of pre-tax earnings. Entry is effectively closed — state-by-state insurance licensing, capital reserve requirements, CMS contracting and multi-year Medicare Advantage Star Ratings history make greenfield competition impossible, and employer benefit contracts are sticky on three-year cycles. The company also enjoys classic insurance float: premiums arrive before claims are paid, funding ~$31B of cash and a buyback program.

The uncomfortable corollary: that same integration is precisely what regulators, plaintiffs' lawyers and both political parties have identified as the problem. The moat and the legal exposure are the same asset.

Key Vulnerabilities

Department of Justice criminal and civil investigations

UNH has confirmed it is under both criminal and civil DOJ investigation over Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment practices — specifically whether diagnosis codes were added to inflate CMS payments. This is not a settled-and-move-on matter: it goes to the mechanics of how the most profitable segment earns revenue. A negative resolution could bring a large monetary penalty, a corporate integrity agreement, and — most damaging — a forced change to coding practice that permanently lowers MA revenue per member. Timing is unknowable and headlines are the single largest source of single-day drawdown risk in this name.

Vertical integration under political attack

PBM reform bills targeting spread pricing and rebate retention have bipartisan support; separate proposals would force insurers to divest owned pharmacies. Optum Rx's own May 2026 move to transparent fee-based pricing is an acknowledgement that the old economics are not defensible. Meanwhile the FTC and state AGs continue to scrutinise insurer ownership of physician practices. Any structural remedy would hit the earnings engine, not just the growth rate.

Medical cost trend and the limits of repricing

The 2024–25 earnings collapse happened because utilisation — outpatient procedures, behavioural health, specialty drugs, GLP-1s — ran materially above pricing assumptions for six consecutive quarters. Management's own CFO has warned that Q2 2026's improved ratio reflects UNH pushing down an elevated number, not industry-wide relief. Insurers price annually; if trend surprises again mid-year, there is no lever to pull until the next bid cycle.

Membership attrition and reputational damage

UNH is shrinking on purpose, but shrinking is dangerous in a business with fixed administrative costs and Star Ratings tied to scale. Medicare Advantage is down 965,000 lives since year-end; ACA exchange membership is falling with the expiry of enhanced subsidies. Layer on the post-2024 public backlash over claim denials and prior authorisation — which drove the company's own May 2026 pledge to eliminate 30% of prior-authorisation requirements — and the brand is a liability in employer and Medicaid procurement in a way it was not five years ago.

Concentration in government-funded revenue

Roughly half of UnitedHealthcare's premium revenue ultimately comes from federal and state governments via Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and exchange subsidies. That is a single counterparty with the unilateral ability to change rates, audit methodology (RADV) and eligibility rules. Annual CMS rate notices are, functionally, the most important earnings input the company does not control.

• GAAP vs adjusted gap of ~$1.05/share in FY2026 guidance ($18.45–$18.95 GAAP vs $19.50–$20.00 adjusted) is almost entirely amortisation of acquisition-related intangibles — a recurring consequence of two decades of dealmaking, not a true one-off.

The five-year trajectory

From $460.47 at the end of October 2021 to $375.93 on September 15, 2026, UNH has lost about 18% on price over five years — still negative once dividends are added back. Over the same window the S&P 500 roughly doubled. An investor who bought the largest, most dominant healthcare company in the world at the start of the period and held has lost money in nominal terms while taking a 60% drawdown along the way. That is the whole lesson of this chart.

The path was not a slow grind. It was a three-year bull market, an eight-month collapse, and then a second, smaller collapse most people have already forgotten. From late 2021 through November 2024 the stock compounded from ~$460 to a month-end record of $610.20 (an intraday high near $630), on Medicare Advantage growth, Optum acquisitions and margins near 8.8% at the operating line. Then, between the November 2024 close and the July 2025 close of $249.56, it lost 59% — a sequence of a murdered division CEO and a public backlash, a 22% single-day guidance cut, a CEO resignation, suspended guidance and a reported DOJ criminal probe, each landing within weeks of the last. The recovery off the ~$237 August 2025 intraday low ran to $345 by September — and then failed: the stock slid through the winter to a $270.59 close in March 2026, setting the current 52-week low of $255.97 along the way, before an extraordinary +37% April (to $370.48) around the Q1 print and the 2027 Medicare Advantage rate cycle. July 2026's $414.40 month-end was the recovery high; the two months since have given back $38 of it.

The moving averages tell a clean version of this. The 10-month line (the "200-day" proxy) capped every rally from December 2024 into early 2026 and was reclaimed decisively only in April 2026; at ~$353 it still sits below the current price, so the longer trend has not broken. The 3-month line (~$393) has, though — price closed September 15 below it for the first time since the spring. Monthly RSI(6) bottomed near 20 in July 2025 and near 29 in March 2026 — two genuine capitulation readings in nine months — peaked around 66 in July, and now sits near 52: dead neutral. The momentum surge is spent, but nothing in the monthly data yet says the trend has reversed.

Summary & Key Risks

BULL CASE

UnitedHealth is the lowest-cost, most vertically integrated operator in a structurally growing $1.6T market, and it is emerging from a two-year margin reset with pricing discipline restored: MCR improved 270 bp year over year, operating margin at UnitedHealthcare doubled to 4.6%, and FY26 adjusted EPS guidance was raised to $19.50–$20.00. At 17.5x forward earnings — down from 19.5x in early August after a 10% slide on no new company news — for a business generating ~$24B of operating cash flow, paying a 2.5% dividend with 16 years of growth, and buying back at least $5B of stock, the market is paying a shrinking premium for a franchise whose normalised ROE is closer to 20–25% than today's 14%.

If 2027 marks the return to a clean double-digit EPS algorithm off a $20 base — and Berkshire's continued ownership suggests at least one very patient investor thinks so — the stock is worth the $475–$490 the sell side has on it — a 26–30% gap to the Sep 15 close — with optionality in a sum-of-the-parts where Optum Insight alone earns a 26% margin.

BEAR CASE

The recovery is being bought at 17.5x forward earnings on a company whose revenue is guided to shrink in 2026, whose largest profit pool is shedding 1.1M members, and whose most profitable practice — Medicare Advantage risk coding — is the explicit subject of an open DOJ criminal investigation with no timetable and no quantifiable outcome. Margin recovery achieved by exiting customers is finite; you cannot shrink to a growth multiple forever.

The July 16 tape is the warning: a 30% earnings beat with raised guidance produced a 1.2% close after opening up 9%. That is a stock where good news is already in the price — and the two months since, in which the shares fell 11% from the earnings-day close with no guidance change, no DOJ headline and no downgrade of substance, say the market is now pricing something the Street's $475 average target is not. Meanwhile PBM reform, prior-authorisation concessions and RADV audits each chip at economics that were never politically durable, and management's own CFO says the cost trend has not improved — only UNH's pricing against it.

Top 3 Risks

  1. DOJ resolution on Medicare Advantage risk adjustmentAn adverse criminal or civil outcome could combine a large penalty with mandated coding changes that permanently reduce revenue per Medicare Advantage member — the highest-margin revenue in the company. Because timing and magnitude are both unknowable, the market cannot price it, which is precisely why headline risk here dominates day-to-day volatility.
  2. Medical cost trend reaccelerating before the next bid cycleQ2's 86.7% MCR sits well below full-year guidance of 88.1%, implying management expects H2 to deteriorate. Insurers can only reprice annually; a repeat of 2024–25, when utilisation ran above assumptions for six straight quarters, would break the recovery narrative with no lever available until the 2028 bids.
  3. Structural regulation of vertical integrationPBM reform, forced pharmacy divestiture proposals, prior-authorisation mandates and FTC scrutiny of insurer-owned physician practices all target the same thing: UNH's ability to capture margin at every step of its own claims. Optum Rx's voluntary move to transparent fee-based pricing signals management believes the old economics are indefensible — which means some of them are already being given away.

Industry Commentary

Medical cost trend is still the only number that matters

Critical Constraint

Every managed-care thesis since 2023 reduces to one question: is utilisation normalising or permanently higher? Post-pandemic deferred care, an aging Medicare Advantage cohort, behavioural health expansion, outpatient surgical volume and GLP-1 spend all pushed industry medical cost ratios up 200–400 bp between 2023 and 2025. UNH's MCR went from ~83.2% in FY2022 to 89.4% in Q2 2025 before recovering to 86.7% in Q2 2026 — but the company's own full-year guide of 88.1% ± 25 bp implies H2 2026 runs around 89%. CFO Wayne DeVeydt has been explicit that the improvement reflects UNH "pushing down what is already an elevated number," not sector-wide relief. On a $450B revenue base, 100 bp of MCR is roughly $3B pre-tax. Nothing else in the model has that leverage.

Managed care has become a political asset class

Ongoing Headwind

Prior authorisation, claim denial rates, PBM spread pricing, insurer-owned physician networks and Medicare Advantage upcoding are now bipartisan targets, and the December 2024 killing of Brian Thompson converted a policy debate into a public one. The industry's response has been pre-emptive concession: UnitedHealthcare pledged in May 2026 to remove 30% of prior authorisations immediately and two-thirds of paediatric ones by year-end; Optum Rx replaced traditional PBM pricing with a transparent fee model. Each move reduces legislative tail risk and reduces earnings. Meanwhile the expiry of enhanced ACA subsidies is pushing an estimated 500,000 members off UNH exchange plans in 2026 alone — a reminder that half of this industry's revenue is set by legislators, not customers.

The margin-over-membership pivot — and what it means for 2027

Emerging Tailwind

For fifteen years the entire sector optimised for membership growth, on the theory that scale eventually converts to margin. The 2024–25 reset ended that. UNH is now voluntarily shedding 1.1M Medicare Advantage lives, 500,000 exchange lives and ~700,000 value-based care patients, exiting counties and redesigning benefits — and its operating margin doubled while doing so. The bull framing: this is a one-time purge that leaves a smaller, structurally more profitable book, and 2027 becomes the first clean year since 2023, supporting a return to a low-teens EPS growth algorithm off a ~$20 base and justifying the current 17.5x forward multiple. The bear framing: shrinking is not a strategy, fixed administrative costs and Star Ratings both scale with membership, competitors are taking the lives UNH gives up, and a company guided to negative revenue growth should not trade at a ~25% premium to its peer group. The December 2026 investor day and the October 2026 Star Ratings release will settle which framing the market adopts.
