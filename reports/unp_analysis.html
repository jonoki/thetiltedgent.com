01 Company Overview

Union Pacific operates the largest railroad franchise in the western United States — roughly 32,200 route miles across 23 states west of Chicago and New Orleans, hauling agricultural goods, chemicals, coal, metals, forest products, automobiles and containerized freight. Fiscal 2025 operating revenue was $24.51B, up 1.1% YoY, with net income of $7.14B and diluted EPS of $11.98 (+8%); the trailing-twelve-month figures have since accelerated to $25.41B of revenue (+4.2%) and $12.35 of TTM EPS. It is the 68th most valuable public company in the United States and roughly #124 globally by market capitalization, and the largest of the 12 US-listed railroad stocks, representing about a third of the group's combined $553B market value.

The company is in the middle of the most consequential corporate event in modern American railroading: its proposed ~$85 billion acquisition of Norfolk Southern, which would create the first single-line transcontinental railroad in US history. That transaction — announced July 29, 2025 and now in a formal Surface Transportation Board evidentiary proceeding with a decision targeted for the second quarter of 2027 — dominates the investment case, the risk profile, and the capital allocation policy (buybacks have been effectively halted to fund the cash portion of the consideration).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
IRREPLACEABLE RIGHT-OF-WAY WESTERN DUOPOLY vs BNSF 32,200 ROUTE MILES / 23 STATES CAPTIVE SHIPPER PRICING 4x FUEL EFFICIENCY vs TRUCK REGULATORY ENTRY BARRIER ROIC 16.05% ON $208B EV

Union Pacific's moat is the physical network itself. The 32,200 route-mile western system was assembled from 19th-century land grants and a century of consolidation and could not be rebuilt at any price — the land assembly, environmental permitting and grade engineering alone are prohibitive, and the Surface Transportation Board's control rules make new Class I entry effectively impossible. West of the Mississippi the practical competitive set is Union Pacific and BNSF; large stretches of chemical, coal and grain origination are served by a single railroad, which is why "captive shipper" pricing is a live regulatory issue rather than a theoretical one. The economics that fall out of this structure are conspicuous: a gross margin of 56.28%, an operating margin of 40.81%, a net profit margin of 28.85%, ROIC of 16.05% and ROE of 39.70%, all on a business whose principal asset is dirt, steel and dispatching software. Pricing power is the durable part: even in a flat-volume 2025, freight revenue excluding fuel surcharge grew 3% on 1% carload growth, and management's stated 2026 objective remains "pricing dollars in excess of inflation."

KEY VULNERABILITIES

1. Regulatory capture of the merger thesis. The Norfolk Southern deal is not a shareholder vote — that already passed with over 99% support in November 2025 — it is an STB adjudication. The Board rejected the original application as incomplete in January 2026, accepted a revised filing in May 2026 only with supplemental-information conditions, and on August 18, 2026 adopted a 395-day evidentiary period, explicitly "the longest evidentiary period permitted by law", rejecting the applicants' request for an expedited schedule. Every month of delay erodes the present value of the $2.75B synergy claim and extends the period during which Union Pacific's own capital allocation is frozen.

2. Volume is not in management's control. Roughly 60% of freight revenue rides on the industrial economy and agricultural exports. FY2025 carloads grew 1%; Q2 2026 grew 2%. Bulk carloads actually declined 1% in Q2 2026, and management flagged an uncertain second-half volume outlook. Much of the headline 12% Q2 freight revenue growth was fuel surcharge — ex-fuel freight revenue grew only 4%, meaning roughly eight of the twelve points came from a diesel-indexed, broadly margin-neutral pass-through.

3. Trucking and tariff exposure in Premium. Intermodal is the one book where a determined competitor can take share overnight. Cheap diesel, loose truckload capacity, or a collapse in West Coast import volumes all hit Premium directly; the group already shrank 2% in FY2025. Premium is also where tariff policy transmits fastest, because international intermodal is a derivative of containerised imports through Los Angeles/Long Beach, Oakland, Seattle and Tacoma.

4. Balance sheet and buyback dependence. Union Pacific carries $31.17B of gross debt against $2.11B of cash, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.51 and a current ratio of 0.99. Much of that leverage exists because the company retired shares aggressively for a decade — $2,679M of buybacks in FY2025 alone. That flywheel has stopped: only $26M of repurchases year-to-date in 2026 versus $2,679M in the comparable 2025 period. Roughly a third of historical EPS growth came from share count reduction, and that contribution is now near zero while the merger is pending.

NOTABLE ONE-OFF ITEMS

Reported GAAP results carry several material non-recurring distortions that materially change the trend line:

Industrial-park land sales (FY2025): +$234M pre-tax, +$0.30 to EPS. Recognised in Q4 2025, this is the single largest item in the year. It is why reported FY2025 EPS of $11.98 (+8%) overstates the underlying result versus adjusted EPS of $11.66 (+5%), and why reported full-year OR of 59.8% (−10 bps) looks worse on an adjusted basis at 59.3% (−60 bps) — the adjusted number is actually the better one here because the gain sat in revenue.

Merger-related costs: $30M in Q4 2025 (−$0.05 EPS) and $35M in Q2 2026 (−$0.05 EPS). Advisory, legal and regulatory expense tied to the Norfolk Southern transaction. Q2 2026 reported EPS was $3.36 versus adjusted $3.41; reported OR 59.7% versus adjusted 59.2%. These costs will continue to accrue through the entire 395-day STB evidentiary period and should be treated as a run-rate drag, not a true one-off, until a decision lands.

Deferred tax adjustment (FY2025): −$115M. A discrete tax item that reduced reported net income without any change in cash taxes or operations.

Buyback discontinuity. Not an accounting item, but the most important non-operating change in the numbers: repurchases collapsed from $2,679M in the first half of 2025 to $26M in the first half of 2026 as cash is preserved for the $88.82-per-share cash component of the Norfolk Southern consideration. Any model that extrapolates historical EPS growth without removing the buyback contribution will overstate 2026–2027 earnings.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast and may be stretched; readings below 30 mean it has fallen hard and may be washed out. Neither is a signal on its own — it is a measure of momentum, not of value.

Five-year trajectory. From a September 2021 close of $196.01 to $301.80 on August 19, 2026, Union Pacific returned +54.0% on price alone over the five-year window, roughly +9.0% annualised; reinvested dividends at a ~1.9% yield add approximately 10–12 percentage points (est.), for a total return in the mid-60s. Over the identical window the S&P 500 (measured by SPY closes) rose from 429.14 to 776.34, or +80.9%. Union Pacific therefore underperformed the index by roughly 27 percentage points over five years — and essentially all of that underperformance was incurred between March 2022 and mid-2025. The shape matters more than the endpoint: the stock spent three full years going nowhere before the merger re-rated it.

The path splits cleanly into three regimes. Regime one (Sep 2021 – Mar 2022): a melt-up to a $273.21 close in March 2022 on pandemic-era pricing, congestion-driven surcharges and record fuel recovery. Regime two (Mar 2022 – Jul 2025): a three-and-a-half-year dead zone. Rate hikes compressed the multiple, a genuine freight recession suppressed carloads, and service problems drew regulatory attention; the stock closed as low as $192.52 in May 2023 and was still only $221.97 in July 2025 — 19% below its March 2022 close more than three years later, while the S&P 500 gained 40% over the same stretch. Regime three (Aug 2025 – present): the merger trade. From the July 29, 2025 announcement the stock has compounded steadily: $223.57 (Aug 2025) → $231.32 (Dec 2025) → $264.98 (Feb 2026) → $272.00 (Jun 2026) → $292.13 (Jul 2026) → $301.80. Year-to-date 2026 UNP is +30.5% versus +13.8% for the S&P 500, and +37.1% over the trailing 52 weeks. The entire relative-performance story of this stock right now is a regulatory one.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

An irreplaceable 32,200-mile western duopoly network throwing off 28.85% net margins, 16.05% ROIC and $6.50B of free cash flow, trading at 24.43x — below both its own industry (26.37x) and the S&P 500 (28.00x). Operations are demonstrably improving: Q2 2026 delivered a 59.7% operating ratio with record freight car velocity of 231 miles per day and terminal dwell down to 19.7 hours, while freight revenue rose 12% and adjusted EPS 13%. On top of that sits free optionality: STB approval of the Norfolk Southern deal creates the first US transcontinental single-line railroad — 50,000+ route miles, 43 states, ~100 ports — with $2.75B of claimed annualised synergies against a combined enterprise value above $250B. Rejection returns a $2.5B fee and restarts a buyback that historically retired ~1.5% of shares a year.

Bear Case

Strip the fuel surcharge out and Q2 2026 freight revenue grew just 4% on 2% carload growth — this is a low-single-digit volume business whose reported acceleration is largely a diesel-indexed pass-through. Bulk carloads actually fell 1%, management flagged an uncertain second-half volume outlook, and the buyback that supplied roughly a third of historical EPS growth has been switched off ($26M year-to-date versus $2,679M a year ago) with $31.17B of debt and a 0.99 current ratio behind it. Meanwhile the STB has imposed the longest evidentiary period the law allows — 395 days — against organised opposition from BNSF, CSX, seven shipper associations, the National Grain and Feed Association, nine state attorneys general and two rail unions that withdrew support. A 2.34 PEG and 4.5% below the 52-week high means much of the good outcome is already in the price; the bad outcome is not.

TOP 3 RISKS
01
STB rejection or value-destroying conditions. This is the dominant risk and it is binary, non-diversifiable and roughly nine months from resolution. The Board has already rejected one application outright, held the revised one in abeyance, forced the disclosure of confidential employee data, and then adopted the maximum permissible evidentiary schedule. Conditions such as forced trackage rights, divestitures or open-gateway mandates could preserve the deal while destroying most of the $2.75B synergy claim — the worst of both outcomes, since the $2.5B reverse termination fee is only payable on rejection or unworkable conditions.
02
Volume and industrial-economy exposure. Carloads grew 1% in FY2025 and 2% in Q2 2026, with Bulk down 1% in the most recent quarter. Roughly two-thirds of freight revenue is levered to the industrial and agricultural economy, and Premium — 28.7% of FY2025 revenue — is levered to containerised imports through West Coast ports, making it the fastest transmission channel for tariff and trade-flow shocks. A fuel-price decline would simultaneously remove the surcharge revenue that flattered 2026 and improve trucking's competitive position against intermodal.

Financial flexibility during the pendency period. $31.17B of gross debt, $29.06B net debt, 1.51 debt-to-equity, a 0.99 current ratio and a suspended buyback while $3.2B a year goes out the door in dividends. Rail Consolidation Meets a Regulator That Has Not Approved a Class I Merger Since 2001
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

The UP–NS transaction is the first major rail merger to be judged under the Surface Transportation Board's post-2001 rules, which require applicants to demonstrate that a combination enhances competition — not merely that it does not harm it. That is a materially higher bar than standard antitrust review, and it was written precisely in response to the service meltdowns that followed the last wave of consolidation. The procedural history to date reads as an unusually skeptical Board: the original December 19, 2025 application was rejected as incomplete on January 16, 2026; the revised April 30 filing was accepted on May 28, 2026 but immediately placed in abeyance pending supplemental information due July 27; on July 22 the Board ordered the applicants to make employee data public over their confidentiality objections; and on August 18, 2026 it removed the case from abeyance and adopted a 395-day evidentiary period — expressly "the longest evidentiary period permitted by law" — rejecting the railroads' request for expedition, on the reasoning that the nation's first transcontinental railroad could have "far-reaching effects on the rail industry, shippers, and the public." The formal opposition is broad and well-funded: BNSF Railway, CSX, the Reading Blue Mountain & Northern, the Freight Rail Customer Alliance and the National Grain and Feed Association all pushed for the extended timeline; seven shipper associations, the American Chemistry Council and the Soy Transportation Coalition have warned of cost increases; nine state attorneys general raised national-security objections in November 2025; and BLET and BMWED withdrew union support in December 2025. The second-order concern the Board must weigh is endgame: approving UP–NS makes a BNSF–CSX response nearly inevitable, leaving roughly 90% of US freight rail with two companies.

Freight Recession Versus Recovery — and How Much of the Recovery Is Just Diesel
ONGOING HEADWIND

North American freight has been in a volume recession for most of three years, and Union Pacific's own numbers show how shallow the recovery is. FY2025: revenue +1.1%, carloads +1%, freight revenue ex-fuel +3%. Q2 2026 looks dramatically better on the surface — freight revenue +12%, total carloads 2,163K (+2%) — but ex-fuel-surcharge freight revenue grew only 4%, meaning roughly eight of the twelve percentage points came from a diesel-indexed pass-through that broadly offsets the company's own $938M quarterly fuel bill. Underneath, the mix is uneven: Premium carloads +4% and revenue +21%, Industrial carloads +3%, but Bulk carloads −1%. Management explicitly signalled an uncertain second-half volume outlook even while raising full-year reported EPS growth guidance to a high-single-digit range. What is unambiguously improving is the network: freight car velocity of 231 daily miles per car (+5%), terminal dwell of 19.7 hours (−7%), locomotive productivity of 142 gross ton-miles per horsepower-day (+1%) and workforce productivity of 1,176 car miles per employee (+5%), all building on FY2025 records. Service improvement is what converts pricing power into operating-ratio gains — and at $24.5B of revenue, each 100 basis points of OR is worth roughly $245M of operating income.

Tariffs, West Coast Ports and the Intermodal Swing Factor — Bull Versus Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND

Premium is where trade policy meets the income statement. International intermodal originates as containers landing at Los Angeles/Long Beach, Oakland, Seattle and Tacoma and moving inland on UP and BNSF; tariff escalation in early 2025 coincided with UNP's worst month of the five-year window (April 2025, $215.66) and a 2% decline in full-year Premium revenue. The 2026 reversal has been sharp — Premium revenue +21% and carloads +4% in Q2 — but the driver is disputable between restocking, tariff front-running and genuine truck-to-rail conversion.

Bull framing: intermodal is rail's largest untapped share pool, rail is roughly four times more fuel-efficient than trucking per ton-mile, and a merged UP–NS network promises 24–48 hours of transit-time reduction on approximately one million shipments a year across ~100 ports and 43 states — precisely the service improvement that unlocks highway conversion. Management attributes about $1.75B of the $2.75B synergy target to exactly this revenue opportunity in central-US "watershed" markets, with roughly $1B from cost and productivity.

Bear framing: those synergy figures are company claims that have not been tested in the STB record, they assume a merger that a skeptical Board will not rule on until the second quarter of 2027, and Premium's $1,694 FY2025 average revenue per car is 44% of Industrial's $3,840 — meaning intermodal growth dilutes yield even when it works. If diesel falls, both the surcharge revenue and the intermodal cost advantage over trucking shrink at the same time.
