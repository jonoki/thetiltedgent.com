01 Company Overview

United Parcel Service is the largest package delivery company in the world by revenue, moving parcels, freight and healthcare shipments through an integrated air-and-ground network across more than 200 countries and territories. Full-year 2025 revenue was $88.7 billion, down 2.65% from $91.07 billion in 2024 and down roughly 12% from the 2022 pandemic peak of $100.3 billion — the third consecutive year of flat-to-negative top line. UPS ranks #138 in the S&P 500 by market capitalisation (October 28, 2026), and captured roughly 34% of US parcel industry revenue ($69.8B in 2024) on only ~21% of volume, a spread that is the entire point of the current strategy.

The company is in the middle of the largest network restructuring in its history. Since the start of 2025 it has removed roughly 78,000 positions, closed about 150 buildings, taken out ~50 million operational hours and eliminated approximately $4.5 billion of expense — all while deliberately shedding around 2 million Amazon packages per day. Management declared that "glide-down" complete with its 2Q 2026 results (July 28, 2026). With third-quarter results on October 27, 2026 it raised full-year guidance again, to ~$92.6B revenue, ~$8.75B adjusted operating profit, ~$7.28 adjusted EPS and ~$6.1B of adjusted free cash flow (the cash left after capital spending) — more than the ~$5.4B it plans to pay in dividends this year.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of moat:
INTEGRATED AIR + GROUND NETWORK US RESIDENTIAL DENSITY ~460,000 EMPLOYEES ~$3B/YR CAPEX & AUTOMATION HEALTHCARE COLD CHAIN — 17M SQ FT B2B CONTRACT BOOK & SLAs CUSTOMS / BROKERAGE SCALE

UPS's advantage is a single integrated network that carries residential, commercial, freight and healthcare volume over the same assets — the reason it can convert ~21% of US parcel volume into ~34% of US parcel revenue. Density is the mechanism: each additional stop on an existing route is close to pure contribution margin, which is why the company can afford to give away 2 million low-yield Amazon packages a day and still widen U.S. Domestic adjusted margin (by ~100 basis points in 2Q 2026 and 60 in 3Q). The moat is reinforced by capital intensity (~$3.0B of capex per year), by regulatory and customs infrastructure that a new entrant cannot replicate quickly, and by the healthcare franchise, where cGMP/GDP-certified space, validated cold-chain lanes and pharma qualification cycles create genuine switching costs. That last piece is where UPS is deliberately reallocating capital — Andlauer at ≈$1.6B was the largest expression of it.

Key vulnerabilities:

1. Customer concentration inverting into competition. Amazon was over 13% of UPS volume at the pandemic peak and is now roughly 9%. But Amazon Logistics delivered 6.7 billion US parcels in 2025 (+9.8%), overtaking USPS to become the largest US parcel carrier — and it is increasingly selling third-party shipping. The Big Three (USPS, UPS, FedEx) now handle about 61% of ~23.9 billion annual US parcels, down from ~85% pre-pandemic. UPS chose to exit low-margin Amazon work; Amazon did not stop growing.

2. A unionised cost base its largest competitors do not carry. The Teamsters national agreement runs through July 31, 2028 and delivers $7.50/hour of increases to seniority employees over 2023–2028, plus cost-of-living escalators — Teamsters who completed progression received a $1.22/hour raise on August 1, 2026, including a $0.22 COLA tied to 4.4% May-to-May inflation. Part-time starting pay is $21/hour rising to $23/hour after August 1, 2027, and UPS committed to at least 22,500 permanent full-time openings and 28,000 replacement vehicles. Amazon Logistics' delivery-service-partner model and USPS carry structurally different labour economics.

3. Dividend coverage. The declared annual dividend of $6.56 exceeds TTM GAAP EPS of $5.20, a payout ratio of 126.15%. Cash is in better shape than earnings: TTM free cash flow per share is $7.46 (StockAnalysis), and on October 27 UPS raised its 2026 free-cash-flow guide to ~$6.1B against ~$5.4B of planned dividends — a cushion of about $0.7B, where the July guide (~$5.5B) left almost none. The gap between earnings and cash is the core of the debate over whether the stock is cheap or cheap for a reason.

4. Regulatory and trade-policy exposure. The elimination of the de minimis duty exemption re-routed a large slice of Asia-origin e-commerce; tariffs continue to reshape lane mix. International operating profit grew year over year in 3Q 2026 ($746M against $691M adjusted), the quarter UPS lapped the change, but the same mechanism can reverse. UPS also flies US Postal Service air cargo, which it was onboarding through the third quarter of 2024 (UPS Q3 2024 earnings release).

Notable One-Off Items — the gap between GAAP and adjusted results is unusually wide right now, and reported earnings should not be taken at face value.

3Q 2026 items. GAAP results include a net charge of $279 million ($0.32 per diluted share): after-tax transformation costs of $130 million ($171 million pre-tax, $0.15/share) and a $182 million goodwill impairment in Supply Chain Solutions ($0.21/share), partly offset by a $33 million after-tax gain on selling MD-11 aircraft assets. That is why GAAP EPS was $1.37 against $1.69 adjusted. The year-earlier quarter carried a $330 million pre-tax sale-leaseback gain in Supply Chain Solutions ($0.30/share).

2Q 2026 transformation charge. UPS booked $1,172 million pre-tax / $891 million after-tax ($1.05 per diluted share) under "Network Reconfiguration & Efficiency Reimagined," principally employee separation costs from the completed Driver Choice Program (a voluntary buyout aimed at 7,500 full-time driver positions). This drove U.S. Domestic GAAP operating profit to $16M against $1,188M adjusted, and consolidated GAAP EPS to $0.71 against $1.76 adjusted.

Full-year 2026 charge guidance. Management now expects $1.4–$1.6 billion of transformation costs for 2026 (up from $1.3–$1.5 billion in July), of which $1.2 billion relates to the Driver Choice Program. Through September 30, 2026 it had incurred $1.4 billion in 2026 and $1.9 billion in total, and the Efficiency Reimagined initiative is not expected to conclude until the fourth quarter of 2027.

FY2025 items. A $182 million pre-tax ($137 million after-tax, $0.16/share) impairment on retirement of the MD-11 aircraft fleet; total transformation costs of $593 million ($0.53/share after tax — Transformation 2.0 $37M, Fit to Serve $47M, Network Reconfiguration $509M); and a +$109 million ($0.13/share) benefit from reversal of an income-tax valuation allowance.

M&A and divestitures. The Andlauer Healthcare Group acquisition closed November 3, 2025 for approximately US$1.6 billion, adding inorganic revenue to Supply Chain Solutions and healthcare from 4Q 2025 onward — 2026 segment growth rates are therefore not fully organic. UPS sold its Coyote Logistics truckload brokerage in the third quarter of 2024 (UPS Q3 2024 earnings release). Supply Chain Solutions took the $182 million goodwill impairment above in 3Q 2026. Pension remeasurement has historically been a recurring 4Q distortion for UPS, so the 4Q 2026 report warrants attention.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 means the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 means it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a forecast.

Five-year trajectory. The chart starts at the October 2021 month-end close of $213.47. UPS closed October 28, 2026 at $105.62 — a −50.5% price return over the window, before dividends. The monthly-close peak was $214.46 in March 2022; from there to the September 2025 monthly-close low of $83.53 the stock lost 61%. That is not a growth de-rating — it is a full cycle unwind of pandemic parcel economics, layered on top of a self-inflicted revenue reduction.

The last twelve months have been a partial recovery: +9.6% from the October 28, 2025 close, and +26.4% off the September 2025 monthly-close low. September 2026 alone cost 10.4% (to $93.44); the 3Q beat on October 27 added 5.75% in one session. Relative performance. Over the trailing year UPS's +9.6% price change lagged SPY's +12.4% and QQQ's +14.6% (price returns, October 28, 2025 close to October 28, 2026 close, Yahoo Finance). Over the chart's window the gap is far larger: UPS −50.5% against SPY +68.2% (October 2021 month-end to the October 28, 2026 close, price only). UPS is a recovery that has only just begun to show in the price, not a steady long-term winner that stumbled.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The hard part is done. UPS removed ~2 million low-margin Amazon packages a day, ~78,000 positions, ~150 buildings, ~50 million hours and ~$4.5 billion of cost — and U.S. Domestic adjusted margin is still widening (up ~100bp to 8.0% in 2Q, up 60bp to 7.0% in 3Q) as revenue per piece outruns cost per piece. Guidance was raised at both the 2Q and 3Q prints, now ~$92.6B / ~$8.75B / ~$7.28, with ~$6.1B of free cash flow that covers the ~$5.4B dividend. International is back to profit growth with revenue per piece up 15.0%, and healthcare tops $3B a quarter, reinforced by the $1.6B Andlauer deal. At a 13.26 forward P/E — about 1.9 points below FedEx's and well below the index's — you are paid a 6.21% yield to wait for 2027 consensus EPS of $8.09.

Bear Case

The dividend costs more than the company earns. $6.56 per share against $5.20 TTM GAAP EPS is a 126.15% payout, on a balance sheet at 2.02 debt-to-equity, and the cash cover rests on a ~$6.1B free-cash-flow guide with about $0.7B to spare. The 3Q print added a $182M goodwill write-down in Supply Chain Solutions, the fastest-growing segment this quarter (revenue +18.2%). Net margin has fallen from 13.25% (2021) to 4.84%, U.S. Domestic volume is still shrinking (−3.3% in 3Q), and the customer UPS fired — Amazon Logistics — became the largest US parcel carrier in 2025 at 6.7 billion parcels and is taking third-party freight. The Big Three's share of US parcels has fallen from ~85% to 61%. Teamsters escalators run to 2028 regardless. A yield above 6% on a 126% payout is not a value signal; it is the market pricing a cut.

Top 3 Risks
01
Dividend rebasing. The payout is 126.15% of GAAP earnings even though TTM free cash flow of $7.46 a share now covers the $6.56 dividend. Institutions hold 63.87% of the shares, and funds that own the stock for its income could sell well beyond the arithmetic of a smaller payout. The board's next declaration is the single highest-variance event in the story.
02
The yield gains prove to be a mix artefact. U.S. Domestic revenue per piece rose 9.3% in 2Q 2026 and 6.1% in 3Q, largely because two million of the cheapest packages left the network. The planned Amazon cuts finished in 2Q, so by mid-2027 the comparison resets, and the underlying question — can UPS raise price on the volume it kept, against a growing Amazon Logistics and alternative carriers up 13% — is still open. U.S. Domestic volume fell 3.3% in 3Q.
03
Operating leverage cuts both ways in a stripped-down network. After ~150 building closures and 50 million hours removed, UPS has far less capacity slack. Management's Q4 plan assumes US daily volume up about 25% from Q3; a soft peak, a tariff shock, or an unexpected volume surge into a thinned network would all damage margin, and $1.4–$1.6B of 2026 transformation charges are still flowing through GAAP results while savings arrive on a lag (~$1.8B realised in the first nine months versus nearly $3B expected for the year).
  Industry Commentary
Parcel volume and pricing after the e-commerce normalisation
ONGOING HEADWIND
The US parcel market grew to 22.4 billion shipments and $203.2 billion of revenue in 2024 — volume up just 3.4% and revenue up 2.7%, with revenue per parcel falling to $9.09 from $9.10. Pitney Bowes projects ~5% annual volume growth to 30.5 billion shipments by 2030 (+36%), which is healthy but nothing like the pandemic step-function. The structural problem is share: the Big Three now handle roughly 61% of ~23.9 billion annual US parcels, down from ~85% pre-pandemic, while alternative and regional carriers grew 13% in 2025 to 2.6 billion parcels. A market growing at 5% with price flat and share leaking to lower-cost entrants is a market where incumbents must earn margin from cost, not from volume — which is exactly the bet UPS has made.
Tariffs and cross-border e-commerce flows
EMERGING TAILWIND
The elimination of the de minimis exemption removed the duty-free treatment that made ultra-low-value Asia-to-US parcels economic, gutting a high-volume, low-yield lane. UPS is now on the other side of that shock: the China-to-US lane returned to year-over-year volume growth in 2Q 2026 (STAT Times), and in 3Q International revenue rose 7.1% to $5.01B with revenue per piece up 15.0% and operating profit growing year over year for the first time since the 2025 trade-policy changes (Logistics Management). The catch is that this is policy-derived, not demand-derived: a change in trade rules can reverse it as quickly as it arrived. International is 21% of revenue but earned a 15.8% adjusted margin in FY2025, roughly double U.S. Domestic, so the lane mix matters far more to profit than to revenue.
Labour cost structure versus non-union competitors
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
UPS employs roughly 460,000 people under a Teamsters national agreement running to July 31, 2028: $7.50/hour of increases across 2023–2028, a $1.22/hour raise on August 1, 2026 including a $0.22 COLA, part-time entry at $21/hour rising to $23/hour after August 2027, plus commitments to 22,500 permanent full-time openings and 28,000 replacement vehicles. Amazon Logistics' delivery-service-partner network and the alternative carriers taking share operate on materially different labour economics. Even shrinking the workforce is expensive — the Driver Choice Program, a voluntary buyout aimed at 7,500 full-time driver positions, accounts for $1.2B of the $1.4–$1.6B of 2026 transformation charges UPS now expects.

Bull vs bear framing: the bull argues UPS has already absorbed the worst of it — $4.5B of cost removed, 78,000 positions gone, U.S. Domestic adjusted margin up on the year in both 2Q and 3Q despite the escalators, and a 13.26 forward P/E that assumes none of it sticks. The bear argues that a fixed, contractually rising cost base against declining volume is precisely the definition of negative operating leverage, that the 126% GAAP payout ratio removes the balance-sheet cushion needed to absorb it, and that every dollar of savings from here is harder than the last because the easy 150 buildings are already closed.
