01 Company Overview

United Rentals is the largest equipment rental company in the world. It owns a fleet with a total original equipment cost (OEC — what the company paid for the machines, before depreciation) of $23.75 billion and rents it out of a North American branch network to contractors, industrial plants, utilities, municipalities and homeowners. FY2025 revenue was $16.10B, up 4.91% on FY2024's $15.35B; the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $16.83B, up 6.88%, and management has guided full-year 2026 revenue to $17.5–17.8B. At a $62.86B market capitalisation the company ranks roughly #409 globally.

Understand the model before the multiple. United Rentals buys equipment at a known cost, rents it at a rate designed to recover that cost over a few years of useful life, and then sells the machine into the used market — so the business earns twice from the same asset, and the second earning is also the market's live appraisal of the first. In Q2 2026 it sold $330 million of used equipment at a 52.9% OEC recovery rate: fifty-three cents back on every dollar of original cost, on gear that had already been rented for roughly four years. That recovery rate is the single most informative number on this page. It is simultaneously a revenue line, a margin line, and a mark-to-market on the entire $23.75B fleet.

The debate this page arbitrates: rental has structurally consolidated — contractors increasingly rent rather than own, and the top operators hold far more share than a decade ago, which is the case for paying a higher multiple than a cyclical would normally get. Against that, the demand engine is non-residential construction and the megaproject pipeline — data centres, chip fabs, power, LNG, infrastructure — which is lumpy, financed, and capable of stopping. Section 02 quantifies the specialty-versus-general split that sits at the centre of the structural case; Section 07 shows what the market has already paid for it.

When a machine reaches the end of its rental life the company sells it — through auctions, brokers, retail and trade packages — and books the proceeds. Two things make this line matter more than its size suggests. First, it is high-margin: Q2 2026 GAAP gross margin on used sales was 46.7%, up from 46.1% a year earlier, and the adjusted gross margin was 47.3%. Second, the OEC recovery rate is the market's live appraisal of the fleet. A falling recovery rate means used values are deflating, which means the residual assumption baked into every depreciation schedule is too generous, which means reported rental margins are flattered. At 52.9% on a fleet averaging 47.4 months old, the used market is currently confirming rather than contradicting the balance sheet. Watch this number ahead of the rental rate.

The cycle tell: in an equipment downturn, recovery rates fall first, then fleet productivity, then rental revenue. Recovery rate is the leading indicator; revenue is the lagging one.

This is not an asset-light business and does not pretend to be. TTM revenue of $16.83B sits on a fleet that cost $23.75B to buy, and 2026 guidance calls for $4.85–5.25B of gross rental capital expenditure against $3.4–3.8B net — the difference being what used sales bring back in. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE OF FLEET ($23.75B OEC) BRANCH DENSITY NATIONAL ACCOUNTS SPECIALTY CROSS-SELL USED-CHANNEL DEPTH OEM PURCHASING POWER MEGAPROJECT QUALIFICATION

The moat is scale expressed as availability. A general contractor building a multi-year, multi-billion-dollar facility cannot afford to discover that the boom lift is three weeks out; it needs one supplier that can put thousands of pieces of varied equipment on one site, invoice it centrally, and swap a failed unit the same day. Only two operators in North America can credibly do that at megaproject scale, which is why the industry's consolidation matters more than a share-of-market table would suggest. Scale also compounds on the buy side and the sell side: United Rentals buys iron in volumes that earn OEM pricing others cannot match, and disposes of it through channels deep enough to clear $330M of used gear in a single quarter at a 52.9% recovery rate without moving the market against itself. The financial result is a business earning a 46.6% adjusted EBITDA margin and a 28.90% ROE on rented steel. The structural argument is also visible in the customer's behaviour: renting converts a contractor's fixed cost into a variable one, and that preference has proved sticky through cycles rather than reversing when balance sheets improve.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Megaproject concentration. Management said demand for large projects "remains strong" with visibility "for the next 12 to 18 months" across power, data centres, semiconductors and infrastructure — while describing local-market growth as only low single digits. That is the vulnerability stated as a strength: the growth is concentrated in a small number of very large, capital-market-dependent projects. If data-centre or fab capex is deferred, there is no broad local recovery underneath to catch the fleet.
▸
Residual-value risk on a $23.75B fleet. Every reported margin depends on a depreciation schedule, and every depreciation schedule depends on an assumed residual. The used market currently validates the assumption at a 52.9% OEC recovery rate. If used prices deflate — because peers dispose into the same channel at the same time, or because a demand air-pocket leaves the industry over-fleeted — the accounting hit arrives before the rental revenue hit does, and it arrives on the balance sheet as well as the income statement.
▸
Deliberate leverage into a cyclical. Net leverage is 1.8× net debt/EBITDA against total debt of $15.39B and cash of just $112M, inside a stated 1.5×–2.5× target. That is comfortable at a 46.6% EBITDA margin. It is less comfortable if EBITDA falls 25% in a downturn, because the leverage ratio moves on the denominator without a single dollar of debt being added — and the D/E of 1.67 and current ratio of 0.76 leave little slack.
▸
A consolidating competitor set that can now match scale. Herc Holdings grew TTM revenue 28.5% and completed its H&E integration in Q1 2026, and Ashtead's Sunbelt franchise took a US listing (NYSE: SUNB) in March 2026 — both moves narrow the availability gap that is the core of the moat. Consolidation cuts both ways: it removes irrational small players, and it creates a second and third operator big enough to bid for the same megaprojects.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The industry has structurally consolidated and the customer has structurally changed behaviour — renting instead of owning — which turns a cyclical into something closer to a utilisation-driven compounder. The evidence is in the mix: specialty has gone from 16.0% of revenue a decade ago to 36.5% in 2025 and grew 24.8% year-on-year in Q2 2026, against 6.6% for general rentals. Fleet productivity of +3.4% on average OEC up 7.1% says the incremental machine is earning its keep, and a 52.9% used recovery rate says the residual assumptions underpinning every margin are holding. At 19.23× forward earnings with a 46.6% adjusted EBITDA margin, 13.17% ROIC, 1.8× net leverage and $1.5B of buyback, you are paying a below-index multiple for above-index returns on capital.

Bear Case

This is a $15.39B-debt, 1.79-beta business whose growth is concentrated in a handful of enormous, financing-dependent projects, while management itself describes the local market as growing low single digits. FY2025 already showed what a soft year looks like: revenue +4.91% but net income down 3.15%. The 2026 reacceleration is real but has been paid for — the stock rose 55.5% in the quarter before the guidance raise. A single-digit deterioration in used-equipment recovery rates would hit the income statement and the carrying value of a $23.75B fleet at the same time, and Herc and Sunbelt are both now scaled enough to compete for the same megaprojects. Barclays raised its target 33% and still says Underweight — that is a cycle call, and the cycle is what this is.

Top 3 Risks

01
Megaproject deferral. Twelve-to-eighteen-month visibility is a comfort, not a contract. Data-centre, semiconductor and power projects can be pushed on a financing decision made far from any construction site, and there is no broad local-market recovery underneath to absorb the fleet if they are.
02
Used-value deflation. The 52.9% OEC recovery rate is simultaneously a revenue line, a margin line and the market's appraisal of $23.75B of assets. It is also the first thing that breaks in a downturn — before rental rates, and well before revenue.
03
Leverage in the denominator. Net debt/EBITDA of 1.8× is comfortable at a 46.6% margin and uncomfortable at a 35% one. With $112M of cash against $15.39B of debt and a 0.76 current ratio, the balance sheet has no shock absorber other than the ability to stop buying fleet — which is exactly what makes free cash flow rise in a downturn, and revenue fall.
Industry Commentary
The megaproject capex wave is doing the heavy lifting
EMERGING TAILWIND

Management named power, data centres, semiconductors and infrastructure as the demand drivers and said visibility extends "for the next 12 to 18 months" on strong customer backlogs. That is what carried specialty to +24.8% growth in Q2 2026 and total rental revenue to +12.7% while general rentals grew 6.6% and local markets grew low single digits. The tailwind is genuine and it is also narrow: this is one capex theme, financed by a small number of very large balance sheets, expressed through a rental fleet. Every quarter that the pipeline converts is a quarter the multiple gets defended; the first quarter it does not is a de-rating, not a miss.

Consolidation cuts both ways
ONGOING HEADWIND

The consolidation that supports rental pricing discipline is the same consolidation that is building rivals. Herc completed its H&E integration in Q1 2026, grew TTM revenue 28.5% to $4.86B, and guided 2026 adjusted EBITDA to $2.05–2.125B; Ashtead's Sunbelt franchise listed in New York (NYSE: SUNB) in March 2026. Meanwhile Ashtead's ADR is down 23.02% over twelve months while URI is up 6.83% — the equity market is discriminating sharply between operators inside one industry, which suggests investors are underwriting execution and mix, not the rental category. That is a compliment to URI and a warning: multiples awarded for relative execution are withdrawn for relative execution.

Bull vs bear, framed on one number
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

If you want a single dial to turn, use the OEC recovery rate. The bull case is that 52.9% — on gear averaging 47.4 months old, sold at a 46.7% GAAP gross margin — proves the used market still values the fleet at levels that make the depreciation schedule conservative, which means the 46.6% adjusted EBITDA margin is real and the $4.85–5.25B of gross capex is being deployed into an accretive asset. The bear case is that recovery rates are set by the same non-residential construction cycle that sets rental demand, so they fall exactly when EBITDA falls, marking down a $23.75B asset base against $15.39B of debt at the worst possible moment. Both cases agree on the mechanism. They disagree only on where in the cycle September 2026 sits — and the stock, 10.9% below its June closing high after a record quarter and a raised guide, suggests the market has not fully decided either.
