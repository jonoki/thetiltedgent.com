01 Company Overview

United Rentals is the largest equipment rental company in the world. It owns a fleet with a total original equipment cost (OEC — what the company paid for the machines, before depreciation) of $24.77 billion and rents it out of 1,686 locations in North America, plus branches in Europe, Australia and New Zealand, to contractors, industrial plants, utilities, municipalities and homeowners. FY2025 revenue was $16.10B, up 4.91% on FY2024's $15.35B; the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $17.50B, up 9.45%, and after a record third quarter management raised its full-year 2026 revenue guide to $18.0–18.2B, from $17.5–17.8B. At a $68.04B market capitalisation the company ranks #182 in the S&P 500.

Understand the model before the multiple. United Rentals buys equipment at a known cost, rents it at a rate designed to recover that cost over a few years of useful life, and then sells the machine into the used market — so the business earns twice from the same asset, and the second earning is also the market's live appraisal of the first. In Q3 2026 it sold $348 million of used equipment at a 51.4% OEC recovery rate: about fifty-one cents back on every dollar of original cost, on gear that had already spent years on rent. That recovery rate is the single most informative number on this page. It is simultaneously a revenue line, a margin line, and a mark-to-market (a check of book values against today's prices) on the entire $24.77B fleet — and it slipped from 52.9% in Q2, while CFO Ted Grace described the used market on the call as "normalized".

The debate this page arbitrates: rental has structurally consolidated — contractors increasingly rent rather than own, and the top operators hold far more share than a decade ago, which is the case for paying a higher multiple than a cyclical would normally get. Against that, the demand engine is non-residential construction and the megaproject pipeline — data centres, chip fabs, power, LNG, infrastructure — which is lumpy, financed, and capable of stopping. Section 02 quantifies the specialty-versus-general split that sits at the centre of the structural case; Section 07 shows what the market has already paid for it.

When a machine reaches the end of its rental life the company sells it — through auctions, brokers, retail and trade packages — and books the proceeds. Two things make this line matter more than its size suggests. First, it is high-margin: Q3 2026 GAAP gross margin on used sales was 44.8%, up from 44.1% a year earlier, and the adjusted gross margin was 46.3%; proceeds rose 4.5%. Second, the OEC recovery rate is the market's live appraisal of the fleet. A falling recovery rate means used values are deflating, which means the residual assumption (the resale value the company expects at the end of a machine's life) baked into every depreciation schedule is too generous, which means reported rental margins are flattered. The rate was 51.4% in Q3, down from 52.9% in Q2 — a small step, and Grace described the used market as "normalized" with "plenty of demand", so the used market is still confirming rather than contradicting the balance sheet. Watch this number ahead of the rental rate.

The cycle tell: in an equipment downturn, recovery rates fall first, then fleet productivity, then rental revenue. Recovery rate is the leading indicator; revenue is the lagging one.

This is not an asset-light business and does not pretend to be. TTM revenue of $17.50B sits on a fleet that cost $24.77B to buy, and the raised 2026 guidance calls for $5.2–5.5B of gross rental capital expenditure (up from $4.85–5.25B) against $3.75–4.05B net — the difference being what used sales bring back in. Disclosure note: fleet productivity is a company-defined, non-GAAP construct. It is useful, but it also means investors cannot separately see whether growth came from price or from putting idle machines to work.
03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SCALE OF FLEET ($24.77B OEC) BRANCH DENSITY NATIONAL ACCOUNTS SPECIALTY CROSS-SELL USED-CHANNEL DEPTH OEM PURCHASING POWER MEGAPROJECT QUALIFICATION

The moat is scale expressed as availability. A general contractor building a multi-year, multi-billion-dollar facility cannot afford to discover that the boom lift is three weeks out; it needs one supplier that can put thousands of pieces of varied equipment on one site, invoice it centrally, and swap a failed unit the same day. Very few operators in North America can do that at megaproject scale, which is why the industry's consolidation matters more than a share-of-market table would suggest. Scale also compounds on the buy side and the sell side: United Rentals buys iron in volumes that earn OEM pricing others cannot match, and disposes of it through channels deep enough to clear $348M of used gear in a single quarter at a 51.4% recovery rate without moving the market against itself. The financial result is a business earning a 47.6% adjusted EBITDA margin (Q3 2026) and a 30.56% ROE on rented steel. The structural argument is also visible in the customer's behaviour: renting converts a contractor's fixed cost into a variable one, and that preference has proved sticky through cycles rather than reversing when balance sheets improve.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Megaproject concentration. On the Q3 call Flannery said large projects are driving the growth and that he expects the momentum to carry into next year, and management said 2027 is expected to be another growth year, supported by large projects (Investing.com's call summary), while Flannery called local markets "stable". That is the vulnerability stated as a strength: the growth is concentrated in a small number of very large, financing-dependent projects. If data-centre or chip-plant spending is deferred, there is no broad local recovery underneath to catch the fleet.
▸
Residual-value risk on a $24.77B fleet. Every reported margin depends on a depreciation schedule, and every depreciation schedule depends on an assumed residual. The used market currently validates the assumption at a 51.4% OEC recovery rate (52.9% in Q2). If used prices deflate — because peers dispose into the same channel at the same time, or because a demand air-pocket leaves the industry over-fleeted — the accounting hit arrives before the rental revenue hit does, and it arrives on the balance sheet as well as the income statement.
▸
Deliberate leverage into a cyclical. Net leverage is 1.8× net debt/EBITDA against $14.67B of debt on the balance sheet and cash of just $153M at September 30, inside a stated 1.5×–2.5× target. That is comfortable at a 47.6% EBITDA margin. It is less comfortable if EBITDA falls 25% in a downturn, because the leverage ratio moves on the denominator without a single dollar of debt being added — and the D/E of 1.64 and current ratio of 0.80 leave little slack.
▸
A consolidating competitor set that can now match scale. Herc Holdings grew TTM revenue 28.5% (through Q2 2026, a period that includes the acquired H&E business) and completed its H&E integration in Q1 2026, and Ashtead Group moved its main listing to New York as Sunbelt Rentals Holdings (NYSE: SUNB) in March 2026 — both moves narrow the availability gap that is the core of the moat. Consolidation cuts both ways: it removes irrational small players, and it creates a second and third operator big enough to bid for the same megaprojects.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has been bought hard enough that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 it has been sold hard enough that sellers may be. It says nothing about the business — only about the recent balance of buying and selling pressure.

From the October 2021 month-end close of $379.11 (October 29, 2021) to the October 23, 2026 close of $1,098.88, URI returned +189.9% on price alone, about a 23.8% compound annual rate over those 4.98 years, before the dividend that only began part-way through the period. Over the same dates SPY, an S&P 500 index fund, returned +65.5% and QQQ, a Nasdaq-100 fund, +82.4% on price. But the path is the point. The stock lost 35.9% from October 2021 to the June 2022 close of $242.91 as rising interest rates hit every heavily borrowed, economy-sensitive stock; it then gained +366.4% from that low to the June 2026 peak. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The industry has structurally consolidated and the customer has structurally changed behaviour — renting instead of owning — which turns a cyclical into something closer to a utilisation-driven compounder. The evidence is in the mix: specialty has gone from 16.0% of revenue a decade ago to 36.5% in 2025, and its rental revenue grew 34.7% year-on-year in Q3 2026, against 8.8% for general rentals. Fleet productivity of +6.5% on average OEC up 7.9% says the incremental machine is earning its keep, and a 51.4% used recovery rate says the residual assumptions underpinning every margin are holding. Management has now raised full-year guidance three times this year, in April, July and October. At 18.55× forward earnings with a 47.6% adjusted EBITDA margin, 13.79% ROIC, 1.8× net leverage and $1.5B of buyback, you are paying a below-index multiple for above-index returns on capital.

Bear Case

This is a business with $14.67B of debt and a 1.83 beta whose growth is concentrated in a handful of enormous, financing-dependent projects, while management itself calls local markets merely "stable". FY2025 already showed what a soft year looks like: revenue +4.91% but net income down 3.15%. The 2026 reacceleration is real but much of it was paid for in advance — the stock rose 55.5% from the March to the June close, before the July and October raises. The used recovery rate already slipped from 52.9% to 51.4% between Q2 and Q3; a larger slide would hit the income statement and the carrying value of a $24.77B fleet at the same time, and Herc and Sunbelt are both now scaled enough to compete for the same megaprojects. Barclays raised its target 33% in July and still says Underweight, and JPMorgan moved to Neutral in September — that is a cycle call, and the cycle is what this is.

Top 3 Risks

01
Megaproject deferral. Management's expectation that 2027 will be another growth year is a comfort, not a contract. Data-centre, semiconductor and power projects can be pushed on a financing decision made far from any construction site, and there is no broad local-market recovery underneath to absorb the fleet if they are.
02
Used-value deflation. The 51.4% OEC recovery rate (52.9% a quarter earlier) is simultaneously a revenue line, a margin line and the market's appraisal of $24.77B of assets. It is also the first thing that breaks in a downturn — before rental rates, and well before revenue.
03
Leverage in the denominator. Net debt/EBITDA of 1.8× is comfortable at a 47.6% margin and uncomfortable at a 35% one. With $153M of cash against $14.67B of debt and a 0.80 current ratio, the balance sheet has no shock absorber other than the ability to stop buying fleet — which is exactly what makes free cash flow rise in a downturn, and revenue fall.
Industry Commentary
The megaproject capex wave is doing the heavy lifting
EMERGING TAILWIND

On the Q3 call Flannery said large projects across a variety of end markets are driving the growth and that he expects the momentum to carry into next year, with power again growing by double digits. That is what carried specialty rental revenue to +34.7% in Q3 2026 and total rental revenue to +17.8%, while general rentals grew 8.8% and local markets were "stable", adding probably a couple of points of growth. The tailwind is genuine and it is also narrow: this is one capex theme, financed by a small number of very large balance sheets, expressed through a rental fleet. Every quarter that the pipeline converts is a quarter the multiple gets defended; the first quarter it does not, investors are likely to pay less for each dollar of earnings even if the numbers themselves do not miss.

Consolidation cuts both ways
ONGOING HEADWIND

The consolidation that supports rental pricing discipline is the same consolidation that is building rivals. Herc completed its H&E integration in Q1 2026, grew TTM revenue 28.5% to $4.86B (through Q2), and guided 2026 adjusted EBITDA to $2.05–2.125B; Ashtead Group moved its main listing to New York as Sunbelt Rentals Holdings (NYSE: SUNB) on March 2, 2026. At the October 23 close the market valued each dollar of URI's revenue at about $3.89, Sunbelt's at $2.80 and Herc's at $0.88 (market cap ÷ TTM revenue, stockanalysis figures) — the equity market is discriminating sharply between operators inside one industry, which suggests investors are paying for execution and mix, not the rental category. That is a compliment to URI and a warning: multiples awarded for relative execution are withdrawn for relative execution.

Bull vs bear, framed on one number
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

If you want a single dial to turn, use the OEC recovery rate. The bull case is that 51.4% — on gear sold at a 44.8% GAAP gross margin — proves the used market still values the fleet at levels that make the depreciation schedule conservative, which means the 47.6% adjusted EBITDA margin is real and the $5.2–5.5B of gross capex is being deployed into an asset that earns more than it costs. The bear case is that recovery rates are set by the same non-residential construction cycle that sets rental demand, so they fall exactly when EBITDA falls, marking down a $24.77B asset base against $14.67B of debt at the worst possible moment — and Q3's 51.4% was a step down from Q2's 52.9%. Both cases agree on the mechanism. They disagree only on where in the cycle October 2026 sits — and the stock, 3.0% below its June closing high after two record quarters and three raised guides this year, suggests the market is leaning toward the bull case without having fully decided.
