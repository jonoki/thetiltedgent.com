01 Company Overview

U.S. Bancorp is the parent of U.S. Bank National Association, the fifth-largest commercial bank in the United States, with $725.9B in total assets, $532.1B of deposits and $410.3B of period-end loans as of June 30, 2026. What separates it from an ordinary regional bank is scale in payments: card issuing, merchant acquiring and corporate payment/treasury management together produced $4.29B of fee revenue in FY2025, roughly 15% of total net revenue — fee income that carries essentially no credit risk and consumes almost no balance sheet.

FY2025 total net revenue (net interest income plus noninterest income) was $28.54B, up from $27.34B in FY2024, with net income of $7.57B and diluted EPS of $4.62. Momentum has accelerated in 2026: Q2 2026 total net revenue of $7.71B was a quarterly record, up 10.1% year over year, with EPS of $1.35 (+21.6% YoY) and 400 basis points of positive operating leverage. USB ranks roughly #255 globally and #126 in the US by market capitalisation.

Note on revenue definitions: data aggregators such as StockAnalysis report USB's "revenue" net of the provision for credit losses ($26.35B for FY2025, $27.32B TTM). This report uses total net revenue as U.S. Bancorp itself reports it ($28.54B FY2025) wherever segment or fee-mix arithmetic is involved, and flags which basis is in use.

The core spread business: $410.3B of period-end loans funded by $532.1B of deposits. NIM (net interest margin — the spread between what the bank earns on loans and securities and what it pays for deposits and debt) was 2.79% in Q2 2026, up 13 basis points from 2.66% a year earlier and up 2bp sequentially; CFO John Stern told the Q2 call the company sees "a path on the 3% journey… at some point in 2027." Average loans of $405.5B grew 7.1% YoY, led by commercial loans (+14.1% to ~$157B), credit card (+8.4% to ~$38B) and commercial real estate (+5.8% to ~$51B). Average deposits of $515.1B grew only 2.4% — a slower pace than loans, which is what puts upward pressure on deposit pricing.

Deposit beta (the share of a change in market interest rates that a bank must pass through to its depositors — a high beta means rising rates help less and falling rates help more) is the swing factor. USB does not publish a headline cumulative beta in the materials retrieved here; what it does disclose is the outcome. The rate paid on interest-bearing liabilities fell to 2.82% in FY2025 from 3.09% in FY2024, and Q2 2026 deposit interest expense fell 8.3% YoY to $2.33B against average deposits of $515.1B — an implied all-in cost of total deposits of roughly 1.81% annualised (computed). Deposit mix is shifting toward rate-sensitive buckets: savings +26.7% and money-market +2.8% YoY, noninterest-bearing only +1.9%, time deposits −18.4%. That mix drift is a slow, structural upward pull on beta.

This is the part of U.S. Bancorp that is not a bank. FY2025 payments fee revenue of $4,292M breaks into merchant processing services $1,792M (Elavon — acquiring and processing for merchants in the US and Europe), card revenue $1,735M (interchange and fees on consumer and small-business credit and debit cards), and corporate payment products $765M (commercial cards, purchasing cards, freight and government payment programmes; renamed "corporate payment and treasury management" from January 1, 2026). This revenue is transaction-linked, recurring, and — critically — consumes essentially no risk-weighted assets and carries no credit risk on the acquiring and corporate-payments side. A dollar of merchant-processing fee does not need capital held against it the way a dollar of commercial loan does.

Q2 2026 showed a genuine split inside the segment. Consolidated payments fees rose 4.0% YoY to $1.36B, but the components diverged: corporate payments and prepaid +11.8% (against −1.3% a year earlier), card issuing +6.1% (accelerating from 4.4%), and merchant processing only +2.3% (decelerating from 4.4%). CFO Stern attributed the merchant slowdown to "slowness in Europe" and the "loss of non-strategic distribution partners." Payment Services delivered $225M of net income in Q2 2026. The bear reading is that merchant acquiring is the most commoditised, most price-competitive corner of payments and USB is losing share of it; the bull reading is that the higher-margin issuing and corporate franchises are accelerating while a low-quality distribution book is being pruned.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
$532B DEPOSIT BASE PAYMENTS SCALE 43% FEE MIX CORPORATE TRUST STICKINESS SWITCHING COSTS REGULATORY MOAT (CATEGORY II) 1863 BRAND / 15M CLIENTS

USB's moat is a funding moat wrapped around a fee moat. The funding side is $532.1B of deposits gathered across a national branch and digital footprint that grew materially with Union Bank on the West Coast — deposits are the cheapest liability in finance and the hardest asset for a new entrant to assemble. The fee side is where the premium multiple actually comes from: 41.7% of FY2025 revenue and 43.1% of Q2 2026 revenue was noninterest income, versus a typical regional bank in the high-20s to low-30s. Merchant acquiring, corporate card programmes and corporate trust all have high switching costs — a merchant re-platforming its payment stack, a corporate treasury re-papering its purchasing-card programme, or a bond issuer replacing its indenture trustee all face real operational cost and risk for little gain. That produces low churn and revenue that recurs without balance-sheet growth, which is why USB has historically earned a ROTCE in the high teens (18.7% in Q2 2026, 18.1% for FY2025) against a mid-teens peer norm. Scale is itself regulatory: at $725.9B of assets USB sits in a supervisory tier whose compliance, capital and resolution burden is a genuine barrier below it.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. The securities book and AOCI. At December 31, 2025 USB carried net unrealised losses of $4.4B on available-for-sale securities (improved from $6.8B a year earlier) and a held-to-maturity portfolio with $76.2B of amortised cost against $67.1B of fair value — a $9.1B unrealised loss that never touches reported earnings or reported tangible book. AOCI is the accounting bucket where AFS marks sit; HTM marks sit nowhere at all. The regulatory version of this is visible: CET1 was 10.8% in Q2 2026 but only 9.4% including AOCI, a 140bp gap implying roughly $6B (est.) of capital that regulatory arithmetic currently lets USB ignore. This is precisely the mechanism that destroyed Silicon Valley Bank: the losses are economically real and become accounting-real only if a deposit run forces sales. USB's deposit base is vastly more diversified than SVB's, but the exposure is not zero.

2. Merchant acquiring is commoditising. Merchant processing revenue grew just 2.3% in Q2 2026, down from 4.4%, on European weakness and lost distribution partners. Acquiring is the segment where independent processors, integrated software vendors and JPMorgan's payments arm compete hardest on price. If the highest-multiple part of the fee stack grows at 2%, the argument for a premium-to-peers valuation weakens materially.

3. Commercial real estate concentration. CRE loans were $48.9B at December 31, 2025 against $391.3B of total loans — 12.5% — and grew to roughly $51B of a $405.5B average book (~12.6%) by Q2 2026. That is a moderate exposure by regional-bank standards, but office valuations remain the most uncertain area of US credit, and management explicitly flagged CRE as a growth area in Q2 ("commercial real estate saw some nice uptick this quarter"). Growing into a stressed asset class is a risk decision, not a neutral one.

4. Deposit competition and mix drift. Average deposits grew 2.4% YoY while average loans grew 7.1% — a widening funding gap that must be closed with either higher-cost deposits or wholesale debt (total debt rose to $99.7B by June 2026 from $82.7B at December 2025). Meanwhile the deposit mix is rotating out of noninterest-bearing (+1.9%) and time deposits (−18.4%) into savings (+26.7%) and money market (+2.8%). Both trends push funding costs up over time and cap how much of the "path to 3% NIM" management can actually deliver.

Notable One-Off Items

BTIG acquisition. The largest current distortion is inorganic. BTIG contributed $98M of the $512M capital markets revenue in Q2 2026, and management's own full-year 2026 revenue guidance separates the effect: +7–9% growth including BTIG versus +5–7% excluding it. Roughly two points of the top-line acceleration is acquired, not organic.

Securities repositioning. USB sold approximately $1.6B of securities in Q2 2026 as part of a balance-sheet repositioning, and FY2025 carried $61M of net securities losses within noninterest income. These are deliberate, recurring-in-kind actions rather than one-time shocks, but they depress the reported fee line.

January 2026 reporting revision. Effective January 1, 2026 USB reclassified several revenue and loan categories: corporate payment products was renamed "corporate payment and treasury management" (absorbing treasury management services and stored-value card revenue from Card revenue), service charges became "lending and deposit-related fees" (absorbing loan and leasing fees), Impact Finance moved into the Wealth/Corporate/Commercial/Institutional segment, and small-business credit card loans moved from Commercial into Credit Card. USB states these changes had no impact on historical total net revenue, provision, expense or net income — but year-over-year growth rates for individual fee lines and loan categories are not clean across the boundary.

Historical items for context. The Union Bank acquisition closed December 1, 2022 and carried substantial merger and integration charges through 2023; 2023 also included a special FDIC assessment tied to the Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank failures. Both are outside the current TTM window and do not distort today's numbers, but they explain the depressed FY2023 EPS of $3.27 and FY2024 efficiency ratio of 62.3%.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent price movement has been upward versus downward. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been sold hard and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation tool.

Five-year trajectory. USB closed September 2021 at $59.44 and finished August 28, 2026 at $62.47 — a price gain of just +5.1% over five years. Including dividends the picture is better but still unimpressive: on a dividend-adjusted basis the same window returned roughly +28%, or about +5.1% annualised. The path was violent. The stock lost more than half its value from the October 2021 high of $60.37 to the May 2023 trough of $29.90, then more than doubled off that low. Anyone who bought USB five years ago and held has been paid almost entirely in dividends; anyone who bought the March–May 2023 panic has roughly doubled their money. That is the character of this security: a low-beta name (0.97) that periodically behaves like a high-beta one when the market re-prices bank credit or bank funding.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

This is not a regional bank priced as one. A 43.1% fee mix — merchant acquiring, card issuing, corporate payments, corporate trust and wealth — produces high-teens ROTCE (18.7%) and a 57.1% efficiency ratio without consuming balance sheet, and it is doing so at 12.47× earnings, a discount to the 13.39× regional-bank aggregate and less than half the S&P 500's 29.7×. NIM is climbing toward a stated 3% target, credit is improving (net charge-offs 0.53%, NPAs 0.33%), tangible book grew 13.3% year over year, and CET1 at 10.8% leaves roughly 80bp of excess capital that management has said will fund a 70–75% payout. Revenue guidance was raised twice. If merchant processing merely stabilises, the re-rating from 2.08× tangible book has room to run toward the $70.32 consensus target.

Bear Case

The premium multiple rests on payments, and payments is where growth is slowing: merchant processing decelerated to 2.3% from 4.4%, and the fastest-growing revenue line is an acquired brokerage (BTIG, $98M of a $512M capital markets quarter) that is precisely the volatile, capital-intensive kind of revenue the fee thesis is supposed to avoid — strip it out and guided growth falls from 7–9% to 5–7%. Meanwhile $9.1B of unrealised HTM losses and $4.4B on AFS sit outside reported tangible book; CET1 including AOCI is 9.4%, not 10.8%. Loans are growing at 7.1% against 2.4% deposit growth, forcing wholesale debt from $82.7B to $99.7B in six months and pushing the deposit mix into rate-paying buckets. Five years of ownership has produced +5.1% on price. The market has been right to be sceptical more often than not.

Top 3 Risks

01
Unrealised securities losses meeting a funding shock. $9.1B of HTM marks and $4.4B of AFS marks (December 2025) are economically real and accounting-invisible. If deposits ever leave fast enough to force sales, roughly $6B (est.) of currently-excluded capital becomes very visible — the CET1 gap between 10.8% and 9.4% is the measure of it. USB's deposit base is far more diversified than SVB's was, which is why this is a tail risk rather than a base case, but it is the tail that killed a bank in 2023.
02
Payments share loss rather than portfolio pruning. Merchant processing at 2.3% growth is the weakest link in the premium-multiple argument. Management attributes it to European weakness and deliberately shed distribution partners; if it is instead structural share loss to independent processors, integrated software vendors and JPMorgan Payments, USB converges toward a plain-regional 1.3–1.6× tangible book from today's 2.08× — roughly a 25–35% valuation reset with no change in earnings.
03
Deposit beta and the funding gap. Loans grew 7.1% against 2.4% deposit growth, total debt rose $17B in six months, and the deposit mix is rotating from noninterest-bearing (+1.9%) into savings (+26.7%). If competition forces USB to pay up for deposits, the "path to 3% NIM" stalls and net interest income — still 57% of revenue — flattens. Rate cuts help this, rate stability at high levels hurts it, and neither is under management's control.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters Right Now
The AOCI Overhang Across US Banking
ONGOING HEADWIND

Every large US bank that bought long-duration securities in 2020–2021 still carries the mark. For USB the numbers are $4.4B of net unrealised AFS losses and $9.1B on the $76.2B HTM book at December 31, 2025 — though both are improving ($6.8B AFS a year earlier; HTM fair value up to $67.1B from $66.3B). The regulatory tell is the CET1 spread: 10.8% reported versus 9.4% including AOCI, a 140bp gap that has narrowed from 190bp a year earlier (10.6% vs 8.9%). The overhang is shrinking as bonds roll to par, but it will take years, and it caps how aggressively any bank in this position can return capital.

Margin Recovery Without Rate Help
EMERGING TAILWIND

The interesting feature of USB's 13bp NIM expansion (2.66% → 2.79%) is that it came alongside falling deposit costs: Q2 2026 deposit interest expense fell 8.3% YoY to $2.33B and the FY2025 rate paid on interest-bearing liabilities dropped to 2.82% from 3.09%. Low-yielding pandemic-era securities and fixed-rate loans are repricing upward into today's curve while funding costs ease — a self-financing margin recovery that does not require the Fed to do anything. The offset is the funding gap: loans growing 7.1% against deposits at 2.4% is what would force USB to bid for deposits and break the trend.

The Bull/Bear Frame: Does the Payments Business Still Earn Its Premium?
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Everything reduces to one question. Bulls point to a 43.1% fee mix, 18.7% ROTCE, a 57.1% efficiency ratio, $900M of Union Bank synergies delivered on schedule, CET1 at 10.8% with buybacks about to step up, and a 12.47× multiple below the regional-bank aggregate — a payments company trading inside a bank wrapper at a bank discount. Bears point to merchant processing at 2.3%, the fee acceleration being bought (BTIG) rather than earned, 2.08× tangible book that already assumes the premium, $13.5B of securities marks parked outside book value, CRE at 12.5% of loans and rising, and a five-year price return of 5.1%. The Q3 print on October 15, 2026 is the next real evidence: if merchant processing reaccelerates, the bulls are right; if it does not, the multiple has nothing to stand on.
