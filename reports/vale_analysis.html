01 Company Overview

Vale is a Brazilian miner that describes itself as a leader in high-quality iron ore — the raw material for steel — with a significant position in copper and nickel. It runs two businesses: Iron Ore Solutions (fines, pellets and the railways and ports that move them, centred on the Carajás mines in northern Brazil and the Minas Gerais complexes) and Vale Base Metals (copper and nickel in Brazil and Canada, 90% owned since Manara Minerals took 10% in April 2024). Its common shares trade on B3's Novo Mercado as VALE3; each New York ADS represents one common share. Over the twelve months to June 30, 2026 Vale booked $41.24B of revenue and $17.19B of proforma EBITDA, both in US dollars, its reporting currency.

The headline earnings number tells a misleading story. Reported net income attributable to shareholders over those twelve months was only $2.11B ($0.50 a share), because the fourth quarter of 2025 carried a $3.5B impairment on the Canadian nickel assets after a cut in long-term nickel price assumptions and a $2.8B write-off of deferred tax assets. Strip those and the other one-offs out and Vale's own proforma net income was $7.67B. That gap — 27.5x reported earnings versus about 7.6x proforma earnings — is the first thing to understand about the stock. The second is the dams. The Fundão dam at Samarco, the Vale–BHP joint venture, failed at Mariana on November 5, 2015, and Vale's own B1 dam at Brumadinho collapsed on January 25, 2019. Both still draw cash: $1.2B in the first half of 2026. The third is the commodity mix. In 2Q26 iron ore made 75% of revenue, but copper's realised price was up 57% year over year and base metals EBITDA rose 79%, so the share of profit that does not depend on Chinese steel demand is rising. The news since the July 30 print has been incremental: a Salobo copper expansion went ahead with start-up a year earlier than first planned, a partial restart at the Fábrica iron ore mine left production guidance unchanged, and Vale agreed to take a 30% stake in the small Carajás producer Ligga.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Carajás ore body (S11D) Owned railway + port Pellet & premium-product capacity By-product-funded copper Scale: 84.3 Mt a quarter Low leverage (0.8x net debt/EBITDA)

Mining moats are geological and logistical. Vale's Northern System combines three mining complexes (Serra Norte, Serra Sul and Serra Leste), the Carajás Railroad and the Ponta da Madeira maritime terminal. Ore, rail and port are all owned, so the whole chain from pit to ship is under one operator, and nobody can build a second Carajás. The Ligga deal shows the other side of owning the railway: third-party ore from the region will ship on Vale's Carajás Railroad, and Vale will buy all of Ligga's sinter feed. The copper business has a second, quieter moat: by-product credits pushed its all-in cost below zero in 2Q26. In a down-cycle, the lowest-cost producer keeps earning after its rivals stop. The limit of the moat is that Vale sets none of its prices. Iron ore, copper and nickel all trade on global benchmarks, and the real, which drives most of Vale's costs, floats.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One commodity still pays most of the bills. Iron Ore Solutions was 75% of 2Q26 revenue and $3,056M of the $4,066M proforma EBITDA. Every dollar a tonne on the realised fines price moves annual revenue by roughly the volume sold: about $0.3B on 2026 production guidance of 335–345 Mt (our arithmetic: volume × $1).
02
The dams are a liability that keeps drawing cash. Since 2019 Brumadinho and the decharacterization (decommissioning) of upstream dams have cost $18.3B in EBITDA charges and $15.2B in payments, with $3.9B of provisions left at June 30, 2026. The Mariana definitive agreement of October 25, 2024 totals about R$170B (US$30.4B) across Samarco, Vale and BHP. Vale's provision for its share was R$14.5B (US$2.6B) at the end of 2025, including a UK lawsuit for which it added $449M in 4Q25. Each new estimate update can bring another charge.
03
A strong real raises costs. Revenue is in dollars; much of the cost base is in reais. The real's strength cost $218M of 2Q26 EBITDA year over year and forced the C1 cost guidance up from $20.0–21.5/t to $22.5–23.5/t. USD/BRL was 5.4762 at the end of 2025 and 5.2226 on October 2, 2026 (Yahoo Finance): the real gained 4.9% against the dollar.
04
Reported earnings are volatile. Impairments, tax-asset write-offs and derivative revaluations moved attributable net income from +$2.69B in 3Q25 to −$3.84B in 4Q25. In 2Q26 derivatives marked to market took $798M off proforma net income year over year. Investors who screen on trailing P/E will see an expensive stock; investors who use proforma earnings will see a cheap one.

Notable One-Off Items

Three items dominate the reported numbers. 4Q25: a $3.5B impairment of the Canadian nickel assets, a $2.8B write-off of deferred tax assets at subsidiaries, and a $449M increase in the Samarco provision for the UK claim turned a $1.46B proforma quarter into a $3.84B attributable loss. 2Q26: $289M of non-recurring expenses (a provision) cut attributable net income to $1.375B against $1.566B proforma, and a $798M swing in derivative marks against a strong 2Q25, together with higher income tax, cut proforma net income 26% despite EBITDA rising 19%. Capital returns: Vale bought back 8.77 million shares for $140M in 2Q26, and the board approved a new programme of up to 100 million shares over 18 months, starting when the previous one ended in August 2026. The 1H26 payout of $1.701B is 52% of 1H26 attributable net income.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF OCT 2, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $13.95 to the October 2, 2026 close of $13.76, the ADS went nowhere on price: −1.4% in five years. Vale's dividends make the difference. On Yahoo's dividend-adjusted series the same window returned about +49%, because Vale paid out a large share of its cash through the 2022–2023 commodity upswing. The path between the endpoints swung widely. The stock peaked at $19.99 in March 2022, slid to a five-year low of $8.87 in December 2024, then climbed to $17.18 by February 2026 before giving back a fifth of that level to reach today's price.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows the same cycle. It bottomed at 22.4 in December 2024, the most oversold reading in the window, and peaked at 92.0 in February 2026, the most overbought. It has since cooled to 42.6, below neutral, after an 11.1% fall in September 2026. The 10-month moving average sits at about $15.41, above the price, so the longer trend has rolled over. The 3-month average is $14.09. The September drop hit the whole iron ore group: BHP fell 9.7% and Rio Tinto 8.1% on their September month-end ADR closes, while the Ibovespa rose 5.0%.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

On proforma earnings you pay about 7.6x, and about 4.4x proforma EBITDA including every dam provision in the debt. The business generated $5.57B of recurring free cash flow in the last twelve months, about 9.5% of its market value, and leverage is 0.8x net debt to EBITDA. Iron ore volumes are at their best second-quarter level since 2018, with S11D expansions starting in 2026. Copper is the hidden asset: realised prices are up 57%, by-products push all-in costs below zero, Bacaba is running ahead of schedule, the Salobo flotation expansion was pulled forward a year at lower capex, and management wants to double output by 2035. Copper and nickel added seven times as much EBITDA growth as iron ore in 2Q26. Investors are paid about 6% a year in ordinary distributions while they wait, plus buybacks.

Bear Case

Three-quarters of revenue still rides on one commodity whose price Vale does not set. Costs are rising in dollars as the real strengthens and fuel gets dearer, and cost guidance went up mid-year. Brumadinho and dam decharacterization have cost $18.3B in charges since 2019, and the Mariana provision keeps getting updated: $449M more in 4Q25 for the UK claim, with cash payments planned for years. Reported earnings swing on impairments and derivative marks, and the nickel business has already been written down $3.5B. The stock has lost money on price over five years while the Ibovespa rose 73%, and 14 of 25 analysts sit at Hold; Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and Bank of America all moved to neutral between July 8 and August 5.

Top 3 Risks

01
Iron ore price. Iron Ore Solutions produced $3,056M of the $4,066M 2Q26 proforma EBITDA. Vale's realised fines price of $95.0/t sits about $33/t above its $61.6/t all-in cost, so a fall in the benchmark goes almost straight to EBITDA. The September 2026 sell-off across Vale, BHP and Rio Tinto shows how closely the three trade together.
02
Dam liabilities and litigation. At June 30, 2026 Vale carried $1.8B of Brumadinho reparation provisions and $2.1B of Samarco provisions inside expanded net debt, plus $2.1B for dam decharacterization outside it. The Samarco provision now includes the UK lawsuit. Every revision to these estimates goes through the income statement and any acceleration in payments through cash flow. A separate dispute opened in August: Brazil's National Mining Agency has pressed mining royalty (CFEM) charges that Vale calls unfounded; the filing gave no amount.
03
Brazilian real and input costs. The real's strength took $218M from 2Q26 EBITDA year over year, and bunker fuel raised freight by $241M. A Brent oil hedge returned a $99M economic gain in 2Q26, booked in financial results rather than costs. If the real keeps strengthening, iron ore margins shrink even at steady prices.
Industry Commentary
Iron ore: firm prices, rising costs
MIXED

Vale's average iron ore reference price was $105.0/t in 2Q26, 11% above a year earlier, and its realised fines price rose 12% to $95.0/t. Volumes were up too: 79.7 Mt sold, +3%. The squeeze is on the cost side. Freight, driven by bunker fuel, and the stronger real together took $400M off iron ore EBITDA year over year, and all-in cost guidance moved up $6/t at the midpoint. Iron ore EBITDA rose only 3% in a quarter when prices rose 12%. The other lever is quality: fines premiums fell $1.1/t quarter on quarter, and the all-in premium fell to $4.4/t from $6.2/t in 1Q26.

Copper and by-products: the profit shift
EMERGING TAILWIND

Vale Base Metals' EBITDA was $1,289M in 2Q26, up 79%, and it generated $545M of free cash flow on its own. Copper EBITDA nearly doubled to $1,026M from $538M a year earlier. The drivers came from Vale's own price disclosure: realised copper up 57% to $14,062/t, plus higher gold and other by-product prices. Those credits pushed copper's all-in cost to −$257/t and cut nickel's 17% to $10,340/t. The 2026 copper all-in guidance fell to $0–500/t from $1,000–1,500/t. One caveat sits in the footnotes: at June 30 Vale had 67,669 t of copper provisionally priced at $13,389/t, so a fall in the copper price would reverse part of the gain through price adjustments.

The dam legacy: Brumadinho and Mariana
LONG-TAIL LIABILITY

Brumadinho: the B1 dam at the Córrego do Feijão mine collapsed on January 25, 2019; Vale's published list of identified deaths carries 272 names. The Integral Reparation Agreement signed on February 4, 2021 with the Minas Gerais government and public bodies was valued at R$37.69B; about 83% of its commitments were complete by 2Q26, and Vale expects to finish its payment obligations in 2026 and the programme in 2031. Mariana: the Fundão dam at Samarco, the Vale–BHP joint venture, failed on November 5, 2015. The October 25, 2024 definitive agreement totals about R$170B (US$30.4B) of past and future obligations. Samarco is the primary debtor, with each shareholder obliged to fund up to 50% of whatever Samarco cannot pay. R$82.4B had been disbursed by June 30, 2026, and the Renova Foundation that ran the earlier programme was dissolved in October 2025. By August 20, 2026, 45 of the 49 eligible municipalities had joined the agreement, each waiving its lawsuits over the collapse. For investors these are now scheduled cash outflows rather than open-ended unknowns. The UK lawsuit is the part that still moves.
