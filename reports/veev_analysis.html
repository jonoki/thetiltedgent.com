01 Company Overview

Veeva Systems sells industry-specific cloud software and data to life sciences — pharmaceutical, biotech and medical-device companies — covering everything from clinical trial management and regulatory submissions through to the tablet a sales rep carries into a physician's office. Fiscal 2026, which ended January 31, 2026, produced $3,195M of revenue, up 16.3% from $2,747M in FY2025, with GAAP operating income of $947M (29.6% margin) and $1,386M of free cash flow. On October 30, 2026 the market capitalisation was roughly $50.7B, ranking #230 in the S&P 500 (161.91M shares × the $312.96 close). Veeva joined the S&P 500 on May 7, 2026, replacing Coterra Energy — confirmed on S&P Global's own announcement page.

A note on the calendar before anything else, because almost every number on this page depends on it. Veeva's fiscal year ends on January 31. So "FY2026" is the year that ended January 2026 — it is mostly calendar 2025. The most recent reported quarter is fiscal Q2 FY2027, the three months ended July 31, 2026, reported on August 26, 2026; the October quarter (Q3 FY2027) closes on October 31 and has not been reported yet. Anyone comparing Veeva's "2026" to a calendar-year company's 2026 is off by roughly eleven months. Every fiscal label below is stated explicitly.

The central debate is a migration. Veeva's original commercial CRM was built on Salesforce's platform; the company has spent years building Vault CRM on its own Vault architecture and moving customers onto it. That transition is the live story, and it is visible in the segment numbers: in Q2 FY2027, R&D and Quality subscription revenue grew 19% while Commercial subscription revenue grew 13%. Management said in the Q2 release that Vault CRM had its best quarter ever, with more than 180 customers live and 12 of the top 20 biopharmas committed; by Veeva's September 23, 2026 release it had more than 190 customers live and 14 of the top 20 biopharmas committed globally. The question this page works through is whether the Commercial half is mid-transition and about to reaccelerate, or whether that 13% is simply what a mature CRM franchise grows at — and whether a 44.8% non-GAAP operating margin with no borrowings deserves 51× trailing earnings.

The sales-and-marketing side: CRM for pharmaceutical field forces, plus content management, medical-affairs tooling and commercial data. This is where the migration story lives. Veeva's legacy CRM ran on Salesforce's platform; Vault CRM is the replacement built on Veeva's own Vault architecture, and moving a global field force between the two is a multi-year, customer-by-customer project. The verified state of play as of the Q2 FY2027 release: more than 180 Vault CRM customers live, including five of the top 20 biopharmas; two further top-20 biopharmas plus one large enterprise committed during August, taking total top-20 commitments to 12 globally; and one top-20 customer deployed Vault CRM together with Agentic Call Report across its entire U.S. field team in the quarter. The falsifiable claim: 14 of the top 20 biopharmas committed (September 23, 2026), 5 live at the Q2 release. If commercial subscription growth has not moved off 13% once the rest go live, the migration was not the reason it was 13%.

Structurally, consulting is lower-margin and less valuable per dollar than software, so a rising services mix is a modest drag on blended gross margin. 03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
REGULATORY VALIDATION LOCK-IN SINGLE-INDUSTRY DEPTH SYSTEM-OF-RECORD DATA TOP-20 BIOPHARMA PENETRATION MULTI-PRODUCT ATTACH 44.8% NON-GAAP OP MARGIN ZERO-DEBT BALANCE SHEET

The moat is regulatory, not technical. Veeva's software holds the records that health authorities inspect: trial master files, submission dossiers, quality deviations, promotional-material approvals. Those systems are formally validated when installed and re-validated when changed, and the customer's own standard operating procedures reference them by name. Switching is therefore not a software project with a budget — it is a compliance project with an audit trail, and the executive who authorises it owns the risk personally. That is a materially harder wall to climb than "our product is better," and it explains a set of numbers that do not usually coexist: 74.96% gross margin, a 44.8% non-GAAP operating margin in Q2 FY2027 (29.6% on a GAAP basis), $1.64B of trailing free cash flow, and a balance sheet carrying $7.24B of cash and short-term investments against $151.7M of reported total debt — a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.02, and an enterprise value (market cap plus debt minus cash, our calculation at the October 30 close: about $43.6B) roughly $7B below the market cap. Veeva is one of the few scaled software companies with essentially no borrowings, and it funds every expansion from operating cash.

The second layer is single-industry depth. A horizontal platform vendor has to build life-sciences features as one vertical among twenty; Veeva builds nothing else. The 14 of the top 20 biopharmas now committed to Vault CRM did not choose it on price, and in October IQVIA, a competitor in clinical software, standardised its own trial operations on Veeva's applications. But note what the moat does not cover: it protects the R&D and Quality franchise far more completely than the Commercial one. A CRM system is not inspected by the FDA. That asymmetry is visible in the growth rates — 19% versus 13% — and it is the honest starting point for the vulnerabilities below.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The CRM franchise is the contestable half. Commercial subscription growth of 13% is the slowest line in the business, and CRM is precisely the product where the regulatory switching cost is weakest. Salesforce, having lost the Veeva partnership, has every incentive to compete directly for life-sciences CRM with its own vertical offering, and it can subsidise that effort from a revenue base many times Veeva's. Veeva is simultaneously migrating its own installed base onto a new platform and defending it — the most exposed moment in any platform transition.
▸
Customer concentration in a consolidating industry. Veeva's revenue rests on a small number of very large pharmaceutical companies — the release itself counts progress in units of "top 20 biopharmas." Pharma mergers remove customers by arithmetic, and a single large customer rationalising its systems after a merger can cost more than a dozen mid-size wins. R&D budget cuts at two or three majors would show up in Veeva's numbers within a few quarters.
▸
The GAAP / non-GAAP gap is not cosmetic. Q2 FY2027 non-GAAP operating income was $415.9M against GAAP operating income of $275.0M — a $140.9M quarterly difference, 15.2 percentage points of margin. The headline "44.8% operating margin" that makes Veeva look like one of the most profitable software businesses in the market becomes 29.6% on a GAAP basis. GAAP 29.6% is still excellent; the point is that the stock's high price-to-earnings ratio is often defended with the 44.8% figure.
▸
Valuation leaves no margin for a slip. At $312.96 the stock trades at roughly 51.3× trailing GAAP earnings of $6.10 and about 34× the ~$9.21 of non-GAAP FY2027 EPS the company guides to, on 15–16% revenue growth, and above the average analyst target. That is a price that requires both halves of the business to work. The April 2026 close of $155.97, a 47.6% fall from the September 2025 month-end close, shows how fast the price investors pay for each dollar of earnings can shrink when growth is questioned; the stock has since doubled with one new quarter reported.

Notable One-Off Items

Two recurring — not one-off — items deserve the attention that one-offs usually get:

The GAAP-to-non-GAAP bridge. In Q2 FY2027 the gap between $415.9M non-GAAP operating income and $275.0M GAAP operating income was $140.9M in a single quarter — roughly 15.2 points of operating margin. This recurs every quarter and is predominantly stock-based compensation and related items. It is not a one-off, which is precisely why it should not be mentally filed as one. The company's FY2027 guidance of ~$1,640M of operating income and ~$9.21 of EPS is stated on the non-GAAP basis; the GAAP equivalents will be materially lower.

The fiscal-calendar trap. FY2026 ended January 31, 2026 and FY2027 ends January 31, 2027. Screeners and news summaries routinely compare Veeva's "FY2026" revenue of $3.195B against calendar-2026 figures for peers. Growth rates built that way are wrong by a quarter. Every figure on this page is labelled with its fiscal period for that reason.

Margin trajectory, for context on whether 29.6% GAAP is the new normal. GAAP operating margin by fiscal year: FY2022 27.3%, FY2023 21.3%, FY2024 18.2%, FY2025 25.4%, FY2026 29.6%. The FY2023–FY2024 trough coincided with the platform-rebuild investment period; the recovery since is the clearest single piece of evidence that the Vault transition is passing its peak cost.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 is conventionally read as "overbought" — the stock has risen fast and may be due a pause; below 30 is "oversold." It measures momentum, not value: a company can be expensive with a low RSI or cheap with a high one. Calculated here on the 61 monthly closes above, using a 6-month lookback.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $317.01 to the October 30, 2026 close of $312.96, VEEV is down 1.3% over five years — about −0.3% a year, with no dividend to soften it. Over the same five years revenue went from $1.85B (FY2022) to a trailing $3.46B and GAAP EPS from $2.63 to $6.10. The business roughly doubled and the share price is back where it started. That is the five-year story in one sentence: investors now pay far less for each dollar of Veeva's earnings than they did in 2021, when software stocks traded at the high prices of the zero-rate years, and the earnings growth has only just made up for it.

Inside that flat line, the swings have been severe despite a reported beta of only 0.88. The stock fell 49.1% from October 2021 to the December 2022 close of $161.38, spent 2023 and 2024 moving sideways in a $165–$232 band, climbed to $297.91 in September 2025, then lost 47.6% in seven months to the April 2026 close of $155.97 — the five-year low, and the month before S&P 500 inclusion was announced. From there it has doubled (+100.7%) in six months: +40.2% in August 2026 around the Q2 FY2027 print, a flat September (−0.1%), and +9.6% in October to $312.96, its highest month-end close since October 2021. An investor's outcome here has depended almost entirely on entry date.

Relative performance. Over the year to October 30, 2026 VEEV rose 9.8%, against 11.2% for the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY) and 10.3% for the Invesco QQQ (Nasdaq-100) ETF. Over the five-year window (October 29, 2021 to October 30, 2026) it is down 1.3%, while SPY rose 64.6% and QQQ 78.8%, all price returns without dividends. The S&P 500 is the benchmark for this NYSE listing, which joined the index on May 7, 2026. The underperformance is a valuation story rather than an earnings story. The 0.88 beta deserves suspicion: a stock that fell 47.6% from peak to trough in seven months and then rose 40% in a single month is not tracking the market, and the low measured beta mostly reflects that its big moves have been its own rather than the market's.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Veeva owns the regulated systems of record for the pharmaceutical industry, and that position produces financials almost no software company at $3.5B of revenue can match: 74.96% gross margin, a 44.8% non-GAAP operating margin, $1.64B of free cash flow on $26.7M of capex, and $7.24B of cash against effectively no debt. The Vault CRM migration is visibly working — 190+ customers live, 14 of the top 20 biopharmas committed, and commercial services growing 25% as the implementation pipeline builds — and IQVIA's October decision to run its trials on Veeva software shows the clinical side still winning too. If commercial subscription growth moves from 13% toward the R&D segment's 19% as those commitments go live, revenue growth reaccelerates from a 15–16% base with no incremental capital required, and a PEG of 0.95 on 34× guided FY2027 earnings starts to look like the right price rather than a full one.

Bear Case

You are paying 51.3× trailing GAAP earnings for 16% revenue growth that has been remarkably stable — and stable is another word for not accelerating. FY2027 guidance implies 15.3% growth, slightly slower than FY2026, and Q3 guidance puts non-GAAP EPS fractionally below Q2's. The headline 44.8% margin is non-GAAP; the GAAP figure is 29.6%, a $140.9M-per-quarter difference that recurs forever. Commercial, the contestable half of the business, is growing at 13% while a well-capitalised Salesforce competes directly for life-sciences CRM. And the price chart is a warning: this stock fell 47.6% in seven months as recently as April 2026, on no identifiable disaster, purely because investors paid less for each dollar of earnings. It has since doubled on one good quarter and a string of customer announcements, and now trades above the average analyst target of $303.52.

Top 3 Risks

01
The Commercial migration stalls or leaks. Fourteen of the top 20 biopharmas are committed, but only five were live at the Q2 release. Every migration is a re-evaluation moment, and CRM is the one Veeva product a customer can change without a regulatory filing. If commercial subscription growth is still 13% two or three quarters from now, the "mid-transition" explanation expires and what remains is a mature franchise growing at low-teens rates and priced at 51× earnings.
02
The price investors pay for each dollar of earnings can fall without any operating deterioration. The five-year record is unambiguous: revenue nearly doubled, GAAP EPS more than doubled, and the share price ended the five years 1.3% lower. The 2025–26 drop of 47.6% happened without a broken quarter. At 51.3× trailing and 34× guided non-GAAP earnings, the same thing can happen again, and nothing in the operating results would warn you first.
03
Customer concentration in a consolidating, budget-constrained industry. Veeva's disclosures measure progress in top-20 biopharmas. That concentration cuts both ways: each win is large, and each loss, merger or R&D budget cut at a major customer is correspondingly large. Pharmaceutical R&D spending is not a stable input — it moves with drug-pricing policy, patent cliffs and the cost of capital — and Veeva has no diversification outside the industry by design.
Industry Commentary
Agentic AI arrives in a regulated industry
EMERGING TAILWIND

The Q2 FY2027 release led, in the CEO's own words, with AI: "AI is opening up the next big chapter for Veeva and life sciences," alongside a named agentic product — Agentic Call Report — deployed across an entire U.S. field team at a top-20 biopharma. The structural point in Veeva's favour is that life sciences cannot adopt general-purpose AI casually: outputs that touch clinical records, regulatory submissions or promotional claims must be traceable, validated and auditable. A vendor that already owns the validated system of record is better positioned to sell an agent that writes into it than a horizontal AI vendor is. The structural point against: Veeva has not disclosed any revenue figure for Falcon or its agentic products, so the contribution is currently narrative rather than measurable. It is, however, the most plausible mechanism by which a 15%-growth company reaccelerates.

The two halves of pharma software are diverging
ONGOING HEADWIND

Veeva's own segment data is the cleanest available read on where life-sciences technology budgets are going: R&D and Quality subscription +19%, Commercial subscription +13% in the most recent reported quarter (Q2 FY2027, to July 31, 2026). Money is flowing toward clinical, regulatory and quality systems — the spending a drug company cannot defer without delaying a programme — and more slowly toward commercial tooling, which is discretionary and competitive. That divergence is not unique to Veeva; it describes the category. For Veeva specifically it means the safer, stickier half of the business is also the faster-growing half, which is unusual and favourable, but it also means the migration story attached to the Commercial segment is being asked to overcome a genuine sector headwind rather than simply a product transition.

Index inclusion changed who owns the stock — and what a miss costs
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Joining the S&P 500 on May 7, 2026 changed who owns the stock: index funds now hold a larger share, and they buy and hold whatever is in the index, so active investors trading the remaining shares set the price day to day. That can make moves bigger in both directions, and the record since fits: +100.7% from the April low to the October 30 close, including a single +40.2% month. Bull framing: a steady base of index-fund holders under a business generating $1.64B of free cash with no debt, at a PEG of 0.95, with 14 top-20 Vault CRM commitments against 5 live at the Q2 release. Bear framing: a stock that has doubled in six months, guides Q3 non-GAAP EPS slightly below Q2, trades at 51.3× trailing GAAP earnings and above the average analyst target, and showed in the seven months to April 2026 that it can lose nearly half its value without a single bad quarter. Both framings use the same numbers; the difference is entirely what you pay.
