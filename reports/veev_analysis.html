01 Company Overview

Veeva Systems sells industry-specific cloud software and data to life sciences — pharmaceutical, biotech and medical-device companies — covering everything from clinical trial management and regulatory submissions through to the tablet a sales rep carries into a physician's office. Fiscal 2026, which ended January 31, 2026, produced $3,195M of revenue, up 16.3% from $2,747M in FY2025, with GAAP operating income of $947M (29.6% margin) and $1,386M of free cash flow. On September 10, 2026 the market capitalisation was roughly $42.3B, ranking about #608 globally. Veeva joined the S&P 500 on May 7, 2026, replacing Coterra Energy — confirmed on S&P Global's own announcement page.

A note on the calendar before anything else, because almost every number on this page depends on it. Veeva's fiscal year ends on January 31. So "FY2026" is the year that ended January 2026 — it is mostly calendar 2025. The most recent reported quarter is fiscal Q2 FY2027, the three months ended July 31, 2026, reported on August 26, 2026. Anyone comparing Veeva's "2026" to a calendar-year company's 2026 is off by roughly eleven months. Every fiscal label below is stated explicitly.

The central debate is a migration. Veeva's original commercial CRM was built on Salesforce's platform; the company has spent years building Vault CRM on its own Vault architecture and moving customers onto it. That transition is the live story, and it is visible in the segment numbers: in Q2 FY2027, R&D and Quality subscription revenue grew 19% while Commercial subscription revenue grew 13%. Management says Vault CRM had "its best quarter ever," with over 180 customers live and 12 of the top 20 biopharmas committed globally. The question this page works through is whether the Commercial half is mid-transition and about to reaccelerate, or whether that 13% is simply what a mature CRM franchise grows at — and whether a 44.8% non-GAAP operating margin with no borrowings deserves 43× trailing earnings.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
REGULATORY VALIDATION LOCK-IN SINGLE-INDUSTRY DEPTH SYSTEM-OF-RECORD DATA TOP-20 BIOPHARMA PENETRATION MULTI-PRODUCT ATTACH 44.8% NON-GAAP OP MARGIN ZERO-DEBT BALANCE SHEET

The moat is regulatory, not technical. Veeva's software holds the records that health authorities inspect: trial master files, submission dossiers, quality deviations, promotional-material approvals. Those systems are formally validated when installed and re-validated when changed, and the customer's own standard operating procedures reference them by name. Switching is therefore not a software project with a budget — it is a compliance project with an audit trail, and the executive who authorises it owns the risk personally. That is a materially harder wall to climb than "our product is better," and it explains a set of numbers that do not usually coexist: 74.96% gross margin, a 44.8% non-GAAP operating margin in Q2 FY2027 (29.6% on a GAAP basis), $1.64B of trailing free cash flow, and a balance sheet carrying $7.24B of cash and short-term investments against $151.7M of reported total debt — a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.02, and an enterprise value ($35.46B) roughly $7B below the market cap. Veeva is one of the few scaled software companies with essentially no borrowings, and it funds every expansion from operating cash.

The second layer is single-industry depth. A horizontal platform vendor has to build life-sciences features as one vertical among twenty; Veeva builds nothing else. The 12 of the top 20 biopharmas now committed to Vault CRM did not choose it on price. But note what the moat does not cover: it protects the R&D and Quality franchise far more completely than the Commercial one. A CRM system is not inspected by the FDA. That asymmetry is visible in the growth rates — 19% versus 13% — and it is the honest starting point for the vulnerabilities below.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
The CRM franchise is the contestable half. Commercial subscription growth of 13% is the slowest line in the business, and CRM is precisely the product where the regulatory switching cost is weakest. Salesforce, having lost the Veeva partnership, has every incentive to compete directly for life-sciences CRM with its own vertical offering, and it can subsidise that effort from a revenue base many times Veeva's. Veeva is simultaneously migrating its own installed base onto a new platform and defending it — the most exposed moment in any platform transition.
▸
Customer concentration in a consolidating industry. Veeva's revenue rests on a small number of very large pharmaceutical companies — the release itself counts progress in units of "top 20 biopharmas." Pharma mergers remove customers by arithmetic, and a single large customer rationalising its systems after a merger can cost more than a dozen mid-size wins. R&D budget cuts at two or three majors would show up in Veeva's numbers within a few quarters.
▸
The GAAP / non-GAAP gap is not cosmetic. Q2 FY2027 non-GAAP operating income was $415.9M against GAAP operating income of $275.0M — a $140.9M quarterly difference, 15.2 percentage points of margin. The headline "44.8% operating margin" that makes Veeva look like one of the most profitable software businesses in the market becomes 29.6% on a GAAP basis. GAAP 29.6% is still excellent; the point is that the multiple is frequently justified with the 44.8% figure.
▸
Valuation leaves no margin for a slip. At $260.98 the stock trades at roughly 42.8× trailing GAAP earnings of $6.10 and about 28× the ~$9.21 of non-GAAP FY2027 EPS the company guides to, on 15–16% revenue growth. That is a price that requires both halves of the business to work. The April 2026 low of $155.97 — a 47.6% drawdown from the September 2025 monthly close — is a recent demonstration of how fast this multiple can compress when growth is questioned.

Notable One-Off Items

No acquisitions, restructurings, impairments or litigation settlements of a size that would distort the reported figures were identified on the pages fetched for this report. The trailing numbers appear to be clean operating results. Two structural — not one-off — items deserve the attention that one-offs usually get:

The GAAP-to-non-GAAP bridge. In Q2 FY2027 the gap between $415.9M non-GAAP operating income and $275.0M GAAP operating income was $140.9M in a single quarter — roughly 15.2 points of operating margin. This recurs every quarter and is predominantly stock-based compensation and related items. It is not a one-off, which is precisely why it should not be mentally filed as one. The company's FY2027 guidance of ~$1,640M of operating income and ~$9.21 of EPS is stated on the non-GAAP basis; the GAAP equivalents will be materially lower.

The fiscal-calendar trap. FY2026 ended January 31, 2026 and FY2027 ends January 31, 2027. Screeners and news summaries routinely compare Veeva's "FY2026" revenue of $3.195B against calendar-2026 figures for peers. Growth rates built that way are wrong by a quarter. Every figure on this page is labelled with its fiscal period for that reason.

Margin trajectory, for context on whether 29.6% GAAP is the new normal. GAAP operating margin by fiscal year: FY2022 27.3%, FY2023 21.3%, FY2024 18.2%, FY2025 25.4%, FY2026 29.6%. The FY2023–FY2024 trough coincided with the platform-rebuild investment period; the recovery since is the clearest single piece of evidence that the Vault transition is passing its peak cost.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $317.01 to the September 10, 2026 close of $260.98, VEEV is down 17.7% over five years — roughly −3.8% compounded annually, with no dividend to soften it. Over the same five years revenue went from $1.85B (FY2022) to a trailing $3.46B and GAAP EPS from $2.63 to $6.10. The business roughly doubled and the share price fell by a sixth. That is the entire five-year story in one sentence: this has been a multiple-compression name, not a fundamental disappointment. The starting point was a peak-liquidity software multiple; the market has spent five years taking it apart.

Inside that flat-to-down line, the volatility has been severe despite a reported beta of only 0.97. The stock fell 49.1% from October 2021 to the December 2022 close of $161.38, spent 2023 and 2024 chopping sideways in a $165–$232 band, broke out to a monthly-close high of $297.91 in September 2025, then lost 47.6% in seven months to the April 2026 close of $155.97 — the five-year low, and the month before S&P 500 inclusion was announced. From there it has risen 67.3% in five months, including a +40.2% single month in August 2026 around the Q2 FY2027 print, before giving back 8.6% in the first ten days of September. An investor's outcome here has depended almost entirely on entry date.

Relative performance. Total-return series for the S&P 500 and the NASDAQ-100 over the identical October 2021 – September 2026 window were not fetched for this report, so no precise relative figure is stated. What can be said from verified data on this page: VEEV's 52-week price change is −4.24%, and over the five-year window it is down 17.7% in price terms. Both the S&P 500 — the benchmark index for this NYSE listing, which VEEV joined on May 7, 2026 — and the NASDAQ-100 posted positive total returns over the same five years; VEEV has therefore underperformed both, and the underperformance is a valuation story rather than an earnings story. The 0.97 beta is worth treating with suspicion: a stock that fell 47.6% peak-to-trough in seven months and then rose 40% in a single month is not behaving like a market-tracking asset, and the low measured beta largely reflects the fact that its drawdowns have been idiosyncratic rather than correlated.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Veeva owns the regulated systems of record for the pharmaceutical industry, and that position produces financials almost no software company at $3.5B of revenue can match: 74.96% gross margin, a 44.8% non-GAAP operating margin, $1.64B of free cash flow on $26.7M of capex, and $7.24B of cash against effectively no debt. The Vault CRM migration is visibly working — 180+ customers live, 12 of the top 20 biopharmas committed, and commercial services growing 25% as the implementation pipeline builds. If commercial subscription growth moves from 13% toward the R&D segment's 19% as those commitments go live, revenue growth reaccelerates from a 15–16% base with no incremental capital required, and a PEG of 0.77 on a 28× forward multiple starts to look like the right price rather than a full one.

Bear Case

You are paying 42.8× trailing GAAP earnings for 16% revenue growth that has been remarkably stable — and stable is another word for not accelerating. FY2027 guidance implies 15.4% growth, slightly slower than FY2026, and Q3 guidance puts non-GAAP EPS fractionally below Q2's. The headline 44.8% margin is non-GAAP; the GAAP figure is 29.6%, a $140.9M-per-quarter difference that recurs forever. Commercial, the contestable half of the business, is growing at 13% while a well-capitalised Salesforce competes directly for life-sciences CRM. And the price chart is a warning: this stock fell 47.6% in seven months as recently as April 2026, on no identifiable disaster, purely because the multiple moved.

Top 3 Risks

01
The Commercial migration stalls or leaks. Twelve of the top 20 biopharmas are committed but only five are live. Every migration is a re-evaluation moment, and CRM is the one Veeva product a customer can change without a regulatory filing. If commercial subscription growth is still 13% two or three quarters from now, the "mid-transition" explanation expires and what remains is a mature franchise growing at low-teens rates inside a 43× multiple.
02
Multiple compression without any operating deterioration. The five-year record is unambiguous: revenue nearly doubled, GAAP EPS more than doubled, and the share price fell 17.7%. The 2025–26 drawdown of 47.6% happened without a broken quarter. At 42.8× trailing and 28× guided non-GAAP earnings, the same thing can happen again, and nothing in the operating results would warn you first.
03
Customer concentration in a consolidating, budget-constrained industry. Veeva's disclosures measure progress in top-20 biopharmas. That concentration cuts both ways: each win is large, and each loss, merger or R&D budget cut at a major customer is correspondingly large. Pharmaceutical R&D spending is not a stable input — it moves with drug-pricing policy, patent cliffs and the cost of capital — and Veeva has no diversification outside the industry by design.
Industry Commentary
Agentic AI arrives in a regulated industry
EMERGING TAILWIND

The Q2 FY2027 release led, in the CEO's own words, with AI: "AI is opening up the next big chapter for Veeva and life sciences… Veeva Falcon accelerated rapidly," alongside a named agentic product — Agentic Call Report — deployed across an entire U.S. field team at a top-20 biopharma. The structural point in Veeva's favour is that life sciences cannot adopt general-purpose AI casually: outputs that touch clinical records, regulatory submissions or promotional claims must be traceable, validated and auditable. A vendor that already owns the validated system of record is better positioned to sell an agent that writes into it than a horizontal AI vendor is. The structural point against: Veeva has not disclosed any revenue figure for Falcon or its agentic products, so the contribution is currently narrative rather than measurable. It is, however, the most plausible mechanism by which a 15%-growth company reaccelerates.

The two halves of pharma software are diverging
ONGOING HEADWIND

Veeva's own segment data is the cleanest available read on where life-sciences technology budgets are going: R&D and Quality subscription +19%, Commercial subscription +13% in the most recent quarter. Money is flowing toward clinical, regulatory and quality systems — the spending a drug company cannot defer without delaying a programme — and more slowly toward commercial tooling, which is discretionary and competitive. That divergence is not unique to Veeva; it describes the category. For Veeva specifically it means the safer, stickier half of the business is also the faster-growing half, which is unusual and favourable, but it also means the migration story attached to the Commercial segment is being asked to overcome a genuine sector headwind rather than simply a product transition.

Index inclusion changed who owns the stock — and what a miss costs
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Joining the S&P 500 on May 7, 2026 permanently changed the shareholder register: a meaningful slice of the float is now held by index funds that will never sell for fundamental reasons and never buy more on good news. Active money at the margin sets the price against a thinner effective float, which amplifies both directions — and the price record since is exactly that, +67.3% from the April low including a single +40.2% month. Bull framing: a permanent passive bid underneath a business generating $1.64B of free cash with no debt, at a PEG of 0.77, with a migration pipeline that has 12 top-20 commitments and only 5 live. Bear framing: a stock that just re-rated 40% in a month, guides Q3 non-GAAP EPS slightly below Q2, trades at 42.8× trailing GAAP earnings, and demonstrated in the seven months to April 2026 that it can lose nearly half its value without a single bad quarter. Both framings are built from the same verified numbers; the difference is entirely what you pay.
