01 Company Overview

Every poker player learns the old line: the house always wins. VICI Properties is the answer to the next question — who owns the house? VICI is a REIT that owns the buildings and land under 103 "experiential" assets — 63 gaming properties and 40 other leisure properties, roughly 130 million square feet and about 66,000 hotel rooms — and leases them back to the companies that run them on very long triple-net leases. Occupancy is 100%, the weighted average lease term is 39.4 years, and the business ran on 28 employees at last count. It never touches a chip; it collects rent from the people who do.

VICI was born out of a bankruptcy. On October 6, 2017 it was spun off to the creditors of an operating company going through Chapter 11 — a company whose group is still VICI's largest tenant today. It listed on the NYSE in February 2018, raising $1.2B, bought a rival gaming-property REIT for $17.2B in April 2022, and joined the S&P 500 on June 8, 2022. For a REIT, the number that matters is not EPS but AFFO: $2.38 per share in FY2025 (+5.1%), $0.62 in Q3 2026 (+4.3%), with FY2026 guidance raised for the third time this year, to $2.47–$2.48. The stock closed at $24.46 on October 30, 2026 — down 17.1% over 52 weeks, at about 9.9x guided AFFO with a 7.52% dividend yield. Q3 added two headlines: a $458.0M non-cash credit-loss charge that cut net income per share 56.4% to $0.31 while AFFO per share rose, and VICI's first-ever share buyback, up to $2.0B through September 30, 2029. The cash flow is growing and the price is falling. That gap is the whole report, and it still comes down to a single lease — whose tenant has agreed to be bought.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

39.4-year average lease term Irreplaceable Strip real estate Master leases (all-or-nothing) Triple-net cost structure Investment-grade balance sheet Inflation-linked escalators 100% occupancy

The key structure is the master lease: many properties bundled into one contract, so a tenant cannot keep the winners and hand back the losers — it pays for all of them or defaults on all of them, including the flagship buildings its brand depends on. The weakness is the flip side of the same design: when two tenant groups pay 70% of the rent, the landlord's bargaining power is only as good as the tenant's ability to pay. A master lease protects the contract; it cannot create cash flow the properties do not produce.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One lease, a fifth of the rent. The largest tenant's regional master lease — about 22% of VICI's real-estate rent per the Q3 supplement, at ~1.03x coverage per a December 2025 J.P. Morgan estimate — is the live risk. A rent cut would reduce AFFO directly; the question is how much VICI gets back in exchange (more real estate, a longer term). The tenant is being bought in a pending cash deal, and on the Q3 call management said it would work through its options after the buyout (CDC Gaming Reports).
02
Tenant concentration. 38% of rent from one group, 32% from another. Even with 16 tenants, VICI's credit quality is effectively the weighted credit quality of two companies.
03
Cost of capital. A REIT grows by issuing stock and debt to buy property yielding more than the capital costs. With the shares at ~9.9x AFFO (a ~10.1% AFFO yield) and new deals at around 7.5–9%, issuing equity to buy property now shrinks AFFO per share. That is why management is leaning on lending and has authorized its first buyback, up to $2.0B: buying back stock at this price retires cash flow yielding about 10%.
04
Escalators capped below inflation spikes. Inflation-linked escalators are typically capped at 2.5–3%. In a 2022-style inflation year, the rent grows more slowly than prices, while the stock trades like a long-term bond, falling when rates rise.

Notable One-Off Items

The big one is the CECL allowance. VICI books most of its leases as financial assets (sales-type leases and financing receivables), so it must carry an estimated lifetime credit-loss allowance and re-mark it every quarter. At September 30, 2026 the allowance stood at $1,282.4M on sales-type leases, $884.9M on lease financing receivables, $159.0M on loans and securities and $29.7M on sub-leases — $2,356.0M in total (our sum), against $1,769.4M at December 31, 2025 (+33%). In Q3 2026 the allowance change was a $458.0M expense versus a $20.2M benefit a year earlier (a $478.2M swing, our arithmetic); for nine months it was $610.3M against $24.8M. That is why net income per share fell 56.4% in Q3 while AFFO rose. It is non-cash and moves with macro forecasts, but it is not meaningless: it is the accounting system's running estimate of tenant credit risk, and it rose this year. Second, share issuance: 24,296,255 shares went to former Golden Entertainment shareholders in that transaction — the reason AFFO grew 7.5% in dollars but 4.3% per share. Net income guidance is not given because of CECL volatility; AFFO guidance is.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 9.9x AFFO — a ~10.1% cash-earnings yield — for a landlord with a 39.4-year lease book, 100% occupancy, investment-grade ratings, 98.4% fixed-rate debt and 4.8x net leverage, whose AFFO per share rose 5.1% in FY2025 and whose 2026 guidance has now been raised three times (April, July and October). You are paid 7.52% to wait, with ~25% of AFFO retained, and the board has authorized a $2.0B buyback that would retire cash flow at roughly that 10% yield. The stock trades at 0.93x book. The regional-lease risk is real but bounded: even a meaningful rent reset on a lease worth about a fifth of the rent would likely come with extra real estate or term in exchange, and resolution would remove the overhang that has cost the stock 28% from its August 2025 level. The average analyst target is $30.24, about 24% above the price.

Bear Case

A landlord is only as good as its tenant's ability to pay, and 70% of VICI's rent comes from two groups. The largest one's regional properties cover their rent about 1.03x on a December 2025 analyst estimate, which leaves little room if the tenant's new owners want a lower rent. The CECL allowance, the accounting system's own read of tenant risk, rose a third in nine months to $2.36B, and Q3 net income per share fell 56%. With the stock at a ~10% AFFO yield, VICI cannot sell new shares to buy property without shrinking AFFO per share, so its main way of growing is stalled until the price recovers. The RSI has bounced to 33.9 from a five-year low of 20.0, but the price is still 9% below its 10-month average; a cheap REIT with a concentration problem can stay cheap for a long time.

Industry Commentary
Rent coverage: the landlord's real risk
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Gaming real estate was built on a simple trade: operating companies sold their buildings to landlords for cash and signed decades-long master leases with fixed escalators. The escalators compound whether or not the property's profits do. Where profits grew faster — the Strip — coverage stayed comfortable. Where they did not — parts of regional gaming — rent crept up towards the cash flow available to pay it. At ~1.03x, per the December 2025 estimate, there is almost no room left. This is not a VICI-specific problem so much as a structural feature of the sector, and it is the reason the gaming landlords' yields sit well above generic net-lease REITs.

Accounting noise: CECL versus AFFO
READ CAREFULLY

Because VICI records most leases as financing assets, headline EPS and FFO move with a credit-loss allowance that changes with macro forecasts. Q3 2026 showed the effect at full size: net income per share −56%, AFFO per share +4.3%. Screens and consensus EPS "misses" pick up the headline number; the dividend is paid out of the AFFO number. Anyone valuing VICI on P/E alone is using the wrong ruler — but the allowance direction is still worth watching as a signal.

Diversification: from casino landlord to experiential landlord
EMERGING TAILWIND

Management's long-run answer to concentration is to own and lend against other "experiential" real estate: a Club Med resort in St. Croix, a mezzanine loan on One Beverly Hills, golf, and — per the Q2 call — possible arena and university sports infrastructure. Three new tenants in Q2 took the roster to 16, and the Highfield lease announced in Q3 adds another once it closes. The bull framing: each deal lowers the top-two share and proves the model works outside gaming. The bear framing: 70% concentration does not dilute quickly at a few hundred million dollars a deal, and at today's share price the equity needed to scale it is expensive. Both are true; the regional lease will decide which one the market listens to first.
