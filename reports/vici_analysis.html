01 Company Overview

Every poker player learns the old line: the house always wins. VICI Properties is the answer to the next question — who owns the house? VICI is a REIT that owns the buildings and land under 103 "experiential" assets — 63 gaming properties and 40 other leisure properties, roughly 130 million square feet and about 66,000 hotel rooms — and leases them back to the companies that run them on very long triple-net leases. Occupancy is 100%, the weighted average lease term is 39.6 years, and the business ran on 28 employees at last count. It never touches a chip; it collects rent from the people who do.

VICI was born out of a bankruptcy. On October 6, 2017 it was spun off to the creditors of an operating company going through Chapter 11 — a company whose group is still VICI's largest tenant today. It listed on the NYSE in February 2018, raising $1.2B, bought a rival gaming-property REIT for $17.2B in April 2022, and joined the S&P 500 on June 8, 2022. For a REIT, the number that matters is not EPS but AFFO: $2.38 per share in FY2025 (+5.1%), $0.62 in Q2 2026 (+4.6%), with FY2026 guidance raised to $2.45–$2.47. The stock, meanwhile, closed at $24.05 — its lowest point on the five-year chart below, down 24.5% over 52 weeks, at about 9.8x guided AFFO with a 7.65% dividend yield. The cash flow is growing and the price is falling. That gap is the whole report, and it comes down to a single lease.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

39.6-year average lease term Irreplaceable Strip real estate Master leases (all-or-nothing) Triple-net cost structure Investment-grade balance sheet Inflation-linked escalators 100% occupancy

The key structure is the master lease: many properties bundled into one contract, so a tenant cannot keep the winners and hand back the losers — it pays for all of them or defaults on all of them, including the flagship buildings its brand depends on. The weakness is the flip side of the same design: when two tenant groups pay 70% of the rent, the landlord's bargaining power is only as good as the tenant's ability to pay. A master lease protects the contract; it cannot create cash flow the properties do not produce.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
One lease, one quarter of the story. The largest tenant's regional master lease — roughly a quarter of NOI by analyst estimates, at ~1.03x coverage per a December 2025 J.P. Morgan estimate — is the live risk. A rent cut would reduce AFFO directly; the question is how much VICI gets back in exchange (more real estate, a longer term). Management gave no timetable, and noted the tenant is working through its own corporate transaction.
02
Tenant concentration. 38% of rent from one group, 32% from another. Even with 16 tenants, VICI's credit quality is effectively the weighted credit quality of two companies.
03
Cost of capital. A REIT grows by issuing stock and debt to buy property yielding more than the capital costs. With the shares at ~9.8x AFFO (a ~10.2% AFFO yield) and new deals at around 7.5–9%, issuing equity to buy property now shrinks AFFO per share. VICI's main growth engine is throttled until the price recovers — which is why management is leaning on lending instead.
04
Escalators capped below inflation spikes. Inflation-linked escalators are typically capped at 2.5–3%. In a 2022-style inflation year, the rent grows more slowly than prices, while the stock trades as a long-duration bond.

Notable One-Off Items

The big one is the CECL allowance. VICI books most of its leases as financial assets (sales-type leases and financing receivables), so it must carry an estimated lifetime credit-loss allowance — $1.14B gross at June 30, 2026 — and re-mark it every quarter. In Q2 2026 the allowance change was a $271.1M charge versus a $142.0M benefit a year earlier; that $413.1M swing is why net income and FFO fell ~41% while AFFO rose. It is non-cash and moves with macro forecasts, but it is not meaningless: it is the accounting system's running estimate of tenant credit risk, and it rose this year. Second, share issuance: ~24.3M shares went to the seller in the Nevada acquisition and 7.75M forward-sale shares settled in Q2 — the reason AFFO grew 7.8% in dollars but 4.6% per share. Net income guidance is not given because of CECL volatility; AFFO guidance is.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 9.8x AFFO — a ~10.2% cash-earnings yield — for a landlord with a 39.6-year lease book, 100% occupancy, investment-grade ratings, 98.4% fixed-rate debt and 4.9x net leverage, whose AFFO per share rose 5.1% in FY2025 and whose 2026 guidance was just raised. You are paid 7.65% to wait, with ~25% of AFFO retained. The stock trades at 0.91x book. The regional-lease risk is real but bounded: even a meaningful rent reset on a lease worth roughly a quarter of NOI would likely come with extra real estate or term in exchange, and resolution would remove the overhang that has cost the stock 29% from its August 2025 level. Every analyst target on the board, including the lowest at $26, sits above the price.

Bear Case

A landlord is only as good as its tenant's ability to pay, and 70% of VICI's rent comes from two groups. The largest one's regional properties cover their rent about 1.03x on a December 2025 analyst estimate — which means a rent cut is a question of when and how much, not if. The CECL allowance, the accounting system's own read of tenant risk, rose in 2026. With the stock at a ~10% AFFO yield, VICI cannot issue equity accretively, so its main growth engine is throttled until the price recovers. No September analyst action on the pages fetched raised a target. Momentum is at its most oversold in five years and still falling; a cheap REIT with a concentration problem can stay cheap for a long time.

Top 3 Risks

01
A rent reset on the largest tenant's regional master lease. Roughly a quarter of NOI by analyst estimates, at thin coverage. Size, timing and what VICI receives in exchange are all unknown; management has given no timetable.
02

Industry Commentary
Rent coverage: the landlord's real risk
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Gaming real estate was built on a simple trade: operating companies sold their buildings to landlords for cash and signed decades-long master leases with fixed escalators. The escalators compound whether or not the property's profits do. Where profits grew faster — the Strip — coverage stayed comfortable. Where they did not — parts of regional gaming — rent crept up towards the cash flow available to pay it. At ~1.03x, per the December 2025 estimate, there is almost no room left. This is not a VICI-specific problem so much as a structural feature of the sector, and it is the reason the gaming landlords' yields sit well above generic net-lease REITs.

Accounting noise: CECL versus AFFO
READ CAREFULLY

Because VICI records most leases as financing assets, headline EPS and FFO move with a credit-loss allowance that changes with macro forecasts. Q2 2026 showed the effect at full size: net income −41%, AFFO per share +4.6%. Screens and consensus EPS "misses" pick up the headline number; the dividend is paid out of the AFFO number. Anyone valuing VICI on P/E alone is using the wrong ruler — but the allowance direction is still worth watching as a signal.

Diversification: from casino landlord to experiential landlord
EMERGING TAILWIND

Management's long-run answer to concentration is to own and lend against other "experiential" real estate: a Club Med resort in St. Croix, a mezzanine loan on One Beverly Hills, golf, and — per the Q2 call — possible arena and university sports infrastructure. Three new tenants in one quarter took the roster to 16. The bull framing: each deal lowers the top-two share and proves the model works outside gaming. The bear framing: 70% concentration does not dilute quickly at a few hundred million dollars a deal, and at today's share price the equity needed to scale it is expensive. Both are true; the regional lease will decide which one the market listens to first.
