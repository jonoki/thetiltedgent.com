01 Company Overview

Valero Energy describes itself as the world's largest independent petroleum refiner (valero.com): 14 refineries across the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom with about 3.0 million barrels per day of throughput capacity, 12 ethanol plants in the US Mid-Continent with 1.7 billion gallons a year of capacity, and a half-share of Diamond Green Diesel, a joint venture that makes renewable diesel and sustainable aviation fuel. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $122.69B as reported, and revenue fell in each of the three years after the 2022 peak (2023–2025 on stockanalysis.com's figures, which net out excise taxes). Then the cycle turned. Revenue for the four quarters to September 2026 was $153.33B (+24.6%), and trailing EPS has gone from $7.57 in FY2025 to $44.46 TTM, more than half of it ($23.89 a share) earned in the third quarter of 2026 alone. The stock is up 142.2% over the past year, against 13.1% for the S&P 500 (SPY) over the same dates.

First, the one sentence that governs this entire report. The crack spread is the difference between what a refiner pays for a barrel of crude oil and what it receives for the gasoline, diesel and jet fuel it "cracks" that barrel into — it is the refiner's gross margin, and it is set by product markets, not by the price of oil itself.

That distinction is why Valero is the purest big refining stock in the US market. Marathon Petroleum (MPC, covered on this site) owns the general partner and the bulk of MPLX, a midstream business that throws off fee-based cash regardless of the crack spread; Phillips 66 (PSX, also covered here) has midstream, a 50% share of CPChem, and a marketing arm. Valero has refining, an ethanol business that is itself margin-cyclical, and a renewable diesel joint venture. There is no toll-road cushion. The central debate of this report is therefore not whether Valero is a good business — the cost structure says it plainly is — but whether an investor buying at $419.28, 5.4 times the October 2021 month-end close, is buying a structurally tighter refining market or the top of an ordinary crack-spread cycle dressed up as a structural one. The two look identical in the rear-view mirror and different only in the multiple you are willing to pay.

Throughput and utilisation. Valero ran 2,972 thousand barrels per day in Q3 2026 against about 3.0 million bpd of capacity, about 99% utilisation (our division of stated throughput by stated capacity; the release speaks of "near-record utilization rates" without printing a percentage). Every region came in at or above the Q3 ranges guided on the July 30 call: US Gulf Coast 1,874 kbpd (guided 1.78–1.83 million), Mid-Continent 472 (460–480), North Atlantic 507 (450–470) and US West Coast 119 (110–120). For Q4 the CFO guided Gulf Coast 1.855–1.91 million bpd, Mid-Continent 440–460 thousand, North Atlantic 480–500 thousand and West Coast 100–110 thousand. The West Coast is the story of the year: Benicia stopped refining in April 2026, and the region ran 119 thousand bpd in Q3 against 259 thousand a year earlier.

Margin per barrel. Q3 2026 refining margin was $39.24 per barrel, against $13.14 a year earlier and $23.62 in Q2. The release's own reference prices show where it came from: US Gulf Coast diesel sold $72.27 a barrel above Brent crude in Q3, against $21.05 a year earlier. That sensitivity to the margin is the point of owning a pure refiner: the margin per barrel roughly tripled and segment operating income rose more than fivefold, from $1.61B to $8.78B, because the cost per barrel underneath barely moved.

Cost per barrel — the long-standing edge. Refining cash operating expense was $5.03 per barrel in Q3 2026, above the $4.75 guided in July and $4.71 a year earlier, and is guided to $4.85 for Q4. Depreciation and amortisation added $2.12 per barrel, so the fully loaded cost of turning a barrel of crude into product was about $7.15. Against a $39.24 margin, that left $32.09 per barrel of adjusted operating income. At 2.97 million barrels a day, every $1.00 of margin per barrel is worth about $1.1 billion of operating income a year (2.972 million barrels × 365 days). Hold that number; it is the most useful piece of arithmetic in this report. A refiner running near $5/bbl stays cash-positive at crack spreads that force a $7–8/bbl operator to idle units, so a low-cost system can keep running through a downturn that pushes others to cut runs.

Regional profit split (Q3 2026). Refining operating income was US Gulf Coast $5,246M, North Atlantic $1,837M, Mid-Continent $1,394M and US West Coast $305M. The Gulf Coast ran 63% of throughput and earned about 60% of refining profit; it is the export engine, with the equipment to run heavy and sour (high-sulphur) crudes (the system processed 512 thousand bpd of heavy sour and 274 thousand bpd of medium/light sour crude in Q3). The North Atlantic earned the highest margin per barrel, $45.58.

Diamond Green Diesel is a joint venture between Valero and Darling Ingredients with about 1.2 billion gallons a year of renewable diesel capacity on the US Gulf Coast (Q3 2026 release); it also makes SAF (sustainable aviation fuel, jet fuel made from fats, oils and greases rather than crude). The swing here is even more violent than in refining, in both directions. Renewable diesel went from a $28 million operating loss in Q3 2025 to $353 million of operating income in Q3 2026, with margin per gallon up from $0.50 to $1.62. But from Q2 to Q3 it halved: Q2 2026 earned $717M on a $2.52-a-gallon margin and 3.8M gallons a day; Q3 earned $353M on $1.62 and 3.4M gallons a day. At the Q3 pace the segment earns about $1.4B a year; at the Q2 pace it was nearly $2.9B. That is how far one quarter can move it.

On 45Z. The 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit was discussed on the Q1 and Q2 2026 calls in an ethanol context, not a renewable diesel one. No separate per-gallon 45Z value for DGD renewable diesel was disclosed on either call. What management did flag for renewable diesel on the Q2 call was the RVO (Renewable Volume Obligation, the EPA's required blending volumes): it said the RVO and RIN values (RINs are the tradable credits that prove blending quotas were met) had risen far faster than fat prices and that renewable diesel looked more positive for the rest of 2026 and into 2027. So the 45Z regime is a live variable in the segment's economics, but neither the Q2 nor the Q3 margin can be attributed to it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

~$5/bbl Cash Opex High-Complexity Coking Heavy-Sour Crude Flexibility Gulf Coast Export Position Four-Region Diversification 4% Net Debt/Cap Irreplaceable Permits

The core advantage is arithmetic, not strategy. Refined products are a commodity — nobody pays a premium for Valero's diesel. In a commodity, the only durable edge is being lower on the cost curve than the marginal producer, and Valero's refining cash operating expense, $5.03 per barrel in Q3 2026, is the number that does the work. It comes from three things that compound: scale (2.97 million bpd spread across 14 plants, so fixed costs per barrel are thin), complexity (coking and hydrocracking capacity lets the system run cheap heavy and sour crude — in Q3 the system ran 512 thousand bpd of heavy sour and 274 thousand bpd of medium/light sour crude — and sell the same finished products as a refiner buying expensive light sweet), and geography (Gulf Coast plants sit on tidewater with export access, so when US demand softens the barrels leave the country rather than depressing the domestic crack).

Layer on the balance sheet — 4% net debt to capitalisation at September 30, $9.8B of cash against $8.6B of debt plus $2.4B of finance leases — and the moat becomes a survival mechanism. A refiner with high costs and high leverage can be forced to sell assets at the bottom of a cycle; one with low costs and little net debt is not. And the deepest moat of all is one nobody built on purpose: the last major new US refinery started up in 1977, at Garyville, Louisiana; since then US capacity has grown through small refineries and expansions of existing plants (EIA). Existing plants like Valero's are very hard to replicate.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
No non-cyclical cushion — by design. This is the flip side of the entire investment thesis. Marathon Petroleum's MPLX midstream business and Phillips 66's midstream-plus-chemicals stack both generate fee-based cash that is largely indifferent to the crack spread. Valero has none of that. Its two "diversifying" segments — renewable diesel and ethanol — are themselves margin businesses that swung from a combined $155M in Q3 2025 (a $28M renewable diesel loss plus $183M from ethanol) to $625M in Q3 2026, which means they amplify the cycle rather than damp it. When the crack spread compresses, all three segments compress together.
02
Policy is a margin input, not a background condition. Renewable diesel and ethanol earnings depend directly on the RVO, RIN prices, LCFS credits (California's low-carbon fuel credits) and the 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit. On the Q1 call Valero said it was booking $0.10/gallon on 10 plants and that the Treasury guidance it is waiting on might come by the end of 2026 or not until 2027; on the Q2 call it put the credit at about $0.17 a gallon for 2026 and $0.19 for 2027–2029. A hostile or merely delayed regulatory outcome removes a few hundred million dollars of annual operating profit with no operational warning.
03
Structural demand decline in gasoline. Electrification erodes the gasoline pool over a decade or two, and the West Coast — the region Valero has just shrunk — is the leading indicator. Diesel and jet are far more defensible, and Valero's complexity skews it toward distillate, but a refiner is ultimately long a product slate that policy is designed to shrink. The counter-argument is that capacity is shrinking faster than demand; that is a race, and it is not obviously won.
04
Single-asset operational risk at scale. Running at ≈99% utilisation means there is no spare capacity to absorb an unplanned outage. The March 2026 fire at Port Arthur's diesel hydrotreater is still being repaired: management put the total repair bill at about $250 million on the Q3 call and expects insurance to cover a substantial part. A fire, a hurricane through the Gulf Coast corridor, or an extended turnaround at a large plant removes hundreds of thousands of barrels a day of throughput at precisely the moment when each barrel is worth $30+ of operating profit.

Why trailing P/E is the wrong tool here — stated plainly. A refiner's trailing P/E is at its lowest when the crack spread is at its highest, because the denominator is a peak number that will not repeat; and at its highest — often absurdly high, or negative — at the bottom, when earnings have collapsed but the assets have not. The multiple therefore looks most attractive at the moment of greatest danger and most expensive at the moment of greatest opportunity. Valero's own record demonstrates it: FY2022 EPS $29.04, FY2024 EPS $8.58, FY2025 EPS $7.57, TTM $44.46, consensus FY2026 $59.64 (stockanalysis.com). Five numbers, one company, a 7.9× spread from lowest to highest. 9.4× is not a valuation conclusion — it is a snapshot of where in the cycle the trailing window happens to sit. The defensible ways to value this business are replacement cost per barrel of complexity-adjusted capacity, mid-cycle normalised earnings power, and free cash flow through a full cycle. This report deliberately does not tell you the stock is cheap at 9.4× trailing or 6.8× forward, because neither statement would mean anything.

Notable One-Off Items. Few, and small, this quarter. (1) Port Arthur: $4 million of repair costs in Q3 ($19 million so far in 2026) from the March 2026 fire, excluded from adjusted earnings; management puts the total repair bill at about $250 million and expects insurance to cover a substantial part. (2) Benicia: about $133 million of extra depreciation in the first nine months of 2026 as the refinery was written down to salvage value, now complete, and a $44 million LIFO benefit in the first nine months (an accounting gain when older, cheaper inventory is used up) as West Coast inventories fell. (3) The St. Charles FCC optimisation project was completed in Q3, on schedule and within budget. The gap between Q3 GAAP EPS ($23.89) and adjusted EPS ($23.90) was one cent. Valero's quarter was as clean as it looked.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has risen a long way in a short time relative to its own history (“overbought”); below 30 it has fallen a long way (“oversold”). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — an overbought stock can keep rising for months.

The five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 month-end close of $77.33 to the October 23, 2026 close of $419.28, Valero returned +442% on price alone, about 40% a year compounded before dividends, against +65% for the S&P 500 (SPY) and +82% for the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ) over the same dates. But the path is the point, and it is a textbook refining cycle rather than a growth curve. The stock rose 68% from the October 2021 close to May 2022 as diesel margins blew out after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, fell 17% by May 2023, recovered to $170.69 in March 2024, then slid 32% to $116.09 in April 2025, its lowest month-end close since May 2023. Nearly all of the five-year gain has come since then.

The current leg is the violent one. From that April 2025 low the stock is up 261% in eighteen months, with 2026 doing most of the work: $162.79 at end-December 2025 → $419.28 on October 23, 2026, +158% in under ten months. July, August and September 2026 added 20.1%, 14.7% and 8.0%. The intraday high of $461.52 came on October 14; the close is 9.2% below it, 6.9% above its 50-day average ($392.41) and 49.5% above its 200-day average ($280.40). The 6-period monthly RSI has been above 70, the overbought line, at every month-end since January 2026, while the 14-day daily RSI has cooled to 51, mid-range. Momentum this persistent rarely reverses on schedule, but the price is a long way above any long-run average.

Relative performance. Valero's one-year price return of +142.2% (October 23, 2025 close to October 23, 2026 close) leads a refining group that has moved as one: Marathon Petroleum +133.3%, PBF Energy +123.4%, HF Sinclair +121.8%, Phillips 66 +108.5% and Delek +98.1% over the same dates (Yahoo Finance closes, price only), against +13.1% for the S&P 500 (SPY) and +15.4% for the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ). That uniformity is the most important single fact on this page. When a large low-cost operator and much smaller refiners all move within the same band, the market is not paying for company-specific execution: it is repricing the product. Valero's lead over Phillips 66 (+142.2% against +108.5%) is the extra swing you would expect from the refiner with no pipeline or chemicals business to cushion it. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Refining capacity is being permanently removed while demand holds up: Valero itself shut Benicia, about 1.4 million bpd of Russian capacity is still offline, and Gary Simmons, Valero's chief operating officer, said on the Q3 call: "For the most part, I would say we are not seeing any signs of demand destruction at all." Into that, Valero runs a low-cost system at about $5 per barrel of cash operating cost, at ≈99% utilisation, with 4% net debt to capitalisation and $14.3B of free cash flow over the past year against about $2B a year of capital investment. Every $1.00 of margin per barrel is worth roughly $1.1B of operating income a year. Renewable diesel has turned a $28M quarterly loss into $353M of income, ethanol set a production record with a $0.17–0.19/gallon 45Z credit in front of it, and Valero paid out 52% of its adjusted operating cash flow in Q3, roughly $3.8 billion in three months.

Bear Case

Everything above is a description of a peak. The stock is up 442% over the chart window and 158% since the end of 2025, and trades within 1% of the $423.53 average price target: after a round of post-print raises the sell side sees almost no upside, and the consensus rating has slipped to Hold. The margin driving it, $39.24/bbl, tripled year on year and rests partly on Russian outages that could reverse without warning; renewable diesel income already halved from Q2 to Q3. There is no pipeline business to catch the fall: all three segments are margin businesses that move together, as the Q3 2025 comparison (a renewable diesel loss and $13.14/bbl refining) shows. FY2024 EPS was $8.58 and FY2025 was $7.57. The business does not need to be badly run for the earnings to fall by two-thirds or more — it only needs the crack spread to normalise.

Top 3 Risks

01
Crack spread mean reversion. The entire earnings step-up is margin, not volume — throughput was 2,972 kbpd against 3,087 a year earlier, while margin went from $13.14 to $39.24. If the roughly 1.4 million bpd of Russian capacity returns, or global demand softens, the margin retraces and 93% of segment operating income retraces with it. The ~$5/bbl cost base protects Valero from losing money; it does not protect the multiple from a two-thirds cut in earnings.
02
Buying peak earnings at a low trailing multiple. This is the specific trap of refining equities, and the price action is currently set up for it: 9.4× trailing looks undemanding, 6.8× forward looks cheap, and both are computed off the highest earnings in the five years this page covers (FY2022's $29.04 was the previous high). The stock trades at the average analyst target and has risen 158% since the end of 2025. The historical pattern — 2022's peak, then two years of decline — is exactly this shape.
03
Policy reversal in the non-refining legs. Renewable diesel and ethanol together contributed 6.6% of Q3 segment operating income (18.8% in Q2), and both depend on regulatory constructs — RVO volumes, RIN and LCFS credit prices, and the 45Z credit, whose Treasury guidance may not publish until 2027. Valero said in the spring it was booking $0.10/gallon on 10 plants, and in July put the credit at about $0.17 a gallon for 2026; if that expectation is wrong, roughly $300M of annual pre-tax income does not appear, and the diversification argument for owning a pure refiner gets weaker at exactly the wrong moment.
  Industry Commentary
Capacity Is Being Destroyed Faster Than Demand
EMERGING TAILWIND
The medium-term refining margin outlook rests on a simple asymmetry: closing a refinery is a one-way decision, and building one in the developed world is effectively impossible. Valero has just supplied a live example — Benicia ceased refining in April 2026, and US West Coast throughput fell to 119 thousand bpd in Q3, against 259 thousand a year earlier, with 100–110 thousand guided for Q4. California specifically has been losing capacity for years while its fuel demand falls more slowly, which is why Phillips 66, Kinder Morgan and HF Sinclair announced a final go-ahead for the $5 billion Western Gateway Pipeline joint venture in August 2026 to move fuel from the Gulf Coast and St. Louis to Arizona and California. Every closure hands surviving capacity — disproportionately Gulf Coast capacity, which is where 63% of Valero's throughput sits — more pricing power. Lane Riggs's framing on the Q1 call was that global demand would outpace new capacity from the end of 2026, implying several years of tightness, and on the Q3 call management pointed to low product inventories and limited spare refining capacity. On the Q1 call Riggs said Valero uses a conservative mid-cycle price to justify new capital; on the Q3 call the CFO said Valero now has a higher mid-cycle view. Investors who have bid the stock up 158% since the end of 2025 are betting that higher view is right.
The Russian Outage Is Doing More Work Than the Structural Story
ONGOING HEADWIND
Management put a number on it: about 1.4 million barrels per day of Russian refining capacity offline on the Q3 call (a three-month average against the same period of 2024), down from the 1.7–1.9 million bpd it cited after drone attacks on the Q2 call. For scale, that is close to half of Valero's entire 3.0 million bpd system missing from world product supply, on top of low global product inventories. This page reads it as a large part of why a $13.14/bbl margin became $39.24/bbl in twelve months, and the narrowing in a single quarter shows how fast it can move. The uncomfortable part is that this is a war outcome, not an industry structure — it can reverse on a timeline no analyst can model, and unlike a demolished US refinery, damaged Russian units can be repaired. Any investor treating the current crack spread as the new normal is implicitly making a geopolitical forecast. Distinguishing the permanent capacity loss (Benicia, and the broader Atlantic-basin closure wave) from the temporary one (Russia) is the single most important analytical task in this sector right now, and no public disclosure separates them for you.
Capital Returns: The Payout Framework Is the Real Product
STRUCTURAL FEATURE
Valero returns a share of its adjusted operating cash flow to shareholders: 59% in Q2 2026 ($2.6 billion) and 52% of $7.25B in Q3, roughly $3.8 billion, and on the Q3 call it said it continues to pay out more than half while also building cash. The dividend is $1.20 a quarter ($4.80 annualised, a 1.14% yield and only 10.8% of TTM EPS), so most of each quarter's return is buyback. The share count shows it: diluted weighted shares were 285 million in Q3 2026 against 309 million a year earlier, 8% fewer. Bull framing: a percentage-of-cash-flow framework is self-regulating — it automatically buys the most stock when cash flow is highest, and a low-cost operator with a 4% net-debt-to-cap balance sheet still generates cash at the bottom, so the buyback keeps running when levered peers are suspending theirs. Bear framing: that same mechanism buys the most stock at the highest prices. Valero is retiring shares at roughly twice last year's prices with the crack spread at a cyclical extreme; the disciplined version of this framework repurchases into weakness, and by construction this one cannot. A dividend-plus-buyback yield built on peak cash flow is not a yield you can underwrite through a cycle.
