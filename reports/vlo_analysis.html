01 Company Overview

Valero Energy is the largest independent petroleum refiner in the world: 14 high-complexity refineries across the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom with roughly 3.0 million barrels per day of throughput capacity, 12 Midwest ethanol plants at 1.7 billion gallons a year, and a half-share of Diamond Green Diesel, the largest renewable diesel producer in North America. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $115.94B, down 6.5% year on year — the third consecutive annual decline as refining margins normalised from the 2022 spike. Then the cycle turned. Trailing-twelve-month revenue through June 2026 is $132.43B (+12.7%), and trailing EPS has gone from $7.57 in FY2025 to $23.93 TTM. The stock is up 138.6% over 52 weeks and ranks roughly #223 globally by market capitalisation.

First, the one sentence that governs this entire report. The crack spread is the difference between what a refiner pays for a barrel of crude oil and what it receives for the gasoline, diesel and jet fuel it "cracks" that barrel into — it is the refiner's gross margin, and it is set by product markets, not by the price of oil itself.

That distinction is why Valero is the purest large-cap refining instrument in the US market. Marathon Petroleum (MPC, covered on this site) owns the general partner and the bulk of MPLX, a midstream business that throws off fee-based cash regardless of the crack spread; Phillips 66 (PSX, also covered here) has midstream, a 50% share of CPChem, and a marketing arm. Valero has refining, an ethanol business that is itself margin-cyclical, and a renewable diesel joint venture. There is no toll-road cushion. The central debate of this report is therefore not whether Valero is a good business — the cost structure says it plainly is — but whether an investor buying at $370.72, up 4.8× from the October 2021 trough, is buying a structurally tighter refining market or the top of an ordinary crack-spread cycle dressed up as a structural one. The two look identical in the rear-view mirror and different only in the multiple you are willing to pay.

Throughput and utilisation. Valero ran 2,950 thousand barrels per day in Q2 2026 against roughly 3.0 million bpd of nameplate capacity — call it ≈98% utilisation (computed from stated throughput and stated capacity; Valero does not print a utilisation percentage in the release). Regional throughput guidance for Q3 2026, given on the August call, sets the current shape of the system: US Gulf Coast 1.78–1.83 million bpd, Mid-Continent 460–480 thousand bpd, North Atlantic 450–470 thousand bpd, and US West Coast 110–120 thousand bpd. That West Coast number is the story of the year: it is what remains after Benicia stopped refining, and it is roughly a third of what the region used to contribute.

Margin per barrel. Q2 2026 refining margin was $23.62 per barrel, against $12.35 a year earlier — a 91% jump that produced a 252% jump in segment operating income. That gearing is the whole point of owning a pure refiner: the margin line roughly doubled and the profit line more than tripled, because the cost per barrel underneath it barely moved.

Cost per barrel — the long-standing edge. Refining cash operating expense was $4.70 per barrel in Q2 2026, guided to $4.75 for Q3. Depreciation and amortisation added $2.36 per barrel, so the fully-loaded cost of turning a barrel of crude into product was about $7.06. Against a $23.62 margin, that leaves roughly $16.56 per barrel of operating profit — and at 2.95 million barrels a day, every $1.00 of margin per barrel is worth about $2.7 billion of annualised operating income. Hold that number; it is the single most useful piece of arithmetic in this report. A refiner running at $4.70/bbl stays cash-positive at crack spreads that force a $7–8/bbl operator to idle units, which is why Valero's assets are usually the last to come down in a downcycle and the first to be full in an upcycle.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

$4.70/bbl Cash Opex High-Complexity Coking Heavy-Sour Crude Flexibility Gulf Coast Export Position Four-Region Diversification 11% Net Debt/Cap Irreplaceable Permits

The core advantage is arithmetic, not strategy. Refined products are a commodity — nobody pays a premium for Valero's diesel. In a commodity, the only durable edge is being lower on the cost curve than the marginal producer, and Valero's $4.70 per barrel of refining cash operating expense is the number that does the work. It comes from three things that compound: scale (2.95 million bpd spread across 14 plants, so fixed costs per barrel are thin), complexity (coking and hydrocracking capacity lets the system run cheap heavy and sour crude — Q2's Gulf Coast slate included 514 thousand bpd of heavy sour and 252 thousand bpd of medium/light sour — and sell the same finished products as a refiner buying expensive light sweet), and geography (Gulf Coast plants sit on tidewater with export access, so when US demand softens the barrels leave the country rather than depressing the domestic crack).

Layer on the balance sheet — 11% net debt to capitalisation, $7.9B of cash against $9.1B of debt — and the moat becomes a survival mechanism. A refiner with high costs and high leverage is forced to sell assets at the bottom of a cycle. Valero buys back stock at the bottom. That is the entire difference between the 4.8× move since October 2021 and the sector's long history of value destruction. And the deepest moat of all is one nobody built on purpose: the United States has not permitted a new greenfield refinery of consequence in decades. The replacement cost of Valero's asset base is effectively infinite, because you cannot replace it.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
No non-cyclical cushion — by design. This is the flip side of the entire investment thesis. Marathon Petroleum's MPLX midstream business and Phillips 66's midstream-plus-chemicals stack both generate fee-based cash that is largely indifferent to the crack spread. Valero has none of that. Its two "diversifying" segments — renewable diesel and ethanol — are themselves margin businesses that swung from a combined $25M loss-plus-scraps in Q2 2025 to $1,035M in Q2 2026, which means they amplify the cycle rather than damp it. When the crack spread compresses, all three segments compress together.
02
Policy is a margin input, not a background condition. Renewable diesel and ethanol earnings depend directly on the RVO, RIN prices, LCFS credits and the 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit. The 45Z guidance Valero is waiting on has already slipped from an expected 2026 publication into a possible 2027 one, and the company is currently booking $0.10/gallon on 10 plants while expecting $0.17–0.20 across all of them. A hostile or merely delayed regulatory outcome removes a few hundred million dollars of annual EBIT with no operational warning.
03
Structural demand decline in gasoline. Electrification erodes the gasoline pool over a decade or two, and the West Coast — the region Valero has just shrunk — is the leading indicator. Diesel and jet are far more defensible, and Valero's complexity skews it toward distillate, but a refiner is ultimately long a product slate that policy is designed to shrink. The counter-argument is that capacity is shrinking faster than demand; that is a race, and it is not obviously won.
04
Single-asset operational risk at scale. Running at ≈98% utilisation means there is no spare capacity to absorb an unplanned outage. Q2 2026 already carried an estimated $250 million of Port Arthur repair cost. A fire, a hurricane through the Gulf Coast corridor, or an extended turnaround at a large plant removes hundreds of thousands of barrels a day of throughput at precisely the moment when each barrel is worth $16+ of operating profit.

Why trailing P/E is the wrong tool here — stated plainly. A refiner's trailing P/E is at its lowest when the crack spread is at its highest, because the denominator is a peak number that will not repeat; and at its highest — often absurdly high, or negative — at the bottom, when earnings have collapsed but the assets have not. The multiple therefore looks most attractive at the moment of greatest danger and most expensive at the moment of greatest opportunity. Valero's own record demonstrates it: FY2022 EPS $29.04, FY2024 EPS $8.58, FY2025 EPS $7.57, TTM $23.93, consensus FY2026 $43.83. Five numbers, one company, a 5.8× spread. 15.49× is not a valuation conclusion — it is a snapshot of where in the cycle the trailing window happens to sit. The defensible ways to value this business are replacement cost per barrel of complexity-adjusted capacity, mid-cycle normalised earnings power, and free cash flow through a full cycle. This report deliberately does not tell you the stock is cheap at 15.5× or expensive at 8.7× forward, because neither statement would mean anything.

Notable One-Off Items. Three items distort the recent numbers and should be stripped out before extrapolating. (1) Benicia cessation depreciation: $100 million of incremental depreciation in Q1 2026 and a further $33 million in Q2, an estimated $0.09 per share of combined earnings impact — a non-cash charge that will not recur. (2) Port Arthur repairs: an estimated $250 million, disclosed alongside Q2. (3) The St. Charles FCC optimisation project at $230 million, on track for Q3 2026 completion — capital, not expense, but it consumed turnaround capacity. Beyond those, the gap between Q2 GAAP net income ($3.7B, $12.62/share) and adjusted net income ($3.7B, $12.54/share) is unusually narrow at roughly $0.08 per share, which is worth noting in a market where the adjusted-versus-GAAP gap is often the whole story. Valero's quarter was as clean as it looked.

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has risen a long way in a short time relative to its own history (“overbought”); below 30 it has fallen a long way (“oversold”). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — an overbought stock can keep rising for months.

The five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $77.33 to the September 4, 2026 close of $370.72, Valero returned +379% on price alone — roughly 36% a year compounded, before dividends. But the path is the point, and it is a textbook refining cycle rather than a growth curve. The stock rose 68% in the first eight months of 2022 as diesel cracks blew out post-invasion, gave most of it back into 2023, chopped sideways through 2024, and then bottomed at $116.09 in April 2025 — a 32% drawdown from the March 2024 high and the worst monthly close in the window. Every dollar of the 4.8× move happened in two bursts separated by three years of nothing.

The current leg is the violent one. From that April 2025 low, the stock has tripled in seventeen months, with the last nine of those doing most of the work: $162.79 at end-December 2025 → $370.72 on September 4, 2026, +128% in eight months. July and August 2026 alone added 20.1% and 14.7%. The 6-period monthly RSI has been pinned in overbought territory since the spring, which does not mean the move is over — momentum this persistent rarely reverses on schedule — but it does mean the price is a long way above any reasonable mean and there is no technical support anywhere close.

Relative performance. Valero's 52-week return of +138.6% sits in the middle of a refining sector that has moved as one: Marathon Petroleum's market cap is up 100.7% over the same window, Phillips 66's up 93.0%, HF Sinclair's up 94.2%, Delek's up 129.2%, and PBF Energy's up 175.5%. That uniformity is the most important single fact on this page. Valero's out-performance of MPC and PSX (+138.6% versus +100.7% and +93.0%) is precisely the extra torque you would expect from the operator with no midstream cushion, which is the thesis working exactly as advertised. It will work identically in reverse. Against the S&P 500 and the NASDAQ-100, no directly comparable index return over this identical five-year window was sourced within this research budget, and none has been estimated — but a +379% five-year price return would place Valero well ahead of the broad market over the period, driven entirely by a 5.8× swing in earnings rather than by any change in the multiple.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Refining capacity is being permanently removed while demand for distillate is not falling — Valero itself shut Benicia, roughly 1.7–1.9 million bpd of Russian capacity is offline, and Lane Riggs has said global demand will outpace new capacity with "several years of tightness" ahead. Into that, Valero runs the lowest-cost large system in the industry at $4.70 per barrel, at ≈98% utilisation, with 11% net debt to capitalisation and $10.12B of free cash flow against just $2B of capex. Every $1.00 of margin per barrel is worth roughly $2.7B of annualised operating income. Renewable diesel has flipped from a $79M quarterly loss to $717M of income, ethanol has a $0.17–0.19/gallon 45Z annuity in front of it, and the capital-allocation framework returns a minimum 50% of operating cash flow — 59% last quarter, $2.6B in a single three-month period.

Bear Case

Everything above is a description of a peak. The stock is up 379% in five years and 128% in the last eight months, sits 1.2% off its all-time high, and trades above a consensus price target of $319.63 — the sell side, having just raised targets across the board, still sees 13.8% downside. The margin driving it, $23.62/bbl, nearly doubled year on year and is underwritten in large part by Russian outages that could reverse without warning. There is no midstream toll road to catch the fall: all three segments are margin businesses that move together, as the Q2 2025 comparison (a renewable diesel loss and a $54M ethanol quarter and $12.35/bbl refining) demonstrates. FY2024 EPS was $8.58 and FY2025 was $7.57. The business does not need to be badly run for the earnings to fall by two-thirds — it only needs the crack spread to normalise.

Top 3 Risks

01
Crack spread mean reversion. The entire earnings step-up is margin, not volume — throughput was 2,950 kbpd and margin went from $12.35 to $23.62. If 1.7–1.9 million bpd of Russian capacity returns, or global demand softens, the margin retraces and 81% of operating income retraces with it. The $4.70/bbl cost base protects Valero from losing money; it does not protect the multiple from a two-thirds cut in earnings.
02
Buying peak earnings at a low trailing multiple. This is the specific trap of refining equities, and the price action is currently set up for it: 15.5× trailing looks undemanding, 8.7× forward looks cheap, and both are computed off the best earnings in the company's history. The stock is above the average analyst target, has risen 128% in eight months, and has no technical support within 30%. The historical pattern — 2022's peak, then two years of decline — is exactly this shape.
03
Policy reversal in the non-refining legs. Renewable diesel and ethanol together contributed 18.8% of Q2 operating income, and both depend on regulatory constructs — RVO volumes, RIN and LCFS credit prices, and the 45Z credit whose Treasury guidance has already slipped once and may not publish until 2027. Valero is booking $0.10/gallon on 10 plants while expecting $0.17–0.20 across all of them; if that expectation is wrong, roughly $290M of annual pre-tax income does not appear, and the diversification argument for owning a pure refiner gets weaker at exactly the wrong moment.
  Industry Commentary
Capacity Is Being Destroyed Faster Than Demand
EMERGING TAILWIND
The medium-term refining margin outlook rests on a simple asymmetry: closing a refinery is a one-way decision, and building one in the developed world is effectively impossible. Valero has just supplied a live example — Benicia ceased refining in April 2026, taking US West Coast throughput guidance down to 110–120 thousand bpd for Q3 from a regional position that used to be multiples of that. California specifically has been losing capacity for years while its fuel demand falls more slowly, which is why Phillips 66, Kinder Morgan and HF Sinclair announced a $5 billion Western Gateway Pipeline joint venture in August 2026 to move product from Texas into California markets. Every closure hands surviving capacity — disproportionately Gulf Coast capacity, which is where 62% of Valero's throughput sits — more pricing power. Lane Riggs's framing on the Q1 call was that global demand outpaces new capacity "starting at the end of this year," implying "several years of tightness." Note carefully that management still underwrites new capital at "conservative mid-cycle" pricing rather than at today's margins. They are not capitalising the spike; investors bidding the stock up 128% in eight months arguably are.
The Russian Outage Is Doing More Work Than the Structural Story
ONGOING HEADWIND
Management put a number on it: approximately 1.7–1.9 million barrels per day of Russian refining capacity offline from drone attacks, with damage that may require extended recovery. For scale, that is more than half of Valero's entire 3.0 million bpd system removed from world product supply, sitting on top of low global product inventories. It is the reason a $12.35/bbl margin became a $23.62/bbl margin in twelve months. The uncomfortable part is that this is a war outcome, not an industry structure — it can reverse on a timeline no analyst can model, and unlike a demolished US refinery, Russian units are being repaired rather than permanently retired. Any investor treating the current crack spread as the new normal is implicitly making a geopolitical forecast. Distinguishing the permanent capacity loss (Benicia, and the broader Atlantic-basin closure wave) from the temporary one (Russia) is the single most important analytical task in this sector right now, and no public disclosure separates them for you.
Capital Returns: The Payout Framework Is the Real Product
STRUCTURAL FEATURE
Valero commits to returning a minimum of 50% of adjusted operating cash flow to shareholders and delivered 59% in Q2 2026 — $2.6 billion against $4.49B of adjusted operating cash flow. The dividend is $1.20 a quarter ($4.80 annualised, a 1.30% yield and only a 20.1% payout of EPS), which means the overwhelming majority of that $2.6B was buyback. The arithmetic is visible in the headline numbers: over 52 weeks the share price rose 138.6% while market cap rose 121.1%, and the gap is retired shares. Bull framing: a percentage-of-cash-flow framework is self-regulating — it automatically buys the most stock when cash flow is highest, and a low-cost operator with an 11% net-debt-to-cap balance sheet still generates cash at the bottom, so the buyback keeps running when levered peers are suspending theirs. Bear framing: that same mechanism buys the most stock at the highest prices. Valero is currently retiring shares at $370 with the crack spread at a cyclical extreme; the disciplined version of this framework repurchases into weakness, and by construction this one cannot. A dividend-plus-buyback yield built on peak cash flow is not a yield you can underwrite through a cycle.
