01 Company Overview

Vulcan sells rocks. Specifically, it produces and supplies construction aggregates in the United States — crushed stone, sand and gravel — plus the asphalt and ready-mixed concrete that consume them. In the twelve months to June 30, 2026 it shipped 229.6 million tons of aggregates on $8.12B of revenue, earning $1.12B of net income and $2.35B of adjusted EBITDA. FY2025 revenue was $7.94B, up 7.06%, with diluted EPS of $8.12, up 18.4%.

The whole business rests on one physical fact: stone is heavy and cheap, so it cannot travel far before the truck costs more than the rock. A quarry is a local monopoly with a radius, and new quarries take years of permitting that neighbours fight. That is how Vulcan keeps raising price while volume stalls — freight-adjusted price reached $22.97 a ton in Q2 2026, up 4% reported and 5% mix-adjusted — while shipments barely moved (+1%). The 2026 story is a tug-of-war between that pricing power and everything else: diesel inflation pushed cash cost per ton up 7% in Q2, rain soaked Texas and the Southeast, single-family housing "continues to struggle," and on September 9, 2026 Wells Fargo cut the stock to Underweight on slowing construction and infrastructure-funding doubts. The shares have fallen from a $310.00 February month-end to $240.83 — down 22% in seven months — while the company reiterated its full-year earnings guide. The market is asking whether price can keep outrunning cost when volume stops helping.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Local quarry monopolies (freight radius) Permitting scarcity of new quarries Decades of reserves in place Pricing power through volume cycles Rail & yard logistics network Public-infrastructure demand base

The moat is geology plus geography. Because stone sells for about $23 a ton freight-adjusted and trucking eats that fast, a customer's real choice is between the two or three quarries within reach, and a new one must win zoning, environmental permits and a fight with neighbours. That is why aggregates pricing rose 4% in FY2025 on the reported line (6% mix-adjusted) while volume grew 3%, and rose again in Q2 2026 while volume grew 1%. The same logic shows up in the returns: Vulcan reports ROIC of 16.1% on its own definition for the trailing twelve months. The vulnerability is that the moat protects price, not volume or cost. When housing stalls, infrastructure money slows and diesel spikes at the same time, the price lever has to do all the work.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Price has to outrun cost, every year. In Q2 2026 price rose $0.86/ton ($22.11 to $22.97) while cash cost rose $0.72 ($10.23 to $10.95). The spread still widened, but by 14 cents. If diesel stays high, as the CFO warned it might, and price growth lands at the low end of the 4–6% guide, unit margin growth stalls.
02
Volume has no cushion. Shipments grew 1% in Q2 and are guided +1–3% for the year. Residential is weak ("continues to struggle due to the ongoing lack of affordability," per Pruitt), and Wells Fargo's September 9 downgrade cited "slowing construction trends, uncertainty around federal infrastructure funding, constrained state budgets and limited benefit from data-center related demand."
03
Weather is a real variable. Heavy May–June rainfall in Texas and the Southeast held back Q2 shipments, and unusual Southern California rainfall was cited in the Q4 2025 miss. Quarterly numbers in this business are partly a weather report.
04
The multiple leaves little room. At 28.4x trailing earnings and roughly 15x EV/EBITDA, the stock is priced as a compounder. When a compounder stops compounding, the multiple usually falls faster than the earnings do.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things distort the comparisons. First, portfolio changes: the Q4 2025 sale of the Houston asphalt/construction business and the June 2026 sale of California ready-mixed concrete reduce reported Asphalt and Concrete revenue in 2026, while small bolt-ons — a southern Colorado quarry and a Dallas–Fort Worth rail yard bought from Brannan Sand & Gravel — add aggregates. Reported revenue growth understates the aggregates business and overstates the decline downstream. Second, diesel: per the Q2 2026 call, Vulcan absorbed a $26M diesel headwind in the quarter, which is why adjusted EBITDA was flat at $654.0M (vs $659.5M) despite higher prices. Third, adjusted versus GAAP: Q2 2026 GAAP diluted EPS from continuing operations was $2.47 against adjusted $2.59. Separately, a NAFTA tribunal found Mexico's actions against Vulcan's operations there "arbitrary, grossly unfair, and unjust" but awarded only immaterial damages — no windfall is coming from it. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are buying a US aggregates franchise that calls its unit profitability "industry-leading" 22% below its February month-end, with the stock sitting on its 52-week low and a monthly RSI at the bottom of its five-year range. The business has not broken: aggregates price is still rising 4–5%, cash gross profit per ton rose above $12 through a diesel spike and a wet quarter, leverage is 1.9x net debt/EBITDA, and management reiterated a $2.4–2.6B EBITDA guide while cutting SG&A. Highway awards in its markets are up double digits, and the downstream businesses it is selling earned 4–15% margins against 32% in stone. Consensus FY2027 EPS of $10.64 would put the stock at 22.6x — both estimates, not results. Even Wells Fargo's bear target is above today's price.

Bear Case

The bear case says the price lever is being asked to do all the work and is starting to tire. Cash cost per ton rose 7% in Q2 against a 4% reported price gain; H1 cash gross profit per ton is up about 2%, short of what February's price-and-cost guide implied; shipments are growing 1%; housing is stuck on affordability; and the most visible downgrade on the Street is about 2027 infrastructure and state budgets, not this quarter. At 28.4x trailing earnings and ~15x EV/EBITDA the stock still carries a compounder's multiple. If the H2 ramp needed to hit even the bottom of the guide (+3.6% EBITDA growth) slips, the multiple and the estimates fall together.

Top 3 Risks

01
Unit-margin stall. Diesel-driven cost inflation (+7% per ton in Q2, +3% ex-diesel) is eating most of the price gain. The CFO called the inflation "likely to remain sticky." If Q3 cash gross profit per ton does not accelerate, the 2026 guide gets harder to defend.
02
Demand air pocket in 2027. Wells Fargo's downgrade is explicitly about infrastructure funding uncertainty and constrained state budgets. Public work is the steadiest leg of aggregates demand; residential is already weak. Volume guidance of +1–3% leaves no room for a public-side slowdown.
03
Valuation de-rating. A 28.4x trailing P/E assumes the pricing story continues for years. The stock has already fallen 22% from February without any cut to guidance; a guide cut on October 29 would hit a multiple that has not yet adjusted to a slower-growth world.
Industry Commentary
Energy inflation vs. aggregates pricing power
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Quarrying is diesel-heavy — haul trucks, loaders and crushers — so fuel is the cost line that moves fastest. In Q2 2026 Vulcan absorbed a $26M diesel headwind, per the earnings call, and adjusted EBITDA came in flat at $654.0M against $659.5M a year earlier. The industry's answer is always price, and Vulcan pulled its mid-year increase forward to June, with sequential pricing nearly double the prior year's level per management. The contest is simple arithmetic: +$0.86 per ton of price against +$0.72 of cost in Q2. When fuel eases the spread widens quickly; while it stays high, the spread barely moves.

Construction demand: public strong, private split
ONGOING HEADWIND

On the Q2 call management said highway awards in Vulcan's markets remain up double digits and public infrastructure awards rose 20% YoY, with data centers, LNG and power generation as private-side bright spots, while single-family housing "continues to struggle due to the ongoing lack of affordability." Wells Fargo's September 9 sector call took the other side: slowing construction trends, uncertainty around federal infrastructure funding, constrained state budgets and limited benefit from data-center demand, with caution on 2027. The sector's share prices have sided with the bank for now — CRH's market cap is down 22.5% and Knife River's 26.1% year-over-year on StockAnalysis's September 21 figures.

Portfolio purification — the aggregates pure-play
EMERGING TAILWIND

Vulcan is steadily becoming more of a quarry company: out of Houston asphalt in Q4 2025, out of California ready-mix and U.S. Virgin Islands assets in 2026, into a Colorado quarry and a DFW rail yard. The bull framing: every divested dollar of concrete revenue earned a ~4.5% gross margin; every dollar of stone earns ~32%, so group margins and returns rise as reported revenue shrinks, and a purer aggregates franchise deserves the premium multiple. The bear framing: purification does not change the cycle. A pure-play is more exposed, not less, to what happens to tons shipped when public budgets tighten — and the market is currently pricing that exposure. The way to judge it is cash gross profit per ton and shipments over the next four quarters, both reported every quarter.
