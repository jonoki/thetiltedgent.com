01 Company Overview

Every time an American property-and-casualty insurer writes a policy, there is a decent chance a Verisk form, rule or loss-cost table sits somewhere underneath it. The company began in 1971 as the Insurance Services Office (ISO), created by consolidating state, regional and national insurance rating bureaus into one shared pool of industry data. The insurers who owned it sold down in a $1.9B IPO in October 2009 (Verisk raised nothing for itself), and for the next decade it grew by acquisition: AIR Worldwide in catastrophe modelling, Xactware in claims estimating, Wood Mackenzie in energy research. Then it reversed course. Health services went in 2016, financial services to TransUnion and Wood Mackenzie to Veritas Capital in the 2022–23 clean-up, leaving a pure insurance data company. FY2025 revenue was $3.07B, up 6.63%, with diluted EPS of $6.48.

The business model is very good. Gross margin is 70.2%, operating margin 44.9%, and in Q2 2026 83% of revenue was subscription, growing 8.0% on an organic constant-currency basis. The stock has not been. It closed at $314.14 at the end of May 2025 and at $172.02 on September 21, 2026, down 45%. Three things caused it, one after another: an October 2025 guidance cut after a hurricane season with no US landfalls starved the claims business of volume; a $2.35B deal for roofing software maker AccuLynx that Verisk walked away from in December 2025 and that a Delaware court put back on the table in August 2026; and a market-wide worry that AI turns data vendors into commodities. Adjusted earnings kept rising through all of it. The debate is whether Verisk's data is the thing AI needs, or the thing AI replaces.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Industry-contributed data pool since 1971 Standard policy forms & rules 2,000+ regulatory filings a year Xactware estimating standard 83% subscription revenue 70% gross margin

The moat is contributed data. Insurers send Verisk their premium and loss data, Verisk pools it, and insurers buy it back as rating inputs no one of them could build alone. The more insurers contribute, the better the pool, and the harder it is for anyone else to match. That is the answer to the AI question, and Shavel gave it in February 2026: a model is only as good as its data, and this data is proprietary, regulated and built into insurers' filed rates. What the moat doesn't cover is the edges: transactional revenue that depends on the weather, and acquisitions made outside the core.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A deal it tried to walk away from. Verisk agreed to buy AccuLynx for $2.35B on July 29, 2025, got an FTC second request on October 22, 2025, terminated the agreement on December 26, 2025 and repaid $1.5B of acquisition notes on January 6, 2026. On August 7, 2026 Vice Chancellor Bonnie David of the Delaware Court of Chancery ordered specific performance, which means Verisk must use commercially reasonable efforts to get FTC clearance. The shares fell 5.55% on the next trading day. Verisk said it strongly disagrees and, per law firm Axinn, appealed on August 18, 2026. The FTC review is still pending.
02
The weather is a line item. The 2025 guidance cut was blamed on a quiet hurricane season, which shows how much of the transactional business depends on catastrophe activity. Nobody can forecast that part of the P&L.
03
Negative equity by design. StockAnalysis puts book value at −$1.19B after $1.9B of first-half buybacks, against $4.62B of debt and $552M of cash (debt/EBITDA 3.0x). That is a deliberate choice, and the cash flow can carry it. But it leaves less room if Verisk is forced to fund a $2.35B acquisition it no longer wants.
04
Index removal took away a buyer. Verisk was removed from the NASDAQ-100 effective June 22, 2026, in the same rebalance that removed Cognizant, Charter, Insmed and Zscaler. Funds that track that index had to sell whatever the valuation, and that bid does not come back unless Verisk qualifies again.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 close of $200.27 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $172.02, VRSK lost 14.1% of its price. Add back dividends of roughly $7–8 per share over the period (an estimate from StockAnalysis's annual dividend-per-share series) and the total return is about −10%. Then it gave all of that back in sixteen months, and today it sits within a few dollars of the 2022–23 lows.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows how steady the selling was. It stayed below 30 for nine straight month-ends, from September 2025 to May 2026, and bottomed at 11.5 in May 2026, the lowest reading in the five-year series. The July bounce lifted it to 37.7. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 22x this year's consensus earnings for a business with 70% gross margins, 57.5% adjusted EBITDA margins, 83% subscription revenue growing 8% OCC, 31.7% ROIC and $1.24B of free cash flow. Every published price target in the StockAnalysis panel of 19 is above the price, the lowest at $195. The fall from $314 came from a quiet hurricane season, a deal Verisk tried to leave, and a sector-wide AI scare. None of those touched the subscription franchise. Management bought $1.9B of stock at an average of $186.32, above today's price. If AI turns out to need exactly the kind of proprietary, regulator-embedded data Verisk owns, this is a quality compounder at its cheapest multiple in years.

Bear Case

A Delaware court has ordered Verisk to try to complete a $2.35B acquisition it had already walked away from, with a balance sheet showing negative equity and 3.0x debt/EBITDA. Net income is down 3.3% TTM, and per-share growth is being bought with debt-funded repurchases. Transactional revenue depends on the weather. The index bid is gone. The chart has fallen in almost a straight line for sixteen months and is back at the 2022 lows despite beats. Markets don't usually do that to a franchise they believe in: they are pricing a terminal-value question, whether AI erodes the premium for curated data, that no quarterly beat can answer.

Top 3 Risks

01
AccuLynx. A court-ordered path to a $2.35B acquisition, pending FTC review and Verisk's appeal. If it closes, Verisk re-levers a negative-equity balance sheet for an asset its own management chose to exit.
02
AI eroding the data premium. The bull case assumes contributed, regulated data stays scarce. If AI tools let insurers or rivals build good-enough rating and claims analytics from other sources, the pricing power behind "strong price realization" weakens. There is no sign of it in the numbers yet (subscription growth 8.0%), but the stock is priced as if there might be.
03
Transactional volatility and concentration. About 17% of revenue depends on events like catastrophe activity, and almost all of it comes from one customer industry, property-and-casualty insurance. A second quiet storm season or an insurer budget squeeze would show up quickly.
Industry Commentary
AI and the information-services multiple
CRITICAL QUESTION

In 2026 the market applied one blunt question to every data vendor: if a model can read, summarise and reason over information, what is the information worth? Verisk's February 2026 answer was that high-quality, aggregated data is the input AI needs, and that few providers can pool data from so many insurers at once. The numbers back it for now: Q2 subscription growth of 8.0% OCC is the fastest part of the business. But the stock's derating ran through two beat-and-reaffirm quarters, which suggests the market is discounting what happens to pricing power a few years out rather than anything in the current P&L.

Weather as a revenue driver
ONGOING SENSITIVITY

Verisk's claims tools are used when property is damaged, so fewer landfalling storms mean fewer estimates and less transactional revenue. The 2025 Atlantic season, the first in a decade with no US hurricane landfall through the end of September, turned directly into the October 2025 guidance cut. Investors have to treat the transactional line as a weather option inside a subscription business.

Capital returns into a falling price
SUPPORT

Verisk has turned its cash flow into shrinking the share count: $1.9B in H1 2026, 3.58% fewer shares than a year ago, and a dividend raised from $1.80 (FY2025) to a $2.00 annual rate. The bull framing: management is buying at prices it considers cheap, with $1.24B of free cash flow a year behind it. The bear framing: buybacks averaged $186.32 and the stock is at $172.02. Debt-funded buybacks on a negative-equity balance sheet only look smart if the multiple recovers, and the AccuLynx ruling could redirect $2.35B away from repurchases.
