01 Company Overview

Every time an American property-and-casualty insurer writes a policy, there is a decent chance a Verisk form, rule or loss-cost table sits somewhere underneath it. The company began in 1971 as the Insurance Services Office (ISO), created by consolidating state, regional and national insurance rating bureaus into one shared pool of industry data. It had already bought AIR Worldwide in catastrophe modelling (2002) and Xactware in claims estimating (2006) when the insurers who owned it sold down in a $1.9B IPO in October 2009 (Verisk raised nothing for itself). In 2015 it added Wood Mackenzie in energy research. Then it reversed course. Health services went in 2016, financial services to TransUnion and Wood Mackenzie to Veritas Capital in the 2022–23 clean-up, leaving a pure insurance data company. FY2025 revenue was $3.07B, up 6.63%, with diluted EPS of $6.48.

The business model is very good. Gross margin is 70.2%, operating margin 45.0%, and subscriptions, which were 84% of revenue in Q3 2026, grew 8.2% in the quarter on an organic constant-currency basis, which strips out acquisitions, disposals and exchange rates. The stock has had a harder time. It closed at $314.14 at the end of May 2025 and at $167.85 at the end of September 2026, down 46.6%, before recovering to $204.59 on November 6, 2026. Three things weighed on it, one after another: an October 2025 guidance cut after a hurricane season with no US landfalls through the end of September starved the claims business of volume; a $2.35B deal for roofing software maker AccuLynx that Verisk walked away from in December 2025 and that Delaware's courts put back on the table in August and again in October 2026; and a market-wide worry that AI turns data vendors into commodities. Adjusted earnings kept rising through all of it. The debate is whether Verisk's data is the thing AI needs or the thing AI replaces, and whether AccuLynx gets through the FTC.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Industry-contributed data pool since 1971 Standard policy forms & rules About 2,000 regulatory filings a year Xactware estimating standard 84% subscription revenue (Q3 2026) 70% gross margin

The moat is contributed data. Insurers send Verisk their premium and loss data, Verisk pools it, and insurers buy it back as rating inputs no one of them could build alone. The more insurers contribute, the better the pool, and the harder it is for anyone else to match. That is the answer to the AI question, and Shavel gave it in February 2026: a model is only as good as its data, and this data is proprietary, regulated and built into insurers' filed rates. What the moat doesn't cover is the edges: transactional revenue that depends on the weather, and acquisitions made outside the core.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
A deal it tried to walk away from, and lost in court. Verisk agreed to buy AccuLynx for $2.35B on July 29, 2025, got an FTC second request (a demand for more information that lengthens the antitrust review) in October 2025, terminated the agreement on December 26, 2025 and repaid $1.5B of acquisition notes on January 6, 2026. On August 7, 2026 Vice Chancellor Bonnie David of the Delaware Court of Chancery ordered specific performance, which means Verisk must keep working to get FTC clearance. The shares fell 5.55% on the next trading day. Verisk appealed on August 18, and on October 23, 2026 the Delaware Supreme Court affirmed the ruling. Verisk says it will proceed in accordance with the order and that "there is no guarantee that the transaction will be completed." The FTC review and customary closing conditions still stand between it and a close.
02
The weather is a line item. The 2025 guidance cut was blamed on a quiet hurricane season, which shows how much of the transactional business depends on catastrophe activity. On the Q3 2026 call the CFO said "the overall environment that we see still does have below-average weather events on our claims side." Nobody can forecast that part of the income statement.
03
Negative equity by design. Verisk had a stockholders' deficit of $1.11B at September 30, 2026 after $1.9B of buybacks in the first nine months, against $4.50B of borrowings and $557M of cash. Management puts debt at 2.5 times adjusted EBITDA (a yearly measure of operating earnings before interest, tax and non-cash charges); StockAnalysis, counting leases, shows 2.96 times. That is a deliberate choice, and the cash flow can carry it. But it leaves less room if Verisk has to fund a $2.35B acquisition it no longer wants.
04
Index removal took away a buyer. Verisk was removed from the NASDAQ-100 effective June 22, 2026, in the same rebalance that removed Cognizant, Charter, Insmed and Zscaler. Funds that track that index had to sell whatever the price, and they will not buy again unless Verisk qualifies again.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 26.6x this year's consensus adjusted earnings for a business with 70% gross margins, 56.7% adjusted EBITDA margins, subscription revenue growing 8.2% OCC, 31.9% ROIC and $1.10B of free cash flow. The average target in the StockAnalysis panel of 19 is $235.06, about 15% above the price. The fall from $314 came from a quiet hurricane season, a deal Verisk tried to leave, and a sector-wide AI scare. None of those touched the subscription business. Verisk bought back $1.9B of stock in the first nine months of 2026 at an average of $185.46, below the current price. If AI turns out to need exactly the kind of proprietary, regulator-embedded data Verisk owns, this is a high-quality business at about a third below where it traded eighteen months ago.

Bear Case

Delaware's courts have twice told Verisk to keep pursuing a $2.35B acquisition it had already walked away from, with a balance sheet showing negative equity. Net income is down 4.6% TTM, Q3 free cash flow fell 40.5%, and per-share growth is being bought with debt-funded repurchases. Transactional revenue depends on the weather. NASDAQ-100 index funds no longer have to own the stock. The price fell 46.6% in sixteen months despite earnings beats, and the rebound since September has come before any FTC decision on AccuLynx. Investors may be asking whether AI erodes the premium for curated data years from now, a question no quarterly beat can answer.

Top 3 Risks

01
AccuLynx. A court-ordered path to a $2.35B acquisition, upheld on appeal and now dependent on the FTC review. If it closes, Verisk adds debt to a negative-equity balance sheet for an asset its own management chose to exit.
02
AI eroding the data premium. The bull case assumes contributed, regulated data stays scarce. If AI tools let insurers or rivals build good-enough rating and claims analytics from other sources, the pricing power behind "strong price realization" weakens. There is no sign of it in the numbers yet (subscription growth 8.2% in Q3 2026).
03
Transactional volatility and concentration. About 16% of Q3 2026 revenue depended on events like catastrophe activity, and almost all of it comes from one customer industry, property-and-casualty insurance. A second quiet storm season or an insurer budget squeeze would show up quickly; management says guidance is likely to land near the midpoint partly because of lighter weather and longer sales cycles.
Industry Commentary
AI and what data companies are worth
CRITICAL QUESTION

In 2026 the market applied one blunt question to every data vendor: if a model can read, summarise and reason over information, what is the information worth? Verisk's February 2026 answer was that high-quality, aggregated data is the input AI needs, and that few providers can pool data from so many insurers at once. The Q3 2026 numbers back it for now: subscription growth of 8.2% OCC, the fastest part of the business. On the November 5 call CEO Lee Shavel said "AI is top of mind, both from an opportunity and a risk perspective" and that "there is an increasing demand from clients to validate a true return on their AI investments." He also reported a commercial win in its forms platform: "We have added 9 new customers this year, including our largest ever agreement for Mozart with a Tier-1 carrier undertaking an enterprise-wide review of its forms process." The share price fell through two quarters of earnings beats with guidance held, before the October rebound, which suggests the market is weighing pricing power a few years out rather than anything in the current income statement.

Weather as a revenue driver
ONGOING SENSITIVITY

Verisk's claims tools are used when property is damaged, so fewer landfalling storms mean fewer estimates and less transactional revenue. The 2025 Atlantic season, the first in a decade with no US hurricane landfall through the end of September, turned directly into the October 2025 guidance cut. On the Q3 2026 call the CFO described softer property markets with lower transactional volumes that could persist into the fourth quarter. Investors have to treat the transactional line as a piece of the business that depends on the weather, inside a subscription business.

Capital returns
SUPPORT

Verisk has turned its cash flow into shrinking the share count: $1.9B in the first nine months of 2026, 5.05% fewer shares than a year ago, and a dividend raised from $1.80 (FY2025) to a $2.00 annual rate. The bull framing: buybacks averaged $185.46 and the stock is at $204.59, so the purchases are in profit, with $1.10B of free cash flow a year behind them and $700M of authorisation left. The bear framing: debt-funded buybacks on a negative-equity balance sheet only look smart if the valuation holds, and the AccuLynx ruling could redirect $2.35B away from repurchases. On the November 5 call Shavel said "there is no meaningful change in our capital priorities."
