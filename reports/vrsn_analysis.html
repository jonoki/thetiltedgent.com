01 Company Overview

VeriSign is the sole registry operator for the .com and .net top-level domains, and it operates two of the internet's thirteen root name servers. It does not sell domains to the public; it sells the wholesale right to enter a name into the authoritative .com zone file, and every registrar on earth — GoDaddy, Namecheap, Squarespace, Cloudflare, Tucows — buys that right from VeriSign at the same regulated price. FY2025 revenue was $1,657M, up 6.37% from $1,557M, with operating income of $1,121M — a 67.7% operating margin that would be conspicuous in a software company and is close to unheard-of in infrastructure. The company ranks roughly #939 globally by market capitalisation at $26.4B (companiesmarketcap.com, September 2026).

The central debate on this page is not whether the business is good. It plainly is: 928 employees, an 88% gross margin, a customer base that renews at 76%, and a contractual right to raise price. The debate is what you should pay for a regulated monopoly whose volume growth is structurally low-single-digit and whose price increases are granted by a government contract that has to be renewed. At 31× trailing earnings for a business guiding to ~6% revenue growth, the multiple is doing a lot of work, and three things have to hold for it: the domain name base has to keep growing, the renewal rate has to keep holding, and the Cooperative Agreement has to keep permitting 7% annual .com price increases. Sections 03 and 04 take each of those apart.

One structural point to fix before anything else: VeriSign has negative shareholders' equity of −$2.25B. This is not distress. It is the arithmetic result of buying back more stock, at prices above book value, than the company has ever retained in earnings — share count has fallen 3.30% in the last twelve months alone. It does, however, make ROE and debt-to-equity mathematically meaningless, and both are marked n/m in Section 04 rather than quoted as a nonsense number.

This is the asset. VeriSign is the exclusive registry operator for .com under a contract structure with two parts: an ICANN .com Registry Agreement and a Cooperative Agreement with the U.S. Department of Commerce / NTIA. Between them, those documents set the maximum wholesale price and grant a presumptive right of renewal. The economics are volume × price, and both moved in VeriSign's favour in 2026. Volume: the company processed 12.7 million new .com and .net registrations in Q2 2026, against 10.4 million in Q2 2025 — a 21% increase and, per trade press, a record quarter, which after deletions produced a net addition of 3.05 million names. Price: the wholesale .com fee rises from $10.26 to $10.97 on November 1, 2026, a 7% increase. Under the contract terms reported by Domain Name Wire, VeriSign may raise .com prices by up to 7% in each of the last four years of each six-year contract cycle; the current cycle began October 26, 2024, which puts the permitted windows at November 2026, 2027, 2028 and 2029 and — if taken in full every year — a wholesale price of $13.42 by the end of the cycle. That is a contractually visible ~31% price runway on the largest revenue line, requiring no competitive win of any kind.

The longest-running loose end in the company's history finally moved. .web was delegated on July 22, 2026, after roughly a decade of contested auction proceedings, and management confirmed it will offer registrations "through our registrar channel later this year." VeriSign operates two of the internet's thirteen root name servers and maintains the authoritative root zone file under agreement. This generates little or no direct revenue but is the reason the contracts get renewed: the company's argument in every regulatory proceeding is that it has run .com resolution without an outage for more than two decades, and that switching registry operators for 179 million names is a systemic risk nobody wants to take. The moat is a legal document, not a technology. Any competent infrastructure team could resolve .com queries; nobody else is allowed to. The ICANN .com Registry Agreement and the NTIA Cooperative Agreement together give VeriSign exclusivity, a presumptive right of renewal, and — critically — a price schedule rather than a price ceiling set by competition. The wholesale .com fee goes to $10.97 on November 1, 2026, and may rise up to 7% again in each of November 2027, 2028 and 2029 under the reported terms of the current six-year cycle. Almost no other business in the S&P 500 can tell you in advance, with contractual support, what its unit price will be four years from now.

The second-order moat is the one that actually shows up in the numbers: the customer does not feel the price. A 71-cent wholesale increase reaches a registrant who is already paying $12–$20 retail for something they consider load-bearing infrastructure for a business, and the Q1 2026 renewal rate of 76.3% — up from 75.5% a year earlier, and per trade press the highest in twenty years — is the empirical proof that the pricing power is real rather than theoretical. When a monopolist raises price and retention goes up, the demand curve is telling you something.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The contract is the company — and Washington owns one end of it. The Cooperative Agreement is with the U.S. Department of Commerce. Its terms have been amended repeatedly across administrations, and the pricing latitude VeriSign enjoys today (7% in each of the last four years of a six-year cycle) was itself the product of a 2018 amendment after a period in which .com prices were frozen. There is no contractual guarantee that the next cycle looks like this one. A single adverse renegotiation would not damage the business — it would remove the entire growth algorithm, because volume alone is running at only ~5%.
02
Volume growth is structurally low and partly speculative. The base grew 5.1% YoY to 179.1M and new registrations hit a record 12.7M in Q2 2026 — but a meaningful share of new gTLD-era registration spikes have historically come from low-priced promotional channels and domain investors rather than durable end users, and those names show up as deletions a year later. The renewal rate is the tell to watch: 76.3% means roughly one name in four does not come back, so the company must sell tens of millions of new registrations annually just to stand still. A cyclical downturn in small-business formation hits this line directly.
03
Negative equity plus a 0.59 current ratio leaves no balance-sheet slack. Shareholders' equity is −$2.25B and the current ratio is 0.59 against $2.34B of total debt and $1.03B of cash. For a business with $1.07B of annual free cash flow and near-perfect revenue visibility this is a rational structure, not a solvency question. But it does mean the company has chosen to run with no cushion: a refinancing window that closes, or a regulatory shock that impairs the contract, arrives at a balance sheet with nothing to absorb it. The low current ratio is partly a deferred-revenue artefact — cash collected up front for registrations not yet earned — and should not be read like a manufacturer's.
04
Substitution at the edges, and the AI-navigation question. .com is not threatened by another registry; it is slowly threatened by the internet needing fewer domain names. Businesses that live inside Instagram, Shopify, Amazon or a mobile app need less of a domain than a business in 2005 did, and if AI assistants become the dominant navigation layer, the memorable-address premium that underwrites .com's pricing power erodes at the margin. This is a decade-scale risk, not a quarter-scale one, and it is invisible in any current metric — which is precisely why a 31× multiple on a 6%-growth business is a live question rather than an obvious bargain.

RSI measures how much of a stock's recent movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen steeply in a short span and is described as "overbought"; below 30 means the opposite. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — an overbought reading says a move has been fast, not that the price is wrong.

Five years of VRSN is a study in what a low-beta compounder actually looks like — which is to say, not very much like a chart. From the October 2021 close of $222.67 to the September 10, 2026 close of $288.85, the price return is +29.7%, roughly 5.4% annualised before the dividend that began in 2025. Over the same window the S&P 500 compounded at a materially faster rate on the back of the mega-cap technology complex, and VRSN was a significant underperformer for the first three of those five years. It was not a bad business during that stretch; it was a bond proxy in a rising-rate environment, and a 0.70-beta name with a 30× multiple is exactly the thing that de-rates when the discount rate moves. The relevant comparison for a Nasdaq-listed name would normally be the NASDAQ-100 — but VRSN is not a NASDAQ-100 constituent (verified against the current 102-ticker component list), so the S&P 500, of which it is a member, is the correct benchmark here and the index used throughout Section 04.

The shape breaks cleanly into three regimes. December 2021 to June 2022 was the rate shock: $253.82 to $167.33, a 34% drawdown with no deterioration whatsoever in the underlying business — revenue grew 7.33% in FY2022. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

A legally protected monopoly on the internet's most valuable namespace, run by 928 people at a 67.9% operating margin and converting revenue to free cash flow at 129% of net income. The growth algorithm is contractual rather than competitive: the domain base grew 5.1% to 179.1M with a record 12.7M new registrations in Q2 2026, the renewal rate hit a twenty-year high of 76.3%, and the wholesale .com price rises 7% on November 1 with three more permitted 7% windows through 2029 — a visible path to roughly $13.42 versus $10.26 today. Layer on a share count shrinking 3.3% a year, a beta of 0.70, a new dividend, and Berkshire Hathaway holding nearly 10% of the company, and you have a business where the forecast error is genuinely small.

Bear Case

You are paying 31× trailing and 28× forward earnings for approximately 6% revenue growth — an implied PEG near 3.0 — and the entire excess-return case rests on a pricing schedule granted by a U.S. government agreement that has been renegotiated before and will be again. Volume growth is structurally low and one name in four does not renew, so tens of millions of new registrations are needed annually just to hold the base flat. The balance sheet has negative equity of $2.25B and a 0.59 current ratio, leaving no cushion. And the long-horizon question is unfalsifiable in any current metric: if AI assistants and platform-native commerce keep reducing the need for a memorable web address, the demand curve that underwrites the pricing power thins out quietly, years before it shows up in the domain base.

Top 3 Risks

01
Adverse renegotiation of the Cooperative Agreement or the ICANN .com Registry Agreement. The price ladder is the growth story. A future amendment that freezes or caps .com pricing — as has happened in prior cycles — removes roughly half of consensus EPS growth without touching a single operating metric, and would likely compress the multiple at the same time. This is a binary, non-diversifiable, politically-determined risk sitting at the centre of the thesis.
02
The renewal rate mean-reverting from a twenty-year high. 76.3% in Q1 2026 against 75.5% a year earlier is the best number in the release, and the best numbers are the ones most likely to revert. Roughly 1.5 points of renewal rate on a 179M base is over 2.5 million names of annual run-rate. If the record 12.7M new registrations in Q2 were partly promotional or speculative, the deletions show up twelve months later and the raised 5.2–6.0% base-growth guidance becomes hard to hold.
03
Multiple compression on a duration-sensitive name. VRSN fell 34% between December 2021 and June 2022 while growing revenue 7.3% — the whole move was the discount rate. With a 0.70 beta, a 1.11% yield and a 31× multiple, this equity behaves like a long bond with an inflation link. A sustained rise in long rates, or simply a market that decides 6% growth deserves 22× rather than 31×, costs roughly 30% with nothing going wrong operationally at all.
  Industry Commentary
The Contract Is the Sector — and It Is Politically Determined
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Domain registries do not compete on price, service or technology; they operate under agreements that specify all three. VeriSign's .com economics are set jointly by ICANN and the U.S. Department of Commerce, and the current pricing latitude — up to 7% in each of the last four years of a six-year cycle, with the cycle having begun October 26, 2024 — exists because of a specific 2018 amendment following a period of frozen .com prices. Registrars and consumer groups object publicly to every increase, and the November 1, 2026 move from $10.26 to $10.97 drew the same objections. The point for an investor is not who is right; it is that the single largest driver of this company's forward earnings is decided in a regulatory proceeding, not a market. No amount of operational excellence hedges that.
Namespace Fragmentation and the Slow Erosion of the Address
ONGOING HEADWIND
Two things are chipping at the .com premium simultaneously. The first is supply: the 2012 gTLD round plus repurposed country codes (.io, .ai, .co) gave every new business a credible alternative to the .net it would once have settled for, and a further ICANN application round is in motion. The second is demand-side: a growing share of commercial activity lives inside platforms — Instagram handles, Shopify subdomains, app-store listings, marketplace storefronts — that need no domain at all. Neither shows up in a 179.1M base growing 5.1%, and both are why the renewal rate is the most important disclosure in the release. A registry monopoly is only as valuable as the category it monopolises.
Price Is Doing the Work That Volume Used To
EMERGING TAILWIND
Consensus has FY2027 revenue growing 9.23% versus FY2026's 5.87% — a step-up that comes almost entirely from the ratable recognition of the November 2026 price increase, with a second increase permitted in November 2027 on top. Because registry revenue accrues over each registration term, a price move is unusually predictable: you can see it in the contract eighteen months before it reaches the income statement. Bull framing: if VeriSign exercises all four permitted 7% increases, the wholesale fee compounds from $10.26 to about $13.42 by 2029 — roughly 31% of price-only revenue growth on top of whatever the base does, at a ~90% incremental margin, funding a buyback that is already shrinking the share count 3.3% a year. Bear framing: that is the good case, it is fully visible to every analyst, and it is already in a 31× multiple. Meanwhile it makes the equity progressively more dependent on the contract and less on the business — which is a worse risk profile at a higher price, not a better one.
