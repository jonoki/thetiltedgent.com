01 Company Overview

Viatris is what you get when you shuffle two old decks together. On November 16, 2020, generic-drug maker Mylan combined with Upjohn — Pfizer's off-patent medicines division — through a Reverse Morris Trust. The new company inherited Mylan's S&P 500 seat, its NASDAQ listing and a very large balance sheet. It sells brands (mostly established, off-patent medicines that keep their name and loyalty) and generics in markets around the world; the company says it supplies roughly 1 billion patients a year. FY2025 total revenue was $14.30B, down 3% reported, with brands at $9.18B and generics at $5.07B.

The hand Viatris has played since 2020 is a shrink-to-grow hand. It sold its biosimilars business to Biocon Biologics in a deal announced in February 2022 (closed November 2022), then in 2024 sold most of its OTC business, its women's healthcare business and its India API operations. The divestitures show in the numbers: revenue fell from $17.89B in FY2021 to $14.30B in FY2025 (−20%), and the stock drifted between roughly $8 and $15 for four years. Then two things went wrong at once. In December 2024 the FDA issued a Warning Letter and Import Alert for the oral-dose plant in Indore, India — 11 actively distributed products barred from the US, four exempted for shortage reasons — which the company later put at roughly $370M of lost 2025 revenue. And in Q1 2025 it booked a $2.9B non-cash goodwill impairment, which is why FY2025 GAAP EPS was −$3.00.

2026 is the year the story turned, though the third quarter shows how uneven the turn is. Reported revenue grew 8% in Q1 and 5% in Q2, then came in flat at $3.75B in Q3 as supply shortages hit Europe and Emerging Markets. Greater China slowed to +14% from +21%. Profit held up better: adjusted EPS of $0.71 beat the $0.64 Nasdaq consensus, the midpoints of 2026 guidance rose again, and the stock has run from an $8.42 month-end low in April 2025 to $19.91, up 136%. The central debate: has Viatris finished shrinking and become a steady, cash-rich business with a real pipeline, or is it a generics-heavy business whose Indian plants keep dealing it bad cards? A second plant, Nashik, had a fire in February 2026 and received FDA Form 483 observations in May. In September the FDA classified that inspection Official Action Indicated, and in October Viatris stopped production there. It expects to restart in the first quarter of 2027 and puts the revenue hit from supply constraints at about $100M in Q3 and another $100M in Q4.

The other big change is that Viatris is buying again. On October 8, 2026 it agreed to pay $36.50 a share in cash, about $1.65B, for Pacira BioSciences, which sells two patent-protected pain medicines, EXPAREL and ZILRETTA. Pacira had about $746M of revenue and $177M of adjusted EBITDA (a company-defined profit measure before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization) in the twelve months to June 30, 2026. Viatris plans to pay mostly from cash on hand, with the rest from short-term borrowings. The tender offer, in which Pacira holders are invited to sell their shares directly to Viatris, is set to expire on November 16, with closing expected by the end of November. On the Q3 call, Interim CFO Paul Campbell said: "We expect the impact of the transaction to be immediately accretive to all financial guidance metrics in 2027 versus our expected 2026 results" (accretive: it adds to those measures).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Global manufacturing & supply network Established brand names (Upjohn legacy) Greater China brand franchise Complex-generics approvals Scale: $14.3B revenue ~$2B/yr free cash flow

Generics are the poker room's cash game: everyone can sit down, and the house (the pharmacy buyer, or the government agency that sets the price) takes a rake on every pot. Viatris' edge is not any single drug; it is breadth and reach — a very broad portfolio, registered in many countries, made in its own plants and shipped through its own channels. That breadth is what produced $1.94B of free cash flow in FY2025 even in a year of falling revenue and a $2.9B impairment. The weakness is the same breadth: when one plant fails an inspection, many products fail with it. Adjusted gross margin of 58.0% in Q3 2026 versus a GAAP gross margin of 38.4% also tells you how much of the reported cost base sits in items the company excludes from its adjusted measures. Pacira would add something this breadth does not give: two medicines still under patent in the US.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Manufacturing quality in India. Indore: June 2024 inspection, December 2024 Warning Letter and Import Alert, ~$370M of 2025 revenue lost. Nashik: February 2026 fire, May 2026 Form 483, September 2026 Official Action Indicated, October 2026 voluntary halt to manufacturing, with a restart expected in Q1 2027 and about $200M of revenue lost across H2 2026 to supply constraints, mainly from Nashik. Two sites, two years. The FDA judges each site on its own, but a pattern shapes how investors price the next inspection.
02
Generics price erosion. FY2025 generics net sales fell 8% to $5,066.4M, and Q3 2026 generics fell 3%. The company cites competition in North America and government price regulation in Japan. New launches (about $450M now expected for 2026) are the counterweight; they need to keep arriving just to hold the line.
03
Leverage and GAAP losses. $13.31B of reported debt, $11.52B net of cash, and debt/equity of 0.97. Twelve-month GAAP net loss of $607.8M, including a $325.3M loss in Q3 2026 on a $453.2M tax provision. The adjusted numbers look healthy; the GAAP numbers keep carrying write-downs from the 2020 deal and later decisions. Viatris plans to pay for Pacira ($1.65B) mainly from the $1.79B of cash, with the rest borrowed short term.
04
Concentration in Greater China. The one segment still growing is also the one most exposed to a single government's pricing policy. Q3 2026 shows the dependence: Greater China added about $87M of net sales from a year earlier, and without it company net sales would have fallen about $100M rather than $13M.

Notable One-Off Items

Four items distort GAAP earnings. First, the $2.9B non-cash goodwill impairment in Q1 2025, which the company tied to higher discount rates on geopolitical, pricing, FDA and tariff uncertainty; it drives FY2025's −$3.00 EPS. Second, a $177.8M non-cash charge in Q2 2026 on the sale of Tyrvaya's global rights to Harrow ($30M upfront plus up to $70M in milestones); the sale closed after an October 1, 2026 amendment that lets Viatris keep selling it in Japan, subject to royalties on future sales. Third, a $453.2M income tax provision in Q3 2026, which the company attributes to losses in jurisdictions where it can get little tax benefit, and which turned $261.5M of earnings from operations into a $325.3M net loss. Fourth, the enterprise-wide strategic review announced February 26, 2026: $700–850M of expected pre-tax restructuring charges over about three years, a workforce reduction of up to ~10%, and $600–700M of expected savings once fully in place. Nashik's fire has added $98.6M of charges so far in 2026. On the other side, sales of the Biocon Biologics stake brought in about $780M in total. Capital return: about $717M through September 30, 2026, including about $300M of buybacks (8.9M shares for $151.0M in Q3), with about $700M of authorization remaining.

⚠ STATIC DATA AS OF NOV 6, 2026
RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

Over five years, from the November 2021 month-end close of $12.31 to the November 6, 2026 settled close of $19.91, VTRS gained 61.7% on price. Add the $2.28 of dividends over the same window (19 quarterly payments of $0.12 by ex-date, not reinvested) and the total return is about +80%. The whole window is post-merger (Viatris began trading in November 2020) and there has been no stock split since 2003, so the per-share series is clean. But the endpoints hide the shape: more than three years of going nowhere between about $8.50 and $15, a month-end low of $8.42 in April 2025, and then a 136% climb in about eighteen months.

The 6-period monthly RSI shows the swing. It hit 24.3 in April 2025, the most oversold reading of the window, and 84.6 in February 2026, the most overbought. It now reads 81.1, well above the overbought line of 70 and the second-highest reading of the five years. The 10-month moving average is about $16.63, below the price: the trend is up. Momentum is stretched, not broken — the month-end close rose from $15.88 in June to $19.04 in October, with one dip in August.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

You are paying about 7.7x this year's guided adjusted EPS ($2.60 midpoint) for a company that has stopped shrinking. Revenue grew in the first half of 2026 and held flat in Q3 despite about $100M of supply losses, guidance has been raised again, free cash flow is about $2B a year — roughly a 9% yield on the $22.83B market cap — and the 2.41% dividend takes only about 26% of free cash flow. The strategic review targets $600–700M of savings, management has set 2030 targets of 6–7% base-case adjusted EPS growth, and an Indore reinspection that goes well would bring back US products that cost ~$370M of revenue in 2025. Pacira would add two patent-protected US medicines, the fast-acting meloxicam decision is due by December 27, Nashik is expected back in Q1 2027, and Greater China is still growing 14%. That leaves room for the share price to rise relative to earnings.

Bear Case

The low price relative to earnings rests on adjusted figures; GAAP earnings are negative (TTM EPS −$0.53) and keep getting dragged down — $2.9B of goodwill in 2025, $177.8M on Tyrvaya in 2026, then a $453.2M tax provision in Q3. Net debt is $11.52B, about a third of enterprise value (market value plus net debt), and $1.65B is about to go out for Pacira. Revenue stopped growing in Q3, the one growing segment is slowing, and generics sales fell again. New-product revenue is now expected at the bottom of its earlier range. And the manufacturing record is the real problem: Indore in 2024, Nashik in 2026, now with production halted. The stock has more than doubled from its low and RSI sits at 81; the good news is in the price, and average analyst targets are only 3.8% above it.

Top 3 Risks

01
Another FDA action at an Indian plant. Indore cost ~$370M of 2025 revenue; Nashik is classified Official Action Indicated and has stopped production; supply constraints, mainly from Nashik, are costing about $100M a quarter. If remediation falls short or the Q1 2027 restart slips, a Warning Letter could follow the same path as Indore.
02
Greater China pricing. The segment grew 14% in Q3 2026, the only one to grow. A price-regulation round aimed at off-patent brands would take away the growth that supports the raised guidance.
03
Execution and capital allocation. A $700–850M restructuring, an interim CFO, a $1.65B acquisition and a plan to put about half of more than $11B of deployable cash into business development through 2030. Past deals and divestitures have produced large write-downs; the next ones need to earn more than the ~9% free-cash-flow yield (free cash flow as a share of market value) that buybacks offer today.
Industry Commentary
Indian manufacturing and the FDA
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

A generics company is only as good as its worst plant. The FDA inspected Indore in June 2024, issued a Warning Letter and Import Alert in December 2024 and blocked 11 actively distributed products from the US, exempting four for shortage reasons. At the time the company said it did not expect an impact on 2024 guidance; the 2025 impact came to about $370M of revenue, plus knock-on effects in the ARV business and some European generics. In February 2026 a fire hit a service area at Nashik and suspended production; a May 2026 FDA inspection there ended with Form 483 observations and, in September, an Official Action Indicated classification. In October the company voluntarily halted manufacturing to focus on remediation, with a restart expected in Q1 2027. Supply constraints cost about $100M of revenue in Q3 and are expected to cost about the same in Q4. Both plants are supply problems for the income statement and quality problems for the FDA — and investors price them the same way.

Generic price erosion and government pricing
ONGOING HEADWIND

Viatris' own releases name the two forces: competition on certain North American generics and government price regulation in Japan. FY2025 generics net sales fell 8% reported, and Q3 2026 generics fell 3%. The answer is a steady flow of launches — $324M of new-product revenue in 2025, about $450M now expected for 2026 — including complex products such as the generic Sandostatin LAR Depot (approved December 2025) and ferric carboxymaltose injection (approved 2026). New-product revenue has to outrun erosion every year; it is a treadmill, not a finish line.

From shrink-to-grow to capital deployment
EMERGING TAILWIND

Since 2022 Viatris has sold biosimilars, OTC, women's healthcare, India API, its Biocon Biologics stake (~$780M) and now Tyrvaya, while holding gross leverage at 2.9x. The March 2026 Investor Event turned the story forward: more than $11B of cash available for deployment through 2030, about half for business development, alongside the dividend and buybacks. Pacira, at about $1.65B, is now the test case. The bull framing: a company that no longer has to sell assets to cut debt can grow EPS by mid-to-high single digits while paying ~2.4%. The bear framing: business development at this scale is exactly how the goodwill that was written down in 2025 got onto the balance sheet. The test is simple and public — each deal's price against the ~9% FCF yield that buying back its own stock already earns.
