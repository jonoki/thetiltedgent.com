01 Company Overview

Vylor is a new name on an old business. It is Corteva's former seed segment — the Pioneer, Brevant, Dairyland Seed and PhytoGen brands, the germplasm (the bred genetic lines) behind them and the biotech traits built into them — handed to Corteva's shareholders as a separate company. At 12:03 a.m. New York time on October 1, 2026, Corteva distributed every Vylor share to holders of record on September 24, one Vylor share for each Corteva share. Regular-way trading on the NYSE under "VYLR" began the same morning, and S&P Dow Jones Indices added Vylor to the S&P 500 that day in Corteva's place; Corteva, now the crop-protection company, moved down to the S&P MidCap 400 on October 6. There was no "when-issued" trading beforehand, so October 1 was the first price the market ever set for this business on its own.

Who got which half is the first thing to understand. In Corteva's last full year, 2025, the seed segment sold $9.90B of the company's $17.40B and earned $2.64B of segment operating EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, before one-off items), against $1.35B for crop protection. The market agrees with that split: at the first close, a share of Corteva bought for $77.65 on September 30 had become one VYLR at $68.26 and one CTVA at $12.57 — Vylor was about 84% of the combined value. By November 20 the pair was worth $75.98 ($68.09 plus $7.89), down 2.2% from the last pre-spin close, against −9.5% for the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) over the same window. Since October 8 the pair has lost $10.56 (from $86.54), and $5.86 of that is Corteva's own share price ($13.75 to $7.89), which dropped from $12.14 to $9.19 on November 12 alone.

The business is seasonal and North American. Farmers buy seed for spring planting, so the first half carries the year: in the first half of 2026 seed net sales were $7.56B (+4%) and segment operating EBITDA $3.00B (+11%), while the second half of 2025 lost $69M at the same EBITDA line (our arithmetic from Corteva's segment tables). North America was 63% of 2025 seed sales. Management's plan is to grow modestly in sales and faster in profit: net sales of about $11.2–$11.9B and operating EBITDA of about $3.3–$3.7B by 2029, plus a licensing business (Vylor One) it expects to bring in more than $500M of gross licensing income in 2027. The separation was not uncontested: California and other states, pointing to Corteva's alleged liabilities for PFAS (long-lasting industrial chemicals often called "forever chemicals"), tried to block it in court. Corteva called the claims unfounded, and California's motion was denied on the eve of the distribution (section 06). A second suit, by 15 states and one sovereign, was filed on distribution day and is still pending on appeal (section 03).

The first report as Vylor (Q3 2026, released after the close on November 11) came in as the seasonality predicted: pro forma net sales of $938M (−1%), an adjusted pro forma operating EBITDA loss of $269M, and an adjusted pro forma operating EPS of −$0.42. Management reaffirmed its 2026 target of about $2.8B of adjusted pro forma operating EBITDA (about 10% growth at the midpoint) and $2.25–$2.35 of operating EPS. Nine-month EBITDA was $2.66B, so the target implies roughly $140M in the fourth quarter (our arithmetic; the release gives the year as “approximately” $2.8B). Three weeks earlier, on October 22, Vylor had announced a first quarterly dividend of $0.13 a share and a new $3B multi-year buyback, about 6.6% of the $45.4B market value. Both sit under the court fight: Vylor told the court it would not declare dividends or repurchase shares while the stay motion was pending, and on October 20 the Seventh Circuit stayed the restraining order that would have barred them.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Elite germplasm Proprietary traits Pioneer brand Multiple routes to market Licensing income ~$1B a year of R&D

A seed company's moat is time. The company describes its germplasm as the industry's most elite and its focus on seed innovation as more than a century old, and its own pipeline shows how long new traits take: corn platforms from 2028, soybean platforms through 2035. Vylor spent $979M on R&D in pro forma 2025, about 9.7% of sales. BofA's initiation argues that Vylor's innovation, brands and distribution create high barriers to entry, and two other initiating brokers (Oppenheimer and Mizuho) make the same structural point: it is the only scaled, listed company that does nothing but seed and genetics. Mizuho adds a demand argument: it expects seed purchases to stay resilient because growers put money into seed ahead of other farm inputs.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
The separation is under legal attack. California, one of several states pointing to Corteva's alleged PFAS liabilities, went to federal court to delay the spin-off. The district court refused to let it file for an injunction; on September 30 the Fourth Circuit reversed that refusal, and on remand the district court denied the motion the same day. Corteva's board then waived the condition that no legal restraint stand in the way, and the distribution went ahead. The challenge itself was not resolved on its merits. A second suit, by 15 states and one sovereign, was filed on October 1; on October 9 the district court issued a temporary order barring Corteva, EIDP and, separately, Vylor from paying dividends, repurchasing stock or disposing of assets outside the ordinary course. It stayed that order the next day on Vylor's representation that it would not declare dividends or repurchase shares while the stay motion was pending (Vylor 8-K, October 12), and on October 20 a split Seventh Circuit panel stayed the order while the appeal proceeds (Bloomberg Law, October 22). Vylor says it strongly contests the claims. If the order were reinstated, it would again bar the dividend and buyback Vylor announced on October 22.
02
Acreage and the farm economy. The second quarter showed how acreage moves the top line: a corn-to-soybean shift in North America and corn-to-sunflower in EMEA cut seed volume 3%. Farm incomes, crop prices and planting decisions are outside Vylor's control.
03
A balance sheet built for the spin. Vylor borrowed to fund an estimated $3.54B cash distribution to Corteva. At separation the offering memorandum's pro forma estimate was $5.58B of borrowings against $1.10B of cash, about 1.8x pro forma 2025 operating EBITDA. Management expects 0.8–1.1x debt to EBITDA by December 31, 2026.
04
Goodwill-heavy and costly to stand up. Goodwill and other intangibles were $12.4B of $23.7B pro forma assets at March 31, 2026. Pro forma 2025 earnings absorbed $650M of significant items and $305M of separation costs, and the pro forma accounts assume about $270M more of one-off separation spending.

Notable One-Off Items

The pro forma 2025 earnings are mostly one-offs. Starting from $243M of net income, the reconciliation to $2,503M of operating EBITDA adds back $259M of tax, $781M of depreciation and amortisation, $119M of net interest, $143M of exchange losses, $3M of non-operating costs, $650M of significant items and $305M of separation costs. Q1 2026 was cleaner: $471M of net income, $29M of significant items and $51M of separation costs on the way to $979M of operating EBITDA. Vylor's first standalone reports will carry more separation spending and the interest on the debt raised at the spin, so GAAP earnings will trail operating EBITDA for a while yet.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

The only scaled, listed pure play in seed and genetics, carrying the business that earned most of Corteva's profit: $2.93B of trailing segment operating EBITDA at a 28.7% margin, with margins up more than 230bps in Q2 2026 on flat sales. Profit is set to grow faster than sales as licensing income rises and royalty payments fall — management targets $3.3–$3.7B of operating EBITDA by 2029, and Vylor One alone is expected to bring in more than $500M of gross licensing income in 2027. Twelve platform launches start in 2027–2028, debt falls to 0.8–1.1x EBITDA by year-end, and on the 2029 target midpoint the stock trades at about 14x EV/EBITDA. The first report kept the 2026 profit target, and in October the company announced a first dividend and a $3B buyback (6.6% of its market value). Of 17 analysts in the S&P Global consensus, 12 rate it Buy or Strong Buy and none Sell.

Bear Case

A 3–4% sales grower priced at 20x pro forma operating EBITDA and 4.5x sales, with GAAP earnings of $0.36 a share in 2025 under separation and restructuring charges. Its first report showed sales down 1% and volume down 9% in the third quarter. Most of the year's profit is made in two quarters and depends on North American corn acres, which were already slipping toward soybeans in Q2. Vylor carries the debt that paid Corteva an estimated $3.54B, has $12.4B of goodwill and intangibles, and starts life with two legal challenges to the spin-off unresolved on their merits, one of which produced a restraining order on dividends and buybacks that is stayed only while the appeal runs. The targets that matter — new corn platforms, hybrid wheat, $2B of licensing income — lie years out, and KeyBanc already thinks the price reflects the good news.

Top 3 Risks

01
Litigation over the separation. California's bid to delay the spin-off was denied, but the courts did not rule on its substance, and a 15-state suit filed on October 1 is on appeal after a restraining order on dividends and buybacks was stayed. If the claims revive, they land on a company that has just taken on about $5.6B of borrowings.
02
Acreage and farm income. A shift from corn to soybeans, a weak harvest price or a cautious farmer shows up directly in seed volume and mix — as it did in Q2 2026, when volume fell 3%.
03
Execution as a standalone company. New systems, new financing and a first full-year outlook as a standalone company, for 2027. Separation costs and dis-synergies will show in GAAP earnings before the pipeline launches show in sales.
Industry Commentary
Seed as a technology business
STRUCTURAL THEME

The bull argument for Vylor is that seed is closer to a technology business than to a commodity input: the value sits in genetics and traits that can be licensed as well as sold, and the company's own launch plan runs a decade out. Corteva's 2026 numbers point that way — margin gains came from price, licensing income and lower royalty costs rather than volume — and Goldman calls the company an asset-light technology platform. The counterweight is that seed still lives on farm economics, and one year of margin expansion is a direction, not a proof.

Spin-off mechanics
FADING DISTORTION

Vylor started life with a shareholder base that did not choose it (every Corteva holder received it), debt designed by its former parent, and accounts that are still pro forma. S&P 500 index funds received it as Corteva holders, and Vylor took Corteva's seat in the index, so they had no reason to sell. Those distortions fade: the first standalone quarter came on November 11, the pro forma figures give way to reported ones over the next year, and the holder base re-sorts toward investors who want a pure seed company.
