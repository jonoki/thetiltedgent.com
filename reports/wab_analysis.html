01 Company Overview

Wabtec builds and maintains the equipment that moves freight and people on rails: locomotives, braking systems, couplers, wheels, doors, HVAC, positive train control electronics and the aftermarket parts and overhaul services that keep all of it running for decades. It reports in two segments — Freight and Transit. FY2025 revenue was $11,167M, up 7.5%, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through September 2026 is $12.21B, up 13.3%. Sales for the first nine months of 2026 were $9,249M, up 12.8% from $8,202M, but the company's own bridge shows how: of the $1,047M increase, $571M came from acquisitions (Frauscher Sensor Technology, closed December 1, 2025 for about $792M, and Dellner Couplers, closed February 10, 2026 for about $1.05B), $426M was organic (growth excluding acquisitions, sold businesses and currency), $87M was currency, and $37M was subtracted for businesses sold or closed. Wabtec is the largest supplier — as opposed to operator — in stockanalysis.com's twelve-company Railroads group: smaller by market value than the five big railroad operators, and more than twenty times the size of the largest railcar builder (Trinity, $2.01B).

The central debate on this page is still the backlog, and the third quarter changed its shape. Multi-year (total) backlog was $31.44B at September 30, 2026, up 22.9% from a year earlier (up 21.4% excluding currency, per the company) — about 2.6 years of trailing revenue. That is roughly half the pace of the 41.7% reported at June 30, when it stood at $30.93B, and the 12-month backlog slipped to $8.99B (up 8.8%) from $9.14B at June. Management still raised full-year adjusted EPS guidance, to $10.85–11.05, and the shares closed up 1.95% on the day of the print and down 4.32% the next day. The question is the one this page has always asked — a lasting step-up in earning power from fleet modernisation, international locomotive orders and digital products, or a lumpy order cycle plus bought-in revenue — and Q3 evidence cuts both ways: the recurring Services line recovered (+6.7% after −4.2% in Q2) while new-locomotive sales stalled (Equipment +0.3% after +35.0%), and about half of the quarter's sales growth was acquired. Sections 02, 04 and 07 take that apart.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Installed Base 30–40 Year Asset Life Safety Certification $31.4B Backlog Aftermarket Lock-In PTC Standard Position One Main Locomotive Rival

The moat is the installed base and the calendar. A mainline locomotive runs for three to four decades, and over that life it is overhauled, re-powered, re-braked and eventually modernised — almost always by the company that built it, because the parts, the software and the safety certifications are all specific to the platform. That is why Wabtec's Freight segment can swing between product lines from one quarter to the next — Equipment +35.0% and Services −4.2% in Q2, Equipment +0.3% and Services +6.7% in Q3 — without the franchise changing: service revenue is not competitively contested, it is timing. The braking and train-control businesses add a regulatory layer — safety-critical rail equipment must be certified by authorities in each jurisdiction, which turns every incumbent position into a multi-year barrier for a challenger. And in locomotives Wabtec's 10-K names one primary competitor, Caterpillar's Progress Rail. The result is a business with a 36.2% gross margin and a 0.92 beta that grew revenue 8.1% in Q3 and 12.8% over nine months.

Key Vulnerabilities

—
Backlog growth has slowed and half of Q3's sales growth was bought. Multi-year backlog growth fell from 41.7% at June 30 to 22.9% at September 30, and the 12-month backlog slipped from $9.14B to $8.99B. Of Q3's $234M sales increase, $114M came from acquisitions (Frauscher, Dellner), leaving organic growth of $137M, or 4.7%. Frauscher passes its first anniversary in December 2026 and Dellner in February 2027, after which reported growth tends toward the organic rate unless orders pick up.
—
Customer concentration in a consolidating industry. Wabtec's Freight customers are a handful of Class I railroads (the largest North American freight lines) plus a small set of international operators and mining companies. Those customers set capital budgets against freight volumes they do not control, and North American rail is itself consolidating — fewer, larger buyers with more leverage in every negotiation. A single Class I deferring a modernisation programme is a material revenue event.
—
The GAAP-to-adjusted gap is structural, not transitional. FY2025 GAAP EPS was $6.83 against adjusted EPS of $8.97 — a $2.14 gap, 24% of the adjusted figure — and it has persisted for years because it is largely amortisation of intangibles acquired in the 2019 GE Transportation merger plus recurring restructuring. Q3 2026 shows the same shape: GAAP EPS $2.42 against adjusted $2.83, with $89M of non-cash amortisation excluded. Q4 2025 is the clearest illustration: a 12.0% GAAP operating margin against a 17.7% adjusted one. Investors paying 22.9× forward earnings are paying for the adjusted number.
—
Tariffs and input costs on a fixed-price backlog. On the Q3 call the CFO said the tariff headwind felt in the first half should be a smaller drag on a year-over-year basis from here, with margins still expanding. The structural exposure remains: a backlog booked at contracted prices two or three years ahead absorbs any input-cost inflation that arrives between signature and delivery, and $31.4B of multi-year backlog is a long window in which costs can move.

In plain English: RSI measures how much of the recent move has been up versus down. Above 70 the stock has run hard and may be stretched; below 30 it has been beaten up and may be washed out. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal.

Five-year trajectory. From the November 2021 monthly close of $88.77 to the October 26, 2026 close of $277.67, WAB returned +212.8% on price alone — a compound annual rate of about 26%, before a dividend that now yields about 0.45%. That is an extraordinary result for a capital-goods manufacturer with a 0.92 beta, and it was earned in a very particular shape: almost nothing for the first year, then a near-uninterrupted four-year climb. The stock spent 2022 going sideways-to-down, bottoming at a monthly close of $81.35 in September 2022 — an 8.4% loss over ten months while the market worried about industrial recession and freight volumes. From that low it has risen 241.3% to today. It stands 4.5% below the July 2026 monthly close of $290.86, the highest of the sixty. The price rise maps closely onto the earnings: diluted EPS went from $3.46 in FY2022 to $8.04 trailing, a 132% increase, while the price rose 3.4 times from the low — so roughly two thirds of the return is earnings growth and one third is investors paying a higher multiple of earnings (our calculation, from the September 2022 low).

Relative performance. Within its own Railroads industry group, Wabtec at $46.59B now trades at 34.5× trailing earnings against a 25.51× industry weighted average (stockanalysis.com, read October 27, 2026), a 35% premium to the group. The premium is explicit and it is being paid for growth, not for quality of returns — ROIC of 9.56% is not a premium-multiple number on its own.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the backlog, because everything else is downstream of it. At September 30, 2026 Wabtec held a 12-month backlog of $8.99B, up 8.8%, and a multi-year backlog of $31.44B, up 22.9%. Against TTM revenue of $12.21B, the 12-month figure covers about 74% of a year's sales and about 72% of the FY2026 revenue guidance midpoint of $12.45B. The multi-year figure is roughly 2.6 years of revenue. That is the disclosure that makes a cyclical industrial behave like a subscription business, and it fits a beta of 0.92 and a 2022 fall of only 15% at monthly closes.

Now read it sceptically. Three things complicate the number. First, the pace has halved: multi-year backlog growth was 41.7% at June 30 and 22.9% at September 30, and the 12-month backlog fell by $0.15B from June, so incremental orders were smaller this quarter than last. Second, acquisitions: Frauscher and Dellner added to the base, and $114M of Q3's $234M sales increase was acquired. Third, the two backlog measures still diverge — multi-year up 22.9% against 12-month up 8.8% — which means more of the incremental orders are booked for delivery after the next twelve months. Currency is not the issue it was: excluding it, multi-year backlog growth was 21.4% against 22.9% reported.

The segments tell different stories and should not be averaged. Freight (FY2025 $8,036M, +7.6%, 19.5% margin) grew 6.9% in Q3 2026 with a 26.4% adjusted margin (+1.9 points). Services (+6.7%) and Components (+5.3%) recovered from Q2's declines, Digital Intelligence rose 24.2% (helped by the Frauscher acquisition), and Equipment was flat (+0.3%) after +35.0% in Q2 — so the lumpy line has paused and the recurring base is growing again, a better mix than a quarter ago. Transit (FY2025 $3,131M, +7.3%, 13.5% margin) grew 11.3% (4.4% organic) with an adjusted margin up 2.6 points to 18.1%; aftermarket of $475M exceeds OEM of $408M, so more than half of Transit revenue is recurring, and the margin is expanding faster than Freight's from a lower base.

Valuation — and which earnings base. The answer moves a long way depending on the denominator. On trailing GAAP EPS of $8.04, WAB trades at 34.5× — the fully-loaded number, charging the company for merger intangible amortisation and restructuring. On FY2025 adjusted EPS of $8.97 it trades at 31.0×. On the midpoint of FY2026 adjusted guidance ($10.95) it trades at 25.4×, and on the 2026 consensus EPS of $10.98, 25.3× — above the quoted 22.9× forward multiple, which implies a $12.12 base and is therefore reaching into 2027. On trailing free cash flow of $1.78B against an enterprise value of about $52.5B, EV/FCF is about 29× (our calculation). So the honest range is 25× to 35× depending on how much credit you give management's adjustments, against a Railroads industry weighted average of 25.51×. Put differently: the stock is already discounting the full delivery of FY2026 guidance. The PEG of 1.41 says the same thing in one number, and the ROIC of 9.56% says that the quality of the underlying returns does not independently justify the premium. What justifies it, if anything does, is the visibility — and visibility is precisely what the backlog buys.

Bull Case

A record $31.44B multi-year backlog (+22.9%) and $8.99B 12-month backlog (+8.8%) give two and a half years of revenue visibility. Both segments are expanding margins simultaneously — Freight adjusted to 26.4% (+1.9pp) and Transit adjusted to 18.1% (+2.6pp) — and the recurring Services line is growing again (+6.7%). Trailing free cash flow reached $1.78B with cash conversion above 100% of earnings. Management has raised FY2026 guidance three times, from $10.05–10.45 to $10.85–11.05 adjusted EPS, and bought back $305M of shares in the quarter. Ten of twelve analysts rate it Buy or better, with even the lowest target above the current price.

Bear Case

34.5× trailing GAAP earnings and a 1.41 PEG on a 9.56% ROIC business is a premium the returns do not support on their own. Q3 organic sales growth was 4.7% — about half of the 8.1% reported came from acquisitions — and new-locomotive sales were flat. Multi-year backlog growth halved in a quarter (41.7% to 22.9%) and the 12-month backlog fell sequentially. The GAAP-to-adjusted gap was 24% of adjusted earnings in FY2025 and is largely recurring amortisation, the current ratio is 1.10 against $6.38B of debt, and the stock fell 4.32% on the second day after a beat-and-raise print. If Q4 shows Transit's 18.1% margin was a high and Equipment does not recover, there is no valuation support underneath.

Top 3 Risks

01
Backlog conversion at the booked margin. $31.44B of multi-year backlog was contracted at prices set before the current tariff environment. Wabtec expanded adjusted operating margin 1.9 points in Q3 even with tariffs, and the CFO said the year-over-year tariff drag should ease — but with prices fixed in the backlog, Wabtec carries the risk of any rise in input costs for years. Any sustained increase in steel, electronics or labour costs lands on gross margin with no ability to reprice.
02
Reported growth leans on acquisitions. Q3 organic growth was $137M, or 4.7%, against 8.1% reported, and Equipment sales were flat. Frauscher passes its anniversary in December 2026 and Dellner in February 2027; unless orders accelerate, reported growth converges toward the organic rate — and that is arithmetic, not a forecast.
03
Valuation with no sell ratings. No analyst carries a Sell, and the low target of $292 is 5.2% above the price. That is a risk, not a comfort: with every analyst already positive, any downgrades that come are not yet in the price. The 4.32% fall on October 22, the day after a beat and a raise, shows how little room a stock at 34.5× trailing earnings has for anything short of a clean quarter.
  Industry Commentary
Wabtec Grows While Its Customers Do Not
ONGOING HEADWIND
The twelve US-listed companies in the Railroads industry generate $95.43B of combined revenue, and the five large operators — Union Pacific ($26.36B), CSX ($14.94B), CPKC ($10.88B), Canadian National ($12.51B), Norfolk Southern ($12.96B) — are the customers who write Wabtec's Freight cheques. North American freight volumes have not been a growth story, and the operators' capital budgets follow volumes. Wabtec's 12.8% nine-month revenue growth is therefore not being funded by a rising customer market: $571M of the $1,047M increase was acquired, and the rest came from share of customer spend — modernisation instead of new build, digital retrofits instead of new signalling, and international orders outside the North American network. It is a harder engine to sustain than riding a customer's growth, and it means the addressable pool is finite in a way a genuine end-market expansion would not be.
Fleet Modernisation Is a Multi-Decade Replacement Cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
A locomotive built in the 1990s is now thirty years old, and replacing it outright is far more expensive than modernising it — new power assemblies, new controls, new braking, new digital. That arithmetic favours the builder of the platform. It is visible in the numbers: Freight Equipment sales are up 26.1% over nine months (though flat in Q3), and the company credits higher modernization deliveries, alongside core services demand, for Services rising 6.7% in Q3. The same logic runs through Transit, where the aftermarket ($475M) exceeds OEM ($408M). The replacement cycle is measured in decades and does not turn on a quarter's freight volumes.
Backlog Is Visibility, Not Demand — and the Market Is Paying for Demand
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A backlog tells you what has already been sold. It tells you nothing about what will be sold next. The divergence in Wabtec's own disclosure makes the point precisely: multi-year backlog +22.9% against 12-month backlog +8.8%. The long book is growing about 2.6 times faster than the near book, which means more of today's orders are for delivery after the next twelve months. Bull framing: that is exactly what you want — the company has locked in years of revenue at contracted prices, which fits a 0.92 beta and a 2022 fall of only 15% at monthly closes, and it gives management room to invest through a downturn that competitors will have to cut through. Bear framing: the market is paying 34.5× trailing GAAP earnings — a 35% premium to the industry's 25.51× weighted average — for a company earning 9.56% on invested capital, while the backlog's growth rate has halved since June. Backlog is the best disclosure this company has. It is not the same thing as growth, and the multiple currently treats it as if it were.
