01 Company Overview

Wabtec builds and maintains the equipment that moves freight and people on rails: locomotives, braking systems, couplers, wheels, doors, HVAC, positive train control electronics and the aftermarket parts and overhaul services that keep all of it running for decades. It reports in two segments — Freight and Transit. FY2025 revenue was $11,167M, up 7.5% from $10,387M in FY2024, and the trailing-twelve-month figure through June 2026 is $11.98B, up 13.4% as order momentum accelerated through the first half of 2026. At a $47.34B market capitalisation the company ranks roughly #538 globally, and is the largest supplier — as opposed to operator — in the listed railroad complex, sitting just behind Norfolk Southern and ahead of every railcar builder by an order of magnitude.

The central debate on this page is the backlog. Wabtec's multi-year backlog reached $30.93B at June 30, 2026 — up 41.7% year over year — against TTM revenue of $11.98B. That is roughly two and a half years of revenue already booked, and it is the single disclosure that justifies a 37.7× trailing multiple on a cyclical capital-goods name with a 0.92 beta. The question is whether that backlog represents a structural step-change in the earnings base (fleet modernisation, international locomotive orders, digital retrofits) or a lumpy, front-loaded order cycle — flattered by a weak dollar and by a handful of very large single contracts — that will convert into revenue at a lower margin than the 25.8% adjusted Freight margin printed in Q2. Sections 02, 04 and 07 take that apart.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Installed Base 30–40 Year Asset Life Safety Certification $30.9B Backlog Aftermarket Lock-In PTC Standard Position Duopoly in NA Locomotives

The moat is the installed base and the calendar. A mainline locomotive runs for three to four decades, and over that life it is overhauled, re-powered, re-braked and eventually modernised — almost always by the company that built it, because the parts, the software and the safety certifications are all specific to the platform. That is why Wabtec's Freight segment can show Equipment up 35% and Services down 4% in the same quarter without anyone worrying about the franchise: the service revenue is not competitively contested, it is timing. The braking and train-control businesses add a regulatory layer — safety-critical rail equipment must be certified by authorities in each jurisdiction, which turns every incumbent position into a multi-year barrier for a challenger. And North American mainline locomotive manufacturing is effectively a duopoly between Wabtec and Caterpillar's Progress Rail. The result is a business with a 35.7% gross margin and a 0.92 beta that nonetheless just grew revenue 17.5% in a quarter.

Key Vulnerabilities

—
Order lumpiness disguised as growth. A single $1.0B Australian order is more than 8% of a full year's revenue and roughly one third of the year-over-year increase in the 12-month backlog. Multi-year backlog up 41.7% in twelve months is not a rate of demand growth — it is a handful of very large awards landing in one window. If the next twelve months produce no comparable order, backlog will fall on flat underlying demand, and the market will read the decline as a cycle turning.
—
Customer concentration in a consolidating industry. Wabtec's Freight customers are a handful of Class I railroads plus a small set of international operators and mining companies. Those customers set capital budgets against freight volumes they do not control, and North American rail is itself consolidating — fewer, larger buyers with more leverage in every negotiation. A single Class I deferring a modernisation programme is a material revenue event.
—
The GAAP-to-adjusted gap is structural, not transitional. FY2025 GAAP EPS was $6.83 against adjusted EPS of $8.97 — a 24% gap, and it has persisted for years because it is largely amortisation of intangibles acquired in the 2019 GE Transportation merger plus recurring restructuring. Q4 2025 is the clearest illustration: a 12.0% GAAP operating margin against a 17.7% adjusted one. Investors paying 24.4× forward earnings are paying for the adjusted number.
—
Tariffs and input costs on a fixed-price backlog. Management explicitly cited tariff headwinds in Q2 2026 while still expanding margins. That worked this quarter. The structural exposure remains: a backlog booked at contracted prices two or three years ahead absorbs any input-cost inflation that arrives between signature and delivery, and $30.9B of multi-year backlog is a long window in which for costs to move.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Start with the backlog, because everything else is downstream of it. At June 30, 2026 Wabtec held a 12-month backlog of $9.14B, up 11.3%, and a multi-year backlog of $30.93B, up 41.7%. Against TTM revenue of $11.98B, the 12-month figure covers about 76% of a year's sales and about 73% of the FY2026 revenue guidance midpoint of $12.45B. The multi-year figure is roughly 2.6 years of revenue. That is the disclosure that makes a cyclical industrial behave like a subscription business, and it is why the beta is 0.92 and why the 2022 drawdown was 10% instead of 40%.

Now read it sceptically. Three things complicate the number. First, currency: the company itself disclosed that the December 2025 multi-year backlog rose 20.5% excluding FX while the reported increase was $5,135M — so a meaningful slice of the headline growth is translation, not demand, and the same caveat has to be applied to the +41.7% at June. Second, concentration: a single $1.0B Australian order is 8% of a year's revenue and a large share of the year-over-year backlog increase. Backlog built from a handful of mega-orders does not annualise; it creates a comparison problem twelve months later. Third, and most telling, the two backlog measures diverged — multi-year up 41.7% against 12-month up only 11.3%. That gap means the incremental orders are being booked for delivery beyond the next twelve months. It is genuinely good news for 2028 and genuinely neutral news for 2027.

The segments tell different stories and should not be averaged. Freight (FY2025 $8,036M, +7.6%, 19.5% margin) accelerated to +16.9% in Q2 2026 with a 25.8% adjusted margin — but the growth is concentrated in Equipment (+35.0%) and Digital Intelligence (+88.5%) while Services (−4.2%) and Components (−0.7%) shrank. Equipment and Digital are the order-driven lines; Services and Components are the recurring base. A quarter in which the lumpy lines grow 35–89% and the recurring lines shrink is a quarter that should be discounted, not extrapolated. Transit (FY2025 $3,131M, +7.3%, 13.5% margin, up from 11.6%) is the quieter and arguably better story: Q2 sales +18.9% with aftermarket $525M exceeding OEM $411M, and an adjusted margin up 2.5 points to 17.7%. More than half of Transit revenue is recurring, the margin is expanding faster than Freight's, and it is starting from a structurally lower base — meaning Transit has more room left to run than Freight does.

Valuation — and which earnings base. The answer moves a long way depending on the denominator. On trailing GAAP EPS of $7.43, WAB trades at 37.7× — the fully-loaded number, charging the company for merger intangible amortisation and restructuring. On FY2025 adjusted EPS of $8.97 it trades at 31.2×. On the midpoint of FY2026 adjusted guidance ($10.75) it trades at 26.1×, and on the 2026 consensus EPS of $10.86, 25.8× — close to the quoted 24.44× forward multiple, which implies an $11.47 base and is therefore reaching into 2027. On trailing free cash flow of $1.71B against a $53.62B enterprise value, EV/FCF is about 31×. So the honest range is 26× to 38× depending on how much credit you give management's adjustments, against a Railroads industry weighted average of 26.37×. Put differently: the stock is already discounting the full delivery of FY2026 guidance. The PEG of 1.80 says the same thing in one number, and the ROIC of 9.18% says that the quality of the underlying returns does not independently justify the premium. What justifies it, if anything does, is the visibility — and visibility is precisely what the backlog buys.

Bull Case

A record $30.93B multi-year backlog (+41.7%) and $9.14B 12-month backlog (+11.3%) give two and a half years of revenue visibility in an industry where competitors have none. Both segments are expanding margins simultaneously — Freight adjusted to 25.8% (+0.8pp) and Transit adjusted to 17.7% (+2.5pp) — while revenue accelerated to +17.5% and free cash flow reached $1.71B with cash conversion above 100% of earnings. Management has raised FY2026 guidance twice, from $10.05–10.45 to $10.60–10.90 adjusted EPS. Digital Intelligence at +88.5% is a genuinely higher-margin, software-like line compounding off a small base. Ten of twelve analysts rate it Buy or better, with even the lowest target above the current price.

Bear Case

37.7× trailing GAAP earnings and a 1.80 PEG on a 9.18% ROIC business is a premium the returns do not support on their own. Q2's Freight growth came entirely from the lumpy lines — Equipment +35.0%, Digital +88.5% — while the recurring aftermarket actually shrank (Services −4.2%, Components −0.7%). The multi-year backlog is flattered by currency (management disclosed 20.5% constant-currency growth against a much larger reported figure at December 2025) and by single mega-orders like the $1.0B Australian contract that cannot repeat annually. The GAAP-to-adjusted gap is a permanent 24% of earnings, the current ratio is 1.12 against $6.94B of debt, and every analyst target on the visible table predates the July print. If the October quarter shows Transit's 17.7% margin was a one-quarter high, there is no valuation support underneath.

Top 3 Risks

01
Backlog conversion at the booked margin. $30.93B of multi-year backlog was contracted at prices set before the current tariff environment. Wabtec expanded adjusted operating margin 0.8 points in Q2 despite tariffs, which is genuinely impressive — but a fixed-price backlog is a multi-year short position in input costs. Any sustained increase in steel, electronics or labour costs lands on gross margin with no ability to reprice.
02
The recurring base is not growing. Freight Services fell 4.2% and Components fell 0.7% in Q2 2026. These are the lines that are supposed to be uncorrelated with the order cycle and to provide the floor under the earnings. If they stay flat while Equipment and Digital normalise from +35% and +88.5%, total Freight growth converges toward zero very quickly — and that is arithmetic, not a forecast.
03
Valuation with no short base and no sell ratings. Short interest is 1.65% of shares and no analyst carries a Sell. That is a positioning risk, not a comfort: there is no bearish base to squeeze and no downgrade cycle already priced. A single guidance disappointment in October would force revisions across a consensual book at 37.7× trailing earnings, and the low target of $292 gives no indication of what the downside case looks like because nobody in the visible coverage has modelled one.
  Industry Commentary
Wabtec Grows While Its Customers Do Not
ONGOING HEADWIND
The twelve US-listed companies in the Railroads industry generate $93.55B of combined revenue, and the five large operators — Union Pacific ($25.41B), CSX ($14.51B), CPKC ($10.88B), Canadian National ($12.51B), Norfolk Southern ($12.54B) — are the customers who write Wabtec's Freight cheques. North American freight volumes have not been a growth story, and the operators' capital budgets follow volumes. Wabtec's 13.4% TTM revenue growth is therefore being funded not by a rising customer market but by share of customer spend: modernisation instead of new build, digital retrofits instead of new signalling, and international orders outside the North American network entirely. That works, and it has worked for three years. It is also a harder engine to sustain than riding a customer's growth, and it means the addressable pool is finite in a way a genuine end-market expansion would not be.
Fleet Modernisation Is a Multi-Decade Replacement Cycle
EMERGING TAILWIND
A locomotive built in the 1990s is now thirty years old, and replacing it outright is far more expensive than modernising it — new power assemblies, new controls, new braking, new digital. That arithmetic favours the incumbent builder absolutely, because only Wabtec can modernise a Wabtec/GE platform economically. It is visible in the numbers: Freight Equipment sales grew 35.0% to $737M in Q2 2026, and the $1.0B Australian order explicitly bundles "new locomotives, services, components and digital solutions" — a modernisation-plus-service contract rather than a pure equipment sale. The same logic runs through Transit, where the aftermarket ($525M) now exceeds OEM ($411M). The replacement cycle is measured in decades and does not turn on a quarter's freight volumes.
Backlog Is Visibility, Not Demand — and the Market Is Paying for Demand
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A backlog tells you what has already been sold. It tells you nothing about what will be sold next. The divergence in Wabtec's own disclosure makes the point precisely: multi-year backlog +41.7% against 12-month backlog +11.3%. The long book is growing four times faster than the near book, which means today's orders are for delivery beyond 2027. Bull framing: that is exactly what you want — the company has locked in years of revenue at contracted prices, which is why the beta is 0.92 and the 2022 drawdown was only 10%, and it gives management room to invest through a downturn that competitors will have to cut through. Bear framing: the market is paying 37.7× trailing GAAP earnings — a 43% premium to the industry's 26.37× weighted average — for a company earning 9.18% on invested capital, and it is doing so on the strength of a number that is partly currency translation and partly one $1.0B order. Backlog is the best disclosure this company has. It is not the same thing as growth, and the multiple currently treats it as if it were.
