01 Company Overview

Workday sells cloud software that runs the two systems every large organisation cannot switch off: human capital management (HCM — payroll, HR records, talent, scheduling) and financial management (the general ledger, planning, procurement, spend). It has been named a Leader in Gartner's Cloud HCM Magic Quadrant for eleven consecutive years and serves more than 11,500 customers. Fiscal 2026 (ended January 31, 2026) revenue was $9.55B, up 13.1%; trailing-twelve-month revenue through July 2026 is $10.16B, up 13.4%. At $47.19B of market value it ranks roughly #507 globally (est.).

The question on this page is the one the whole application-software group has been fighting over since early 2026: is Workday cheap, or is it broken? Subscription growth has decelerated from the high teens to 13.9%, and the stock was cut in half between December 2025 and April 2026 on the fear that AI agents will do the work of the seats Workday charges for. Then, on August 13, 2026, Reuters reported that Silver Lake was in talks to take the company private, and the stock had its best day in a decade. So there are now two bids for this name — the public market's and, possibly, a private one — and the report below tries to work out what the public one is actually paying for: a 6.0% free-cash-flow yield, a 4.7× EV/sales multiple, a 4.3% buyback yield, and a non-GAAP operating margin that has climbed to 31%. Whether that is a bargain or a value trap depends almost entirely on whether 13–14% subscription growth holds.

The strategic answer to the "AI kills seats" bear case is to sell the AI. Illuminate is Workday's AI platform, trained on the company's proprietary corpus of HR and finance transactions; the Agent System of Record is the pitch that an enterprise will need one governed registry to onboard, permission and audit both human employees and software agents — i.e. Workday wants to be the HR system for the bots too. The Sana acquisition (announced September 2025; price not disclosed on sources fetched) supplied the conversational front-end and now powers Workday Learning; Evisort (contracts), Paradox (conversational recruiting) and Flowise (low-code agent builder) round out the stack. Q2 FY27 shipped a Developer Agent, Agent Passport, Adaptive Decision Intelligence and a Financial Audit Agent. The verified adoption data: more than 5,500 customers using Workday's own agents (+35% quarter-on-quarter) and AI driving more than 25% of new annual contract value; Wells Fargo cited more than 200% YoY growth in new agentic-AI ACV. What is not disclosed is AI revenue in dollars, so the size of this line is unknown — it is an attach story, not yet a P&L line.

Watch item: the pricing model. If agents are sold per-consumption or per-outcome rather than per-seat, Workday can grow revenue while customer headcount shrinks — that is the whole bull case, and it is not yet proven in the numbers.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
SYSTEM-OF-RECORD STICKINESS PAYROLL SWITCHING COST SINGLE DATA MODEL (HCM + FIN) $27.4B CONTRACTED BACKLOG PROPRIETARY HR/FINANCE DATA CORPUS 11-YEAR GARTNER LEADER PARTNER ECOSYSTEM

The moat is inertia, and it is a very good moat. Nobody rips out payroll for fun: a Workday HCM migration is a multi-year, eight-figure project touching every employee's paycheque, and the failure mode is a front-page story. Once Financials is layered on the same data model, the two systems reinforce each other — headcount plans feed the budget, the budget feeds the ledger — and the exit cost roughly doubles. That is why $27.4B of contracted subscription backlog sits on the books and why gross retention in this category is historically very high. The eleventh straight Gartner Leader placement matters less as marketing than as evidence that the product has not fallen behind the two giant incumbents, SAP and Oracle, that own most of the remaining on-premise base Workday still wants to convert. The newest layer — the proprietary corpus of HR and finance transactions that trains Illuminate — is a genuine data advantage if agents need domain-specific grounding, and a much smaller one if a general-purpose model with API access can do the same job. That single uncertainty is what the market has been repricing all year.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Seat-based pricing in an agent world. Workday charges largely per employee under management. If AI agents allow customers to run leaner HR and finance teams — or if they eliminate categories of knowledge-worker jobs altogether — the denominator Workday bills on shrinks. Management's counter is to sell agents on their own price line (they already drive >25% of new ACV), but there is not yet a disclosed AI revenue figure to prove that new revenue exceeds seat leakage. Total backlog growing only 8.0% while 12-month backlog grows 14.2% is consistent with customers hedging on contract length.
▸
Growth deceleration with a rich cost of talent. Subscription growth has stepped from ~19% (FY23) to 16% (FY25) to 13.9% today, and Q3 is guided to 12%. The margin story has been funded by three rounds of layoffs (3% in early 2023, 8.5% — roughly 1,750 roles — in February 2025, and a further ~2% in February 2026) and by outsourcing services. There is a limit to how many times a software company can expand margins by shrinking headcount before product velocity suffers, and the company is simultaneously promising the most ambitious product roadmap in its history.
▸
Incumbent bundling. SAP and Oracle sell HCM and ERP inside far larger suites, often at a discount to protect the database or the broader cloud contract; Microsoft is pushing Copilot agents into the same workflows from the productivity layer. Workday is a best-of-breed vendor in a market that periodically swings back toward suites, and the AI cycle is exactly the kind of platform shift that has historically favoured the platform owner.
▸
Leadership churn and a founder-control structure. Two CEO changes in four years (Eschenbach in, then out on February 9, 2026), a rebuilt technology leadership team drawn from Google Cloud (Gerrit Kazmaier, Peter Bailis), a $2B activist on the register and now reported take-private talks. Bhusri's Class B super-voting shares mean public holders get the economics but not the decision on whether — and at what price — the company is sold.

Notable One-Off Items

The $374M tax benefit. Q2 FY27 GAAP net income was $632M on GAAP operating income of just $313M. The difference is an intra-entity intellectual-property transfer that created a $374M deferred tax asset, worth $1.52 of the $2.57 GAAP EPS. It is real, but it is not earnings power: strip it and the quarter earned about $1.05 GAAP, and TTM EPS falls from $4.86 to roughly $3.34. Any trailing P/E you see quoted for WDAY this quarter is about a third too low. The effective tax rate of 2.57% on the statistics page is the same artefact.

Restructuring. Three rounds of layoffs — 3% in early 2023, 8.5% (about 1,750 roles) announced February 2025, and roughly 2% in February 2026 — carried severance charges that GAAP absorbs and non-GAAP excludes. The 31.1% non-GAAP operating margin is a post-restructuring run-rate; the 11.8% GAAP margin has paid for it.

The GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap. Q2 non-GAAP operating income of $824M versus GAAP $313M means $511M of quarterly add-backs, overwhelmingly stock-based compensation plus amortisation of acquired intangibles (Sana, Evisort, Paradox, HiredScore). Annualised that is roughly $2B a year, or about 19% of revenue. The buyback — $1.3B in Q2, shares down 4.32% YoY — is more than offsetting the dilution, which is why per-share numbers are improving faster than the business, and why "how much of the story is buyback plus margin" has a concrete answer: most of the EPS growth, and none of the revenue growth.

Takeover premium in the price. The stock closed at $175.29 on August 12 and $206.45 on August 13 after the Silver Lake report. Roughly $30 of the current $195.79 — about 15% — is arguably deal speculation rather than fundamentals. If talks end without a deal, that is the size of the air pocket.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $289.98 to $195.79 today, WDAY has returned −32.5% over five years — roughly −7.6% a year, with no dividend, over a period in which the NASDAQ-100 and the S&P 500 both roughly doubled (index total-return figures for the exact window were not verified from sources fetched, so no precise relative number is stated). The shape is a double top and a collapse: a 52% drawdown from October 2021 to $139.58 in June 2022 as rates repriced software; a full recovery to a monthly-close high of $294.66 in February 2024; eighteen months of grinding between $211 and $263 while growth decelerated; then a 58.5% fall from the February 2024 peak to $122.40 in April 2026 as the AI-disruption narrative took hold and the CEO changed. The stock has since rallied 60% off that low in five months — but almost half of that rally was two days in August (the Silver Lake report and the post-earnings move). A shareholder who bought this stock at any point between March 2021 and February 2025 is under water.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Cheap or broken — the arithmetic. Start with what the public market is paying. Market cap $47.19B; net debt about $0.37B; enterprise value roughly $47.6B. Against TTM revenue of $10.16B that is 4.7× EV/sales (the statistics page shows 4.95× on the higher Sep 3 close), for a business growing 13% with a 76% gross margin and a 31% non-GAAP operating margin. Against TTM free cash flow of $2.84B, it is 16.7× EV/FCF, a 6.0% FCF yield. For context, at the April low the same FCF yield was above 9%. Application-software peers with 13% growth have historically traded at 6–9× sales and 25–35× FCF; on those historical norms, yes, WDAY is cheap. The trailing GAAP P/E of 40× looks expensive but is mostly a denominator problem — stock comp and amortisation — and even the honest ~59× ex-tax-benefit figure is on earnings that FCF exceeds by more than two to one.

How much of the story is buyback plus margin? Almost all of the per-share story. Revenue is guided to grow 13% this year and consensus has it at 11.7% next year. Non-GAAP EPS consensus goes from $9.23 (FY27) to $11.06 (FY28), or +19.9%. Decompose that: roughly 12% from revenue, another 3–4 points from the margin walking from 31% toward the low-to-mid 30s, and about 4 points from the share count shrinking (−4.32% YoY; 9.8M shares retired in Q2 alone, with a fresh $4.0B authorisation equal to 8.5% of the market cap). So of the ~20% EPS growth investors are paying 17.7× for, roughly 40% is financial engineering in the neutral sense — margin and buyback — and 60% is the business. That is a perfectly respectable mix for a maturing franchise. It is a poor mix for a company whose bull case is that it is about to re-accelerate on AI.

What "broken" would actually look like — and whether it does. The bear case says AI agents shrink the seat count Workday bills on. The verifiable leading indicators are: (1) 12-month backlog growth — 14.2%, still above revenue growth, which is not what erosion looks like; (2) total backlog growth — 8.0%, which is soft and suggests shorter or smaller renewal commitments, which is consistent with customers hedging; (3) AI attach — >25% of new ACV, 5,500+ customers on agents, +35% QoQ, which is real adoption but with no revenue disclosure; (4) professional services flat, which says new implementations are steady rather than accelerating. The honest reading is "decelerating, not broken" — with one yellow flag in total backlog. The seat-erosion thesis is a forecast, not yet a data point; the deceleration from 19% to 12% is a data point.

The Silver Lake overlay. Private equity's view of this asset is straightforward: a 6% FCF yield growing 13% with a 92% subscription mix and $27B of contracted backlog is a leveraged-buyout textbook case, which is why Dayforce ($12.3B) and OneStream ($6.4B) were taken out earlier this year. The report that Silver Lake is in talks is the market's second opinion that the stock is cheap — but it also means about $30 of the current price is a probability-weighted bid rather than a discounted cash flow. If a deal is struck, historical software take-private premiums suggest a price above the pre-report level, but not necessarily far above where the stock already sits. If talks end, the stock likely revisits the $165–180 area where the fundamentals alone were pricing it in early August, which is still a 7% FCF yield.

Bull Case

A system of record with $27.4B of contracted backlog, 12-month backlog growing 14.2%, a 31% non-GAAP margin still expanding, and $2.84B of free cash flow trades at 4.7× sales and a 6.0% FCF yield — multiples last seen in 2022's rate shock. Management is retiring 4%+ of the share count a year with a new $4B authorisation, a $2B activist is on the register, and a top-tier software buyer is reportedly at the table. AI is already more than a quarter of new bookings; if agent pricing decouples revenue from seats, growth re-accelerates from a base the market has priced for decline. Returning founder-CEO Bhusri gives the AI pivot credibility with customers.

Bear Case

Subscription growth has fallen every year from 19% to 13.9% and is guided to 12% next quarter; total backlog is growing just 8%. The margin has been bought with three rounds of layoffs, and per-share growth is 40% buyback-and-margin. GAAP earnings are 12% of revenue after ~$2B a year of stock comp and amortisation, and the headline trailing P/E is flattered by a $374M tax accounting benefit. Roughly 15% of the price is a take-private rumour that may not close. Two CEO changes in four years, a founder with super-voting control who will decide any sale, and Microsoft, SAP and Oracle all pushing AI agents into the same workflows. Morgan Stanley's $145 target is 26% below the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Deal talks collapse. The Aug 13 report added roughly $8B of market value in a day. No price, structure or timetable has been reported, and Silver Lake's history includes walking away. A "talks ended" headline likely returns the stock to the $165–180 range within a session, independent of any change in the business.
02
Seat erosion shows up in backlog. The 8.0% total-backlog growth is the first soft number in an otherwise clean set. If 12-month backlog growth falls below revenue growth at the Q3 print in late November, the "decelerating not broken" verdict flips, FY28 consensus of 11.7% growth is at risk, and the 4.7× sales multiple will have been the right price rather than a cheap one.
03
Margin lever exhausted. Non-GAAP margin has gone from ~19% in FY22 to 31% largely through headcount and services outsourcing. The company is guiding 31.0% for FY27 — flat with Q2 — while promising the biggest product cycle in its history. If AI investment (R&D, GPU compute, acquisitions like Sana) has to be funded from margin, the buyback-plus-margin half of the EPS story stalls exactly when the revenue half is slowing.
  Industry Commentary
The Seat-Based SaaS Repricing
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
Application software de-rated sharply in the first half of 2026 on a single thesis: if AI agents do knowledge work, companies need fewer people, and software priced per person needs fewer seats. Workday — whose entire revenue base is priced on employees under management — became the purest expression of the trade, falling 58.5% peak-to-trough while short interest climbed to 10.4% of float. The thesis has not yet appeared in Workday's 12-month backlog (+14.2%), but it has arguably appeared in total backlog (+8.0%). The industry's response — Workday's "Agent System of Record," ServiceNow's and Salesforce's agent platforms, consumption-based pricing pilots — is a race to reprice before the seat count actually moves. Whoever proves that agent revenue exceeds seat leakage first gets the multiple back.
Growth Bought With Headcount, Not Bookings
ONGOING HEADWIND
Across large-cap SaaS the 2023–2026 playbook has been identical: cut 5–10% of staff, redirect the savings to "AI investment" and margin, and buy back stock. Workday ran it three times (3% in 2023, 8.5% in Feb 2025, ~2% in Feb 2026) and lifted non-GAAP operating margin from ~19% to 31%, while revenue growth slid from 19% to 13%. The result across the group is EPS growth in the high teens on revenue growth in the low teens — and a market that increasingly refuses to pay a growth multiple for it. The activist wave (Elliott at Workday, and similar positions elsewhere in the sector) and the private-equity wave (Dayforce, OneStream, now possibly Workday) are the two natural endpoints when public investors stop paying for margin-driven EPS.
The Private-Equity Put Is Back
EMERGING TAILWIND
After a quiet 2025, software buyouts returned in 2026 at scale: Dayforce for $12.3B in February, OneStream for $6.4B in April, and a reported approach for Workday that would be among the largest software buyouts on record. The mechanism is simple — when a franchise with 92% recurring revenue and a 28% FCF margin trades at a 6–9% FCF yield, it can be levered and taken private at a premium and still clear a PE return hurdle. Bull framing: the floor under Workday and its peers is no longer the public market's opinion of AI risk but the private market's cost of capital, which is a higher floor. Bear framing: Cantor's David Siffringer called it "the PE put is back" with visible scepticism — a sector that is only cheap enough for a buyer who can lever it is, by definition, a sector public investors have stopped wanting to own, and puts expire.
