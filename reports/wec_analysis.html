01 Company Overview

WEC Energy Group is a Milwaukee-based regulated electric and natural gas holding company serving customers across Wisconsin, Illinois, Michigan and Minnesota, with roughly 35,200 miles of overhead electric distribution lines and 47,200 miles of gas distribution mains as of December 31, 2025. FY2025 revenue was $9.80B, up 13.96% from $8.60B in FY2024, with diluted EPS of $4.81 under standard accounting (GAAP), or $5.27 excluding a 46-cent charge for a proposed settlement of the open Illinois proceedings over the QIP rider, a bill surcharge that recovers gas-pipe replacement spending (FY2025 earnings release). The TTM revenue through September 2026 is $10.32B, and the first nine months of 2026 brought in 7.2% more than the same months of 2025. At $38.2B of market value on October 30, 2026 the company ranks #278 in the S&P 500: mid-pack among US regulated electrics, about twice the size of Alliant ($18.6B) and a little over a third the size of Duke ($99.3B) (shares × Oct 30 close, our calculation).

The central debate here is not whether WEC is a good utility. By the usual tests it plainly is, with a 23rd consecutive annual dividend increase in January 2026 and a beta (how much the stock moves with the market) of 0.44. The question is whether the data-centre buildout in southeastern Wisconsin really lifts WEC's earnings growth, and the Q3 2026 update answered with bigger numbers: a $47.5B capital plan for 2027–2031, $10B more than the plan it replaces, and a target of 7% to 9%-plus annual EPS growth from 2027 to 2031, measured from $5.56, the midpoint of the original 2026 guidance. The financing question has not gone away: management plans to raise $5B–$5.5B of new shares over the five years. The stock fell to $100.78 at the end of September, its lowest month-end close since January 2025, then gained 15.2% in October to $116.07, within 1.6% of its April 2026 month-end high of $117.94. Sections 04 and 07 take that apart.

The Wisconsin utilities — principally We Energies (Wisconsin Electric Power) and Wisconsin Public Service — are the business. A regulated utility's economics are not a market share question: inside its service territory it is the only seller, and earnings are set by rate base (the depreciated capital the regulator lets it earn a return on) multiplied by the allowed ROE. Growth therefore comes from spending capital the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin approves, not from winning customers. WEC filed a Wisconsin rate request in April 2026 covering forward-looking test years 2027 and 2028; on the Q3 2026 call management said it expects final orders by the end of the year, with new rates effective in January 2027 and January 2028. This is the segment that carries the data-centre load story below: on the same call the company raised its forecast of electric demand growth to 4.9 gigawatts between 2026 and 2031, 1 gigawatt more than in the previous plan.

Rate-case watch: Wisconsin 2027–28 test-year orders due by year-end 2026 — the single largest near-term determinant of 2027 earnings power.

WEC holds minority interests in transmission companies — chiefly American Transmission Company — which are accounted for on the equity method, so they show up in earnings rather than in revenue. That is why this segment can move the profit line without moving the top line. WEC's consolidated Q3 2026 operating income was $563.7M, against $449.6M a year earlier (release); on the call the CFO credited ATC with 3 cents of the year-on-year EPS gain, and the new plan carries about $5.3B of ATC investment for 2027–2031, $1.2B more than before. Transmission is the quietly attractive part of the utility business in 2026: FERC-regulated returns, a national backlog of power projects waiting to connect to the grid, and load growth that cannot be served without new wires. If data-centre demand in the Midwest is real, transmission spending is downstream of it by construction.

Why it matters: equity-method earnings lift ROE without putting the associated debt on WEC's balance sheet — worth remembering when reading the 12.49% ROE in Section 04.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
LEGAL MONOPOLY FRANCHISE RATE-BASE COMPOUNDING FORWARD TEST YEARS 23-YEAR DIVIDEND STREAK CONSTRUCTIVE WISCONSIN REGULATOR ANCHOR DATA-CENTRE DEMAND TRANSMISSION EQUITY STAKES

The moat is statutory, not commercial. WEC's utilities hold exclusive franchises: no competitor can string a second set of wires down the same street, and the barrier to entry is not capital or technology but a legislature. What varies between good and bad utilities is therefore regulatory quality — whether the commission grants forward-looking test years (so the utility earns on capital as it is spent rather than years later), whether it allows riders for specific programmes, and whether it authorises an ROE and equity layer that let the company finance growth without dilution. On both counts WEC reads well as of October 2026: Wisconsin is running forward test years for 2027 and 2028, and Illinois resolved twelve dockets by unanimous settlement in May 2026. Layered on top is a genuine demand shock: on the Q3 2026 call the CFO said the assets serving very large customers are "projected to represent almost 30% of our total assets by 2031", and more than $20B of the $47.5B plan goes to projects for them. That is the rarest thing in this sector: organic load growth after two decades of flat-to-declining kilowatt-hour demand.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Financing the $47.5B plan without dilution. The first nine months of 2026 show $3.41B of operating cash flow against $3.32B of capex, and the plan is scheduled to grow. TTM free cash flow is −$0.76B (stockanalysis.com), total debt is $22.76B against $58.8M of cash, and debt-to-equity stands at 1.56 (September 30 balance sheet, our calculation). Management's plan funds the five years with $26B–$27B of operating cash, about $19B of new debt and $5B–$5.5B of new shares; the CFO said it locked in about $1.16B of new equity in the first nine months of 2026, $1.1B of it through at-the-market sales (shares sold gradually into the market), with about $765M of that still under forward contracts that deliver the shares later. Every new share spreads the earnings across more owners, which works against the 7%–9%-plus EPS target.
02
Customer concentration where there previously was none. If almost 30% of total assets serve very large customers by 2031, WEC has swapped a diversified residential and commercial base for real exposure to a handful of data-centre operators. A cancelled or deferred campus would leave approved capital without the load that justified it.
03
Interest-rate sensitivity in both directions. A 3.28% dividend yield and a 0.44 beta make WEC trade partly like a bond. With $22.76B of debt and a capital plan that must be continuously refinanced, higher long-term rates raise the cost of capital faster than rate cases can pass it through, and simultaneously make the dividend less competitive against Treasuries. Over the year to October 30, 2026 the stock rose 3.1% on price, against 11.2% for the S&P 500 (SPY).
04
Rate-shock and political risk from the buildout. Large new generation and transmission built for data centres must be paid for by some split between the large cloud and data-centre companies and ordinary household and business customers. If Wisconsin residential bills rise visibly to serve AI infrastructure, the politics of the 2027–28 rate orders change quickly. Regulatory goodwill is the asset most easily spent and hardest to rebuild, and the gas distribution segments carry the parallel long-run risk of decarbonisation policy stranding assets.

Notable One-Off Items. Four distortions are worth naming, only the last of them a one-off in the accounting sense. First, revenue is not a clean growth signal for this company: FY2023 (−7.34%) and FY2024 (−3.30%) fell while earnings rose, because fuel and purchased-power costs are passed through to customers and therefore inflate and deflate the top line without touching margin. Second, quarterly results are sharply seasonal — Q1 2026 earned $2.45 of the year's EPS, Q2 $0.91 and Q3 $1.18 — so any single quarter read in isolation misleads. Third, weather and timing helped Q3: on the call the CFO put warmer-than-normal weather at about 5 cents of the 35-cent year-on-year gain and lower day-to-day operating and maintenance costs at about 8 cents, mostly from a stock-based pay plan, which she said could be timing; the raised $5.60–$5.64 range assumes normal weather for the rest of the year. Fourth, the FY2025 base is distorted: GAAP EPS of $4.81 includes the 46-cent Illinois settlement charge, so trailing GAAP EPS growth looks faster than the underlying $5.27 adjusted figure implies.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen on most of the recent periods and is conventionally called "overbought"; below 30 means the opposite. It is a momentum gauge, not a valuation signal — an overbought stock can keep rising and an oversold one can keep falling.

Over the five years from the October 29, 2021 month-end close of $90.06 to the October 30, 2026 close of $116.07, WEC returned +28.9% on price alone — roughly 5.2% a year compounded. The dividend, now $3.81 a year annualised, comes on top, so the return with dividends was materially higher. That shape — modest price appreciation, a large share of the return arriving as cash — is the honest profile of a regulated utility, and it is what someone buying WEC should expect to own.

The path was not a straight line. The stock spent 2022 as a defensive hiding place while the rest of the market fell, peaking at $105.07 in May 2022. It then spent two years being repriced by Treasury yields rather than by earnings, grinding to a five-year low of $78.46 in June 2024 — a 25.3% drop from the 2022 month-end high with nothing wrong operationally. The recovery from that trough to $117.94 in April 2026 was +50.3%, and it maps almost exactly onto the emergence of the data-centre load story. The stock then gave back 14.5% to a September 2026 month-end close of $100.78, before October's +15.2%, the largest monthly gain on this chart, took it to $116.07. Most of that October gain, 9.6%, came before the October 29 release; the print and the bigger plan added 5.0% over the next two sessions. 08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

WEC has what almost no US utility has had in twenty years: real organic load growth. A $47.5B plan for 2027–2031, 3.2 GW of forecast demand in the I-94 corridor where Microsoft is building and 1.3 GW at the Vantage/Oracle site with potential for 3.5 GW convert into rate base, and rate base converts mechanically into earnings. Management now targets 7% to 9%-plus annual EPS growth from 2027 to 2031, says the extra $10B of spending needs no more equity than the old plan, and just beat the quarter by 20% against Nasdaq's consensus. Buy that at 21.1× with a 3.28% yield backed by 23 consecutive annual increases, and the three brokers who moved after the print set targets of $128–$130, 10%–12% above the close.

Bear Case

The growth has to be paid for before it pays. Trailing free cash flow is −$0.76B, total debt is $22.76B against $58.8M of cash, debt-to-equity is 1.56 and ROIC is 4.38% — thin returns on the total capital base that only become an attractive equity return through borrowing. The plan still needs $5B–$5.5B of new shares and about $19B of new debt, and if the Wisconsin 2027–28 orders authorise a lower ROE or equity layer than assumed, the 7% to 9%-plus target slips. Meanwhile the stock has run 15.2% in a month to within 1.6% of its April high, the average target of $117.59 sits only 1.3% above the price, and the Illinois judge's proposed 9.48% ROE shows where regulators may land. A 21.1× multiple on a 0.44-beta utility leaves little room for a plan that slips.

01
Financing and dilution risk. A $47.5B plan against −$0.76B TTM free cash flow and $22.76B of existing debt has to be funded from somewhere: management's answer is $26B–$27B of operating cash, about $19B of new debt and $5B–$5.5B of new shares. Every share issued spreads the earnings over more owners and works against the EPS growth rate the valuation rests on.
02
Adverse regulatory outcomes. The Wisconsin 2027–28 orders and the Illinois 2027 decision both land by year-end 2026, the Illinois one against a proposed 9.48% ROE. A below-expectation authorised ROE or equity layer, or disallowance of capital, would reset earnings power for the whole plan period.
03
Hyperscaler load failing to arrive on schedule. The gigawatt figures above are company demand forecasts. Data-centre plans are revised frequently. Capital approved and built against load that slips leaves WEC with rate base it must justify to a commission and to ratepayers who are already absorbing higher bills.
Industry Commentary
Emerging Tailwind
Midwest data-centre demand
US electricity demand was essentially flat for two decades as efficiency gains offset growth. AI data centres have ended that, and southeastern Wisconsin has become one of the country's more concentrated build zones. WEC's own figures from the Q3 2026 call: 4.9 GW of forecast electric demand growth between 2026 and 2031, 1 GW more than in the previous plan; 3.2 GW in the I-94 corridor where Microsoft is expanding; the Vantage/Oracle site at Port Washington at 1.3 GW with potential for 3.5 GW; and very-large-customer assets at almost 30% of total assets by 2031. For a sector whose growth has mostly come from replacing old equipment, this is a different equation.
Critical Constraint
Cost of capital
Utilities are among the most rate-sensitive stocks in the market, from both ends. They carry enormous refinanced debt — WEC has $22.76B, with $19.72B of it long-term at September 30, 2026 — and they compete with Treasuries for income buyers at a 3.28% yield. Regulators allow recovery of a cost of debt, but with a lag measured in rate cases, not in weeks. The new plan adds about $19B of debt, including hybrid instruments (part debt, part equity in the rating agencies' eyes), and $5B–$5.5B of new shares; short sellers held 4.46% of the float at the October 15 settlement, a sizeable position for a 0.44-beta utility.
Bull vs Bear — the frame
Growth you must pre-pay
Both cases accept the same facts. The bull reads $47.5B of approved-and-approvable capital as a contracted earnings stream: rate base times allowed ROE, compounding at 7% to 9%-plus a year, bought today at 21.1× and 3.28%. The bear reads the identical plan as an obligation that must be financed at −$0.76B free cash flow, 1.56 debt-to-equity and 4.38% ROIC, with the terms set by two commissions before year-end and a stock that has already rallied to the analysts' average target. The resolution is not analytical — it is two rate orders and the 2027 guidance, all due by December. That is an unusually clean setup: rarely is so much of the argument for a $38B company scheduled to be settled on a known calendar.
