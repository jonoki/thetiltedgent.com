01 Company Overview

Wells Fargo is the fourth-largest bank in the United States by assets and the third-largest by domestic deposits, running roughly $1.48 trillion of average deposits and $1.03 trillion of average loans across consumer banking, commercial banking, corporate and investment banking, and wealth management. Full-year 2025 revenue was approximately $83.7B, up about 1.7% YoY, on net income of $21.3B and diluted EPS of $6.26. The far more important number is the current one: Q3 2026 revenue of $21.9B (+2% YoY) and EPS of $1.87 (+13%), from a bank now free of the Federal Reserve's seven-year asset cap — average loans grew 11% and deposits 11% from a year earlier, growth rates this bank was legally prohibited from producing between February 2018 and June 2025. Revenue fell 3% from Q2's $22.6B and came in below the roughly $22.3B analysts expected, even as EPS beat the $1.84–1.85 consensus.

The stock has lagged in 2026: at $82.75 it is down about 11.2% year-to-date (from the $93.20 close on Dec 31, 2025) against an S&P 500 up about 13.2% (SPY price return, $681.92 to $771.79), even as earnings per share kept rising. The market spent 2024 and 2025 paying up for the asset-cap-removal story; 2026 has been the year of asking what the removal actually earns, and whether a CET1 ratio that has fallen from 11.0% to 10.0% — the bottom of management's 10% to 10.5% target range — is the price of admission.

Notable Executives & Holders
Chief Executive Officer
Charlie Scharf
CEO since October 2019, recruited from BNY Mellon (and before that Visa and JPMorgan) explicitly to fix the regulatory mess. He closed more than a dozen consent orders, exited correspondent mortgage, and delivered the asset-cap removal in June 2025 — the defining achievement of his tenure. On the Q3 2026 call he said headcount fell 2% in the quarter, 8% from a year earlier and 30% from its second-quarter 2020 peak, and argued that scale matters with AI: an agent built for the call centers or operations sites costs what it costs anyone else, but the bank can deploy it across a very large part of the company.
Chief Financial Officer
Mike Santomassimo
Named CFO in July 2020 and started that fall. Before that he was CFO of BNY Mellon (from 2018) and, earlier, CFO for Banking at JPMorgan Chase, which included investment banking and treasury services. He owns the expense narrative — the efficiency ratio (costs as a share of revenue) was 63% in Q3 2026, from 65% a year earlier and 60% in Q2 — and the capital plan: on the Q3 call he said the bank manages CET1 within a 10% to 10.5% range.
Key Institutional Investors

The moat is funding, and it is a real one. Roughly $1.48 trillion of average deposits — a large share of it in operating and primary-checking accounts that reprice slowly and leave slowly — gives Wells Fargo a cost of funds that non-bank lenders, fintechs and most regional banks structurally cannot match. That advantage is reinforced by regulation: as a US G-SIB, Wells Fargo operates behind capital, liquidity and stress-testing requirements that are punishing to comply with but nearly impossible for a new entrant to replicate — the regulatory burden is simultaneously the biggest cost and the deepest barrier. Layered on top are switching costs that are genuinely high in the businesses that matter: middle-market treasury-management relationships, where payments plumbing is embedded in a client's operations, and primary-checking households, where direct deposit and bill pay create inertia measured in years. What Wells Fargo does not have is a proprietary technology edge, a differentiated product, or a fee franchise on JPMorgan's scale. This is a distribution and balance-sheet business, and the thesis is that a distribution and balance-sheet business finally allowed to grow its balance sheet should out-earn its recent past.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Regulatory and operational-risk track record. The asset cap is gone, but the institution that earned it is the same institution. Wells Fargo resolved 13 consent orders between 2019 and June 2025, yet other provisions of the Fed's 2018 order stayed in place when the cap was lifted, and the firm continues to carry elevated compliance and remediation spending inside its expense base. A new supervisory action — in any business, from sales practices to anti-money-laundering to fair lending — would not just cost money; it would reset the credibility that took Scharf seven years to rebuild, and investors would immediately reprice the growth story.

2. Net interest income sensitivity to the rate path. Roughly 57% of revenue is net interest income, and part of the bank's assets carry floating rates. That helped in 2022–23 and hurts on the way down: each Fed cut reprices those yields quickly while deposit costs — especially in wealth sweep and commercial operating accounts — grind lower only with a lag. Q3 2026 NII of $12.5B (+5%) was driven, per the release, by higher loan and investment securities balances, balance-sheet growth in Markets and fixed-rate asset repricing, partly offset by lower rates on floating-rate assets and higher deposit costs; the net interest margin was 2.43%, and volume growth requires capital.

3. Thin fee diversification and a lost mortgage franchise. The strategic retreat from home lending removed a business that once produced double-digit origination share and a large servicing book. Compared with JPMorgan (payments, asset management, card) or Morgan Stanley (wealth), Wells Fargo's fee mix is narrower, and its wealth business earns a materially lower margin. If capital-markets revenue falls back from what looks like peak-cycle activity, there is less non-cyclical fee income to cushion it.

4. Commercial real estate concentration. Wells Fargo has historically been among the largest US CRE lenders, with an office exposure that has already required reserve building. The allowance for credit losses stands at $14.5B, or 1.38% of loans, down from 1.52% a year ago — a ratio decline that flatters current provisions. If office valuations or non-office CRE deteriorate from here, the reserve build required would come straight out of an EPS line that consensus has running to $7.19 for 2026.

Five-year trajectory. Wells Fargo went from $51.16 at the October 2021 month-end to $82.75 on October 15, 2026 — a price return of about +62% (month-end close to the banner-date close; dividends not included). That is a solid five years, and it was earned in an unusual shape: almost nothing happened for the first two and a half years, and then everything happened at once. The stock spent 2022 and 2023 chopping between $37 and $54 while the asset cap capped the earnings power and the March 2023 regional-bank crisis briefly repriced every deposit franchise in America. The move began in earnest in October 2024, accelerated through the November 2024 election on deregulation expectations, and peaked at a monthly close of $93.20 in December 2025, six months after the Federal Reserve lifted the asset cap. 2026 has been a give-back year: the stock is down about 11.2% year-to-date against an S&P 500 up roughly 13.2% (SPY), and it now sits about 15% below its 52-week high of $97.76 (intraday, Jan 5, 2026) even as ROTCE was 16.3% in Q3 and 17.7% in Q2.

Relative performance. Over the five years from the October 2021 month-end, WFC's price return of about +62% trailed the S&P 500's roughly +68% (SPY price return, $459.25 to $771.79; same dates, dividends excluded for both). Year-to-date WFC is −11.2% against SPY's +13.2%. The setup today is the mirror image of 2024: then, the operating results were poor and the multiple was pricing a fix; now earnings per share are rising (TTM $7.09 against $6.26 for FY2025) and the multiple has compressed to 11.7x trailing earnings.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Wells Fargo is the only large US bank whose earnings power was artificially suppressed for seven years and has just been released. The first year of unconstrained operation delivered 11% loan growth, 19% CIB loan growth, ROTCE of 16.3% in Q3 (17.7% in Q2) and an efficiency ratio of 63% (60% in Q2) — while the stock de-rated to 11.5x forward earnings, well below the S&P 500 column's multiple. If ROTCE holds in the mid-to-high teens, a bank compounding tangible book value while shrinking its share count about 4.7% a year at 1.86x tangible book does not stay at 11.5x for long. The $97.48 average target implies +17.8%, and even the lowest published target ($88) sits above today's price.

Bear Case

The market may be right to discount it. CET1 has fallen from 11.0% to 10.0% in a year — the bottom of management's 10% to 10.5% range — so the growth is being funded out of the capital cushion, and it cannot run alongside $2.5B-a-quarter buybacks indefinitely. The allowance ratio dropped 14bp to 1.38% of loans while provision expense rose 51% YoY to $1.03B, against a CRE book that is among the largest in the country. Roughly 57% of revenue is rate-dependent NII in a cutting cycle, and revenue fell 3% from Q2 and missed forecasts, with markets revenue down 17% on the quarter as rate swings hit fixed-income valuations and client activity. Q3 EPS also included a $170M discrete tax benefit worth about 6 cents (our arithmetic), and eight analysts rate the stock Hold.

01
Capital constraint replaces the regulatory constraint. CET1 of 10.0% against a regulatory minimum plus buffers of 8.5% (per the CFO) leaves a 1.5-point cushion and sits at the bottom of the 10% to 10.5% range management says it manages to. Continued double-digit loan growth plus buybacks of $2.5B a quarter pulls the ratio down; the 2027 stress test will help set the balance. Period-end shares fell about 4.7% in a year (Q3 supplement), so a buyback slowdown would remove most of that from per-share growth.
02
Credit normalization, concentrated in commercial real estate. Net charge-offs were $983M in Q3 (0.38% of loans annualized, from 0.40% a year ago) and the reserve ratio is declining, a late-cycle pattern. Wells Fargo's CRE and office exposure is among the largest in US banking. A rise in the allowance to 1.6% of the $1.05T period-end loan book would require roughly $2.3B of extra reserves — about $0.60 a share after tax at a 21% rate (our arithmetic).
03
A new regulatory action. The asset cap took seven years and an entirely rebuilt management team to remove. The market has only just stopped applying a governance discount. Any new consent order, supervisory finding or enforcement action would re-impose that discount immediately, and given this institution's history the market would assume the worst before waiting for details.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters Most Right Now
The Post-Asset-Cap Growth Runway
EMERGING TAILWIND
In February 2018 the Federal Reserve capped Wells Fargo's total assets at roughly $1.95 trillion in response to the sales-practices scandal — an unprecedented growth prohibition that lasted seven years and, by most estimates, cost the bank billions in forgone revenue while competitors compounded. The Fed lifted it in June 2025. The effect is now measurable: average loans +11% YoY, deposits +11% and CIB loans +19% in Q3 (markets revenue was down 1% YoY in Q3 after rising 24% in Q2). Critically, the constraint was binding on the highest-return marginal businesses — every dollar of trading inventory competed directly with a consumer loan for space under the cap. The runway is real, but it is not free: CET1 fell from 11.0% to 10.0% financing it, and management says it manages CET1 within a 10% to 10.5% range. Wells Fargo resolved 13 consent orders between 2019 and June 2025, and other provisions of the Fed's 2018 order stayed in place when the cap was lifted; the compliance cost still embedded in the expense base is an underappreciated source of future operating leverage.
The Rate Cycle and NII Trajectory
ONGOING HEADWIND
Net interest income of $12.5B in Q3 2026 (+5%) is about 57% of revenue; the release credits higher loan and securities balances, balance-sheet growth in Markets and fixed-rate asset repricing, and the net interest margin was 2.43%. Management guides to about $50B of NII for 2026 (plus or minus) and about $13B in Q4 with a stable margin. That is the crux of the 2026 de-rating: the market will pay a growth multiple for spread expansion and a value multiple for volume growth funded by capital. Floating-rate assets reprice quickly, which helps when rates rise and hurts when they fall (the release lists lower rates on floating-rate assets as a drag on Q3 NII); deposit costs, particularly in wealth sweep and commercial operating accounts, lag on the way down. Meanwhile noninterest income fell 1% in Q3, as higher investment advisory, deposit-related, investment banking and card fees were more than offset by lower trading gains and lower lease income after the first-quarter 2026 sale of the rail car leasing business (per the release). The honest read: NII growth from here is a function of how much balance sheet the CET1 ratio permits, not of the rate environment doing the bank any favors.
Credit Normalization & CRE — Bull vs Bear
ONGOING HEADWIND
Credit metrics look excellent and that is precisely why they deserve scrutiny. Net charge-offs of $983M rose 3% YoY, nonperforming assets dropped $585M from Q2 to $7.36B, and the allowance for credit losses declined to 1.38% of loans from 1.52% — on a loan book that grew 11%. Bull framing: the mix shift is genuine. Growth is concentrated in CIB and commercial lending with structurally lower loss content than the consumer and mortgage assets Wells Fargo shed, so a lower allowance ratio is the correct answer, and provision expense should stay benign while unemployment holds. Bear framing: reserve ratios that decline into a growing loan book are the textbook late-cycle setup, Wells Fargo carries one of the largest US commercial real estate books with unresolved office exposure, and a rise in the allowance to 1.6% of loans would cost roughly $2.3B pre-tax — about $0.60 a share after tax, or about 8% of the year's consensus EPS (our arithmetic). Both readings are defensible from the same disclosure, and the sell side is split: eight analysts rate it Hold while fourteen rate it Strong Buy.
