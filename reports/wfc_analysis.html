01 Company Overview

Wells Fargo is the fourth-largest bank in the United States by assets and the third-largest by domestic deposits, running roughly $1.47 trillion of average deposits and $1.03 trillion of average loans across consumer banking, commercial banking, corporate and investment banking, and wealth management. Full-year 2025 revenue was approximately $83.7B, up about 2.5% YoY, on net income of $20.3B and diluted EPS of $6.26. The far more important number is the current one: Q2 2026 revenue of $22.6B (+9% YoY) and EPS of $2.00 (+25%), the first clean quarter to show what the franchise looks like with the Federal Reserve's seven-year asset cap finally gone — average loans grew 12% and deposits 10%, growth rates this bank was legally prohibited from producing between February 2018 and June 2025.

The stock is the odd one out among the money-center banks in 2026: at $88.93 it is down roughly 4.6% year-to-date against an S&P 500 up about 13%, despite posting the sector's cleanest earnings inflection. The market spent 2024 and 2025 paying up for the asset-cap-removal story; 2026 has been the year of asking what the removal actually earns, and whether a CET1 ratio that has fallen from 11.1% to 10.3% is the price of admission.

Notable Executives & Holders
Chief Executive Officer
Charlie Scharf
CEO since October 2019, recruited from BNY Mellon (and before that Visa and JPMorgan) explicitly to fix the regulatory mess. He closed more than a dozen consent orders, cut headcount by roughly a quarter, exited correspondent mortgage, and delivered the asset-cap removal in June 2025 — the defining achievement of his tenure. His current framing is deliberately restrained: "selective about how much and where to grow."
Chief Financial Officer
Mike Santomassimo
CFO since October 2020, also from BNY Mellon and previously CFO of JPMorgan's corporate and investment bank. He owns the expense narrative — the efficiency ratio has come from the mid-70s at the trough to 60% in Q2 2026 — and the capital plan now redeploying the balance sheet into loans, trading assets, and buybacks.
Key Institutional Investors
Vanguard · BlackRock
Institutional ownership is roughly 76%, led by Vanguard (~9–10%), BlackRock (~7–8%) and State Street (~4–5%) — index money, not conviction money. Worth flagging: Berkshire Hathaway fully exited in Q1 2022 after more than three decades as a cornerstone holder. Buffett's exit removed the single most-cited bull anchor and has never been reversed.

The moat is funding, and it is a real one. Roughly $1.47 trillion of average deposits — a large share of it in operating and primary-checking accounts that reprice slowly and leave slowly — gives Wells Fargo a cost of funds that non-bank lenders, fintechs and most regional banks structurally cannot match. That advantage is reinforced by regulation: as a US G-SIB, Wells Fargo operates behind capital, liquidity and stress-testing requirements that are punishing to comply with but nearly impossible for a new entrant to replicate — the regulatory burden is simultaneously the biggest cost and the deepest barrier. Layered on top are switching costs that are genuinely high in the businesses that matter: middle-market treasury-management relationships, where payments plumbing is embedded in a client's operations, and primary-checking households, where direct deposit and bill pay create inertia measured in years. What Wells Fargo does not have is a proprietary technology edge, a differentiated product, or a fee franchise on JPMorgan's scale. This is a distribution and balance-sheet business, and the thesis is that a distribution and balance-sheet business finally allowed to grow its balance sheet should out-earn its recent past.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Regulatory and operational-risk track record. The asset cap is gone, but the institution that earned it is the same institution. Wells Fargo has closed more than a dozen consent orders since 2019, yet a handful of outstanding regulatory agreements remain and the firm continues to carry elevated compliance and remediation spending inside its expense base. A new supervisory action — in any business, from sales practices to anti-money-laundering to fair lending — would not just cost money; it would reset the credibility that took Scharf seven years to rebuild, and the market would immediately re-underwrite the growth story.

2. Net interest income sensitivity to the rate path. Roughly 54% of revenue is net interest income, and Wells Fargo's asset mix is more floating-rate and shorter-duration than Bank of America's. That helped enormously in 2022–23 and hurts on the way down: each Fed cut reprices loan yields quickly while deposit costs — especially in wealth sweep and commercial operating accounts — grind lower only with a lag. Q2 2026 NII of $12.3B (+5%) is being driven by volume, not spread, and volume growth requires capital.

3. Thin fee diversification and a lost mortgage franchise. The strategic retreat from home lending removed a business that once produced double-digit origination share and a large servicing book. Compared with JPMorgan (payments, asset management, card) or Morgan Stanley (wealth), Wells Fargo's fee mix is narrower, and its wealth business earns a materially lower margin. If capital-markets revenue mean-reverts from what looks like cycle-peak activity, there is less non-cyclical fee income to cushion it.

4. Commercial real estate concentration. Wells Fargo has historically been among the largest US CRE lenders, with an office exposure that has already required reserve building. The allowance for credit losses stands at $14.4B, or 1.40% of loans, down from 1.58% a year ago — a ratio decline that flatters current provisions. If office valuations or non-office CRE deteriorate from here, the reserve build required would come straight out of an EPS line that consensus has running to $7.20.

Notable One-Off Items: Q2 2026 is a relatively clean quarter, but three distortions deserve flagging. First, reserve ratio release: the allowance for credit losses fell to 1.40% of loans from 1.58% a year ago while the loan book grew 12%. Provision of $914M was actually below net charge-offs of $876M plus the reserve build a 12% larger book would ordinarily require — the mix shift toward lower-loss commercial and CIB lending is real, but it means provision expense is currently a tailwind to EPS rather than a neutral. Second, severance and restructuring: management cited a 7% headcount reduction, and Wells Fargo has run recurring severance charges through the expense line for several years now, which makes the 2% expense growth figure a mix of genuine efficiency and a shrinking one-off base — the "core" expense trend is likely a touch worse than reported. Third, consent-order remediation and operating losses: the compliance build-out that consumed billions between 2019 and 2024 is winding down, and its runoff is an underappreciated structural tailwind to the efficiency ratio, but the pace of that runoff is not separately disclosed. Historically the comparisons also carry the FDIC special assessment (~$1.9B pre-tax in Q4 2023 plus a top-up in 2024) and securities-repositioning losses taken when low-yield bonds were sold to reinvest at higher rates. Bottom line: reported Q2 2026 EPS of $2.00 is probably $0.10–0.15 better than a fully normalized number once provision normalization is assumed — verify against the 10-Q before relying on it.

Five-year trajectory. Wells Fargo went from $45.70 in August 2021 to $88.93 on August 12, 2026 — a price return of about +95%, or roughly +118% including dividends (about 16.8% annualized). That is a strong five years, and it was earned in an unusual shape: almost nothing happened for the first two and a half years, and then everything happened at once. The stock spent 2022 and 2023 chopping between $37 and $54 while the asset cap capped the earnings power and the March 2023 regional-bank crisis briefly repriced every deposit franchise in America. The move began in earnest in October 2024, accelerated through the November 2024 election on deregulation expectations, and peaked at a monthly close of $93.20 in December 2025, six months after the Federal Reserve lifted the asset cap. 2026 has been a give-back year: the stock is down about 4.6% year-to-date against an S&P 500 up roughly 13%, and it now sits about 9% below its 52-week high of $97.76 even as ROTCE reached a cycle-best 17.7%.

Relative performance. Over the full five years WFC's roughly +118% total return comfortably beat the S&P 500's approximately +82% (index levels here are approximate — verify against an index source before relying on the exact figure), and it beat Bank of America's ~+75% over the same window. The picture inverts over shorter horizons: year-to-date WFC is −4.6% against the index's +13.3%, one of the worst showings among the large banks in a year when BAC is up 19%. Against the KBW Bank Index, Wells Fargo led from late 2024 through the asset-cap announcement and has lagged since December 2025 — the classic pattern of a stock that pre-traded its catalyst. The setup today is the mirror image of 2024: then, the operating results were poor and the multiple was pricing a fix; now the operating results are the best in a decade and the multiple has compressed back toward the sector floor.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Wells Fargo is the only large US bank whose earnings power was artificially suppressed for seven years and has just been released. The first full year of unconstrained operation delivered 12% loan growth, 26% CIB loan growth, 24% markets growth, ROTCE of 17.7% and an efficiency ratio down to 60% — while the stock de-rated to 12.3x forward earnings, a discount to peers and less than half the index multiple. If ROTCE holds in the mid-to-high teens, a bank compounding tangible book value while retiring 4–5% of its shares a year at 1.9x tangible book does not stay at 12x for long. Consensus $100 average target implies +12.5%, and even the lowest published target sits above today's price.

Bear Case

The market may be right to discount it. CET1 has fallen from 11.1% to 10.3% in a year — the growth is being funded out of the capital cushion, and it cannot run alongside a $14B/yr buyback indefinitely. The allowance ratio dropped 18bp to 1.40% of loans, meaning provision expense is currently flattering EPS rather than normalizing it, against a CRE book that is among the largest in the country. Roughly 54% of revenue is rate-dependent NII in a cutting cycle, and a meaningful slice of the growth came from capital-markets and trading revenue running at cycle-peak levels. Strip out peak markets revenue and sub-normal provisions and the "clean" earnings number is materially below $7.20 — which is exactly what ten hold ratings are saying.

01
Capital constraint replaces the regulatory constraint. CET1 of 10.3% against roughly a 9.5–10% requirement leaves a thin buffer. Continued 12% loan growth plus $14B of annual buybacks is arithmetically incompatible with a stable capital ratio; something gives, and the 2027 stress test will determine which. A forced buyback slowdown would remove 4–5 points of annual EPS growth.
02
Credit normalization, concentrated in commercial real estate. Net charge-offs of $876M are falling and the reserve ratio is declining, both of which are late-cycle behaviors. Wells Fargo's CRE and office exposure is among the largest in US banking. A normalization of the allowance back toward 1.6% of a $1.03T loan book would require roughly $2B of incremental reserve build — about $0.50 of EPS.
03
A new regulatory action. The asset cap took seven years and an entirely rebuilt management team to remove. The market has only just stopped applying a governance discount. Any new consent order, supervisory finding or enforcement action would re-impose that discount immediately, and given this institution's history the market would assume the worst before waiting for details.
  Industry Commentary — What Matters Most Right Now
The Post-Asset-Cap Growth Runway
EMERGING TAILWIND
In February 2018 the Federal Reserve capped Wells Fargo's total assets at roughly $1.95 trillion in response to the sales-practices scandal — an unprecedented growth prohibition that lasted seven years and, by most estimates, cost the bank billions in forgone revenue while competitors compounded. The Fed lifted it in June 2025. The effect is now measurable: average loans +12% YoY, deposits +10%, CIB loans +26%, markets revenue +24% with equities +64%, and trading assets expanding for the first time in years. Critically, the constraint was binding on the highest-return marginal businesses — every dollar of trading inventory competed directly with a consumer loan for space under the cap. The runway is real, but it is not free: CET1 fell from 11.1% to 10.3% financing it, and management is explicitly rationing it ("selective about how much and where to grow"). More than a dozen consent orders have been terminated since 2019; a handful of regulatory agreements remain outstanding, and the compliance cost still embedded in the expense base is an underappreciated source of future operating leverage.
The Rate Cycle and NII Trajectory
ONGOING HEADWIND
Net interest income of $12.3B in Q2 2026 (+5%) is about 54% of revenue, and the entire increase came from volume — average earning assets grew roughly 10% while NIM (estimated at ~2.75%) was flat-to-lower. That is the crux of the 2026 de-rating: the market will pay a growth multiple for spread expansion and a value multiple for volume growth funded by capital. Wells Fargo's asset mix reprices faster than Bank of America's, which makes it a relative winner when rates rise and a relative loser when they fall; deposit costs, particularly in wealth sweep and commercial operating accounts, lag on the way down. Meanwhile noninterest income (+13%) is doing more of the work, but a meaningful share of that is investment banking fees (+36%) and trading — cyclical revenue that no one should extrapolate. The honest read: NII growth from here is a function of how much balance sheet the CET1 ratio permits, not of the rate environment doing the bank any favors.
Credit Normalization & CRE — Bull vs Bear
ONGOING HEADWIND
Credit metrics look excellent and that is precisely why they deserve scrutiny. Net charge-offs of $876M fell 11% YoY, nonperforming assets dropped $824M sequentially, and the allowance for credit losses declined to 1.40% of loans from 1.58% — on a loan book that grew 12%. Bull framing: the mix shift is genuine. Growth is concentrated in CIB and commercial lending with structurally lower loss content than the consumer and mortgage assets Wells Fargo shed, so a lower allowance ratio is the correct answer, and provision expense should stay benign while unemployment holds. Bear framing: reserve ratios that decline into a growing loan book are the textbook late-cycle setup, Wells Fargo carries one of the largest US commercial real estate books with unresolved office exposure, and a reversion of the allowance to 1.6% would cost roughly $2B pre-tax — about $0.50 of EPS, or seven percent of the year. Both readings are defensible from the same disclosure, which is why ten analysts are sitting on hold ratings while thirteen call it a strong buy.
