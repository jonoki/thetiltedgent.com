01 Company Overview

Waste Management, Inc. — branded simply WM — is North America's largest environmental services company, collecting, transferring, disposing of, recycling and monetizing solid waste, and, since November 2024, regulated medical waste and secure information destruction. FY2025 revenue was $25.204 billion, up 14.2% YoY, though the bulk of that growth was inorganic: the $7.2 billion Stericycle acquisition added a WM Healthcare Solutions line of business that contributed $2.508 billion in 2025 against roughly two months of contribution in 2024. Organic growth is slower and steadier — trailing-twelve-month (TTM) revenue of $25.67 billion is up 7.2%.

The company ranks #138 among US companies and #270 globally by market capitalization at roughly $90 billion. What distinguishes WM from almost every other industrial of its size is not growth but the nature of its asset base: a network of permitted landfills that, as a practical matter, cannot be replicated. FY2025 adjusted operating EBITDA was $7.582 billion at a 30.1% margin — the first time WM has crossed 30% — and management guides FY2026 to $8.15–$8.25 billion at a 31.0%–31.2% margin.

Revenue Mix
Landfill is 15% of revenue and close to 100% of the investment case. WM operates the largest network of active landfills in North America, and those permits are effectively un-reproducible: siting a new landfill in the United States requires state and often federal environmental approval, hydrogeological studies, decades-long liability acceptance, and — the binding constraint — local political consent that almost never arrives. New greenfield landfill permits in populated US markets are, in practice, close to nonexistent. The consequence is that disposal capacity is a fixed, depleting, geographically fragmented resource that only shrinks.

Because trash is heavy and transport economics are brutal, a landfill's pricing power is local and near-absolute within its haul radius. Every competing hauler that lacks a landfill in a given market must bring its tonnage to WM and pay a tipping fee — which is why WM's landfill business generates revenue from its own competitors. Q2 2026 MSW yield of +5.2% against a roughly flat volume backdrop is the quantitative signature of that power: price rising well above inflation with no volume incentive required. It also anchors the entire vertical: internalizing collected waste into WM-owned disposal captures the disposal margin instead of paying it away.
Industry Size: Landfill disposal is a capacity-constrained market with negative supply growth. WM landfill revenue $3.78B FY25; yields running ~5%+ (company-reported) versus low-single-digit volume.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Unpermittable Landfill Base Local Disposal Monopolies Route Density Vertical Internalization Regulatory Barriers Owned Methane Feedstock Contractual Price Escalators

The whole investment case is one sentence: you cannot build a new landfill in the United States. Not "it is expensive" — it is, functionally, not permittable. Siting requires multi-year hydrogeological and environmental review, state permitting, acceptance of perpetual post-closure liability, and, decisively, local political approval that no municipality grants. The practical result is that the stock of US disposal capacity is fixed and depleting: every ton buried is capacity permanently consumed, and no meaningful greenfield supply replaces it. In economic terms, WM owns a portfolio of local natural monopolies in a market where the supply curve is vertical and sloping backward over time.

This produces two effects that show up directly in the numbers. First, structural pricing power: WM raised core price 6.3% in FY2025 and 5.7% in Q2 2026, and guides 5.4%–5.8% for FY2026 — pricing well above CPI, sustained for years, with volume roughly flat. A business that can raise price 5–6% annually while volumes go nowhere and still expand margin (30.1% FY2025 adjusted operating EBITDA margin, guided to 31.0%–31.2% in FY2026) is not competing on price; it is administering it. Second, competitors must pay WM. Waste is heavy and cheap per ton, so haul economics collapse beyond roughly a 50–75 mile radius. Any hauler operating in a market where WM owns the landfill and they do not must tip their tonnage at a WM site and pay a WM-set gate fee. WM literally earns revenue on its rivals' collection volumes. That, in turn, is why internalization matters so much: routing WM-collected waste into WM-owned disposal captures both margins rather than paying one away, and it is the reason vertical integration is the industry's organizing principle.

Layered on top: route density (the marginal stop on an existing route is close to free, which is why regional scale compounds), the sustainability assets that convert an unavoidable liability — landfill methane — into a saleable product using feedstock WM already owns at zero marginal cost, and now a national medical-waste network with its own regulatory permitting barriers. The market prices this accordingly. WM trades at 31.8x trailing earnings and 14.5x EV/EBITDA versus an S&P 500 P/E near 29.7 — a premium multiple for a business with a 0.44 beta and 3.0% top-line organic growth. The multiple is paid for asset scarcity, not growth.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Industrial and construction volume sensitivity. The moat protects price, not volume. Industrial collection ($3.106B in FY2025) is roll-off container work tied directly to construction starts, demolition and manufacturing output; when those slow, containers sit idle. Q2 2026 collection-and-disposal volume was −1.8% headline and −0.4% excluding the prior-year wildfire comparison, and WM trimmed its FY2026 revenue guidance to $26.275–$26.475 billion from the $26.425–$26.625 billion set in January. Price has been more than covering it, but a genuine industrial recession would test whether 5%+ core price survives when customers start shopping.

2. Balance sheet leverage from Stericycle. The $7.2 billion deal was cash-funded with debt. Total debt stood at $23.356 billion at June 30, 2026 against $557 million of cash; debt-to-equity is 2.35x versus roughly 1.2x for Republic Services and Waste Connections. Net debt of ~$22.8 billion against FY2026 guided adjusted operating EBITDA of ~$8.2 billion implies roughly 2.8x net leverage (derived) — inside management's stated 2.75x–3.0x target and down from the ~3.4x at close, but WM is now the most levered of the large-cap peers, and that constrains M&A and buyback flexibility if rates or EBITDA move the wrong way.

3. Commodity and policy exposure in the growth segment. The sustainability business is the growth story, and it is the one part of WM with no pricing power at all. Recycled commodity prices fell to $75/ton in Q2 2026 from $84/ton; RIN credit prices fell to $2.33 from $2.53. Those are exogenous, policy-driven and volatile. WM has re-contracted much recycling volume onto fee-for-service structures to mute the swing, but the guided $235–255 million of FY2026 sustainability EBITDA growth and the "approaching $1 billion by 2027" ambition are explicitly conditioned on current commodity prices holding. A change in the federal Renewable Fuel Standard, or a demand collapse in recycled fibre and plastics, would hit the segment thesis directly.

4. Healthcare Solutions execution and top-line stagnation. Margin recovery at Stericycle has been genuinely impressive — 1.0% to 13.5% operating EBITDA margin in one year, 19.0% adjusted in Q2 2026 — but revenue went backwards 1.2% YoY in Q2 2026 to $638 million. Cost-out is finite; at some point the segment must grow. Secure information destruction in particular faces secular decline as enterprises digitize paper records, and medical waste pricing is negotiated against consolidated hospital systems with real purchasing leverage. Integration costs are still running ($24 million pre-tax in Q2 2026 alone), and the gap between FY2025 GAAP EPS of $6.70 and adjusted EPS of $7.50 is a reminder that the accounting cost of this deal is not yet behind the company.

Notable One-Off Items

Stericycle acquisition and integration costs — material and ongoing. Q2 2026 carried $24 million pre-tax ($18 million after-tax) of Stericycle integration costs, $14 million of which was charged to the Healthcare Solutions segment. A further $13 million pre-tax ($10 million after-tax) of asset impairments and other unusual items hit the quarter. The cumulative effect is visible in the GAAP-to-adjusted spread: FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS of $6.70 versus adjusted $7.50, and Q2 2026 GAAP $1.95 versus adjusted $2.02. Anyone valuing WM on trailing GAAP earnings is over-penalizing the underlying business; anyone valuing it purely on adjusted figures is ignoring costs that have now run for seven quarters.

Recycling commodity and RIN price swings. Two exogenous prices move segment results independently of operations: average recycled commodity prices of $75/ton in Q2 2026 versus $84/ton a year earlier (−10.7%), and RNG credit (RIN) prices of $2.33 versus $2.53 (−7.9%). Both were headwinds, and recycling plus renewable energy EBITDA still grew 32.5% — a useful signal that the growth is structural rather than price-driven, but also a reminder that these lines can swing several hundred million dollars of EBITDA in either direction on prices WM does not set.

Prior-year wildfire cleanup comparison. California wildfire remediation work contributed roughly 50 basis points of volume in the prior-year period. Its absence made Q2 2026 collection-and-disposal volume look far worse than it was: −1.8% headline versus −0.4% excluding wildfire, with landfill volumes actually up 1.7%. This distortion also sits inside FY2026 guidance, which explicitly embeds a 50bp wildfire headwind.

Fuel, litigation and environmental remediation. WM's fleet makes diesel and natural gas prices a recurring cost variable, partially offset by fuel surcharges and by the company's own CNG/RNG production. As a landfill owner WM also carries perpetual post-closure environmental remediation and monitoring liabilities, and periodic environmental litigation, which appear in the "asset impairments and other" line rather than as a separately quantified item in the quarterly release. Neither was flagged as individually material in Q2 2026 beyond the $13 million noted above.

Guidance revision. With Q2 2026 results, WM lowered FY2026 revenue guidance to $26.275–$26.475 billion (from $26.425–$26.625 billion in January) while raising the adjusted operating EBITDA margin range to 31.0%–31.2% (from 30.8%–31.0%) and holding EBITDA at $8.15–$8.25 billion and free cash flow at $3.75–$3.85 billion. Less revenue, more margin — the price-over-volume strategy stated in numbers.

Five-year trajectory. WM closed September 2021 at $149.36 and closed at $225.08 on Aug 19, 2026 — a price return of +50.7% over the 60-month window, or roughly 8.6% annualized. Adding dividends (currently $3.78/share, ~1.7% yield and growing at a 12.4% clip, 22 consecutive years of increases) lifts total return to approximately +62% to +65% (estimated — dividend reinvestment not precisely computed). Over the identical window the S&P 500, proxied by SPY, went from $429.14 to $769.06, a price return of +79.2%. WM therefore underperformed the index by roughly 28 percentage points on price over five years — but it did so with a beta of 0.44, meaning risk-adjusted the gap largely closes. This is the classic profile of a defensive compounder in an era dominated by mega-cap technology: it did not lose you money, it just was not the trade.

The shape matters more than the total. The stock spent 2022 dead flat, re-rated violently through 2023–early 2024 as investors chased quality after the regional banking scare, peaked around $240 in mid-2025, then went nowhere for eighteen months: the 52-week total change is +0.39%. That is the story of a business whose earnings kept compounding while its multiple slowly compressed from a peak. At $225.08 the stock trades 9.3% below its 52-week high of $248.13 and 16.0% above the low of $194.11, with the 6-period monthly RSI in neutral territory — neither stretched nor washed out.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

WM owns an asset class that cannot be recreated. US landfill capacity is fixed and depleting, new permits are effectively unobtainable, and haul economics make each site a local monopoly — which is why WM raised core price 6.3% in FY2025 and 5.7% in Q2 2026 with flat volumes and still expanded adjusted operating EBITDA margin past 30% for the first time. Competitors without disposal assets must pay WM to bury their waste. On top of that annuity, two genuinely new profit pools are compounding: Healthcare Solutions margins went 1.0% → 13.5% → 19.0% adjusted in six quarters against a committed synergy target of only "$125M+", and sustainability EBITDA is guided to grow $235–255M in 2026 toward ~$1B by 2027 from a ~$300M 2023 base. Free cash flow is guided to $3.75–3.85B, funding ~$1.5B of dividends (22 straight annual increases, +12.4% for 2026) and ~$2B of buybacks.

Bear Case

You are paying 31.8x trailing and 26.4x forward earnings — a premium to the S&P 500's 29.7x — for a business growing organic revenue at 3–4% and whose FY2026 revenue guidance was just cut. Volume is negative (−1.8% headline, −0.4% ex-wildfire) and the entire earnings story is price plus cost-out, both of which have finite runway. The Stericycle deal left $23.36 billion of debt and a 2.35x debt-to-equity ratio versus ~1.2x at Republic and Waste Connections, constraining flexibility, while the acquired business shrank 1.2% YoY last quarter and secure information destruction faces secular paper decline. The growth segment that the bull case leans on — recycling and RNG — has zero pricing power: commodity prices fell 10.7% and RIN credits 7.9% year over year, and the "$1B by 2027" target is explicitly conditioned on prices holding. The stock is up 0.39% over twelve months for a reason.

Top 3 Risks
01
Industrial and construction volume contraction. Industrial roll-off collection ($3.106B, 12.3% of FY2025 revenue) is directly geared to construction starts and manufacturing output, and residential/commercial volumes soften with employment. The moat defends price, not tonnage — a real industrial recession would pressure volume, incremental margin and, critically, the credibility of 5%+ annual price increases.
02
Leverage and multiple compression together. With $23.36B of total debt, ~2.8x net leverage (derived) and a 2.35x debt-to-equity ratio, WM has the least balance-sheet slack of the large-cap peers. A stock trading at 31.8x earnings with a 0.39% twelve-month return is already showing multiple fatigue; a rate shock, an EBITDA miss, or a credit-driven pullback in buybacks could compress the multiple faster than earnings grow.
03
Sustainability segment policy and commodity dependence. The growth narrative rests on RNG and recycling economics WM does not control. RIN credit prices ($2.33 vs $2.53) and recycled commodity prices ($75/ton vs $84/ton) both fell year over year, and the path to ~$1B of sustainability EBITDA by 2027 is explicitly conditioned on current prices. Changes to the federal Renewable Fuel Standard or a fibre/plastics demand slump would strand a meaningful part of the ~$200M/year of sustainability growth capital.
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Drives This Stock
Pricing Power from Irreplaceable Landfill Assets
STRUCTURAL ADVANTAGE
This is the single most important fact about the waste industry and it is not cyclical, not competitive and not fixable by rivals: the United States has effectively stopped permitting new landfills. Environmental review, hydrogeology, perpetual post-closure liability and — above all — local political opposition make greenfield siting practically impossible in populated markets. Existing capacity is therefore a fixed, depleting resource, and because waste is heavy and low-value per ton, transport costs make each landfill a local monopoly within roughly a 50–75 mile radius. The quantitative signature: WM raised core price 6.3% in FY2025, guides 5.4%–5.8% for FY2026, printed +5.2% MSW yield in Q2 2026, and did all of it with volumes roughly flat — pricing at 2–3x CPI with no volume concession. Competing haulers without disposal assets pay WM tipping fees, meaning WM earns revenue on its own competitors' collection volumes. This is why the stock earns a premium multiple despite low-single-digit organic growth.
Industrial & Construction Volume Sensitivity
ONGOING HEADWIND
Volume is where the moat stops working. Q2 2026 collection-and-disposal volume was −1.8% headline; excluding the prior-year California wildfire cleanup comparison it was −0.4%, with landfill volume +1.7% and transfer −1.1%. Industrial roll-off — $3.106 billion, 12.3% of FY2025 revenue — is the most cyclical line, tied to construction starts and demolition activity. Management responded to softness by cutting FY2026 revenue guidance to $26.275–$26.475 billion from $26.425–$26.625 billion while simultaneously raising the margin range to 31.0%–31.2%. That is the trade being made explicitly: give up marginal tonnage, keep price. It works while the economy muddles along. It gets tested if construction genuinely rolls over, because 5%+ price increases are far harder to push through customers who are themselves shrinking.
Sustainability Capex Returns — Bull vs. Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND
The bull framing: WM is spending roughly $200 million a year of sustainability growth capital to convert a liability into a product. Landfill methane is a mandatory-capture waste stream WM already owns at zero marginal feedstock cost; RNG plants monetize it. Recycling automation cuts labour out of MRFs and raises bale purity. Seven RNG plants and nine recycling projects were completed in 2025; three more RNG facilities (~3.5M MMBtu run-rate) and the Denver MRF (+60,000 tons) landed in Q2 2026 alone; six RNG and four recycling projects are planned for the rest of 2026. Combined recycling and renewable energy EBITDA grew 32.5% ($39M) in Q2 2026, and FY2026 growth is guided to $235–255 million, on a path toward roughly $1 billion by 2027 versus ~$300 million in 2023.

The bear framing: every dollar of that upside is exposed to prices WM does not set. Recycled commodities averaged $75/ton versus $84/ton and RIN credits $2.33 versus $2.53 year over year — both down, and both capable of moving far more violently than that. The 2027 target is stated at "current commodity prices," which is a disclosure, not a forecast. The genuinely encouraging datapoint is that 32.5% EBITDA growth was achieved into falling prices, implying the gain is volume and cost driven. If commodity prices merely stabilize, this becomes the most attractive part of WM. If federal renewable fuel policy shifts, a material slice of the growth story is capital already spent against economics that no longer clear.
