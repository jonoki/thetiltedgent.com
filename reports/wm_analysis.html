01 Company Overview

Waste Management, Inc. — branded simply WM — is North America's largest environmental services company, collecting, transferring, disposing of, recycling and monetizing solid waste, and, since November 2024, regulated medical waste and secure information destruction. FY2025 revenue was $25.204 billion, up 14.2% YoY, though most of that growth came from an acquisition rather than the existing business: the $7.2 billion Stericycle acquisition added a WM Healthcare Solutions line of business that contributed $2.508 billion in 2025 against roughly two months of contribution in 2024. Organic growth is slower and steadier — trailing-twelve-month (TTM) revenue is $26.07 billion, and third-quarter 2026 revenue grew 6.3% from a year earlier.

The company ranks #127 in the S&P 500 by market capitalization at roughly $93 billion. What distinguishes WM from almost every other industrial of its size is not growth but the nature of its asset base: a network of permitted landfills that, as a practical matter, cannot be replicated. FY2025 adjusted operating EBITDA was $7.582 billion at a 30.1% margin — the first time WM has crossed 30% — and on Oct 27, 2026 management affirmed its FY2026 outlook for adjusted operating EBITDA of $8.15–$8.25 billion, with revenue and free cash flow now expected at the high end of their ranges. Q3 2026 adjusted operating EBITDA was $2.121 billion at a 31.0% margin.

The other news since the last edition is leadership: on Aug 26, 2026 WM said CEO Jim Fish will retire and leave the board on January 4, 2027, and the board named President John Morris to succeed him. The third quarter itself was in line rather than a surprise: adjusted EPS of $2.15 was 3 cents under a $2.18 consensus, and the shares fell 4.52% on the first session after the release before recovering part of it.

Landfill is 15% of revenue and close to 100% of the investment case. WM operates the largest network of active landfills in North America, and those permits are effectively un-reproducible: siting a new landfill in the United States requires state and often federal environmental approval, hydrogeological studies, decades-long liability acceptance, and — the binding constraint — local political consent that almost never arrives. New greenfield landfill permits in populated US markets are, in practice, close to nonexistent. The consequence is that disposal capacity is a fixed, depleting, geographically fragmented resource that only shrinks.

Because trash is heavy and transport economics are brutal, a landfill's pricing power is local and near-absolute within its haul radius. Every competing hauler that lacks a landfill in a given market must bring its tonnage to WM and pay a tipping fee — which is why WM's landfill business generates revenue from its own competitors. Q3 2026 MSW yield of +4.2% (landfill revenue $1.018 billion, up 2.3% from $995 million) against MSW volume of −1.1% is the quantitative signature of that power: revenue per ton keeps rising even as tonnage slips. It also anchors the entire vertical: internalizing collected waste into WM-owned disposal captures the disposal margin instead of paying it away.
Industry Size: Landfill disposal is a capacity-constrained market with negative supply growth. WM landfill revenue $3.78B FY25; Q3 2026 MSW yield +4.2% against MSW volume of −1.1% (company-reported).

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat
Unpermittable Landfill Base Local Disposal Monopolies Route Density Vertical Internalization Regulatory Barriers Owned Methane Feedstock Contractual Price Escalators

The whole investment case is one sentence: you cannot build a new landfill in the United States. Not "it is expensive" — it is, functionally, not permittable. Siting requires multi-year hydrogeological and environmental review, state permitting, acceptance of perpetual post-closure liability, and, decisively, local political approval that no municipality grants. The practical result is that the stock of US disposal capacity is fixed and depleting: every ton buried is capacity permanently consumed, and no meaningful greenfield supply replaces it. In economic terms, WM owns a portfolio of local natural monopolies in a market where the supply curve is vertical and sloping backward over time.

This produces two effects that show up directly in the numbers. First, structural pricing power: WM raised core price 6.3% in FY2025 and 5.8% in Q3 2026, and guides 5.4%–5.8% for FY2026 — pricing well above CPI, sustained for years, with volume roughly flat. A business that can raise price 5–6% annually while volumes go nowhere and still expand margin (30.1% FY2025 adjusted operating EBITDA margin, guided to 31.0%–31.2% in FY2026) is not competing on price; it is setting it. Second, competitors must pay WM. Waste is heavy and cheap per ton, so hauling it long distances quickly eats the margin. Any hauler operating in a market where WM owns the landfill and they do not must tip their tonnage at a WM site and pay a WM-set gate fee. WM literally earns revenue on its rivals' collection volumes. That, in turn, is why internalization matters so much: routing WM-collected waste into WM-owned disposal captures both margins rather than paying one away, and it is the reason vertical integration is the industry's organizing principle.

Layered on top: route density (the marginal stop on an existing route is close to free, which is why regional scale compounds), the sustainability assets that convert an unavoidable liability — landfill methane — into a saleable product using feedstock WM already owns at zero marginal cost, and now a national medical-waste network with its own regulatory permitting barriers. The market prices this accordingly. WM trades at 30.6x trailing earnings, against 25.1x for the S&P 500 (Section 04), and at 14.7x EV/EBITDA: a higher price per dollar of earnings than the market for a business with a 0.43 beta and low-single-digit growth in its existing business. Investors pay that price for scarce assets, not for growth.

Key Vulnerabilities

1. Industrial and construction volume sensitivity. The moat protects price, not volume. Industrial collection ($3.106B in FY2025) is roll-off container work tied directly to construction starts, demolition and manufacturing output; when those slow, containers sit idle. Collection-and-disposal volume was −0.4% in Q3 2026 (−1.8% in Q2 2026, which included a prior-year wildfire cleanup comparison). WM trimmed its FY2026 revenue guidance in July to $26.275–$26.475 billion from the $26.425–$26.625 billion set in January, and on Oct 27 said it expects revenue at the high end of the trimmed range. Price has been more than covering it, but a genuine industrial recession would test whether 5%+ core price survives when customers start shopping.

2. Balance sheet leverage from Stericycle. The $7.2 billion deal was cash-funded with debt. Total debt stood at $23.37 billion at September 30, 2026 against $247 million of cash; debt-to-equity is 2.37x versus roughly 1.2x for Republic Services and Waste Connections. Net debt of ~$23.1 billion against FY2026 guided adjusted operating EBITDA of ~$8.2 billion implies net debt of roughly 2.8 times a year's EBITDA (our calculation), down from the ~3.4x WM expected shortly after closing when it announced the deal. WM remains the most levered of the large-cap peers, and that constrains M&A and buyback flexibility if rates or EBITDA move the wrong way.

3. Commodity and policy exposure in the growth segment. The sustainability business is the growth story, and it is the one part of WM with no pricing power at all. In Q3 2026 recycled commodity prices averaged about $82/ton, up from about $68 a year earlier, but the RNG credit price (RIN) fell to $2.32 from $2.56. Those prices are set by outside markets and government policy, and they swing. The guided $235–255 million of FY2026 sustainability EBITDA growth and the ambition to approach $1 billion by 2027 are explicitly conditioned on current commodity prices holding. A change in the federal Renewable Fuel Standard, or a demand collapse in recycled fibre and plastics, would hit the segment thesis directly.

4. Healthcare Solutions execution. Margin recovery at Stericycle has been genuinely impressive — 1.0% to 13.5% operating EBITDA margin in one year, 22.4% adjusted in Q3 2026 — and revenue grew again, +3.7% to $651 million, after a 1.2% dip in Q2 2026. Cost-out is finite, so the segment now has to keep growing. Secure information destruction faces a long-term decline as enterprises digitize paper records, and medical waste pricing is negotiated against consolidated hospital systems with real purchasing leverage. Integration costs are still running ($30 million pre-tax across the company in Q3 2026), and the gap between GAAP and adjusted EPS (Q3 2026: $2.05 against $2.15; FY2025: $6.70 against $7.50) is a reminder that the accounting cost of this deal is not yet behind the company.

Notable One-Off Items

Stericycle acquisition and integration costs — material and ongoing. Q3 2026 carried $30 million pre-tax of Stericycle acquisition and integration costs, $19 million of it in Healthcare Solutions and $11 million in Corporate & Other (Q3 2025: $34 million). The cumulative effect is visible in the GAAP-to-adjusted spread: Q3 2026 GAAP diluted EPS of $2.05 versus adjusted $2.15 (Q3 2025: $1.49 versus $1.98), and FY2025 GAAP $6.70 versus adjusted $7.50. Anyone valuing WM on trailing GAAP earnings is over-penalizing the underlying business; anyone valuing it purely on adjusted figures is ignoring costs that have now run for eight quarters.

Recycling commodity and RIN price swings. Two prices WM does not set move segment results independently of operations: recycled commodity prices averaged about $82/ton in Q3 2026 versus $68/ton a year earlier (+20.6%), a tailwind, while the RNG credit (RIN) price was $2.32 versus $2.56 (−9.4%), a headwind. Together Recycling Processing & Sales and Renewable Energy earned $156 million of adjusted operating EBITDA in the quarter ($91 million and $65 million). These lines can swing several hundred million dollars of EBITDA in either direction on prices WM does not set.

Accounting change in 2026. Starting in 2026 WM moved landfill accretion (the yearly build-up of the closure and post-closure liability) from operating expenses to depreciation and amortization, and restated 2025 figures for comparability. Adjusted operating EBITDA margins for 2025 in this report are on that restated basis where the Q3 release gives them. Q2 2026 collection-and-disposal volume (−1.8%) had also been distorted by the prior year's California wildfire cleanup work.

Fuel, litigation and environmental remediation. WM's fleet makes diesel and natural gas prices a recurring cost variable, partially offset by fuel surcharges and by the company's own CNG/RNG production. As a landfill owner WM also carries perpetual post-closure environmental remediation and monitoring liabilities, and periodic environmental litigation, which appear in the "asset impairments and other" line rather than as a separately quantified item in the quarterly release.

Guidance. With Q3 2026 results (Oct 27), WM affirmed its 2026 guidance ranges and said it expects revenue and free cash flow at the high end of theirs: revenue of $26.275–$26.475 billion and free cash flow of $3.75–$3.85 billion, with adjusted operating EBITDA staying at $8.15–$8.25 billion. It expects about $2 billion of share repurchases in 2026. In July it had lowered the revenue range (from $26.425–$26.625 billion) while raising the adjusted operating EBITDA margin range to 31.0%–31.2% (from 30.8%–31.0%).

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

WM owns an asset class that cannot be recreated. US landfill capacity is fixed and depleting, new permits are effectively unobtainable, and haul economics make each site a local monopoly — which is why WM raised core price 6.3% in FY2025 and 5.8% in Q3 2026 with flat volumes and still expanded adjusted operating EBITDA margin past 30% for the first time. Competitors without disposal assets must pay WM to bury their waste. On top of that steady income, two newer businesses are growing their profits: Healthcare Solutions margins went 1.0% → 13.5% → 22.4% adjusted (Q3 2026) against a synergy target of only more than $125 million, and sustainability EBITDA is guided to grow $235–255M in 2026 toward ~$1B by 2027 from a ~$300M 2023 base. Free cash flow is guided to $3.75–3.85B (the high end, per the Oct 27 release), funding ~$1.5B of dividends (22 straight annual increases, +14.5% for 2026) and ~$2B of buybacks.

Bear Case

You are paying 30.6x trailing and 28.8x forward (FY2026 consensus EPS) earnings — against 25.1x and 19.6x for the S&P 500 in Section 04 — for a business with low-single-digit organic revenue growth. FY2026 revenue guidance was trimmed in July and is now expected only at the high end of that lower range, and Q3 adjusted EPS of $2.15 came in 3 cents under consensus. Volume is negative (−0.4% in Q3) and the earnings story is price plus cost-out, both of which have finite runway. The Stericycle deal left $23.37 billion of debt and a 2.37x debt-to-equity ratio versus ~1.2x at Republic and Waste Connections, constraining flexibility, and secure information destruction faces a long-term decline in paper. The growth segment the bull case leans on — recycling and RNG — has no pricing power: RIN credits fell 9.4% year over year, recycled commodity prices (+20.6% this quarter) can fall back, and the $1B-by-2027 target is conditioned on prices holding. The shares fell 4.5% on the first session after the Q3 release, and a CEO handover is set for January 4, 2027.

Top 3 Risks
01
Industrial and construction volume contraction. Industrial roll-off collection ($3.106B, 12.3% of FY2025 revenue) is directly geared to construction starts and manufacturing output, and residential/commercial volumes soften with employment. The moat defends price, not tonnage — a real industrial recession would pressure volume, the profit on each extra ton and, critically, the credibility of 5%+ annual price increases.
02
Leverage and a falling valuation together. With $23.37B of total debt, net debt of ~2.8 times a year's EBITDA (our calculation) and a 2.37x debt-to-equity ratio, WM has the least balance-sheet slack of the large-cap peers. A stock at 30.6x trailing earnings with a 4.5% drop on the first session after the Q3 release shows how little room there is for a miss; a rate shock, an EBITDA shortfall, or a credit-driven pullback in buybacks could push the share price down faster than earnings grow.
03
Sustainability segment policy and commodity dependence. The growth narrative rests on RNG and recycling economics WM does not control. The RIN credit price fell ($2.32 vs $2.56 a year earlier) even as recycled commodity prices rose ($82/ton vs $68), and the path to ~$1B of sustainability EBITDA by 2027 is explicitly conditioned on current prices. Changes to the federal Renewable Fuel Standard or a fibre/plastics demand slump would strand a meaningful part of the sustainability growth capital ($191M in the first nine months of 2026).
  Industry Commentary — What Actually Drives This Stock
Pricing Power from Irreplaceable Landfill Assets
STRUCTURAL ADVANTAGE
This is the single most important fact about the waste industry and it is not cyclical, not competitive and not fixable by rivals: the United States has effectively stopped permitting new landfills. Environmental review, hydrogeology, perpetual post-closure liability and — above all — local political opposition make greenfield siting practically impossible in populated markets. Existing capacity is therefore a fixed, depleting resource, and because waste is heavy and low-value per ton, transport costs make each landfill a local monopoly within its haul radius. The quantitative signature: WM raised core price 6.3% in FY2025, guides 5.4%–5.8% for FY2026, printed +4.2% MSW yield in Q3 2026, and did it with collection-and-disposal volume down only 0.4%. Competing haulers without disposal assets pay WM tipping fees, meaning WM earns revenue on its own competitors' collection volumes. This is why investors pay more per dollar of WM's earnings than for the market as a whole, even though its existing business grows in the low single digits.
Industrial & Construction Volume Sensitivity
ONGOING HEADWIND
Volume is where the moat stops working. Collection-and-disposal volume was −0.4% in Q3 2026 (−0.5% adjusted for working days), after −1.8% in Q2 2026, which included a prior-year California wildfire cleanup comparison; MSW landfill volume was −1.1%. Industrial roll-off — $3.106 billion, 12.3% of FY2025 revenue — is the most cyclical line, tied to construction starts and demolition activity, and industrial collection revenue still grew 4.3% in Q3 to $843 million. In July management cut FY2026 revenue guidance to $26.275–$26.475 billion from $26.425–$26.625 billion while raising the margin range to 31.0%–31.2%; on Oct 27 it said revenue should land at the high end. That is the trade being made explicitly: give up marginal tonnage, keep price. It works while the economy muddles along. It gets tested if construction genuinely rolls over, because 5%+ price increases are far harder to push through customers who are themselves shrinking.
Sustainability Capex Returns — Bull vs. Bear
EMERGING TAILWIND
The bull framing: WM is spending a few hundred million dollars a year of sustainability growth capital ($191 million in the first nine months of 2026) to convert a liability into a product. Landfill methane is a waste stream WM already owns at zero marginal feedstock cost; RNG plants monetize it. Recycling automation cuts labour out of MRFs and raises bale purity. Seven RNG plants and nine recycling projects were completed in 2025; six RNG and four recycling projects are planned for 2026. In Q3 2026 Recycling Processing & Sales and Renewable Energy together earned $156 million of adjusted operating EBITDA, and FY2026 growth is guided to $235–255 million, on a path toward roughly $1 billion by 2027 versus ~$300 million in 2023.

The bear framing: every dollar of that upside is exposed to prices WM does not set. Recycled commodities averaged about $82/ton versus $68/ton a year earlier, a gain that can reverse, and RIN credits $2.32 versus $2.56, down 9.4%. The 2027 target is stated at current commodity prices, which is a disclosure, not a forecast. If commodity prices merely hold, this becomes the most attractive part of WM. If federal renewable fuel policy shifts, a material slice of the growth story is capital already spent against economics that no longer clear.
