Section 01

Company Overview

Walmart is the largest company in the world by revenue and the largest grocer in the United States, running roughly 10,500 stores and clubs across ~19 countries alongside a fast-growing digital business. Fiscal 2026 (ended January 31, 2026) revenue was $713.16 billion, up 4.72% YoY, with net income of $21.89B (+12.6%) and diluted EPS of $2.73 (+13.3%). Trailing-twelve-month revenue through the April 2026 quarter has since reached $725.31B, and Q1 FY2027 revenue accelerated to $177.75B, +7.33% YoY — the fastest top-line growth Walmart has printed in four years.

The strategic story of the last five years is a mix shift, not a growth story. Walmart's core merchandise business still runs at a ~25% gross margin and ~4% operating margin — thin by design, because EDLP (Everyday Low Price) is the moat. What has re-rated the stock is the layer on top: Walmart Connect and international advertising, Walmart+ and Sam's Club membership income, marketplace and fulfillment services, and data monetization. These carry software-like incremental margins and are growing at multiples of the core. The market has responded by taking Walmart's P/E from roughly 22x in 2021 to ~39x today — a valuation that leaves very little room for error, and which is precisely what the last three months of trading have been testing.

Walmart is also in the middle of a generational leadership handover. Doug McMillon retired on January 31, 2026 after 11 years as CEO; John Furner, previously CEO of Walmart U.S., took the top job on February 1, 2026 and has been reshaping the operating bench ever since. The company is a Dividend King with 53 consecutive years of dividend increases and is executing against a $30 billion share repurchase authorization.

Section 03

Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of Moat

Scale & Purchasing Power Supply Chain Density Store Proximity (90% of U.S. within 10 mi) EDLP Cost Leadership First-Party Data Flywheel Membership Switching Costs Brand Trust / Price Perception

Moat description. Walmart's advantage is the most boring and most durable kind: it is structurally the low-cost operator, and it uses that position to lower prices rather than raise margins, which widens the gap over time. With ~$713B of annual purchasing volume it can extract terms no competitor can match, and it spreads fixed logistics cost across a store base so dense that roughly 90% of the U.S. population lives within ten miles of one. That store network — historically viewed as a liability against Amazon — has been converted into the cheapest last-mile fulfillment asset in American retail: a store-fulfilled delivery costs a fraction of a dedicated warehouse-to-door shipment, which is why Walmart can profitably offer 30-minute delivery in 33 markets while pure-play e-commerce operators cannot. Layered on top is a data asset covering roughly 250 million weekly customers, which converts into retail media revenue at 50%+ incremental margins, and membership programs (Walmart+, Sam's Club) that create genuine switching costs and a recurring, near-pure-profit revenue line. The combination is a compounding loop: scale funds price investment, price investment drives traffic, traffic generates data, data monetizes at high margin, and that margin funds more price investment.

Key Vulnerabilities

Valuation — the multiple is the risk

At ~39x trailing and ~37x forward earnings on a business growing revenue ~5% and EPS ~10%, Walmart's PEG ratio is 3.95 — roughly double the S&P 500. The stock is priced for the high-margin advertising and membership flywheel to keep compounding and eventually to lift consolidated margins. It has not yet: FY2026 operating margin was 4.18% versus 4.31% in FY2025. The 17% drawdown from the May 19, 2026 record close of $134.20 to today's $110.71 is what a multiple reset looks like when growth is fine but not fine enough.

Amazon's grocery and same-day push

Amazon has spent the last two years attacking the one category where Walmart's lead is structural — perishable grocery — by building same-day fulfillment into its existing network and adding fresh assortment to Prime. Grocery is ~60% of Walmart U.S. sales and the source of its weekly traffic advantage. Any erosion there does not just cost grocery dollars; it costs the general merchandise attach and the ad impressions that ride on top of the trip.

Tariffs, sourcing and the cost of goods

Roughly two-thirds of what Walmart sells in the U.S. is made, grown or assembled domestically, but the remaining third — heavily weighted toward general merchandise from China and Southeast Asia — is where the margin sits. Tariff regimes and the ongoing geopolitical conflict disrupting trade lanes force a choice between passing through price (which damages the value proposition) and absorbing cost (which damages a 4% operating margin). Management has consistently chosen to absorb, protecting share at the expense of near-term earnings.

Consumer stress and the trade-down paradox

Walmart has been the primary beneficiary of higher-income households trading down since 2022. That is a real tailwind, but it cuts both ways: it means a meaningful slice of recent comp growth is macro-borrowed, and it would reverse if consumer conditions normalize. Simultaneously, its core low-income customer base is the most exposed to any labor-market deterioration — a scenario in which unit volumes and basket size compress even as the trade-down cohort leaves.

Labor cost structure and regulatory surface area

With 2.1 million employees, Walmart is the largest private employer on the planet and the most exposed large-cap to minimum-wage legislation, scheduling regulation and unionization pressure. It also carries a permanently elevated regulatory and litigation surface — antitrust scrutiny of its marketplace practices, pricing and advertising disclosure inquiries, and residual settlement obligations from the opioid litigation that produced a $3.1B charge in 2022.

Notable One-Off Items

Nothing of the magnitude that would invalidate the reported numbers, but four things worth adjusting for:

1. Equity investment marks. The gap between Walmart's operating income ($29.83B) and pre-tax income is meaningfully influenced by unrealized gains and losses on minority equity stakes, which flow through GAAP net income each quarter. This makes GAAP EPS noisier than the underlying business; management guides on adjusted EPS for this reason.

2. Vizio purchase accounting. The ~$2.3B Vizio acquisition (closed December 2024) added intangible amortization and integration cost while contributing to advertising revenue growth — flattering the ad growth rate and modestly diluting reported margin.

3. Residual opioid settlement outflows. The $3.1B charge taken in fiscal 2023 continues to produce cash outflows that depress free cash flow relative to the income statement, without appearing as a current-period expense.

4. Restructuring and severance under the new CEO. The Furner leadership reorganization through 2026 carries the usual associated one-time costs. These are not separately quantified in the data available for this snapshot and are flagged as an estimate.

Also worth noting on the capital side: a $30 billion share repurchase authorization is in flight, generating a ~0.73% buyback yield on top of the 0.89% dividend for a combined ~1.6% shareholder yield.

RSI measures how much of recent price movement has been upward versus downward, on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 suggest the stock has risen quickly and may be stretched; below 30 suggests it has fallen quickly and may be washed out. The Five-Year Trajectory

Walmart spent the first half of this window going nowhere. From August 2021 through the end of 2023 the split-adjusted share price oscillated in a rough $38–$55 band, punctuated by the worst single day since 1987 — −11.4% on May 17, 2022, when a Q1 gross-margin miss revealed that the company had misjudged inventory and fuel cost coming out of the pandemic. Through that entire stretch the stock behaved exactly as a low-growth defensive staple should.

The re-rating began in 2024 and it was violent. The 3-for-1 split in February 2024 coincided with a series of beat-and-raise quarters in which e-commerce turned profitable, advertising began compounding at 25%+, and higher-income trade-down showed up in the comps. The stock went from roughly $55 to $90 in twelve months, then to a record $134.20 by May 19, 2026 — a total return of roughly +120% over the five-year window before dividends, or about 17% annualized, which is extraordinary for a business that grew revenue at a ~5% CAGR over the same period. Essentially all of that return came from multiple expansion: the P/E went from ~22x to ~44x at the peak.

The last ten weeks have been the counterpunch. From the May record the stock has fallen 17.5% to $110.71, breaking below both the 50-day ($115.23) and 200-day ($118.02) moving averages, with monthly RSI rolling over from deeply overbought territory. Nothing in the reported fundamentals broke — Q1 FY2027 revenue actually accelerated to +7.33% — which is the tell that this is a valuation event.

Relative Performance

Over the five-year window Walmart's roughly +120% price return has substantially outpaced the S&P 500 and has crushed the Consumer Staples sector, which has been one of the market's worst performers since 2022 as investors rotated into technology and AI. Within retail, Walmart has beaten Target (roughly flat to down over the period) and Kroger, and has traded broadly in line with Costco — the two of them being the only large-cap U.S. retailers that meaningfully re-rated. Against Amazon it has been competitive on price return with dramatically lower volatility (beta 0.61 versus roughly 1.15).

The more useful framing for the next twelve months: Walmart no longer trades as a staple. Its correlation profile over the last two years has looked more like a quality-growth compounder than a defensive, which means the historical assumption that it outperforms in a drawdown is now conditional on the multiple, not just on the business. In a genuine consumer recession Walmart's fundamentals would likely improve on trade-down — but at 37x forward earnings the stock could still fall, because the de-rating risk is larger than the earnings upside. That asymmetry is the central tension in the name today.

Section 08

Summary & Key Risks

BULL CASE

Walmart is taking share in every quarter and in every income cohort, with Q1 FY2027 revenue accelerating to +7.33% — the best in four years — while earnings compound at roughly 2–3x the rate of sales. The mix shift toward retail media, membership and marketplace is real, carries 50%+ incremental margins, and is still less than 2% of revenue: the runway is long. It owns the cheapest last-mile network in American retail, is the structural low-cost operator in a tariff environment that punishes everyone else, holds unpriced optionality in Flipkart and PhonePe, and returns ~1.6% annually through a 53-year dividend growth streak plus a $30B buyback. At $110.71 — 18% off the high, below both key moving averages, with 37 of 43 analysts at Buy and a $138 average target — you are being handed a quality compounder at a discount to where the Street thinks it belongs.

BEAR CASE

You are paying 39x trailing and 37x forward earnings, a PEG of 3.95, and 70x free cash flow for a business with a 3.1% net margin growing revenue ~5%. The margin expansion thesis has not yet shown up where it matters: consolidated operating margin was 4.18% in FY2026, down from 4.31%. Meanwhile Amazon is attacking grocery — 60% of Walmart U.S. sales and the source of its traffic moat — with same-day fulfillment; tariffs and trade disruption are raising landed costs that management is choosing to absorb; and a meaningful slice of recent comp strength is borrowed from a trade-down cycle that reverses when the consumer normalizes. The June–July drawdown was not a fundamentals event, which is precisely the problem: nothing broke, and the stock still fell 17%. There is a long way down to a retailer multiple, and the Street-low $81 target shows what that looks like.

Top 3 Risks

  1. Multiple compressionAt 37x forward earnings for ~10% EPS growth, Walmart carries roughly 60% valuation premium to the S&P 500 on a business with one-quarter the index's net margin. A reversion toward even 28x forward — the market multiple — is a ~25% drawdown with no change in fundamentals whatsoever. This is the dominant risk in the name and it has already begun to express itself.
  2. Margin absorption from tariffs and trade disruptionOn a 4.18% operating margin, a 50-basis-point cost shock that cannot be passed through is a ~12% hit to operating income. Management's strategic choice to absorb rather than pass through protects long-term share but directly compresses the earnings line the market is capitalizing at 37x. The August 20 guide is the first real test of how much has been absorbed.
  3. Consumer deterioration and reversal of the trade-down tailwindWalmart's core customer is the most exposed to labor-market weakness, while the higher-income trade-down cohort that has driven share gains since 2022 leaves when conditions normalize. That is a scenario where Walmart outperforms peers and still misses its own numbers — historically the worst configuration for a stock priced for perfection.

Industry Commentary

Tariffs, Trade Lanes and the Landed Cost of Everything

Critical Constraint

Roughly one-third of what Walmart sells in the U.S. is imported, and that third is weighted toward general merchandise — the higher-margin half of the basket. Tariff regimes plus war-driven disruption to shipping lanes have raised landed cost across the entire discount-retail channel. The strategic asymmetry favors Walmart: on ~$713B of purchasing volume it can negotiate terms, re-source, and absorb cost that a $22B competitor simply cannot, which is how it widens price gaps during inflationary shocks. But the accounting asymmetry cuts the other way. With a 4.18% operating margin, every basis point absorbed is roughly 24 basis points of operating income. Walmart wins the war and pays for it in the quarter.

The Barbelled Consumer

Ongoing Headwind

The U.S. consumer has split. Higher-income households have been trading down into Walmart for four years — the single largest source of its comp outperformance and the reason a mature retailer is growing at 7%. Lower-income households, Walmart's traditional base, are running on thin margins of their own: elevated credit delinquencies, reduced discretionary trips, smaller baskets, more trade-down within the store into private label. The result is a business with excellent traffic and mediocre basket economics. It is a durable structure while it lasts, but it is not stable — it improves in a downturn and deteriorates in a genuine recovery, which is a peculiar and under-appreciated feature of the current setup.

Retail Media: The Highest-Stakes Bet in Retail

Emerging Tailwind

Global retail media is a roughly $180 billion market in 2026 compounding in the low-to-mid teens, and it is the reason a 5% revenue grower carries a 39x multiple. Amazon owns roughly three-quarters of the U.S. pool with ~$65B of annual ad revenue; Walmart is the credible number two, with first-party purchase data on ~250 million weekly customers, a smart-TV platform via Vizio, and international reach through Flipkart Ads. Incremental margins here are estimated above 50% against low single digits on merchandise.

Bull framing: if advertising, membership and marketplace scale from under 2% of revenue to 4–5% over five years, consolidated operating margin could plausibly move from ~4.2% toward 6%+ — which on flat revenue growth would roughly double earnings power and retroactively justify today's multiple.

Bear framing: it hasn't happened yet. FY2026 operating margin was 4.18%, down from 4.31%. The high-margin dollars are being consumed by price investment, tariff absorption and automation capex faster than they accumulate. Investors are paying a full platform multiple today for a margin structure that is, so far, indistinguishable from the retailer Walmart has always been. Which framing proves correct is, functionally, the entire investment case.
