01 Company Overview

Walmart is the world's largest company by revenue — $713.2B in total revenue for fiscal 2026 (ended January 31, 2026), up +4.7% YoY — operating roughly 10,750 stores and clubs across 19 countries plus a rapidly scaling e-commerce and marketplace operation. Once a pure brick-and-mortar discounter, Walmart now generates ~23% of sales from e-commerce (global e-commerce grew +24% in FY26) and is deliberately shifting its profit mix toward higher-margin advertising, membership, and marketplace businesses — a transformation the market has rewarded with a tech-like multiple. In December 2025 the company moved its listing from the NYSE to Nasdaq and joined the Nasdaq-100 index on January 20, 2026. The July quarter (Q2 FY27) showed both sides of the story: revenue of $187.9B (+5.9%), global e-commerce +23%, advertising +38% and membership income +17% — but U.S. comparable sales (growth at stores and websites open at least a year) slowed to +2.6% and much of the profit beat came from roughly $2.9B of tariff refunds that management chose to hand back to shoppers as lower prices. There have been no new results since: after slipping to $103.92 at the end of September, the shares rose 18.9% in October to $123.51, as investors moved money out of tech and into consumer staples (MarketBeat; 24/7 Wall St.) and Deutsche Bank upgraded the stock to Buy.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
Sources of Moat

Retail Media Data (Walmart Connect)

Walmart's moat starts with scale nobody else in physical retail can match: ~$713B of revenue buys the lowest cost of goods in the industry, funding the "Everyday Low Prices" brand promise that keeps 90%+ of Americans shopping there annually. The genuinely new development is that its ~4,600 US stores — once viewed as a liability versus Amazon — have become forward-deployed fulfillment nodes: same-day delivery now reaches 95% of US households, US delivery grew +45% in the April 2026 quarter, and automated distribution centers (via its Symbotic partnership) are compressing cost-to-serve. Each layer feeds the next: traffic generates first-party purchase data, data powers Walmart Connect advertising (+41% US in Q4 FY26 and +38% in Q2 FY27, at very high incremental margins), and advertising profits fund further price investment — a flywheel that is structurally hard for pure-play grocers or pure-play e-commerce to replicate.

▸
Amazon and the e-commerce ceiling. Amazon still commands ~40% of US e-commerce versus Walmart's ~7-9%, wins decisively with higher-income households, and its logistics + Prime + AWS-funded flexibility means Walmart is fighting an entrenched leader in the channel where all incremental retail growth occurs.
▸
Structurally thin margins. A ~3.0% net margin leaves little room for error: tariff absorption, wage inflation (average US hourly rates now well above $18), or a grocery price war with Aldi and Costco can each swing earnings materially. Q2 FY27 showed the flip side too: about 7.5 percentage points of operating-income growth came from tariff refunds, and management is spending them on price rather than banking them. The premium valuation assumes margin expansion continues uninterrupted.
▸
Regulatory & policy exposure. Roughly one-third of US cost of goods is imported (China, Mexico, Vietnam), making Walmart a headline tariff target; pharmacy reimbursement legislation is already costing about 1 percentage point of comparable-sales growth; and its size invites antitrust, labor, and drug-pricing scrutiny.
▸
Disruption of the shopping interface. If agentic AI shopping assistants (including third-party agents) become the default purchase interface, the store visit and app habit that anchor Walmart's flywheel could be disintermediated. Walmart is investing aggressively (Sparky, "super agents") but the interface shift is a genuine wildcard.

08 Summary & Key Risks

▲ Bull Case

Strip out the tariff noise and Q2 was still a good quarter: revenue +5.9%, global e-commerce +23%, advertising +38%, membership +17%, Sam’s comparable sales +4.4% — and management raised the full year. Spending a $2.9B windfall on 11,000+ price rollbacks is exactly the scale advantage that takes share from weaker retailers, and it buys traffic rather than a one-quarter EPS pop. October showed the other half of the appeal: when investors sold tech, they bought Walmart, and Deutsche Bank upgraded it on faster share gains in food. If Q3 shows the price cuts bringing in shoppers, the stock is still 8.6% below its May high.

▼ Bear Case

You are now paying ~45x trailing and ~43x this year’s (FY27) consensus earnings for a 3% net margin business. U.S. comparable sales slowed to +2.6%, the Q2 beat leaned on one-off refunds, and Q3 is guided to operating-income growth of just 2–4%. The 15% gain since the last edition came with no new results, and the $127.48 average analyst target sits only about 3% above the price. Both beats of this fiscal year (May and August) produced sharp drops; money that came in because investors were leaving tech can leave the same way.

01
Valuation. At ~45x trailing earnings, the biggest single risk is the price paid, not the business: if investors paid 30x today’s $2.76 TTM EPS instead, the stock would be about $83, a third below $123.51.
02
Tariff and policy margin squeeze. With ~1/3 of US cost of goods imported and pharmacy legislation already costing about 1 percentage point of comparable-sales growth, an escalation in trade policy or drug-pricing rules could stall the margin-expansion story the valuation depends on. The Q2 tariff refunds cut the other way — a one-off tailwind that has already been spent on price.
03
Amazon and agentic-commerce disruption. Amazon's e-commerce lead remains ~5x Walmart's share, and a shift to AI-agent-mediated shopping could neutralize Walmart's store-proximity and app-habit advantages faster than its own AI stack compensates.
  Industry Commentary: Retail Macro in October 2026
The Tariff Tax on Retail
ONGOING HEADWIND
The 2025–26 tariff regime remains the sector's dominant cost variable: general-merchandise import costs are up meaningfully, and retailers face a choice between eating margin and raising shelf prices into a value-sensitive consumer. Walmart imports roughly a third of its US cost of goods (heavily China, Mexico, Vietnam) but its grocery weighting (~60% of US sales, mostly domestic) and supplier leverage make it structurally the best-insulated large retailer — Target and mid-tier apparel/home players are far more exposed. The risk is political as much as financial: Walmart's 2025 comments about tariff-driven price increases drew direct White House pushback, and pricing decisions are now front-page news. In Q2 FY27 the regime produced a rare windfall: Walmart received roughly $2.9B of tariff refunds and chose to reinvest most of it in lower prices rather than keep it as profit.
The Value-Seeking Consumer & Trade-Down
EMERGING TAILWIND
The US consumer is bifurcated: lower-income households are stretched (elevated grocery prices, resumed loan payments, softening job market) while upper-income households keep spending — and both patterns favor Walmart. Households earning $100K+ have been the company's fastest-growing cohort for over two years, drawn by delivery convenience and Walmart+, while budget pressure pushes everyone else toward EDLP. Q1 FY27 transaction growth was the strongest in six quarters. Add basket effects from GLP-1 drugs (Walmart is a top dispenser — a pharmacy revenue tailwind, with early evidence of smaller food baskets among users) and the demand backdrop skews net-positive for value formats even in a slowdown. In October that defensive appeal showed up in share prices: as inflation worries rose and investors sold tech, money moved into consumer staples (MarketBeat; 24/7 Wall St.).
Retail Media & AI: The New Margin Battleground
EMERGING TAILWIND
US retail media is a ~$60B+ ad market growing ~20% annually, and it is rewriting retail P&Ls: ad dollars carry 70%+ incremental margins against retail's 3-4% net margins. Walmart Connect (+41% US in Q4 FY26; U.S. advertising +38% in Q2 FY27) is the clear #2 behind Amazon, super-charged by VIZIO's ad-supported TV operating system, while agentic AI (Sparky) threatens to reshuffle who owns the customer relationship. Bull framing: if ads, membership, and automation keep growing, Walmart's operating income can grow double digits for years and today's multiple gets earned into. Bear framing: if retail media growth decelerates or AI agents commoditize the storefront, WMT is a 3%-margin grocer trading at ~45x — the spring-to-September slide showed the market testing exactly that question, and October’s rally, with no new results, shows how much of today’s price depends on money moving between sectors.
