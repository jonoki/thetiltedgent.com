01 Company Overview

Williams-Sonoma started in 1956 as a kitchenware shop. Seventy years later it is a $26.75B home-furnishings group that sells sofas, beds, cookware and lighting under Pottery Barn, West Elm, Williams Sonoma, Pottery Barn Kids and Teen, Rejuvenation, Mark and Graham and GreenRow, through its own websites, catalogs and 508 stores. It bought Pottery Barn from Gap in 1986, launched West Elm in 2002 and added Rejuvenation in 2011. Fiscal 2025 (the year ended February 1, 2026) revenue was $7.81B, up 1.24%, with GAAP EPS of $8.84.

That revenue line is the odd thing about this stock. Sales peaked at $8.67B in fiscal 2022, fell 10.65% the next year as the pandemic home-spending boom unwound, and have barely moved since. The share price went the other way: $88.67 (split-adjusted) at the end of September 2021, $227.16 today, up 156%. Almost none of that came from selling more. It came from selling at full price. Gross margin went from 42.40% in fiscal 2022 to 46.45% in fiscal 2024 and has held near 46% since, and the share count keeps shrinking (down 3.45% in the last year). The company joined the S&P 500 on March 24, 2025. The question now is whether a retailer can keep growing earnings without much housing turnover behind it. The latest quarter says yes: comparable brand revenue up 6.2% and management raising its full-year outlook. Tariffs are what could stop it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Owned brand portfolio Full-price selling discipline Digital-first, catalog-trained customer file Own design & sourcing 508-store showroom network Balance-sheet capacity ($1.03B cash)

The moat is pricing power in a category that usually has none. Most home-furnishings retailers make money in good housing years and discount heavily in bad ones. Williams-Sonoma sells its own designs under its own brand names, so a Pottery Barn sofa has no identical listing elsewhere at a lower price. The numbers show it: revenue in fiscal 2025 ($7.81B) was below fiscal 2022 ($8.67B), but the operating margin was higher (18.15% against 17.45%) and trailing return on equity is 54.96%. It has held margins through a down cycle, which is the hard part. The weakness is that owning the design usually also means depending on overseas factories, and tariffs hit exactly that.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Tariffs are already costing margin. Underlying (non-GAAP) gross margin fell 160bps to 45.5% in Q2 even though revenue grew 6.7%. The company says merchandise margin fell 230bps, "primarily driven by tariff costs," and full-year guidance assumes "all tariffs currently in place will remain for fiscal 2026." The one-off refund of IEEPA tariffs flattered GAAP results this quarter, but it does not change the ongoing tariff cost, which is still there.
02
Housing turnover drives furniture demand. Pottery Barn and West Elm, about 65% of Q2 revenue, sell big-ticket furniture that people buy when they move. Both brands' annual revenue is still below fiscal 2022. If the housing market stays frozen, growth has to come from taking share, not from the market growing.
03
The valuation leaves little room for error. At 23.3x trailing and 22.9x forward earnings, the stock is priced as a quality compounder, not as a cyclical retailer. The 52-week low was $165.51, 27% below today's price, and March 2025 shows how quickly that can happen: −18.7% in one month.

Notable One-Off Items

One item matters most: the IEEPA tariff refund. In Q2 fiscal 2026 the company recorded $167.8M of tariff refund income, plus $6.3M of interest on the refunds. It also recorded $47.5M of refund-related vendor concessions and $10.0M of 401(k) employee recognition tied to the refunds. It had collected $200.2M of refunds and interest through August 2, 2026, with $3.2M still receivable, and deferred $29.3M of refund income into inventory that will reach the income statement as that stock sells. Net effect: GAAP EPS of $2.84 (+42.0%) against non-GAAP EPS of $2.10 (+5.0%). The +5% is the real growth rate. The trailing P/E is on GAAP earnings, so it looks cheaper than the business actually is. Management's guidance explicitly excludes any further refund benefit. Separately, share repurchases of $287.8M and dividends of $175.4M in the first half keep per-share figures growing faster than net income.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Williams-Sonoma has already proved it can hold margins through a bad housing market. It kept operating margins in the 16–19% range through a 10.65% revenue decline and has now turned growth back on: Q2 comparable brand revenue +6.2%, every brand positive, guidance raised to +4.7% to +7.2% revenue growth. It converts earnings to cash (TTM FCF $1.34B), retires about 3.5% of its shares a year and has raised its dividend 19 years in a row. If housing turnover ever returns, Pottery Barn and West Elm, both still below their fiscal 2022 revenue, have a lot of catch-up room, and the income statement would benefit on every line. The average target of $247.85 and median of $257 suggest the Street thinks there is more to come.

Bear Case

The headline numbers flatter the quarter. The +42% GAAP EPS was a tariff refund, and underlying EPS grew 5.0% while non-GAAP gross margin fell 160bps. On earnings without the refund the stock is at roughly 25x for high-single-digit growth, with a PEG of 1.80 and half the analysts at Hold. Tariffs are an ongoing cost the refund does not fix, and guidance assumes they stay. Five-year revenue is flat: $8.25B in fiscal 2021 and $8.01B TTM. The whole re-rating has come from margins and buybacks. If margins merely stop improving, a 23x multiple is hard to defend for a furniture retailer with a 1.46 beta, and the $151 low target shows how far the downside could go.

Top 3 Risks

01
Tariff-driven gross-margin erosion. Non-GAAP gross margin fell 160bps to 45.5% in Q2 on a sales gain. The refund was one-off; the tariffs are not. Guidance assumes current tariffs stay in place, so any escalation is not in the numbers.
02
A premium multiple on a cyclical category. 23.3x trailing and 22.9x forward for a business whose five-year revenue is flat. The stock fell 27% from January to April 2025 without any collapse in results, and it could do so again.
03
Housing stays frozen. About 65% of Q2 revenue comes from Pottery Barn and West Elm, whose big-ticket furniture sells when people move. If turnover stays low, growth depends entirely on taking share from weaker competitors, which gets harder the more share you already have.
Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the imported-furniture cost base
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Home furnishings are mostly made overseas, so tariffs go straight into cost of goods. Williams-Sonoma's Q2 fiscal 2026 release shows both sides. The company recorded $167.8M of IEEPA tariff refund income and collected $200.2M of refunds plus interest through August 2, 2026: money paid under tariffs that were later refunded. Yet its underlying gross margin still fell 160bps, and its outlook assumes "all tariffs currently in place will remain for fiscal 2026." The refund was a windfall for past payments. It does not lower the tariffs being paid now. Passing the ongoing cost to customers without discounting is the test of the full-price model.

A housing market that isn't moving
ONGOING HEADWIND

Furniture demand follows home sales: people furnish a house when they move into it. Housing-turnover data was not sourced this session, but the company's own numbers show the effect. Pottery Barn's annual revenue is still about $500M below fiscal 2022 and West Elm's about $360M below. Listed peers are doing worse: RH's market value fell 44.0% over the past year and Arhaus's 21.7%. In a shrinking or flat category the strongest brand can still grow by taking share, and Alber says the company "gained market share" in Q2. That only works for a while, and it gets harder as share grows.

The full-price model — bull and bear framing
STRUCTURAL ADVANTAGE

Williams-Sonoma's gross margin moved from 42.40% (fiscal 2022) to about 46% and has stayed there through a down cycle, with inventory up only 1.0% against 6.7% sales growth in the latest quarter. The bull framing: this is structural. Proprietary design, a large direct customer base and disciplined inventory have turned a cyclical category into a compounder with ~40% ROIC, and a housing recovery would be extra upside the market is not paying for. The bear framing: the margin gain happened in a period of falling freight costs and pent-up pricing power. Tariffs now push the other way, and a 23x multiple assumes the gain is permanent. The Q3 and Q4 non-GAAP gross margins will show which view is right, and both will be reported within six months.
