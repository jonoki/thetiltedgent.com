01 Company Overview

Williams-Sonoma started in 1956 as a kitchenware shop. Seventy years later it is a $24.67B home-furnishings group that sells sofas, beds, cookware and lighting under Pottery Barn, West Elm, Williams Sonoma, Pottery Barn Kids and Teen, Rejuvenation, Mark and Graham and GreenRow, through its own websites, catalogs and 517 stores. It bought Pottery Barn from Gap in 1986, launched West Elm in 2002 and added Rejuvenation in 2011. Fiscal 2025 (the year ended February 1, 2026) revenue was $7.81B, up 1.24%, with GAAP EPS of $8.84.

That revenue line is the odd thing about this stock. Sales peaked at $8.67B in fiscal 2022, fell 10.65% the next year as the pandemic home-spending boom unwound, and have barely moved since. The share price went the other way: $97.42 (split-adjusted) at the end of November 2021, $210.70 at the November 20, 2026 close, up 116%. Almost none of that came from selling more. It came from selling at full price. Gross margin went from 42.40% in fiscal 2022 to 46.45% in fiscal 2024 and has held near 46% since, and the share count keeps shrinking (diluted shares in Q3 were 3.5% fewer than a year earlier). The company joined the S&P 500 on March 24, 2025. The question now is whether a retailer can keep growing earnings without much housing turnover behind it. The latest quarter, Q3 fiscal 2026, says the brands still sell: comparable brand revenue up 5.1% and underlying gross margin slightly higher despite tariffs and diesel. But management only reiterated its full-year outlook, said the housing market had gotten worse, and the stock fell 15.2% on the day of the report. Shipping costs and tariffs are what could stop it.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Owned brand portfolio Full-price selling discipline Digital-first, catalog-trained customer file Own design & sourcing 517-store showroom network Balance-sheet capacity ($992M cash)

The moat is pricing power in a category that usually has none. Most home-furnishings retailers make money in good housing years and discount heavily in bad ones. Williams-Sonoma sells its own designs under its own brand names, so a Pottery Barn sofa has no identical listing elsewhere at a lower price. The numbers show it: revenue in fiscal 2025 ($7.81B) was below fiscal 2022 ($8.67B), but the operating margin was higher (18.15% against 17.45%) and trailing return on equity is 57.17%. It has held margins through a down cycle, which is the hard part. The weakness is that owning the design usually also means depending on overseas factories, and tariffs hit exactly that.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Tariffs and freight are pressing on margin. Underlying (non-GAAP) gross margin fell 1.6 percentage points in Q2 and rose only 0.1 point in Q3, to 46.2%, even with more full-price selling. On the Q3 call the CFO said U.S. diesel prices were up more than 60% from a year earlier and that the higher oil costs went straight into domestic transport expense (Investing.com transcript). The tariff refunds that lifted GAAP results in Q2 and Q3 were for past payments and have been almost entirely collected; they do not lower the tariffs being paid now.
02
Housing turnover drives furniture demand. Pottery Barn and West Elm, about 63% of Q3 revenue, sell big-ticket furniture that people buy when they move. Both brands' annual revenue is still below fiscal 2022. If the housing market stays frozen, growth has to come from taking share, not from the market growing.
03
The valuation leaves little room for error. At 20.7x trailing and 20.9x forward earnings, the stock is priced as a steady, high-quality business, not as a cyclical retailer. The 52-week low was $165.51, 21.4% below the November 20 close, and the stock has shown twice how quickly a premium can shrink: −18.7% in March 2025, and −15.2% on November 19, 2026, the day of a quarter that met estimates.

Notable One-Off Items

One item matters most: the IEEPA tariff refunds (duties paid under emergency-powers tariffs that were later refunded). In Q2 fiscal 2026 the company recorded $167.8M of tariff refund income and deferred $29.3M more into inventory. In Q3 that deferred $29.3M reached the income statement as a lower cost of goods sold, with $0.1M of interest and $0.5M of other tariff-related adjustments. Substantially all of the $197.8M refund claim had been collected by November 1, 2026, and on the call the CFO said no refund adjustments are expected in Q4. Net effect in Q3: GAAP EPS of $2.38 (+21.4%) against non-GAAP EPS of $2.19 (+11.7%); in Q2 it was $2.84 against $2.10. The non-GAAP figures are the real growth rate. The trailing P/E is on GAAP earnings, so it looks cheaper than the business actually is. Separately, the company spent $450.7M on buybacks and $265.2M on dividends in the first 39 weeks ($163M and $90M in Q3 alone), which keeps per-share figures growing faster than net income.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Williams-Sonoma has already proved it can hold margins through a bad housing market. It kept operating margins in the 16–19% range through a 10.65% revenue decline and has turned growth back on: Q3 comparable brand revenue +5.1%, every brand positive, and underlying gross margin edged up even with higher tariffs and diesel. It converts earnings to cash (TTM FCF $1.31B), retires about 3.5% of its shares a year and has raised its dividend 19 years in a row. If housing turnover ever returns, Pottery Barn and West Elm, both still below their fiscal 2022 revenue, have a lot of catch-up room. After the November drop the stock trades at 20.7x trailing earnings, and the average target of $247.76 and median of $250 sit 18–19% above the November 20 close.

Bear Case

The headline numbers flatter the year. Tariff refunds added about $0.93 to trailing GAAP EPS (Q2 and Q3 together); without them the stock is at roughly 23x for high-single-digit growth, with a PEG of 1.65 and 11 of 24 analysts at Hold. The refunds are now collected and done; the tariffs and a diesel bill up sharply are not. Pottery Barn, the largest brand, grew only 1.8% on a comparable basis in Q3, and inventory grew faster than sales (+7.3% against +5.8%). Five-year revenue is flat: $8.25B in fiscal 2021 and $8.11B TTM. The whole climb has come from margins and buybacks. If margins merely stop improving, a premium multiple is hard to defend for a furniture retailer with a 1.49 beta, and the $151 low target shows how far the downside could go.

Top 3 Risks

01
Gross-margin pressure from tariffs and freight. Non-GAAP gross margin fell 1.6 percentage points in Q2 and rose just 0.1 point in Q3. The refunds were one-off; the tariffs and higher diesel costs are not. On the Q3 call the CFO said the guidance allows for today's oil prices and that a further rise could change it (Investing.com transcript).
02
A premium multiple on a cyclical category. 20.7x trailing and 20.9x forward for a business whose five-year revenue is flat. The stock fell 27% from January to April 2025 without any collapse in results, and 19.0% in the two sessions after a Q3 report that met estimates.
03
Housing stays frozen. About 63% of Q3 revenue came from Pottery Barn and West Elm, whose big-ticket furniture sells when people move, and on the Q3 call the CEO said the housing market had gotten worse. If turnover stays low, growth depends entirely on taking share from weaker competitors, which gets harder the more share you already have.
Industry Commentary
Tariffs and the imported-furniture cost base
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Home furnishings are mostly made overseas, so tariffs go straight into cost of goods. Williams-Sonoma's fiscal 2026 releases show both sides. The company recorded $167.8M of IEEPA tariff refund income in Q2 and $29.3M more in Q3, and had collected substantially all of its $197.8M refund claim by November 1, 2026: money paid under tariffs that were later refunded. Yet its underlying gross margin fell 1.6 percentage points in Q2 and was roughly flat in Q3. The refund was a windfall for past payments. It does not lower the tariffs being paid now, and on the Q3 call the CFO added diesel, up more than 60% in a year, to the cost list. Passing those costs to customers without discounting is the test of the full-price model.

A housing market that isn't moving
ONGOING HEADWIND

Furniture demand follows home sales: people furnish a house when they move into it. The company's own numbers show the effect. Pottery Barn's fiscal 2025 revenue was about $560M below fiscal 2022 and West Elm's about $420M below. Listed peers are split: RH's share price fell 26.45% over the past 52 weeks while Arhaus's rose 46.40% (StockAnalysis). In a flat category the strongest brand can still grow by taking share, and Laura Alber said on the Q3 call that the housing market had gotten worse, not better. Growing by taking share only works for a while, and it gets harder as share grows.

The full-price model — bull and bear framing
STRUCTURAL ADVANTAGE

Williams-Sonoma's gross margin moved from 42.40% (fiscal 2022) to about 46% and has stayed there through a down cycle. The bull framing: this is lasting. Its own designs, a large direct customer base and careful inventory have turned a cyclical category into a steady earner with ~40% return on invested capital, and a housing recovery would be extra upside the market is not paying for. The bear framing: the margin gain happened in a period of falling freight costs and pent-up pricing power. Tariffs and diesel now push the other way, inventory grew faster than sales in Q3 (+7.3% against +5.8%), and a 21x multiple assumes the gain is permanent. Q3's non-GAAP gross margin held at 46.2%; the holiday quarter, with fewer markdowns planned and no refund help, is the next test.
