01 Company Overview

Xcel Energy is a holding company for four regulated utilities — Northern States Power-Minnesota (NSPM), Northern States Power-Wisconsin (NSPW), Public Service Company of Colorado (PSCo) and Southwestern Public Service (SPS, Texas and New Mexico) — that generate, transmit and deliver electricity and distribute natural gas across eight Upper-Midwest and Mountain-West states. FY2025 revenue was $14.67B, up 9.1% from $13.44B, on GAAP net income of $2.02B and diluted EPS of $3.42 (ongoing EPS, the company's non-GAAP measure that strips out wildfire and other unusual items, was $3.80). In the third quarter of 2026 it earned $1.52 a share on both measures, up from $1.24 ongoing a year earlier, and over the last twelve months GAAP EPS was $4.29 on revenue of $14.87B. At a $51.23B market capitalisation (641.9M shares at the $79.82 close) it ranks #229 in the S&P 500 — a mid-sized U.S. utility, but one with a $70B five-year building plan, more than its own market value.

A regulated utility is a peculiar machine: it does not really sell electricity for a profit, it earns a regulator-approved return (the allowed ROE, 9.2–9.6% in Xcel's latest rate decisions and settlements) on the capital it has invested (the rate base). So earnings grow when rate base grows, and rate base grows when the company spends money on wires, plants and batteries — money it has to raise partly by selling new shares. The whole debate on this page is therefore arithmetic. With its Q3 2026 results Xcel rolled the plan forward: $70B of capital spending in 2027–2031, rate-base growth of about 11% a year into 2031, EPS growth averaging 9%+ through 2031 (10%+ from 2027 to 2030) against a 6–8%+ long-term objective, and $8B of new equity to help pay for it. Can data-center demand, a year of settled rate cases and the Texas generation build turn that spending into earnings faster than dilution, rising interest costs and wildfire liabilities take it away?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EXCLUSIVE SERVICE FRANCHISES $70B PLAN, ~11% RATE-BASE GROWTH CONSTRUCTIVE REGULATORS (9.2–9.6% ROE) AMONG THE LOWEST BILLS DATA-CENTER LOAD GROWTH GE VERNOVA / NEXTERA ALLIANCES 21 YEARS OF GUIDANCE DELIVERY

A regulated utility's moat is legal, not commercial: Xcel holds exclusive franchises to serve its territories, and no competitor can string a rival wire to a customer's house. What varies between utilities is the quality of the regulatory relationship, and that is where Xcel is genuinely differentiated. CEO Bob Frenzel told the Q2 2026 call that Xcel's total energy bills are among the lowest in the country, at or below the national average, and low bills help it walk into a full round of rate cases and come out with decisions and settlements at 9.2–9.6% allowed ROEs and 52.5–54.7% equity ratios. Regulatory goodwill is the real asset; the wires are just how it earns on it. Layer on top the sector's tailwind — load growth is back after two flat decades, with Xcel's weather-adjusted retail electric sales up 2.5% in the first nine months of 2026 and guided to about 5% in 2027, and a target of another 1 GW of signed data-center contracts by the end of 2026 and 3 GW more by the end of 2027 — and the company has plenty of approved places to put capital. A strategic alliance with GE Vernova (buying five more gas turbines and reserving multiple gigawatts of wind turbines) and a co-development memorandum with NextEra (both February 2026, per POWER magazine) mean it has also lined up scarce equipment. The record — 21 years in a row of delivering on its earnings guidance (Q4 2025 earnings release) — is the evidence that management converts spending into earnings reliably.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Wildfire liability without a California-style backstop. Xcel has been through two large utility-linked fires: the December 2021 Marshall Fire in Colorado (settled September 24, 2025 for $640M, no fault admitted; about $350M from insurance and a $287M pre-tax charge to PSCo) and the February 2024 Smokehouse Creek Fire in Texas ($475M of estimated losses, $410M settled, $525M of insurance that the company warns remaining claims could exceed; Texas AG suit in discovery into 2027). Neither Colorado nor Texas has a wildfire fund or liability cap like California's AB 1054. The wildfire-mitigation spending in the plan earns a return; the next fire earns a charge.
▸
Equity dilution is baked into the model. Shares outstanding rose 6.66% year over year to 641.9M (10-Q cover, October 27, 2026); the 2027–2031 plan needs $8B of new equity (over 40% already contracted), and on October 30 Xcel signed an agreement to sell up to $6B of shares over time at market prices (8-K). Free cash flow is −$7.62B TTM because capex ($12.69B) runs at 2.5× operating cash flow ($5.08B). New shares cost $0.24 of EPS in the first nine months of 2026 (release). If the stock falls, each dollar of equity buys less rate base and the arithmetic degrades.
▸
Interest-rate and credit sensitivity. Total debt is $42.75B against $2.50B of cash (stockanalysis.com); interest coverage is 1.85×; management targets 17% FFO/debt, reaching 18% in 2031. Interest charges rose 23% to $1.20B in the first nine months of 2026, a $0.35-a-share drag. A utility with a 0.37 beta trades partly like a bond — when long-term yields rise, the stock falls and the cost of the next $26.2B of debt goes up at the same time.
▸
Data-center load that does not show up — or is powered by the data center's own plants. More than 2 GW was contracted or under construction in February 2026 (POWER magazine); management wants another 1 GW signed by the end of 2026 and 3 GW more by the end of 2027. The biggest cloud companies can and do build their own generation, and Minnesota's Attorney General is already asking who pays if Google's load disappoints. If the pipeline converts slowly, the more than $10B of projects outside the base plan stays out, and the affordability argument gets harder as remaining customers shoulder the transmission build.

Notable One-Off Items

Wildfire accounting is the main distortion, and it runs through both the income statement and the "ongoing vs GAAP" gap.

Marshall Fire (Colorado, Dec 30, 2021). On September 24, 2025, on the eve of trial in Boulder, Xcel agreed a $640M settlement covering lawsuits by hundreds of fire victims and their insurers, without admitting its equipment caused the fire (which destroyed about 1,000 homes and killed two, with about $2B of damage). Xcel said about $350M of it would come from its insurance and none from customers (Associated Press, September 24, 2025); PSCo booked a $287M pre-tax charge for the settlement in Q3 2025 (a $74M tax benefit against it), per the Q3 2026 release. In the first nine months of 2026 PSCo recognised an $18M net reduction in expenses, mainly because it now expects to recover more from insurance.

Smokehouse Creek Fire (Texas, Feb 2024). Xcel accepted early that its facilities were involved in the ignition of the ~1.06-million-acre fire and set up an expedited claims process. As of the Q3 2026 release SPS had settled 358 complaints and claims for $410M ($405M paid by September 30), recorded $65M of remaining probable losses for a total estimate of $475M, plus about $50M of legal costs — $525M in all, matching the $525M of insurance for the policy period; insurance receivables stood at about $96M. The company warns that remaining claims could exceed that cover. The Texas Attorney General's December 2025 suit seeking damages and civil penalties is in discovery into 2027. If the estimate holds within insurance, the shareholder cost is limited to deductibles, premiums and the legal spend above cover.

GAAP vs ongoing. FY2025 GAAP diluted EPS of $3.42 versus ongoing EPS of $3.80: the $0.38 gap is the Marshall Wildfire litigation charge, per the Q4 2025 earnings release. In the first nine months of 2026 GAAP EPS of $3.34 is $0.03 below ongoing $3.37; Q3 2026 GAAP and ongoing EPS were identical at $1.52.

What drove the quarter. Q3 2026 revenue of $4.17B rose 6.6% year over year. The release credits higher recovery of electric infrastructure investments, earnings on equity-method investments (stakes Xcel accounts for as a share of their profit, such as the WYCO pipeline) and unseasonably warm weather, partly offset by higher financing costs. Over the first nine months higher interest charges (−$0.35 a share) and the cost of new shares (−$0.24) were the biggest drags, against higher electric revenues, lower fuel and purchased-power costs and AFUDC.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The arithmetic, in order. Start with the rate base — the regulator-approved pool of invested capital the utility is allowed to earn on. The new plan spends $70B in 2027–2031 (SPS $25.8B, NSPM $24.8B, PSCo $16.1B, NSPW $3.6B) and, per CFO Brian Van Abel, extends rate-base growth of about 11% a year into 2031 — before more than $10B of further projects from open generation tenders, and the grid build for new data centers, none of which is in the plan. Multiply by the allowed ROE — 9.3% at PSCo electric, 9.6% at NSPM, 9.5% proposed at SPS-New Mexico — on equity ratios of 52.5–54.7%, and you have the earnings the regulators have agreed to let Xcel make. That is the story; everything else is the drag. Drag one: equity issuance. The plan needs $8B of new shares in 2027–2031 (over 40% already contracted), and on October 30 Xcel set up a programme to sell up to $6B of stock over time; shares outstanding are already 641.9M, up 6.7% in a year. Drag two: interest. $26.2B of new debt on top of about $40.6B, with interest charges up 23% to $1.20B in the first nine months, and an FFO/debt target of 17%. Drag three: regulatory lag and affordability — the gap between spending money and being allowed to earn on it, and the political ceiling on how fast bills can rise; NSPM's proposal to freeze Minnesota base rates in 2027–2028 is the company managing that ceiling itself. Net all of that out and management lands on 9%+ average EPS growth through 2031 (10%+ for 2027–2030, measured from the $4.13 midpoint of 2026 guidance), with a 3.0% dividend on top: roughly a 12% total-return promise if both hold.

Is 9% believable? The best evidence is the year so far: ongoing EPS of $3.37 through September, up 19% from $2.84, on rate-case outcomes, higher electric revenue and AFUDC (the return regulators let a utility book on money tied up in construction). Narrowed 2026 guidance of $4.10–$4.16 puts the year about 9% above 2025's $3.80 at the midpoint, and 2027 guidance of $4.48–$4.62 is 10% above that midpoint. Low bills (total energy bills among the lowest in the country, CEO Bob Frenzel told the Q2 2026 call) help regulators keep saying yes — though Colorado granted $184M of the $356M PSCo asked for, a reminder that yes is rarely the full ask. The 21-year streak of delivering on its earnings guidance is a track record, not a guarantee, but for a business whose earnings are quite literally set by commission order, it is a meaningful one.

Data centers, honestly sized. On the Q2 call (July 30, 2026) CEO Bob Frenzel said about 1 GW of data centers was in operation or under construction and another gigawatt was under signed agreements, and put the high-probability pipeline at more than 20 GW. On the Q3 call management said it is on track to sign another 1 GW by the end of 2026 and 3 GW more by the end of 2027, and that the grid investment for that load is not in the $70B base plan. The one big named deal, Google's Pine Island campus in Minnesota, has Google paying all of its service costs; Minnesota's Attorney General's office told regulators on September 28 that Xcel had not yet shown other customers are protected if Google uses less power or leaves (KTTC). The bear's correct response is that the biggest cloud companies build their own generation when utilities are slow. Both things are true. What the stock is paying for is the base plan; the data-center pipeline is the upside.

Wildfire, honestly sized. Marshall (Colorado, 2021) settled for $640M on September 24, 2025 with no fault admitted; insurance covered about $350M and PSCo booked a $287M pre-tax charge in Q3 2025. Smokehouse Creek (Texas, 2024) has $475M of estimated losses plus about $50M of legal costs against $525M of insurance, $410M already settled, and an unresolved Texas AG suit in discovery into 2027. Of roughly $1.1B of liability from two fires, the Marshall charge is the main cost shareholders have borne so far; insurance has carried most of the rest. That is a manageable outcome — and a warning. Xcel's territories have no California-style liability fund (management says it will push for state wildfire legislation in 2027), and the company itself warns Smokehouse claims could exceed its cover. The wildfire-mitigation spending in the plan is both the hedge and, conveniently, rate base.

Valuation. At $79.82, XEL trades at 18.6× trailing GAAP EPS of $4.29, 18.4× trailing ongoing EPS of $4.33, 19.3× the 2026 guidance midpoint of $4.13 and 17.5× the 2027 midpoint of $4.55, with a 2.97% yield. Reported EV/EBITDA is 14.4× and price-to-book 1.99× (stockanalysis.com) — the market pays about twice the book value of a rate base earning ~9.5%, the usual premium for a utility growing faster than its cost of equity. The PEG of 1.86 says you are not getting the growth cheaply; the 0.37 beta and a dividend of 55% of earnings say you are being paid to wait. The average target of $88.58 (+11%) is 19.5× the 2027 guidance midpoint — about the multiple of 2026 guidance the stock trades at today. Whether it gets there depends less on Xcel than on the 10-year Treasury yield and on whether the "AI power" trade holds.

Bull Case

A regulated utility growing fast: about 11% a year of rate-base growth into 2031, backed by a year of rate decisions at 9.2–9.6% allowed ROEs; 9%+ EPS growth guided through 2031 and 10%+ for 2027–2030; a 3.0% dividend raised for 23 straight years; over 40% of the new equity already contracted; and data-center demand whose grid build is not even in the $70B plan. Marshall is settled and Smokehouse Creek is estimated inside its insurance. At 17.5× 2027 guidance, with a Strong Buy consensus and an $88.58 average target, the stock is pricing the base plan — and if long-term rates fall, investors will pay more for each dollar of utility earnings.

Bear Case

Free cash flow is −$7.6B, the share count is growing almost 7% a year and Xcel has just set up a $6B share-sale programme; interest coverage is 1.85× and the plan needs $26B of new debt. The business only works if regulators, bond markets and equity markets all keep saying yes at the same time — and Colorado gave PSCo about half its electric ask, while Minnesota's Attorney General is questioning the Google deal. The payout is already at the top of the 45–55% target band. Texas and Colorado have no wildfire liability cap, the Texas Attorney General is still suing, and the company warns Smokehouse claims could exceed its insurance. At a PEG of 1.86 and a 0.37 beta, this behaves like a bond with share-issuance risk — if the 10-year yield rises a full percentage point, the stock falls and the plan's funding cost rises at the same time.

Top 3 Risks

01
Wildfire ignition in a state without a liability backstop. Two fires have produced roughly $1.1B of liability, most of it paid or expected to be paid by insurance. A third — in a territory that includes the Front Range and the Texas Panhandle, both high-risk — would hit shareholders directly, and the Texas AG suit shows regulators and politicians are not inclined to be gentle. This is the risk that can change the sign of the thesis rather than the magnitude.
02
Funding-cost squeeze. $8B of equity and $26.2B of debt in 2027–2031 have to be raised on terms that keep FFO/debt near its 17% target and the ratings in the "A" range for secured debt. A higher-for-longer rate environment raises the cost of both, narrows the gap between allowed ROE and the cost of capital, and lowers the price investors will pay for the stock — which then makes each equity raise more dilutive. The feedback loop runs the wrong way.
03
Affordability politics catching up with the capital plan. Xcel's bills are low today, but $70B of spending has to be recovered from customers eventually, and data-center load only helps if regulators let the new customers pay for the new wires — the question Minnesota's Attorney General is now asking about Google. A disallowance, a lower allowed ROE in the next round of cases, or a political backlash in Minnesota or Colorado would lower the ceiling on earnings without any operational misstep.
  Industry Commentary
Wildfire Liability Has Become a Western-Utility Tax
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
PG&E's bankruptcy taught the market that a utility's equity can be wiped out by a spark; Hawaiian Electric's 2023 Maui fire repeated the lesson outside California. Xcel's two fires — Marshall ($640M settlement, Sep 2025, about $350M of it from insurance and a $287M pre-tax charge to PSCo) and Smokehouse Creek ($475M of estimated losses against $525M of cover, Texas AG suit pending) — have been carried mostly by insurance, but insurers price that risk into every renewal and Colorado and Texas offer none of the protection of California's AB 1054 wildfire fund. The sector response is to spend on mitigation and lobby for liability caps; Xcel says it will push for state legislation in 2027. Until those caps exist, every dry, windy day on the Front Range is a risk shareholders carry with no cap on the worst case.
The Equity Treadmill: Growth Utilities Are Serial Issuers
ONGOING HEADWIND
Because regulators set equity ratios near 50–55%, every dollar of rate base needs about half a dollar of new equity or retained earnings. Xcel's $8B need for 2027–2031 (shares +6.7% in the past year) is the cost of a $70B plan. This is why utility EPS growth of 6–8% requires net-income growth well into double digits, and why the sector is acutely sensitive to its own share price: a falling price makes the plan more dilutive, which justifies the lower price. Forward sales (agreeing now to deliver shares later at a set price) and at-the-market programmes (selling shares gradually at market prices — Xcel set up a $6B one on October 30, 2026) blunt the risk, and over 40% of Xcel's 2027–2031 need is already contracted; they do not remove it.
Load Growth Is Back — and It Is Concentrated
EMERGING TAILWIND
After two decades of flat U.S. electricity demand, data centers, electrification and reshored manufacturing have turned utilities into growth stocks. Xcel's weather-adjusted electric sales rose 2.5% in the first nine months of 2026 with SPS at 5.1%, the company guides to about 5% growth in 2027, and it targets another 4 GW of signed data-center contracts by the end of 2027. Bull framing: new large loads pay for the transmission everyone else needs, spreading fixed costs and keeping bills low while rate base grows about 11% a year. Bear framing: pipelines are not contracts, the biggest cloud companies are building their own gas plants, and if load underdelivers after the wires are built, the remaining customers eat the cost and regulators respond by cutting allowed ROEs — the risk Minnesota's Attorney General has already raised about the Google deal. The signings of the next 14 months will decide which framing is right for Xcel.
