01 Company Overview

Xcel Energy is a holding company for four regulated utilities — Northern States Power-Minnesota (NSPM), Northern States Power-Wisconsin (NSPW), Public Service Company of Colorado (PSCo) and Southwestern Public Service (SPS, Texas and New Mexico) — that generate, transmit and deliver electricity and distribute natural gas across eight Upper-Midwest and Mountain-West states. FY2025 revenue was $14.67B, up 9.1% from $13.44B, on GAAP net income of $2.02B and diluted EPS of $3.42 (ongoing EPS, the company's non-GAAP measure that strips out wildfire and other unusual items, was $3.80). At a $47.30B market capitalisation it ranks roughly #545 globally — a mid-sized U.S. utility, but one with the fourth-largest capital plan in the sector relative to its size.

A regulated utility is a peculiar machine: it does not really sell electricity for a profit, it earns a regulator-approved return (the allowed ROE, currently 9.3–9.6% at Xcel's operating companies) on the capital it has invested (the rate base). So earnings grow when rate base grows, and rate base grows when the company spends money on wires, plants and batteries — money it has to raise partly by selling new shares. The whole debate on this page is therefore arithmetic: Xcel is promising rate base growth of 11% a year to 2030 ($56B → $94B), EPS growth of 9%+ on a 6–8% long-term target, and needs $7B of new equity to fund it. Can 20 GW of data-center demand, a pile of settled rate cases and Texas generation approvals turn that spending into earnings faster than dilution, rising interest costs and wildfire liabilities take it away?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
EXCLUSIVE SERVICE FRANCHISES $56B RATE BASE, 11% CAGR CONSTRUCTIVE REGULATORS (9.3–9.6% ROE) LOWEST-IN-NATION BILLS 20 GW DATA-CENTER PIPELINE GE VERNOVA / NEXTERA SUPPLY DEALS 21 YEARS OF GUIDANCE DELIVERY

A regulated utility's moat is legal, not commercial: Xcel holds exclusive franchises to serve its territories, and no competitor can string a rival wire to a customer's house. What varies between utilities is the quality of the regulatory relationship, and that is where Xcel is genuinely differentiated. Its residential electric bills run 29% below the national average and have compounded at only 2.0% a year since 2014 ($83 → $105/month), which is what lets it walk into six rate cases in one year and come out with settlements at 9.3–9.6% allowed ROEs and 52.5–54.7% equity layers. Regulatory goodwill is the real asset; the wires are just how it is monetised. Layer on top the sector's structural tailwind — load growth is back after two flat decades, with Xcel's weather-adjusted retail electric sales up 2.1% year-to-date and a high-probability data-center portfolio above 20 GW (against roughly 2 GW contracted or under construction) — and the company has more sanctioned places to put capital than at any time in its history. Supply agreements with GE Vernova (turbines and grid equipment) and a 2 GW co-development with NextEra mean it has also locked up the scarce inputs. The record — 21 consecutive years of meeting or exceeding earnings guidance — is the evidence that management converts spending into earnings reliably.

Key Vulnerabilities

▸
Wildfire liability without a California-style backstop. Xcel has now been through two of the costliest utility-ignited fires outside California: the December 2021 Marshall Fire in Colorado (settled September 24, 2025 for $640M, ~$350M from Xcel's $400M insurance tower, no fault admitted, 2,000+ Coloradans subsequently accepting) and the February 2024 Smokehouse Creek Fire in Texas ($460M accrued, ~$400M paid or finalised, $525M insurance, Texas AG suit pending). Neither Colorado nor Texas has a wildfire fund or liability cap like California's AB 1054. The $5B of wildfire mitigation in the capital plan earns a return; the next fire earns a charge.
▸
Equity dilution is baked into the model. Shares outstanding rose 6.16% year over year to 624.6M, and the base plan needs $7B of new equity through 2030 (85% contracted). Free cash flow is −$7.69B TTM because capex ($12.46B) runs at 2.6× operating cash flow ($4.77B). EPS growth of 9% requires net income growth well into the double digits just to outrun the share count. If the stock de-rates, each dollar of equity buys less rate base and the arithmetic degrades.
▸
Interest-rate and credit sensitivity. Total debt is $41.65B against $2.02B of cash; interest coverage is 1.87×; projected 2026 FFO/debt is ~16% and debt/EBITDA 5.7×. Management guided to a $240–250M increase in net interest expense in 2026 alone. A utility at 0.40 beta trades like a bond proxy — when long yields rise, the stock falls and the cost of the next $22.8B of debt goes up at the same time.
▸
Data-center load that does not show up — or shows up behind the meter. Only ~2 GW of the 20 GW pipeline is contracted or under construction, and management wants ~1 GW signed in 2026 and ~3 GW more in 2027. Hyperscalers can and do build their own generation. If the pipeline converts slowly, the "13% rate-base CAGR with upside" case collapses back to the 11% base case, and the affordability argument gets harder as remaining customers shoulder the transmission build.

Notable One-Off Items

Wildfire accounting is the main distortion, and it runs through both the income statement and the "ongoing vs GAAP" gap.

Marshall Fire (Colorado, Dec 30, 2021). On September 24, 2025, on the eve of trial in Boulder, Xcel agreed a $640M settlement covering hundreds of homeowner plaintiffs, nearly 200 insurers and public entities, without admitting its equipment caused the fire (which destroyed 1,000+ structures and killed two, with losses above $2B). Per Colorado Sun reporting, Xcel's share was funded by roughly $350M of its $400M insurance tower, with no cost to customers; by early November 2025 more than 2,000 Coloradans had accepted individual settlements. Residual litigation cost Xcel a −$0.04/share adjustment year-to-date 2026, excluded from ongoing EPS.

Smokehouse Creek Fire (Texas, Feb 2024). Xcel accepted early that its facilities were involved in the ignition of the ~1.06-million-acre fire and set up an expedited claims process. As of Q2 2026 it had accrued $460M, finalised roughly $404M of settlements ($398M paid) — 237 of 304 claims resolved, 28 of 73 lawsuits settled or dismissed — against $525M of insurance. The Texas Attorney General's December 2024 suit seeking economic damages, civil penalties and injunctive relief over allegedly century-old poles was still outstanding on the sources fetched; Xcel disputes negligence. If the accrual holds within insurance, the shareholder cost is limited to deductibles, premiums and legal spend.

FY2025 GAAP vs ongoing. GAAP diluted EPS of $3.42 versus ongoing EPS of $3.80 implies about $0.38/share of excluded items in 2025. The line-by-line reconciliation was not verified from the sources fetched; wildfire-related charges are the most likely explanation but that is an inference. Q2 2026 GAAP and ongoing EPS were identical at $0.93.

Weather and fuel. Q2 2026 revenue of $3.12B fell 5.1% year over year and missed consensus by ~$490M, largely on mild weather and lower pass-through fuel and purchased-power costs — neither of which affects earnings much. Do not read that miss as a demand problem: weather-adjusted electric sales rose 2.1%.

Five-year trajectory. From the October 2021 close of $64.59 to the September 4, 2026 close of $75.72, XEL's price rose +17.2% — about 3.3% a year. Add the roughly $10–11 per share of dividends paid over the window (est. from the $1.83 → $2.37 annual dividend path) and the total return is on the order of +33–35%, or roughly 6% a year (est.). 08 Summary & Key Risks

The arithmetic, in order. Start with the rate base — the regulator-approved pool of invested capital the utility is allowed to earn on: $56B in 2025, guided to $94B in 2030, an 11% compound rate, or 13%+ if the $10–13B of line-of-sight projects (SPS generation, data-center transmission) make it into the base plan. Multiply by the allowed ROE — 9.30% at PSCo, 9.6% at NSPM, 9.5% at SPS-New Mexico — on equity layers of 52.5–54.7%, and you have the earnings the regulators have agreed to let Xcel make. That is the "story"; everything else is the drag. Drag one: equity issuance. The plan needs $7B of new shares by 2030; shares outstanding already grew 6.16% in the past year to 624.6M. Drag two: interest. $22.8B of incremental debt on top of $41.65B, with 2026 net interest expense guided up $240–250M, and FFO/debt of only ~16%. Drag three: regulatory lag and affordability — the gap between spending money and being allowed to earn on it, and the political ceiling on how fast bills can rise. Net all of that out and management lands on 9%+ EPS growth through 2030, from a 2026 base of $4.04–$4.16, with a 3.1% dividend on top: a 10%+ total-return promise.

Is 9% believable? The best evidence is the quarter just reported: ongoing EPS +24% to $0.93, with every operating company up, driven by six rate cases settled or decided in one year at constructive returns. That is not a run-rate — 2026 guidance implies roughly 8% for the full year — but it shows the machine converting capital into earnings without regulatory pushback. The consolidated earned ROE of 10.96% for the twelve months to June, above allowed, says the same thing. And the affordability numbers (bills 29% below the national average, 2.0% annual bill growth since 2014) are the reason regulators keep saying yes. The 21-year streak of hitting guidance is a track record, not a guarantee, but for a business whose earnings are quite literally set by commission order, it is a meaningful one.

Data centers, honestly sized. The "20+ GW high-probability portfolio" is a marketing number. The verifiable numbers are: ~1 GW operating or under construction, ~1 GW under signed Energy Service Agreements, ~1 GW targeted to sign in 2026, and 4 GW total by end-2027. At $5–8B of investment per gigawatt, converting even the 2027 target adds $20–30B of potential capital — which is why the base plan can already flex from $60B to $70B+ and the rate-base CAGR from 11% to 13%. The bear's correct response is that hyperscalers build their own generation when utilities are slow, and that only ~10% of the pipeline is contracted. Both things are true. What the stock is paying for is the base case; the pipeline is optionality.

Wildfire, honestly sized. Marshall (Colorado, 2021) settled for $640M on September 24, 2025 with Xcel's share inside its $400M insurance tower and no fault admitted; residual claims cost $0.04/share year-to-date. Smokehouse Creek (Texas, 2024) has a $460M accrual against $525M of insurance, ~$400M already paid or finalised, and an unresolved Texas AG suit. Roughly $1.1B of gross liability from two fires has, so far, been absorbed almost entirely by insurance. That is a good outcome — and a warning. Xcel's territories have no California-style liability fund; the next fire starts from a smaller insurance tower and a more expensive premium. The $5B of wildfire mitigation capex in the plan is both the hedge and, conveniently, rate base.

Valuation. At $75.72, XEL trades at 20.75× trailing GAAP EPS of $3.65, 19.9× FY2025 ongoing EPS of $3.80, 18.5× the 2026 guidance midpoint of $4.10, and 16.7× 2027 consensus of $4.54, with a 3.13% yield. Reported EV/EBITDA is 14.3× and price-to-book 1.98× — i.e. the market pays roughly twice the book value of a rate base earning ~9.5%, which is the standard premium for a utility growing faster than its cost of equity. The PEG of ~2.0 says you are not getting the growth cheaply; the 0.40 beta and the 65% payout on GAAP earnings say you are being paid to wait. The consensus target of $91.76 (+21%) essentially assumes the stock returns to the ~22× forward multiple it carried at the February 2026 high. Whether that happens depends less on Xcel than on the 10-year Treasury yield and on whether the "AI power" trade holds.

Bull Case

A regulated compounder at a discount to its own recent history: 11–13% rate-base growth backed by six settled rate cases at 9.3–9.6% allowed ROEs, 9%+ EPS growth guided through 2030, a 3.1% dividend with 23 years of increases, 85% of the equity need already contracted, and a 20 GW data-center pipeline of which only 2 GW is in the numbers. The two big wildfires are settled or reserved inside insurance. At 18.5× 2026 guidance and 34% of the way up its 52-week range, with a Strong Buy consensus and a $92 average target, the stock is pricing the base case and giving away the upside — and if long rates fall, the multiple re-rates on top.

Bear Case

Free cash flow is −$7.7B, the share count is growing 6% a year, interest coverage is 1.87× and FFO/debt of 16% leaves little room; the business only works if regulators, bond markets and equity markets all keep saying yes simultaneously. A 65% GAAP payout is above the company's own 45–55% target. Only ~10% of the vaunted data-center pipeline is contracted, and hyperscalers can self-generate. Texas and Colorado have no wildfire liability cap, and the Texas Attorney General is still suing. At a PEG of ~2.0 and a 0.40 beta, this is a bond proxy with equity-issuance risk — if the 10-year yield rises 100 bp, the stock falls and the plan's funding cost rises at the same time.

Top 3 Risks

01
Wildfire ignition in a state without a liability backstop. Two fires have consumed roughly $1.1B of gross liability and most of two insurance towers. A third — in a territory that includes the Front Range and the Texas Panhandle, both high-risk — would hit shareholders directly, and the Texas AG suit shows regulators and politicians are not inclined to be gentle. This is the risk that can change the sign of the thesis rather than the magnitude.
02
Funding-cost squeeze. $7B of equity and $22.8B of debt through 2030 have to be raised on terms that keep FFO/debt near 16% and the ratings investment grade. A higher-for-longer rate environment raises the cost of both, compresses the spread between allowed ROE and cost of capital, and de-rates the stock — which then makes each equity raise more dilutive. The feedback loop runs the wrong way.
03
Affordability politics catching up with the capital plan. Xcel's bills are low today, but $70B+ of spending has to be recovered from customers eventually, and data-center load only helps if regulators let the new customers pay for the new wires. A disallowance, a lower allowed ROE in the next round of cases, or a political backlash in Minnesota or Colorado would lower the ceiling on earnings without any operational misstep.
  Industry Commentary
Wildfire Liability Has Become a Western-Utility Tax
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
PG&E's bankruptcy taught the market that a utility's equity can be wiped out by a spark; Hawaiian Electric's 2023 Maui fire repeated the lesson outside California. Xcel's two fires — Marshall ($640M settlement, Sep 2025) and Smokehouse Creek ($460M accrued, Texas AG suit pending) — were absorbed by roughly $925M of combined insurance, but insurers now price that risk into every renewal and Colorado and Texas offer none of California's AB 1054 fund protection. The sector response is to spend on mitigation (Xcel: $5B in the plan) and lobby for liability caps. Until those caps exist, every dry, windy day in the Front Range is an unhedged short option written by shareholders.
The Equity Treadmill: Growth Utilities Are Serial Issuers
ONGOING HEADWIND
The sector's capital plans have roughly doubled since 2020, and because regulators cap equity ratios near 50–55%, every dollar of rate base needs about half a dollar of new equity or retained earnings. Xcel's $7B need through 2030 (shares +6.16% YoY) is typical of its large-cap peers. This is why utility EPS growth of 6–8% requires net-income growth of 10–12%, and why the sector is acutely sensitive to its own share price: a de-rating makes the plan more dilutive, which justifies the de-rating. Forward sales and at-the-market programmes (85% of Xcel's need is already contracted) blunt the risk; they do not remove it.
Load Growth Is Back — and It Is Concentrated
EMERGING TAILWIND
After two decades of flat U.S. electricity demand, data centers, electrification and reshored manufacturing have turned utilities into growth stocks. Xcel's weather-adjusted electric sales rose 2.1% year-to-date with SPS at 4.8%, and its high-probability data-center portfolio exceeds 20 GW against ~2 GW contracted. Bull framing: new large loads pay for the transmission everyone else needs, spreading fixed costs and keeping bills low while rate base compounds at 11–13% — the best set-up the sector has had since the 1960s. Bear framing: pipelines are not contracts, hyperscalers are building their own gas plants, and if load underdelivers after the wires are built, the remaining customers eat the cost and regulators respond by cutting allowed ROEs. The next 18 months of signed Energy Service Agreements will decide which framing is right for Xcel.
