01 Company Overview

Xylem is the plumbing behind the plumbing. It makes and services the pumps, treatment systems, meters and sensors that move, clean and measure water for utilities, factories and buildings in more than 150 countries. It was born on October 31, 2011, when ITT Corporation spun off its water businesses, and it has grown mostly by buying: Sensus, the smart-meter maker, for $1.7B in 2016, then Evoqua Water Technologies, a water and wastewater treatment and services provider, in an all-stock deal valued at $7.5B that closed in May 2023. FY2025 revenue was $9.04B, up 5.52%, with net income of $957M and diluted EPS of $3.92. At $107.24 the market values it at $25.04B, which ranks it #979 in the world by market capitalisation.

Here is the puzzle this report is about. The business keeps getting better on paper — adjusted EBITDA margin up 150 bps in Q2 2026, full-year EPS guidance raised, Q2 orders up 42% and backlog at a reported $5.3B. Yet the stock has fallen from a $150.85 month-end close in October 2025 to $107.24, down 28.9%, and it sits barely above its 52-week low. The market is not arguing with the margins. It is arguing with the top line: organic revenue growth guided to just 2–3% for 2026, smart-meter deployments stalling as utilities go quiet on rate increases, China sales down by a quarter or more, and an 80/20 programme that is deliberately walking away from about 2% of sales. The question for an owner is simple to state: is this a slow-growth pump maker with better margins, or a water-technology compounder going through a clean-up year?

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities

Sources of moat:

Installed base in utilities Multi-decade outsourced water contracts Meter + software lock-in Breadth: pump to meter to treatment Service & aftermarket network Largest pure-play water tech (post-Evoqua) Moderate leverage (D/E 0.29)

The moat is trust in the unglamorous. A water utility does not change pump or meter vendor lightly: the equipment runs for decades, failure is a public-health event, and every meter in the field ties the utility to the software that reads it. Evoqua added the service side — running treatment for industrial customers under long contracts, the kind of deal Dow just signed. The weakness is that none of this protects the pace of spending. Utilities fund capex through regulated rates and municipal budgets, and in 2026 that pace is being set by politics, not by need.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Growth is thin. 2026 guidance is ~$9.2B revenue, +2% reported and +2–3% organic, cut from +2–4% at Q2. Consensus has 2026 revenue at $9.21B (+1.89%). A 25x trailing multiple is hard to hold on 2% growth for long without the margin story continuing.
02
Metering is hostage to rate politics. MCS revenue fell 6% in Q2 and its margin fell about 200bps. Management's own explanation is political — utilities holding back on electric-meter rollouts to avoid rate fights. That is not something Xylem can fix with execution.
03
80/20 shrinks the top line on purpose. Walking away from about 2% of 2026 sales raises margins but makes revenue look worse. Management calls 2026 the "high-water mark" for walkaway. If 2027 walkaway is not "significantly lower", as promised, the growth gap stays open.
04
Deal-making while changing CFO. A $1.46B pump acquisition was announced on August 11 and a new CFO started on September 1, three weeks later. Management maintains a ~$1B-a-year M&A target. The integration and the finance handover now overlap.

Notable One-Off Items

Three things bend the reported numbers. First, buybacks: Xylem repurchased $1.243B of stock in the first half of 2026 — about 5% of today's market cap — including $581M in Q1 under a new $1.5B authorisation. Shares outstanding are now 233.49M, down from roughly 244M diluted in FY2025, so per-share growth is running ahead of profit growth. Second, the GAAP-to-adjusted gap is wide: Q2 GAAP EPS was $1.11 against adjusted $1.46, $0.35 a share in one quarter. The reconciling items were not itemised in the sources fetched beyond $42M of restructuring year to date and a $16M benefit from IEEPA tariff refunds, which the company treats as special. Third, portfolio pruning: divestitures brought in $59M, and 80/20 walkaway removes about 2% of 2026 sales — revenue lost by choice, not to competitors.

RSI measures how one-sided recent price moves have been on a 0–100 scale. Readings above 70 mean the stock has risen hard and fast relative to its own recent history ("overbought"); below 30 means it has fallen hard and fast ("oversold"). It is a measure of momentum, not of value — a cheap stock can stay oversold for a long time.

From the September 2021 month-end close of $123.68 to the September 21, 2026 settled close of $107.24, XYL lost 13.3% of its price over five years. Dividends of roughly $1.12–$1.72 a year add back about $7 a share over the period (our estimate from the annual dividend-per-share series), so total return was still negative, in the high single digits. The endpoints hide a round trip. The stock lost 40% into a $78.18 close in June 2022, nearly doubled to a $150.85 month-end in October 2025, and has since given back 28.9% of that in eleven months.

The 6-period monthly RSI reads 27.7 — oversold. It touched 22.1 in May 2026, the lowest reading since the 2022 rate shock, when it bottomed at 11.9 in April 2022. The 10-month moving average sits at about $120.33, 12% above the price. That is the shape of a stock in a downtrend that has become stretched, not one that has turned. Oversold readings in 2022 were followed by a sharp recovery; they were also accompanied by a market-wide selloff, which is not what is happening now.

08 Summary & Key Risks

Bull Case

Margins are doing exactly what 80/20 promised: adjusted EBITDA up 150bps to 23.3% in Q2, guided to 23.1–23.5% for the year, with EPS guidance raised to $5.55–$5.70. The revenue drag is partly self-inflicted and temporary — about 2% of sales walked away from on purpose, with management saying 2026 is the high-water mark. Orders hit $3.09B in Q2, backlog is a reported $5.3B, and an $850M multi-decade Dow contract shows the outsourced-water model can win big, sticky deals. Data-centre orders are up more than 300%. The pump acquisition buys 30%-plus EBITDA margins at ~11.6x. Meanwhile $1.24B of stock was retired in six months, and every one of 21 analysts has a target at least 20% above the price. If metering recovers after the elections, the growth story comes back with better margins underneath it.

Bear Case

This is a 2–3% organic grower priced at 25.5x trailing earnings. Strip out one contract and the other three segments booked orders at about 1.0x revenue in Q2, with Water Infrastructure orders down 4% organically. Metering — the growth engine of the last cycle — is shrinking because of rate politics Xylem cannot control. China sales fell 30% and 27% in the last two quarters. The company is doing $1.6B-plus of acquisitions in the same quarter it changes CFO, and funding them on top of $1.24B of buybacks. Free cash flow is a 3.8% yield, and only $219M of it arrived in the first half. The market sold the Q2 beat within two days, which says investors want proof of growth, not more margin. A premium multiple with no growth premium to justify it can keep de-rating.

Top 3 Risks

01
Organic growth stays at 2–3%. The guidance range was already cut once this year. At 25.5x trailing EPS the stock needs growth to re-accelerate in 2027; if it does not, margin gains alone may not hold the multiple.
02
Metering delays run longer than an election cycle. MCS revenue fell 6% and its margin about 200bps in Q2 on utility caution over rate increases. Management's explanation is political, and political timetables do not follow guidance.
03
Execution load. A $1.46B acquisition closing in Q4, WaterFleet and TriOS bolt-ons, an ongoing 80/20 programme and a CFO in her first quarter. Any one is manageable. All of them at once raise the odds of a slip in guidance or cash conversion.
Industry Commentary
Utility spending meets rate politics
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT

Water and electric utilities pay for new meters and pipes through the rates they charge customers. In 2026, per Xylem's own CEO, "politicians take a hard stand on electricity rate increases, and utilities have pulled back." The need does not go away — ageing networks still leak and old meters still fail — but spending can be pushed back. For Xylem that shows up first in Measurement & Control Solutions: Q1 orders +15%, Q2 orders −2%, Q2 revenue −6%. The key question for the next two quarters is whether this is a delay or a cancellation. A delay builds pent-up demand; a cancellation resets the growth rate.

Simplification vs. growth
ONGOING HEADWIND

Xylem has spent more than two years, in Grogan's words, "doing 80/20". The result is visible in margins — operating margin from 9.8% in FY2023 to 15.5% TTM — and in revenue, where walking away from low-margin customers and China business takes about 2 points a year off growth. That trade is sensible for owners if the walkaway ends and the remaining business grows. It is expensive if the market decides the slower growth is the real Xylem. Management has put a date on it: 2026 is the "high-water mark", and 2027 walkaway should be "significantly lower". That is a falsifiable promise, and it will be tested on the Q4 call.

Water as an industrial input: data centres, chips, mining
EMERGING TAILWIND

Pine lists AI infrastructure, semiconductors, power generation, mining, food and beverage and life sciences as the growth end-markets — all industries that need a lot of clean water and have to deal with what they discharge. The evidence so far: Applied Water data-centre orders up more than 300% in Q2, data-centre revenue expected to rise about 200% this year, the Dow outsourcing contract, and a pump acquisition aimed at mining and construction. The bull framing: Xylem is moving from selling to cash-strapped utilities to selling to industrial customers with money to spend. The bear framing: data centres will be about 2% of sales by year-end, and one large contract does not make a trend. Both are true today. The one to watch is orders outside the Dow contract.
