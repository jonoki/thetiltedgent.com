01 Company Overview

Yum! Brands franchises and operates quick-service restaurants under KFC, Taco Bell and The Habit Burger & Grill, across 63,000+ restaurants in 155 countries. FY2025 revenue was $8.21B, up 8.8% on FY2024's $7.55B, and the trailing twelve months through June 2026 stand at $8.72B, up 10.3%. It is the fourth-largest US-listed restaurant company by market capitalisation — $39.27B against McDonald's $195.10B, Starbucks $123.66B and Chipotle $41.30B.

The company that name badge describes no longer exists in the form most coverage still assumes. Pizza Hut was sold and the transaction closed in early September 2026 — LongRange Capital took Pizza Hut outside Mainland China for roughly $1.5B, Yum China took the Mainland China business, and the announced package was approximately $2.7B with up to $75M of earn-out through 2030, netting Yum about $2.3B earmarked for debt reduction and buybacks. So this page analyses a two-and-a-half brand company: KFC, Taco Bell, and a small Habit Burger tail. And it analyses it at a moment when the good brand has a problem — a mid-July 2026 Cyclospora food-safety incident knocked Taco Bell same-store sales to −2% through July 27 after a quarter that printed +7%, and management guided Q3 Taco Bell margin to 19–21% against 26.2% in Q2. The central question here is whether a business that is now essentially "KFC's unit pipeline plus Taco Bell's US pricing power" deserves a higher multiple than the four-brand conglomerate did, at exactly the point when Taco Bell's traffic is the variable in doubt.

02 Core Businesses

Read this section through the franchise lens or none of the numbers make sense. Yum reported four divisions in Q2 2026 — KFC, Taco Bell, Pizza Hut and Habit Burger — but what it books as revenue and what its restaurants actually sell are two different scales of number, separated by roughly a factor of eight. In Q1 2026 the three big divisions rang up $16.84B of system sales in a single quarter (KFC $9,328M, Taco Bell $4,394M, Pizza Hut $3,114M), or roughly $68B annualised. Yum's own trailing-twelve-month revenue is $8.72B. The company therefore keeps about 13 cents of revenue per dollar spent in its restaurants — the rest belongs to franchisees. That is the arithmetic signature of a near-fully-franchised system, and it is why the operating metrics that drive this equity are system sales growth, same-store sales and net new units, not the revenue line. A single figure for the franchised percentage (commonly cited at around 98%) was not stated on any page fetched for this report, so it is not asserted here; the 13% revenue-to-system-sales ratio is the verified version of the same fact.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
63,000+ units / 155 countries Asset-light royalty model Master franchisee network Taco Bell US pricing power KFC international build pipeline 61% digital system-sales mix Byte by Yum tech stack

The core advantage is structural rather than culinary. Because franchisees own the real estate and the capital expenditure, Yum converts a $68B-annualised system into an 8.7B revenue line that carries a 32.5% operating margin and a 59.7% ROIC. That ROIC number is the moat rendered numerically: Yum earns sixty cents of operating return on every dollar of capital it still has in the business, because it deliberately stopped having capital in the business. The second layer is distribution — 660 gross openings in a single quarter across 55 markets is a capability, not an event, and it requires a functioning master-franchisee bench in dozens of countries that a challenger brand cannot replicate quickly. The third is digital: 61% of system sales now run through digital channels (KFC 67%, Taco Bell 47%), roughly $11B of quarterly digital system sales, built on the company's proprietary Byte by Yum platform — which converts a franchise system into a first-party data asset the franchisees themselves cannot build alone.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Concentration risk just got worse, deliberately. Selling Pizza Hut removed a drag but also removed diversification. Taco Bell now carries a much larger share of a smaller company's profit, and Taco Bell has exactly one franchise — the US consumer. A brand event at Taco Bell is now a company event; the Cyclospora incident is the live demonstration.
02
Food safety is an uninsurable brand risk. Mid-July 2026 saw Taco Bell sales drop roughly 20% at the trough inside days. Yum does not control the kitchens — franchisees do — but it owns the brand equity that gets damaged. The $2.3B of Pizza Hut proceeds cannot buy back a traffic pattern.
03
Franchisee financial health is an off-balance-sheet exposure. The royalty stream is only as durable as franchisee unit economics. With Pizza Hut US system sales at −5% before the sale, the sector has already shown what happens when operators stop reinvesting: units close, and closures compound against the net-new-unit math that the whole model depends on.
04
International exposure means currency and geopolitics. KFC's growth is overwhelmingly non-US and management reports system sales ex-F/X for a reason — the reported dollar figure and the operating figure routinely diverge. Emerging-market boycott risk and Mainland China exposure (now partly via the Yum China relationship rather than ownership) sit on top of that.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The model, in the order it actually works. Start with system sales: roughly $16.84B in Q1 2026 across the three big divisions, about $68B annualised. Two things move that number, and only two. The first is same-store sales — Q2 2026 gave KFC +2%, Taco Bell +7%, Pizza Hut −1%, Habit +3%. The second is net new units — 1,053 gross openings in Q2 2026 (KFC 660 across 55 countries, Pizza Hut 333, Taco Bell 54, Habit 6) on a base of 63,000+ restaurants, with management guiding 5% unit growth for 2026. Notice how the two combine: KFC's system sales grew 6% on only +2% comps — four points of the growth came from opening restaurants, not from selling more in existing ones. That is the whole franchise flywheel in one line.

Then the royalty conversion, which is where most readers get the scale wrong. Yum's trailing revenue is $8.72B against roughly $68B of system sales — about 13 cents of Yum revenue per system dollar. The company does not own most of the restaurants, does not carry most of the inventory, does not pay most of the staff (49,000 employees against a 63,000-restaurant system — fewer than one Yum employee per restaurant). What it owns is a royalty on other people's operations, and the financial signature of that is unmistakable: 45.31% gross margin, 32.47% operating margin, 59.73% ROIC, and $1.68B of free cash flow on $8.72B of revenue — 19% FCF conversion, from a business that sells fried chicken. This is why the revenue line is the least informative number in the release and why the brief for this report was right to lead with system sales, comps and units.

The negative equity is a feature, and you should still know what it costs you. Shareholders' equity is −$7.11B. That is not distress; it is the accumulated bookkeeping of two decades of refranchising (selling company restaurants to franchisees, booking the proceeds, shrinking the asset base) and share repurchase (retiring equity at prices above book value). Both are rational in a royalty model. The consequence is that ROE and debt-to-equity are arithmetically meaningless — a negative denominator produces numbers that look either spectacular or catastrophic depending on sign convention, and neither describes the business. They are marked n/m in Section 04 rather than printed. Use ROA of 22.69% in place of ROE and debt/EBITDA of 4.21× in place of debt-to-equity. That 4.21× is the number that actually constrains management: it is levered, and the $2.3B of Pizza Hut proceeds going partly to debt reduction is the direct acknowledgement. The other consequence is that there is no equity cushion — the balance sheet cannot absorb a shock, so a prolonged brand impairment would show up as a financing problem, not just an earnings one.

What the Pizza Hut sale actually did. It removed the one division that was shrinking: −1% system sales, −1% global comps, −5% US system sales, and Q1 division profit down 14% while KFC and Taco Bell each grew 16%. It converted a declining royalty stream into roughly $2.3B of net cash. And it concentrated the company — Pizza Hut was 11.7% of Q2 revenue and roughly 18% of system sales, and its removal leaves KFC's international unit pipeline and Taco Bell's US comps carrying essentially all of the story. The market's verdict is legible in the multiple: the stock was $152.03 on September 1, just as the deal closed, and $143.89 on September 10. Selling the weak brand did not re-rate the stock. The Taco Bell problem swamped it.

Valuation — pick the right denominator. On trailing GAAP EPS of $7.94, YUM trades at 18.12×, a 36% discount to the 28.23× restaurant-industry average. That is the seductive number and it is the wrong one: the trailing figure includes a non-core Q2 item that took quarterly net income from $374M to $853M, and the market is telling you so — forward P/E of 22.33× sits above trailing, and consensus 2026 EPS of $6.63 sits below both TTM ($7.94) and the trajectory implied by FY2025's $5.55. On free cash flow, $39.27B of market cap against $1.68B of TTM FCF is 23.4× P/FCF; on enterprise value, $51.27B against the same FCF is roughly 30.5× EV/FCF, with the gap being the $13.41B of debt. The defensible range is 22–24× forward earnings or free cash flow — a market-like multiple for a business growing system sales at 6%, guiding 5% unit growth, and paying a 2.09% dividend at a 37.8% payout. At that multiple you are paid for durability, not growth, and the 2.33 PEG says so plainly.

Bull Case

A cleaner, more focused company at a discount to its industry. Pizza Hut — the only shrinking division — is gone for ~$2.3B net, cutting leverage against 4.21× debt/EBITDA and funding buybacks into a stock 15% off its high. What remains compounds: KFC opened a record 660 restaurants in a single quarter across 55 markets and grew system sales 6% on just 2% comps, Taco Bell posted +7% comps before the July incident, 61% of system sales are digital, and the whole thing throws off $1.68B of free cash flow at 59.73% ROIC. At 22.33× forward against a 28.23× industry average, with a 0.55 beta, 2.09% yield and eight straight years of dividend growth, the Cyclospora hit is a one-quarter problem priced as if permanent.

Bear Case

Five years of ownership produced +15.2% of price return — 2.9% a year — and the trailing 18.12× P/E that makes it look cheap is an artefact of a non-core item that consensus explicitly unwinds ($7.94 TTM EPS versus $6.63 for 2026). Strip it out and you own a mid-single-digit grower at a market multiple with −$7.11B of equity, 4.21× leverage, a 0.59 current ratio and no balance-sheet cushion. Selling Pizza Hut removed diversification exactly as the remaining star brand broke: Taco Bell went +7% comps to −2% in six weeks and guided margin down 500–700bp, and it is now a far larger share of a smaller company. Fifteen of 26 analysts are on Hold, and the lowest published target is 2% above spot.

Top 3 Risks

01
Taco Bell does not recover cleanly from Cyclospora. Comps went from +7% to −2% through July 27, with sales down roughly 20% at the July 18 trough and only half recovered by month end. Management guided Q3 division margin to 19–21% from 26.2% to fund promotional recovery — meaning the fix itself costs margin. Post-divestiture, Taco Bell is a materially larger share of group profit, so a slow recovery is now a group-level earnings event rather than a divisional one. The Q3 print is the test.
02
The trailing multiple normalises against you. At 18.12× trailing the stock screens cheap; at 22.33× forward it does not. If 2026 EPS lands at the $6.63 consensus and the market applies the same 18× it currently appears to pay, that is $119 — 17% below the current price. Nothing needs to go wrong operationally for that arithmetic to bite; the trailing EPS simply has to roll off. This is the most underappreciated risk on the page because the headline P/E actively conceals it.
03
Unit growth is the whole model, and it is franchisee-funded. KFC's 6% system sales growth on 2% comps means two-thirds of the growth is construction. That construction is paid for by franchisees, whose willingness to build depends on their own unit economics, their cost of capital, and their confidence in the brand. If financing conditions tighten or franchisee returns compress, the 5% unit growth guide slips — and because Yum's revenue is a royalty on system sales, a slowdown in openings flows straight through to the top line with no operating lever to offset it.
  Industry Commentary
Food Safety Is the Sector's Uninsurable Risk
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A franchisor owns the brand and collects the royalty, but does not run the kitchens. That separation is the source of the 59.73% ROIC — and the source of this risk. Taco Bell's mid-July 2026 Cyclospora incident cut sales roughly 20% within days, left comps at −2% through July 27 against +7% for the quarter, and forced a guided margin step-down from 26.2% to 19–21% to pay for the recovery. The sector has a long memory of this pattern: brand damage arrives in days, recovery takes quarters, and the company that bears the earnings cost is the one furthest from the fryer. Yum's post-divestiture concentration makes the exposure larger, not smaller.
The Value-War Squeeze on the Franchisee
ONGOING HEADWIND
US quick-service has spent two years competing on value menus while food and labour costs stayed elevated. The franchisor is structurally insulated — royalties are a percentage of sales, so discounting that holds traffic flat in units but cuts franchisee margin still pays Yum. The franchisee is not insulated, and Yum's growth depends on that franchisee choosing to build the next restaurant. The visible casualty in Yum's own portfolio was Pizza Hut US at −5% system sales, sold in September 2026. The industry aggregate tells the same story: a 11.12% average profit margin across 54 listed restaurant stocks, against Yum's 25.41% — the franchisors keep the margin, the operators absorb the cycle.
Digital Is Becoming the Franchisor's Second Product
EMERGING TAILWIND
Yum now runs 61% of system sales through digital channels — roughly $11B in a single quarter, with KFC at 67% and Taco Bell at 47% — on its proprietary Byte by Yum platform. That converts a franchise agreement into a data relationship: the franchisor sees demand, can price and promote centrally, and can sell technology back into its own system. Bull framing: if Byte becomes a licensed platform with its own economics, Yum's multiple stops being a restaurant multiple; digital penetration this high in a 63,000-unit system is an asset no competitor can assemble quickly. Bear framing: no separate revenue or margin disclosure for Byte appeared on any page fetched for this report, so the platform story is currently an assertion rather than a checkable line item — and the only numbers you can actually verify are 22.33× forward earnings and 2.9% five-year price CAGR.
