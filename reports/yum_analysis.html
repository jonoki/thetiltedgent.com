01 Company Overview

Yum! Brands franchises and operates quick-service restaurants under KFC, Taco Bell and The Habit Burger & Grill: 35,421 KFC and 9,102 Taco Bell restaurants at September 30, 2026, plus Habit. Q3 2026 revenue was $2,054M, up 4% on the year-ago quarter, and the trailing twelve months through September stand at $8.80B. It is the fourth-largest US-listed restaurant company by market capitalisation — $43.23B against McDonald's $195.16B, Starbucks $120.19B and Chipotle $43.56B (our calculation at the Nov 6 close).

Pizza Hut is gone. Yum signed two agreements to sell it on June 16, 2026 (the company's 8-K of that date): Yum China took the mainland-China business on Aug 7, and LongRange Capital's vehicle bought the rest on Sept 1 for $1,488M in cash. The company says the two sales brought in $2,380M so far this year, booked a $1,683M pre-tax gain in Q3, and now reports three segments. That gain is why reported earnings per share are $5.92 for the quarter against $1.56 excluding one-off items. The other story of the quarter is Taco Bell: a cyclospora outbreak that CNBC links to lettuce cut US same-store sales by 4%, but management says sales were up about 1% in the fourth quarter so far and about 3% over the four weeks before the call. The central question is whether a company that is now essentially KFC's restaurant-opening pipeline plus Taco Bell's US pricing power deserves a higher price than the old four-brand company, with the shares at 25.2 times 2026 profit forecasts.

02 Core Businesses

Read this section through the franchise lens or none of the numbers make sense. What Yum books as revenue and what its restaurants actually sell are two different scales of number. In Q3 2026 KFC and Taco Bell restaurants rang up $14.34B of system sales (KFC $10,046M, Taco Bell $4,292M) — system sales being everything customers spend at franchised and company-run restaurants alike. Yum's own revenue from those two divisions was $1,718M, about 12 cents per dollar spent in the restaurants (our calculation); the rest belongs to franchisees. That is what a nearly fully franchised company looks like in numbers, and it is why the figures that drive this stock are system sales growth, same-store sales and net new restaurants, not the revenue line.

03 Competitive Moat & Vulnerabilities
44,500+ KFC and Taco Bell restaurants Asset-light royalty model Master franchisee network Taco Bell US pricing power KFC international build pipeline 64% digital system-sales mix Byte by Yum tech stack

The core advantage is structural rather than culinary. Because franchisees own the real estate and the capital expenditure, Yum converts roughly $57B of annualised KFC and Taco Bell system sales (Q3's $14.34B × 4, our annualisation) into an $8.80B revenue line (trailing twelve months, Pizza Hut part-included) that carries a 32.0% operating margin and a 51.8% ROIC (stockanalysis.com). That ROIC number is the moat rendered numerically: Yum earns fifty cents of operating return on every dollar of capital it still has in the business, because it deliberately stopped having capital in the business. The second layer is distribution — 878 gross KFC openings in one quarter across 66 countries is a capability, not an event, and it needs large franchise operators in dozens of countries, a network a challenger brand cannot replicate quickly. The third is digital: Yum says digital system sales topped $9B in Q3, a 64% mix (worldwide excluding Pizza Hut), built on its in-house Byte by Yum platform — which gives the company its own customer data, something individual franchisees cannot build alone.

Key Vulnerabilities

01
Concentration risk just got worse, deliberately. Selling Pizza Hut removed a drag but also removed diversification. Taco Bell now carries a much larger share of a smaller company's profit, and its sales depend on the US consumer. A brand event at Taco Bell is now a company event; this quarter's outbreak is the live demonstration.
02
Food safety is an uninsurable brand risk. The outbreak turned Taco Bell's quarter from growth to a 4% same-store sales decline and cut company-restaurant margin by 3.5 points. Yum does not control the kitchens — franchisees do — but it owns the brand equity that gets damaged.
03
Franchisee financial health is an off-balance-sheet exposure. The royalty stream is only as durable as franchisee economics. KFC US system sales fell 6% in Q3 (CNBC) and the KFC US business is, in Turner's description on the call, still in a turnaround; when operators stop reinvesting, restaurants close and closures work against the net-new-restaurant math the model depends on.
04
International exposure means currency and geopolitics. KFC's growth is overwhelmingly non-US and management reports system sales excluding currency for a reason — in Q3 KFC system sales grew 8% as reported and 6% excluding currency. China same-store sales declined in Q3 per management.

RSI compares the size of recent gains with the size of recent losses on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 the stock has risen hard enough, fast enough, that buyers may be exhausted; below 30 the reverse. It says nothing about whether a business is good — only about whether a price move has run ahead of itself.

Five years, and not far. From the December 2021 monthly close of $138.86 to $161.14 on November 6, 2026, YUM returned +16.0% on price alone over 4.85 years — about 3.1% a year compounded (price only; dividends not included). That is the profile the 0.53 beta advertises: this stock does not crash and it does not run away. The five-year path is a wide, slow range — a monthly-close low of $106.34 (September 2022) and a high of $168.16 (February 2026), a band of roughly 58% top to bottom.

The interesting feature is where the range broke. Through the end of 2024 YUM's month-end closes stayed between about $106 and $141 after the 2021 peak and never escaped. The breakout came in February 2025, when the monthly close jumped from $130.50 to $156.37 — a 19.8% single-month move — and the stock has not closed below $130 at a month-end since. It topped out at $168.16 in February 2026, fell to a 52-week low of $134.26 intraday on September 18, 2026 (a closing low of $135.75 on September 17), and has since recovered 18.7% from that closing low to $161.14, 4.2% below the February high.

08 Summary & Key Risks

The model, in the order it actually works. Start with system sales: $14.34B in Q3 2026 across KFC and Taco Bell, about $57B annualised (our ×4). Two things move that number. The first is same-store sales — Q3 2026 gave KFC +1%, Taco Bell −4%, Habit flat, and worldwide excluding Pizza Hut flat. The second is net new units — 955 gross openings in Q3 2026 excluding Pizza Hut (KFC 878, Taco Bell 73, Habit 4), taking units up 7% year over year, against a long-term target of 5% a year. Notice how the two combine: KFC's system sales grew 6% excluding currency on only +1% same-store sales — five points of the growth came from opening restaurants, not from selling more in existing ones. That is the whole franchise model in one line.

Then the royalty conversion, which is where most readers get the scale wrong. Yum's Q3 revenue from KFC and Taco Bell was $1,718M against $14.34B of their system sales — about 12 cents of Yum revenue per system dollar. The company does not own most of the restaurants, does not carry most of the inventory and does not pay most of the staff. What it owns is a royalty on other people's operations, and the signature is unmistakable: 44.70% gross margin, 32.01% operating margin (trailing, stockanalysis.com), 51.78% ROIC, and $1.62B of free cash flow on $8.80B of revenue — 18% FCF conversion, from a business that sells fried chicken. This is why the revenue line is the least informative number in the release and why this report leads with system sales, same-store sales and units.

The negative equity is a feature, and you should still know what it costs you. Shareholders' equity is −$6.17B. That is not distress; it is the accumulated bookkeeping of two decades of refranchising (selling company restaurants to franchisees, booking the proceeds, shrinking the asset base) and share repurchase (retiring equity at prices above book value). The consequence is that ROE and debt-to-equity are arithmetically meaningless — marked n/m in Section 04. Use ROA of 22.05% in place of ROE and debt/EBITDA of 4.00× in place of debt-to-equity. The Pizza Hut proceeds changed the balance sheet: cash rose to $2,108M from $709M at year-end, long-term debt fell to $9,459M from $11,872M, and short-term borrowings rose to $2,141M from $38M as debt moved towards maturity; the CFO said Yum expects to address $1.6B of maturing debt by the first half of 2027. There is no equity cushion, so a prolonged brand impairment would show up as a financing problem, not just an earnings one.

What the Pizza Hut sale actually did. It removed the one division that was shrinking — its Q3 operating profit fell 41% to $50M — and converted it into $2,380M of proceeds and a one-off $1,683M pre-tax gain. It also concentrated the company: KFC's international openings and Taco Bell's US same-store sales now carry essentially all of the story. The market's verdict so far: the stock was $152.03 on September 1, the day the last piece closed, fell to a $135.75 closing low on September 17, and has recovered to $161.14 after the Q3 report showed Taco Bell's sales recovering. The year-to-date buyback is $1,202M and dividends paid $616M, together the $1.8B the company says it has returned.

Valuation — pick the right denominator. On trailing GAAP EPS of $12.40, YUM trades at 13.00× — an artefact of the sale gain. On consensus 2026 EPS of $6.40 it trades at 25.18×, and the nine-month adjusted EPS of $4.68 implies about $1.72 for Q4 to reach that number (our subtraction). On free cash flow, $43.23B of market cap against $1.62B of TTM FCF is 26.7× P/FCF (our division); on enterprise value of $53.87B (stockanalysis.com), about 33×, the gap being $12.75B of debt net of $2.11B of cash. Adjusted EPS fell 1% in Q3 and core operating profit 4%, and the 2.52 PEG says the market is already paying for growth that has yet to show up in a quarter.

Bull Case

A cleaner, more focused company. Pizza Hut — the only shrinking division — is gone for $2,380M of proceeds, leaving a business that opens restaurants quickly: KFC added 878 in one quarter and grew units 7%, and international KFC outside China grew system sales 9%. Taco Bell's outbreak slump is fading — same-store sales up about 1% so far in Q4 and about 3% over the last four weeks, with heavy users fully back — and the CFO has pointed to a better restaurant margin in Q4. Sixty-four percent of system sales are digital, free cash flow is $1.62B at a 51.78% ROIC, and the 1.86% dividend has grown eight years running. With the sale done and the debt and buyback plans in motion, the Q3 dip looks like a one-quarter problem.

Bear Case

Five years of ownership produced +16.0% of price return — 3.1% a year — and the 13.00× trailing P/E that makes the stock look cheap is an artefact of a one-off gain: the market is paying 25.18× 2026 consensus for a company whose adjusted EPS fell 1% in Q3 and whose core operating profit fell 4%. Strip out the gain and you own a company growing in the mid-single digits with −$6.17B of equity, 4.00× leverage, $1.6B of debt to deal with by mid-2027 and no balance-sheet cushion. Selling Pizza Hut removed diversification just as Taco Bell wobbled, KFC US system sales are falling 6%, and Taco Bell's recovery is still partial — medium and light users are at 95% of earlier visit levels. Thirteen of 26 analysts are on Hold, one is on Sell, and the lowest published target ($150) is below the price.

Top 3 Risks

01
Taco Bell's recovery stalls. Same-store sales were −4% in Q3 and are up about 1% so far in Q4, with company-restaurant margin at 20.2% against 23.7% a year ago; the CFO described a Q4 range of 21.5% to 23.5%, still below last year's Q3 margin. Post-divestiture, Taco Bell is a materially larger share of group profit, so a slow recovery is now a group-level earnings event. The Q4 print is the test.
02
The earnings base is thinner than the price implies. Adjusted EPS fell 1% in Q3 and core operating profit 4% (1% growth excluding Pizza Hut). At 25.18× consensus 2026 EPS the shares need growth to resume; the headline 13.00× trailing P/E hides this because trailing EPS includes a $1,683M pre-tax sale gain.
03
Unit growth is the whole model, and it is franchisee-funded. KFC's 6% system sales growth on 1% same-store sales means five-sixths of the growth is construction. That construction is paid for by franchisees, whose willingness to build depends on their own unit economics, their cost of capital and their confidence in the brand. If financing conditions tighten or franchisee returns compress, openings slow — and because Yum's revenue is a royalty on system sales, a slowdown flows straight through to the top line.
  Industry Commentary
Food Safety Is the Sector's Uninsurable Risk
CRITICAL CONSTRAINT
A franchisor owns the brand and collects the royalty, but does not run the kitchens. That separation is the source of the 51.78% ROIC — and the source of this risk. The cyclospora outbreak that CNBC links to lettuce cut Taco Bell's Q3 US same-store sales by 4% and its company-restaurant margin from 23.7% to 20.2%. Management says the brand has since rebounded, with same-store sales up about 1% in Q4 so far. Brand damage arrives in days and recovery takes quarters; the company that bears the earnings cost is the one furthest from the fryer. Yum's post-divestiture concentration makes the exposure larger, not smaller.
The Value-War Squeeze on the Franchisee
ONGOING HEADWIND
US quick-service has spent two years competing on value menus while food and labour costs stayed elevated. The franchisor is structurally insulated — royalties are a percentage of sales, so a discount that holds traffic still pays Yum. The franchisee is not insulated, and Yum's growth depends on that franchisee choosing to build the next restaurant. The visible casualty in Yum's own portfolio was Pizza Hut, whose division profit fell 41% in Q3 before it was sold. The industry aggregate tells the same story: a 12.35% average profit margin across listed restaurant stocks, against about 20.8% for Yum's Q3 net income excluding one-off items — the franchisors keep the margin, the operators absorb the cycle.
Digital Is Becoming the Franchisor's Second Product
EMERGING TAILWIND
Yum reports digital system sales above $9B in Q3, a 64% mix (worldwide excluding Pizza Hut), run on its in-house Byte by Yum platform. That converts a franchise agreement into a data relationship: the franchisor sees demand, can price and promote centrally, and can sell technology back into its own system. Bull framing: if Byte becomes a licensed platform with its own economics, investors may stop valuing Yum like a restaurant company. Bear framing: there is no separate revenue or margin disclosure for Byte, so the platform story is an assertion rather than a checkable line item — and the checkable numbers are 25.18× forward earnings and a 3.1% five-year price CAGR.
