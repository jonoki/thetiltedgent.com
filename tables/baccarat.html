Game 04

Baccarat: coin-flipping in a tuxedo.

The game of Bond, of Macau's billion-dollar rooms, and of people squeezing cards like they're reading tea leaves. Underneath the ceremony it's the simplest game in the building: two hands are dealt by a fixed rulebook, you guess which will be closer to nine, and you have exactly zero influence on anything. The edge is low. The decisions are none. The pace is fast.

How it plays

Two hands are dealt, called Player and Banker — neither is yours. Cards count at face value, tens and pictures are zero, and only the last digit of the total matters (7 + 8 = 15 = 5). Each hand gets two cards; a fixed table of rules decides whether either draws a third. Closest to nine wins. You bet on Player (pays 1:1), Banker (pays 1:1 minus a 5% commission), or Tie (pays 8:1). That's it. There is no strategy because there are no decisions; the "drawing rules" happen automatically.

Banker wins slightly more often than Player (about 45.9% vs 44.6% of all hands, the rest ties) because Banker draws last and reacts to Player's card. The 5% commission exists to claw that back, and it doesn't quite manage it: Banker at 1.06% remains marginally better than Player at 1.24%. That is the entire strategic content of baccarat, and I've just given it to you.

The quirks

  • The scorecards are astrology. Every table hands out a grid for tracking "roads" and "patterns" — big road, bead plate, cockroach pig. Each hand is independent. The patterns are real in the sense that a coin flipped 60 times makes patterns. They predict nothing. The casino provides them because people who chase patterns bet more.
  • The squeeze is theatre. Slowly bending the corner of a card to reveal it changes nothing about the card. It does slow the game down, which — lower hands per hour — is the one thing that actually reduces your hourly cost.
  • "Commission-free" baccarat isn't. EZ Baccarat and its cousins drop the 5% commission but make a winning Banker three-card 7 a push. The edge on Banker becomes about 1.02% — roughly the same — and the new side bets (Dragon 7, Panda 8) run 7–10%.
  • Mini-baccarat is the real game. Same rules, dealer handles all the cards, 120–150 hands an hour instead of 40. Same 1.06% edge, three to four times the hourly bleed. The elegant big table is the cheaper one.
  • The Tie pays 8:1 on a 9.5:1 shot. Some tables pay 9:1, which drops the edge to about 4.8%. Still bad, but fourteen percent is a proposition bet, not a hand.

Best bets
  • Banker, every hand, at the big slow table. 1.06% and forty hands an hour is a cheap evening in a nice room.
  • Player if you can't stand paying commission. It costs you 0.18% for the privilege.
Worst bets
  • The Tie. 14.36%. The worst main bet in the casino outside the craps props and the Big Six wheel.
  • Pair bets and the EZ side bets. 7–10%: seven to ten times the price of the game you sat down for.
  • Switching sides because of the scorecard. Free, in the sense that it doesn't change the edge — but it usually comes with pressing bets, which does.

Edge sorting, and the $20 million lesson

Card counting doesn't work here. The drawing rules are so symmetric that even a perfect count produces a playable edge on Banker or Player almost never; the Tie can be counted, but the opportunities are vanishingly rare. The academic conclusion is that baccarat is effectively count-proof.

Edge sorting is the famous exception. Some card backs are printed with a pattern that's asymmetric by a fraction of a millimetre. If a player can persuade the dealer to rotate the high cards 180° ("for luck") and the casino uses an automatic shuffler that doesn't turn cards, the player can identify high and low first cards from the back before betting — worth a player edge of roughly 6–7%. A world-famous poker player and a partner used it to win about $10 million from an Atlantic City casino and about £7.7 million from a London one in 2012. Both casinos refused to pay or sued to recover. Both won: the UK Supreme Court (2017) and the US Third Circuit (2019) held that the technique was cheating or a breach of the gaming contract, even though the player never touched the cards. The law's line: exploiting the casino's mistake in your head is fine; getting the casino to alter the game for you is not.

Loss rebates are the legal cousin. High rollers negotiate a discount on losses (say, 20% back on a losing trip). One Atlantic City player in 2011 negotiated a rebate and rule set so generous that, combined with a disciplined stop-loss, he had a positive expectation — and took about $15 million across three casinos, in blackjack rather than baccarat, before they stopped offering. Legal, contractual, and unavailable to anyone betting less than five figures a hand.

The Gent's verdict. If you want a low-edge game with no homework, bet Banker at the slowest table you can find and enjoy the ceremony. Just know that you're paying to watch a coin flip in a nice room, and that the scorecard the dealer handed you is a colouring book.
