The Blackjack Family

Blackjack variants: same cards, different contract.

Every variant on this page starts from the same pitch: we'll give you something wonderful — free doubles, the right to swap cards, both dealer cards face up — and take back something you won't notice. What they take back is always one of three things: the dealer's bust (a 22 pushes instead of losing), the blackjack bonus (even money instead of 3:2), or the tens (four per deck, gone). The gift is on the felt in big letters. The price is in the fine print. These are not blackjack with a twist; they are different games that borrow the name, and they need their own strategy charts.

Free Bet Blackjack — the house buys your doubles

Six decks, dealer hits soft 17, blackjack still pays 3:2. The gift: double for free on hard 9, 10 or 11, and split any pair except tens for free — the dealer puts up a lammer for the extra bet, and if you win you're paid on it as if it were your money; if you lose, only your original bet goes. Free splits can be re-split, and you can free-double after a free split. The price: a dealer 22 pushes every hand still standing instead of busting. That one rule is worth about 7% to the house, and the free bets buy back most of it. Net house edge with the right strategy: 1.04%.

The strategy is nearly "take every free bet you're offered", and the chart for the free hands is more aggressive than regular basic strategy because a push costs you nothing on the free money. The mistake players make is the reverse: playing their real-money hands like regular blackjack, where a dealer 22 would have been a win. Against 7 through A you now hit harder totals more often, because standing and waiting for a bust has lost some of its value.

Blackjack Switch — two hands, and you may trade cards

You must play two hands of equal size. After the deal you may swap the second cards between them: 10-6 and 5-A becomes 10-A and 5-6. Any blackjack made by switching is just 21. The gifts are enormous — you turn two stiffs into a twenty and an eleven. The prices: blackjack pays even money, and a dealer 22 pushes all your standing hands. Six decks, dealer hits soft 17: 0.58% with the full switching strategy, which is about the same as ordinary blackjack — except you learned two charts (the switch decision and the post-switch play, which is also more aggressive against 7–A because of the push-22 rule) for the privilege.

The simple switching rule ranks final hands 21, 20, 19, A-A, 11, 10, 9, 18/8 and picks the arrangement with the best hand; against a dealer 7 or 8 you balance the two hands instead. The advanced strategy scores each hand against the upcard and costs 0.08% versus the simple rule's 0.17%. Dealer stands on soft 17 takes 0.30% off; eight decks adds 0.02%.

Spanish 21 — take away the tens, give back everything else

Six or eight 48-card decks with the four tens removed (the face cards stay). Removing tens is worth about 2% to the house; the rulebook then hands most of it back: player 21 always wins, player blackjack beats dealer blackjack, late surrender, double on any number of cards, double after split, re-split aces and hit them, and "double-down rescue" (surrender the doubled portion after a bad card). Plus bonuses: five-card 21 pays 3:2, six-card 2:1, seven-card 3:1; 6-7-8 and 7-7-7 pay 3:2 mixed, 2:1 suited, 3:1 in spades. House edge: 0.40% with dealer standing on soft 17; 0.42% hitting soft 17 with redoubling; 0.76% hitting soft 17 without it. The best of the family on paper — and the strategy chart is the most different from regular blackjack of any game here, because the multi-card bonuses change when you hit and when you double.

Double Exposure — you see everything, and ties go to the house

Both dealer cards are dealt face up. That is worth roughly 10% to the player and the game takes it all back: the dealer wins every tie except a blackjack against a blackjack, player blackjack pays even money, and the doubling and splitting rules are usually clipped (split once only; double on 9–11 or 10–11 only). The house edge swings wildly with the fine print: 0.26% at the best-known rule set, 0.66–0.96% at typical ones, 1.47% at the worst. Read the placard before you sit — there is no such thing as "the" Double Exposure edge.

Super Fun 21 — the one that isn't

Single deck usually, and a gift basket: late surrender on any number of cards, surrender after doubling, double on any number of cards, a five-card 21 pays 2:1 instantly, six cards totalling 20 or less wins automatically, and a blackjack in diamonds pays 2:1. The price is one line: every other blackjack pays even money. Since blackjacks are 4.7% of hands and the 3:2 bonus is worth 2.3%, that single change costs more than all the gifts give back. House edge 1.16% single deck, 1.30% two decks, 1.40% six decks. Playable; not a bargain.

The quirks, across the family

  • Push 22 is the tax. In Free Bet and Switch, a dealer 22 pushes instead of losing. It is worth about 7% to the house before the gifts are handed back, and it changes strategy: you can no longer stand on a stiff and hope the dealer busts as often, so the charts hit more against 7–A. Play regular basic strategy at a push-22 table and you're giving back roughly 0.5–1%.
  • Even-money blackjack is the other tax. Switch, Double Exposure and Super Fun 21 all use it. It's the same 1.4% haircut a 6:5 table takes on a regular game, and it's just as quiet.
  • The gifts only pay if you use them. Spanish 21's double-on-any-cards and rescue rules, Free Bet's free splits, Switch's trades — a player who declines the aggressive plays because they feel risky is paying the full price and pocketing none of the refund.
  • Each variant needs its own chart. Regular basic strategy is wrong at every one of these tables, in different places. That's the real cost: three or four more charts to memorise.
  • Card counting is weaker at push-22 games (the dealer busting less blunts the value of a ten-rich shoe) and at Spanish 21 (fewer tens to begin with), and about as strong as usual at Double Exposure — which is why Double Exposure games tend to have the worst doubling rules.

Best bets
  • Spanish 21 at a stand-on-17 table, played to its own chart: 0.40% and the most player-friendly rulebook in the casino.
  • Blackjack Switch with the full strategy — 0.58%, and the most interesting decision in any table game.
  • Free Bet, taking every free double and split the game offers. Decline them and you've kept the tax and refused the refund.
Worst bets
  • The Push 22 side bet. 11.76% — betting on the rule that's already taxing you.
  • Double Exposure at a "double 10–11 only, split once" table. Seeing both cards feels like cheating; at 1.47% it isn't.
  • Any of these with the regular blackjack chart in your head. The variant's whole edge structure is different; the wrong chart costs more here than at a normal table.

Counting the variants

Free Bet and Switch. The count works but pays less: a ten-rich shoe helps the player mainly by busting the dealer, and push 22 removes part of that. Published analyses put the counter's edge at roughly half of what the same count earns at a 3:2 game, though the free bets in Free Bet get more valuable as the count rises (free doubles on 10 and 11 are worth more with tens coming), and some counters specialise in it precisely because the pit isn't watching those tables as closely.

Spanish 21. Counting is legal and possible, but the tens are already gone, so the swings in ten-density are smaller and the bonus rules complicate the index plays; the practical edge is thin. Double Exposure counts about as well as regular blackjack, which is why its doubling rules are usually the most restrictive in the building. Super Fun 21 is single-deck and countable, with the same shallow-penetration defences every single-deck game runs.

Legality and acceptance are identical to blackjack's: your brain is legal, devices are not, and a casino that identifies you will back you off from any game in the family. Hole-carding is off the table at Double Exposure for the obvious reason.

The Gent's verdict. Spanish 21 and Blackjack Switch are real games with real edges under a percent — but only after you've learned a chart that contradicts the one you already know. Free Bet is a fair price for the fun of doubling with the house's money. Double Exposure depends entirely on the placard. Super Fun 21 charges you 3:2 for a bag of features. In every case the gift is the marketing and the push-22 or even-money line is the product; decide whether you're buying before you sit.
