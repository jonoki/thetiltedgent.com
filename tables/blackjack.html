Game 01

Blackjack: the only game where the house shows you its hand.

Well, half of it. The dealer plays by a rulebook printed on the felt, you get to see one of their cards, and you get to make every decision. That combination doesn't exist anywhere else in the building, and it's why the smart money has been sitting here since 1962. The catch: the casino knows it too, and has spent sixty years quietly making the game worse.

How it plays

You and the dealer each get two cards; the dealer shows one. Get closer to 21 than the dealer without going over. Cards are face value, pictures are 10, aces are 1 or 11. You can hit (take a card), stand, double (double your bet, take exactly one card), split a pair into two hands, or sometimes surrender (fold for half). The dealer has no choices: they hit to 16 and stand on 17. A two-card 21 is a blackjack and pays 3:2 — or it used to.

The whole edge lives in an asymmetry: if you bust, you lose even if the dealer busts afterwards. That single rule is worth about 8% to the house. Everything you're allowed to do — doubling, splitting, 3:2 on naturals, seeing the upcard — claws most of it back. Play the mathematically correct decision every time ("basic strategy", a chart you can learn in an afternoon and that casinos will happily sell you in the gift shop) and you're left paying roughly half a percent.

The quirks

  • Rules are the price tag. Dealer hits soft 17 (+0.22%), no doubling after splits (+0.14%), 8 decks instead of 1 or 2, no surrender — each is a small tax. The rules are posted on the table. Read them the way you'd read a fund's fee schedule.
  • 6:5 is the great heist of the century. Paying 6:5 on a blackjack instead of 3:2 adds about 1.4% to the house edge — it roughly triples the cost of the game — and it's now the default on low-limit tables in the big tourist rooms. A $25 single-deck 6:5 game is worse than a $25 8-deck 3:2 game. Walk.
  • Continuous shufflers remove any counting possibility and speed up the game by ~20%, which costs you 20% more per hour even at the same edge.
  • "Insurance" is a side bet that the dealer has a 10 under their ace. It pays 2:1 on a roughly 9:4 shot. House edge about 7.4% in a 6-deck game. It is not insurance; it's a proposition bet with a reassuring name. Same with "even money" on your blackjack — that's insurance wearing a hat.
  • Your neighbour's play doesn't change your odds. The idiot at third base who hits 16 against a 6 hurts your long-run results exactly zero. He also helps them zero. The tilt he causes is real, though, and that's on you.

Best bets
  • The main bet at a 3:2 table with the most liberal rules you can find, played to the chart.
  • Doubling and splitting when the chart says so — these are the bets where you're the favourite. Refusing to double 11 vs 6 because "you're nervous" is a donation.
  • Surrender 16 vs 10 and 15 vs 10 when offered. Losing half is the good outcome.
Worst bets
  • Any 6:5 table. Not a bet, a tax bracket.
  • Insurance. Unless you're counting, never.
  • Side bets. Every one of them is a 3–25% game bolted onto a 0.5% game so you'll forget which one you sat down for.

Card counting, and its sharper cousins

How it works. When a lot of small cards have been dealt, the remaining deck is rich in tens and aces, which favours the player (more blackjacks at 3:2, more dealer busts, better doubles). A counter keeps a running tally — the Hi-Lo system adds one for every 2–6 and subtracts one for every 10 or ace — divides by the decks remaining, and bets big only when the count says the next hand is +EV. It's not memorising cards; it's tracking one integer while looking bored. Done well against a good game, the player edge is about 0.5–1.5%, with brutal variance. Beyond counting there's shuffle tracking (following clumps of high cards through a sloppy shuffle), hole-carding (a dealer who flashes the down card) and ace sequencing — rarer, stronger, and all reliant on the casino's mistakes rather than yours.

Legality. Using your brain is legal everywhere. Using a device — a phone app, a hidden computer, anything that "projects the outcome" — is a felony in Nevada and most other jurisdictions, and the definition is broad enough that you should assume "any device" means any device. Team play with signals is legal as long as no device is involved; casinos treat it as war.

Acceptance. In most of the world, a private business can refuse to serve you, and a casino that identifies a counter will "back you off" (you can play anything but blackjack), flat-bet you, shuffle up on you, or trespass you entirely. New Jersey is the famous exception — since a 1982 court ruling Atlantic City casinos can't bar counters, so they respond with countermeasures: 8 decks, deep cut cards, continuous shufflers. Casinos also share photos and names through surveillance networks. The bottom line for a hobbyist: you can learn to count for the pleasure of knowing how the game really works. Trying to make a living at it is a job with a boss who fires you on sight.

The Gent's verdict. Learn basic strategy cold, find a 3:2 table with decent rules, bet what you can afford to lose fifteen times in a row, and blackjack is the cheapest evening in the house — at $25 a hand it costs about the price of a cocktail per hour in expectation. Sit at a 6:5 table with a side bet, and it's just a slot machine with a dress code.
