Where the numbers come from. House edges are the standard published figures for each game's rules and paytable (the Wizard of Odds compilations, which are the industry reference), quoted as the edge on the amount initially bet unless stated. Casinos vary their rules and paytables constantly; the edge on the table in front of you is whatever its rules say, and this page teaches you how to read them. Nothing here is advice to gamble — it's the price list.

How the grade works

Five things I weigh before I sit.

A grade isn't just the house edge. A 1% game that drains $2,000 an hour is worse for you than a 3% game that drains $60. Here's the scorecard behind every letter.

01

House edge

What the best available bet costs, and how badly the typical player overpays it. Weighted heaviest — it's the rent.

02

Decision leverage

Do your choices move the number? Blackjack: enormously. Baccarat: not at all. Skill you can learn is edge you can keep.

03

Pace & bleed

Edge × bet × decisions per hour = what the night costs. Slots resolve 600 times an hour; roulette, 40. Speed is a hidden multiplier.

04

Trap density

How many bets on the layout exist purely to punish people who don't read? Craps and blackjack side bets are minefields around a good core.

05

Fun per dollar

Entertainment is a legitimate purchase. A game that's social, slow and cheap earns credit — you're paying for the evening, not the outcome.

Advantage play is noted separately for each game: what it is, whether it's legal, and how the house treats you if it notices. It never changes the grade — the grade is for the player the casino expects, not the one it fears.

Game 01

Blackjack: the only game where the house shows you its hand.

Well, half of it. The dealer plays by a rulebook printed on the felt, you get to see one of their cards, and you get to make every decision. That combination doesn't exist anywhere else in the building, and it's why the smart money has been sitting here since 1962. The catch: the casino knows it too, and has spent sixty years quietly making the game worse.

Gent's Grade
Aat 3:2 · C at 6:5
House edge0.3–0.6% (basic strategy) Decision leverageHighest on the floor Pace & bleed~70 hands/hr, low Trap densitySide bets, 6:5, insurance Fun per dollarHigh, social

How it plays

You and the dealer each get two cards; the dealer shows one. Get closer to 21 than the dealer without going over. Cards are face value, pictures are 10, aces are 1 or 11. You can hit (take a card), stand, double (double your bet, take exactly one card), split a pair into two hands, or sometimes surrender (fold for half). The dealer has no choices: they hit to 16 and stand on 17. A two-card 21 is a blackjack and pays 3:2 — or it used to.

The whole edge lives in an asymmetry: if you bust, you lose even if the dealer busts afterwards. That single rule is worth about 8% to the house. Everything you're allowed to do — doubling, splitting, 3:2 on naturals, seeing the upcard — claws most of it back. Play the mathematically correct decision every time ("basic strategy", a chart you can learn in an afternoon and that casinos will happily sell you in the gift shop) and you're left paying roughly half a percent.

The quirks

  • Rules are the price tag. Dealer hits soft 17 (+0.22%), no doubling after splits (+0.14%), 8 decks instead of 1 or 2, no surrender — each is a small tax. The rules are posted on the table. Read them the way you'd read a fund's fee schedule.
  • 6:5 is the great heist of the century. Paying 6:5 on a blackjack instead of 3:2 adds about 1.4% to the house edge — it roughly triples the cost of the game — and it's now the default on low-limit tables in the big tourist rooms. A $25 single-deck 6:5 game is worse than a $25 8-deck 3:2 game. Walk.
  • Continuous shufflers remove any counting possibility and speed up the game by ~20%, which costs you 20% more per hour even at the same edge.
  • "Insurance" is a side bet that the dealer has a 10 under their ace. It pays 2:1 on a roughly 9:4 shot. House edge about 7.4% in a 6-deck game. It is not insurance; it's a proposition bet with a reassuring name. Same with "even money" on your blackjack — that's insurance wearing a hat.
  • Your neighbour's play doesn't change your odds. The idiot at third base who hits 16 against a 6 hurts your long-run results exactly zero. He also helps them zero. The tilt he causes is real, though, and that's on you.

Card counting, and its sharper cousins

How it works. When a lot of small cards have been dealt, the remaining deck is rich in tens and aces, which favours the player (more blackjacks at 3:2, more dealer busts, better doubles). A counter keeps a running tally — the Hi-Lo system adds one for every 2–6 and subtracts one for every 10 or ace — divides by the decks remaining, and bets big only when the count says the next hand is +EV. It's not memorising cards; it's tracking one integer while looking bored. Done well against a good game, the player edge is about 0.5–1.5%, with brutal variance. Beyond counting there's shuffle tracking (following clumps of high cards through a sloppy shuffle), hole-carding (a dealer who flashes the down card) and ace sequencing — rarer, stronger, and all reliant on the casino's mistakes rather than yours.

Legality. Using your brain is legal everywhere. Using a device — a phone app, a hidden computer, anything that "projects the outcome" — is a felony in Nevada and most other jurisdictions, and the definition is broad enough that you should assume "any device" means any device. Team play with signals is legal as long as no device is involved; casinos treat it as war.

Acceptance. In most of the world, a private business can refuse to serve you, and a casino that identifies a counter will "back you off" (you can play anything but blackjack), flat-bet you, shuffle up on you, or trespass you entirely. New Jersey is the famous exception — since a 1982 court ruling Atlantic City casinos can't bar counters, so they respond with countermeasures: 8 decks, deep cut cards, continuous shufflers. Casinos also share photos and names through surveillance networks. The bottom line for a hobbyist: you can learn to count for the pleasure of knowing how the game really works. Trying to make a living at it is a job with a boss who fires you on sight.

The Gent's verdict. Learn basic strategy cold, find a 3:2 table with decent rules, bet what you can afford to lose fifteen times in a row, and blackjack is the cheapest evening in the house — at $25 a hand it costs about the price of a cocktail per hour in expectation. Sit at a 6:5 table with a side bet, and it's just a slot machine with a dress code.
Game 02

Video Poker: the slot machine that tells you the truth.

It looks like a slot. It sits with the slots. It's nothing like a slot. The deal is a genuinely random 52-card deck, the paytable is printed on the glass, and from those two facts anyone with a calculator can work out exactly what the machine returns. The casino's only defence is that almost nobody bothers.

Gent's Grade
A−full pay · D if short-pay
House edge0.46% (9/6 JoB), >100% exists Decision leverageDecisive Pace & bleed400–800 hands/hr — fast Trap densityThe paytable itself Fun per dollarMedium, solitary

How it plays

Bet one to five coins (always five — see quirks). You're dealt five cards, you hold any you like, the rest are replaced once, and you're paid according to the final hand against a fixed paytable. No dealer, no opponent, no bluffing. Jacks or Better is the base game: a pair of jacks or higher gets your money back, two pair pays 2:1, and so on up to a royal flush at 800:1 (with five coins in). Variants change which hands pay what — Deuces Wild makes every 2 a wild card, Double Bonus pays big for four of a kind — and each variant has its own optimal strategy.

Because the deck is fair and the paytable is public, there's a mathematically perfect way to play every hand, and the return of the machine under that strategy is a known number. That's what "9/6" means: a Jacks or Better game paying 9 coins for a full house and 6 for a flush returns 99.54% with perfect play. Drop the flush to 5 (an "8/5" game) and the same machine returns 97.30%. Same screen, same sounds, five times the house edge.

The quirks

  • The paytable is the whole game. Two identical-looking machines side by side can differ by 3–5% in return. Nobody will tell you which is which. You read the full house / flush line and you know.
  • Always bet max coins. The royal flush pays 250 per coin for one to four coins and 800 per coin for five. That jump is worth about 1.5% of return. If five coins is too rich, drop the denomination, not the coin count.
  • Variance is high and lumpy. A big chunk of the return (about 2% on Jacks or Better) is the royal, which shows up once every 40,000 hands or so — roughly 80 hours of play. Between royals you're playing a ~97.5% game. Budget for that.
  • Strategy is learnable but not obvious. Holding a low pair over four to a flush, breaking a made flush for four to a royal: correct plays that feel wrong. Strategy cards exist and are legal to use at the machine.
  • Comps are real money here. Because the machines are fast and the edge is thin, the player's-club points and cash-back on video poker are often worth more than the house edge itself. This is where the advantage lives.

Full-pay machines, promotions and the progressive meter

How it works. Video poker advantage play is arithmetic, not sleight of hand. You find a paytable at or above 100% (rare now, but they exist off-Strip and in locals' rooms), play it perfectly, and layer the player's-club cash-back, multiplied-point promotions and mailer offers on top. A 99.54% game with 0.33% cash-back and a double-points day is a winning proposition. The second angle is progressive jackpots: a royal flush meter that has climbed high enough pushes an ordinary 8/5 machine over 100%, and pros sit on it until it hits. The third is strategy changes for the meter — as the royal grows you draw to it more aggressively, and the right adjustments are published.

Legality. Completely legal. You're using the machine exactly as designed and reading numbers the casino printed. Strategy cards at the machine are allowed everywhere.

Acceptance. The most tolerated form of advantage play in the building, because the edges are tiny and the players are reliable, high-volume customers. The pushback is quieter: the best paytables get removed, video poker earns fewer points than slots, and the players who visibly grind promotions can find their mailers stop arriving. Nobody gets trespassed for playing 9/6 Jacks correctly. For most people the realistic prize isn't a living, it's turning a ~2% hobby into a ~0% hobby with a free buffet.

The Gent's verdict. The best-value machine in any casino and the worst-value one are both video poker, and the difference is one number on the glass. Learn to read the paytable and this is close to a free game; don't, and it's the fastest way in the building to lose a lot of money slowly.
Game 03

Craps: two great bets, forty terrible ones, one very loud table.

The layout is designed to look like a plumbing diagram so you'll ask the dealer what to do, and the dealer is trained to suggest the bets that pay him. Ignore all of it. The pass line with full odds is the second-best bet in the casino, and the middle of the table is a graveyard of 10%+ propositions with fun names. Learn which is which and craps is the best party in the house at a fair price.

Gent's Grade
B+A on the line, F in the middle
House edge0.37% (pass + 3-4-5x odds) Decision leverageBet selection is everything Pace & bleed~100 rolls/hr, low if you're on the line Trap densityThe highest on the floor Fun per dollarThe best in the building

How it plays

A shooter throws two dice. The first throw is the come-out roll: 7 or 11 wins the pass line, 2, 3 or 12 loses it, and anything else (4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10) becomes the point. Now the shooter keeps rolling until either the point repeats (pass wins) or a 7 shows (pass loses, dice move to the next shooter). That's the entire core game. Everything else on the table is a side bet on some subset of those rolls.

Once a point is set, you can back your pass bet with free odds — an extra wager, usually up to 3x, 4x or 5x your line bet depending on the point, paid at the true odds of the point hitting before a seven. Free odds have zero house edge. It's the only bet in the casino with no built-in vig, offered because you can only make it after paying the 1.41% entry fee on the pass line. Bet the table minimum on the line and the maximum in odds, and your blended edge collapses to under half a percent.

The quirks

  • The "don't" side is slightly better and universally hated. Don't pass is 1.36% vs 1.41%, and laying odds behind it is also free. You're betting against the shooter, and the table will let you know. If you can take the social cost, it's marginally the better bet.
  • The dealers steer. "Hardways for the boys?" "Press it?" "Any seven?" Dealers work for tips and the props pay them the most; they'll never suggest more odds. The right answer to every suggestion is "just the odds, thanks."
  • Place 6 and 8 in multiples of $6 or you get short-paid. Place bets on 6/8 at 1.52% are a fair way to get more action without the come-out; place 4/10 at 6.67% is not.
  • The Field looks like a good bet. Seven numbers win, four lose! But the seven winners are the rare ones. 5.56% edge, or 2.78% if 12 pays triple. Check the felt.
  • Big 6 / Big 8 is the same bet as placing 6/8 but paid even money instead of 7:6. 9.09% versus 1.52%. It exists purely to catch people who don't know the difference. Some jurisdictions have banned it out of embarrassment.
  • Hot tables aren't a thing. Dice don't remember. A shooter who's hit six points in a row has the same 1.41% pass line on the next one. The energy is real; the edge is unchanged.

LEGAL IF DICE HIT THE WALLUNPROVEN

Dice control ("dice setting")

The claim. By gripping the dice a particular way and throwing them with a soft, low, spinning arc, a practised shooter can reduce the frequency of sevens slightly below 1-in-6. If true, it would flip the pass line and the 6/8 place bets to a player edge. Courses and books are sold on it, and some card counters swear by particular shooters.

The evidence. Thin. Casinos require the dice to hit the back wall, which is pyramid-textured specifically to randomise the bounce; no controlled study has shown a statistically significant, repeatable effect in a real casino. The people most confident in dice control are the people selling the seminar. Treat it the way you'd treat a trading course that guarantees returns.

Legality and acceptance. Legal — there's no rule against throwing carefully, as long as both dice reach the wall. Casinos are relaxed about it because they don't think it works; a shooter who consistently fails to hit the back wall will be warned and then made to throw properly. The honest edge in craps is bet selection, not the throw.

The Gent's verdict. Stand at a crowded craps table on a Saturday night with $10 on the line and $50 in odds behind it, and you're getting the most entertainment per expected dollar lost anywhere in the casino. Put that same $60 across the hardways and the horn, and you've turned the best deal in the room into the worst.
Game 04

Baccarat: coin-flipping in a tuxedo.

The game of Bond, of Macau's billion-dollar rooms, and of people squeezing cards like they're reading tea leaves. Underneath the ceremony it's the simplest game in the building: two hands are dealt by a fixed rulebook, you guess which will be closer to nine, and you have exactly zero influence on anything. The edge is low. The decisions are none. The pace is fast.

BBanker only · F on the Tie

How it plays

Two hands are dealt, called Player and Banker — neither is yours. Cards count at face value, tens and pictures are zero, and only the last digit of the total matters (7 + 8 = 15 = 5). Each hand gets two cards; a fixed table of rules decides whether either draws a third. Closest to nine wins. You bet on Player (pays 1:1), Banker (pays 1:1 minus a 5% commission), or Tie (pays 8:1). That's it. There is no strategy because there are no decisions; the "drawing rules" happen automatically.

Banker wins slightly more often than Player (about 45.9% vs 44.6% of all hands, the rest ties) because Banker draws last and reacts to Player's card. The 5% commission exists to claw that back, and it doesn't quite manage it: Banker at 1.06% remains marginally better than Player at 1.24%. That is the entire strategic content of baccarat, and I've just given it to you.

The quirks

  • The scorecards are astrology. Every table hands out a grid for tracking "roads" and "patterns" — big road, bead plate, cockroach pig. Each hand is independent. The patterns are real in the sense that a coin flipped 60 times makes patterns. They predict nothing. The casino provides them because people who chase patterns bet more.
  • The squeeze is theatre. Slowly bending the corner of a card to reveal it changes nothing about the card. It does slow the game down, which — lower hands per hour — is the one thing that actually reduces your hourly cost.
  • "Commission-free" baccarat isn't. EZ Baccarat and its cousins drop the 5% commission but make a winning Banker three-card 7 a push. The edge on Banker becomes about 1.02% — roughly the same — and the new side bets (Dragon 7, Panda 8) run 7–10%.
  • Mini-baccarat is the real game. Same rules, dealer handles all the cards, 120–150 hands an hour instead of 40. Same 1.06% edge, three to four times the hourly bleed. The elegant big table is the cheaper one.
  • The Tie pays 8:1 on a 9.5:1 shot. Some tables pay 9:1, which drops the edge to about 4.8%. Still bad, but fourteen percent is a proposition bet, not a hand.

EDGE SORTING: RULED CHEATING IN THE US & UKLOSS REBATES: LEGAL, RARE

Edge sorting, and the $20 million lesson

Card counting doesn't work here. The drawing rules are so symmetric that even a perfect count produces a playable edge on Banker or Player almost never; the Tie can be counted, but the opportunities are vanishingly rare. The academic conclusion is that baccarat is effectively count-proof.

Edge sorting is the famous exception. Some card backs are printed with a pattern that's asymmetric by a fraction of a millimetre. If a player can persuade the dealer to rotate the high cards 180° ("for luck") and the casino uses an automatic shuffler that doesn't turn cards, the player can identify high and low first cards from the back before betting — worth a player edge of roughly 6–7%. A world-famous poker player and a partner used it to win about $10 million from an Atlantic City casino and about £7.7 million from a London one in 2012. Both casinos refused to pay or sued to recover. Both won: the UK Supreme Court (2017) and the US Third Circuit (2019) held that the technique was cheating or a breach of the gaming contract, even though the player never touched the cards. The law's line: exploiting the casino's mistake in your head is fine; getting the casino to alter the game for you is not.

Loss rebates are the legal cousin. High rollers negotiate a discount on losses (say, 20% back on a losing trip). One Atlantic City player in 2011 negotiated a rebate and rule set so generous that, combined with a disciplined stop-loss, he had a positive expectation — and took about $15 million across three casinos, in blackjack rather than baccarat, before they stopped offering. Legal, contractual, and unavailable to anyone betting less than five figures a hand.

The Gent's verdict. If you want a low-edge game with no homework, bet Banker at the slowest table you can find and enjoy the ceremony. Just know that you're paying to watch a coin flip in a nice room, and that the scorecard the dealer handed you is a colouring book.
Game 05

Ultimate Texas Hold'em: poker's costume, the house's rules.

The most popular carnival game of the last twenty years, because it feels like the poker you watch on TV. It isn't. There's no bluffing and no opponent — you're playing your hand against the dealer's, heads-up, with a betting structure that rewards people who commit early and punishes people who wait for certainty. Which, as a lesson, is worth the price of admission.

Gent's Grade
B−with the strategy · D without
House edge2.19% of ante · 0.53% of money bet Decision leverageSignificant (raise sizing) Pace & bleed~40 hands/hr, bets swell to 4x Trap densityTrips, and timidity Fun per dollarHigh — real decisions

How it plays

You post two equal bets, Ante and Blind, before seeing anything (plus an optional Trips side bet). You get two hole cards; the dealer gets two face down. Now you decide: raise 4x (or 3x) the ante right now, or check. Three community cards come out; if you checked, you can now raise 2x or check again. The last two board cards come out; if you've still not raised, it's raise 1x or fold. Then the dealer's hand is revealed and the best five-card poker hand wins.

The dealer needs a pair to "qualify"; if they don't, your Ante pushes but your Raise still pays. The Blind pays a bonus only when you win with a straight or better, and pushes on lesser winning hands. The design intent is clear once you see it: the earlier you raise, the more you can bet, and the game is built so that raising 4x with less information beats raising 1x with more. Most players do the opposite — wait to see the flop — and that instinct is where the house makes its money.

The quirks

  • Quote the edge honestly. "2.19% house edge" is measured against the ante alone; you'll actually have about 4.15 antes on the table by the end of an average hand. Against the total money you bet, the cost is 0.53% — the "element of risk". Both numbers are true; the second one is what your bankroll feels.
  • Raise 4x with any pair, any ace, K-Q/K-J/K-T, Q-J/Q-T, J-T suited-ish — the published strategy is a short list you can memorise, and it's worth about 3% versus playing "safe".
  • The dealer qualifying rule cuts both ways. You'll often win a raise but push the ante because the dealer had ace-high; it feels like a rip-off, and it's built into the 2.19%.
  • Trips is a separate game with its own paytable and no decisions: it pays on your final hand regardless of the dealer's. The best paytable runs under 1%; the common ones run 1.9–6.2%. Read the felt.
  • The variance is real. Because you're betting 4x early, a bad hour can cost 30–40 antes. Budget accordingly.

  • Raising 1x with nothing because you hate folding. The Blind and Ante are already gone; don't add a third.
  • Trips on the 6.18% paytable. Six times the cost of the game.

HOLE-CARDING: LEGAL IN NEVADA (NO DEVICES)COLLUSION: GREY-TO-ILLEGALBACKED OFF ON SIGHT

Hole-carding and table collusion

How it works. The dealer's two cards are dealt face down, and a sloppy dealer at a badly designed table will flash one of them to a player sitting at the right angle. Knowing one dealer card in Ultimate Texas Hold'em is worth a large player edge — you can raise 4x when you know you're ahead and fold when you know you're not. The second angle is information sharing: at a full table, seven players' hole cards plus the board account for 19 of 52 cards, and knowing what's already out changes the odds of the dealer's pair. Teams signal their cards to each other.

Legality. Nevada courts have held that simply seeing what the dealer accidentally exposes, with your own eyes and no device, is not cheating; other jurisdictions are less settled. Signalling between players isn't a device either, but it can fall under state conspiracy or "alter the outcome" statutes depending on how it's done, and it's a grey zone you don't want to be the test case for.

Acceptance. Zero. A player who's clearly hole-carding gets the dealer replaced, the table closed, and a walk to the door; teams get photographed and shared across the surveillance network. It's a real, documented edge for a small professional community, and a fast way for a hobbyist to become unwelcome.

The Gent's verdict. The best of the carnival games, and the one with an actual lesson in it: when you have the edge, bet big early; when you don't, get out cheap. Play it with the 4x chart and it's a 0.5% game with real decisions. Play it scared and it's the most expensive way to feel like a poker player without meeting one.
Game 06

Three Card Poker: one decision, one trap, one pleasant hour.

Invented in 1994 by a British poker player who wanted a table game simple enough for anyone and fast enough for a casino. He succeeded. There is exactly one strategic decision in the game and it fits on a matchbook: play Queen-Six-Four or better, fold everything else. The rest is a side bet designed to look like the main event.

Gent's Grade
C+Ante/Play only
House edge3.37% of ante · 2.01% of money bet Decision leverageOne rule, then nothing Pace & bleed~70 hands/hr, moderate Trap densityPair Plus and the 6-card bonus Fun per dollarMedium, relaxed

How it plays

Post an Ante. You and the dealer each get three cards; yours face up, theirs down. Look at your hand and either fold (lose the ante) or Play by putting up a second bet equal to the ante. The dealer needs Queen-high to qualify. If the dealer doesn't qualify, your Ante pays even money and Play pushes. If the dealer qualifies, better hand wins both bets, worse hand loses both. Hand rankings are three-card rankings: a straight beats a flush here, because with three cards a straight is rarer. An Ante Bonus pays extra on a straight or better, win or lose.

Pair Plus is the separate bet on the felt: it pays according to your own three cards alone, regardless of the dealer — a pair pays 1:1, a straight flush 40:1 — and it requires no decisions. Because it looks like the "fun" bet and pays out big now and then, it's where most of the money at the table goes. Which is the point.

The quirks

  • Q-6-4 is the entire strategy. Play any hand of queen-six-four or higher, fold anything worse. Playing every hand costs about 2 percentage points more; folding "correctly" by feel costs about 1.
  • Pair Plus paytables moved. The original 40-30-6-4-1 (straight flush / three of a kind / straight / flush / pair) runs 2.32%. The now-common version pays 3:1 on a flush instead of 4:1 — a one-number change that takes it to 7.28%. Same felt, three times the price. Look at the flush line.
  • The Ante Bonus paytable matters too. 5-4-1 (straight flush / trips / straight) gives the 3.37% figure; 4-3-1 is 3.83%; 3-2-1 is 4.28%.
  • Six Card Bonus and the "Progressive" bets that have sprouted on the layout run 6–15% on the common paytables. They're fun the way a scratch card is fun.
  • You'll win the Ante and push the Play a lot when the dealer fails to qualify with a hand better than yours. That's correct and it feels like a bug.

HOLE-CARDING: LEGAL IN NEVADA (NO DEVICES)BACKED OFF ON SIGHT

Hole-carding, again

How it works. The dealer picks up three cards to check for a qualifying hand, and a careless dealer lifts them where a player at first or last seat can see one. Knowing a single dealer card flips the Play decision from a 3.4% loss to a player edge of a few percent — you play more hands when the dealer's card is low, fold more when it's high. It's the same skill as in Ultimate Texas Hold'em and the same small community of professionals who scout dealers across a city.

Legality and acceptance. As above: in Nevada, seeing what's shown to you without a device isn't cheating; elsewhere it depends. Casinos treat it as an emergency — new dealer, table closed, player flagged. There is no version of this for a recreational player beyond knowing that it's why the dealer now uses a card-reading shoe.

The Gent's verdict. A civilised, low-stress table game where the main bet is fairly priced and the flashy bet beside it isn't. Learn one rule, ignore one bet, and you're paying about 2% for a relaxed hour. Do the reverse and you're paying 7% for the same chairs.
Game 07

Roulette: the only choice that matters is which wheel.

Pascal was trying to build a perpetual-motion machine and accidentally built the most elegant tax in Europe. Every bet on the layout — a single number, red, a column, a corner — costs exactly the same, because they all pay as if the zero weren't there. The house edge isn't in the bet; it's in the green pocket. Count the green pockets and you know the price.

Gent's Grade
C−B single zero · D double · F triple
House edge2.70% (0) · 5.26% (00) · 7.69% (000) Decision leverageNone, except wheel choice Pace & bleed30–50 spins/hr — slow, which helps Trap densityLow — one bad bet, plus the wheel itself Fun per dollarHigh if you like ritual

How it plays

A ball is spun around a wheel with numbered pockets — 1 to 36 in red and black, plus one green zero (European), a zero and a double zero (American), or three zeros (the newest tourist-room special). You bet on where it lands: a single number at 35:1, a split at 17:1, a corner at 8:1, a dozen or column at 2:1, red/black or odd/even or high/low at even money. Every payout is set as though there were 36 pockets. There are 37, 38 or 39. That gap is the edge, and it's identical on every bet on the felt except one.

There is no decision to make except which table to sit at. A single-zero wheel costs 2.70%. A double-zero wheel costs 5.26% — nearly double, for the same game, often in the same room. A triple-zero wheel costs 7.69%, and it was introduced precisely because people don't look. If the table offers la partage or en prison (you lose only half, or your bet stays up, when zero hits on an even-money bet), the even-money bets drop to 1.35%. That's a better bet than anything at the craps table except the odds.

The quirks

  • Betting systems don't work and never will. Martingale (double after every loss) turns a 5.26% edge into the same 5.26% edge with a small chance of catastrophe; the table maximum exists to make sure the catastrophe arrives. No sequence of bets on a negative-edge game produces a positive edge. This is the single most-relearned lesson in gambling history.
  • "Hot" and "due" numbers are the display board's job, not the wheel's. Each spin is independent. The board shows the last 20 results because people bet more when they see patterns.
  • Spreading chips across the layout doesn't diversify anything. Seventeen different bets on a 5.26% wheel is a 5.26% bet with more steps. It does slow you down, which is worth something.
  • Rapid roulette and electronic wheels run 60–80 spins an hour and usually on double zero. Same edge, twice the bleed.
  • The five-number bet (0-00-1-2-3 on an American wheel) pays 6:1 and costs 7.89%. It's the one bet on the layout with a different price, and the difference is worse.

WHEEL BIAS: LEGAL, NEARLY EXTINCTCOMPUTERS: FELONY IN NEVADAVISUAL BALLISTICS: LEGAL, IMPRACTICAL

Biased wheels, ballistics and the laser-scanner trio

How it works. Three historical routes. Wheel bias: a physically imperfect wheel (a loose fret, a worn pocket, a slight tilt) favours certain numbers; a patient player clocks thousands of spins, finds the bias, and bets it. An English engineer did it in Monte Carlo in 1873 and a Spanish family did it across Europe in the 1990s, both for six-figure-plus sums. Visual ballistics: a trained eye estimates where the ball will drop from its speed relative to the wheel and bets late, in the few seconds before "no more bets". Computers: a hidden device does the ballistics for you. A group of physics students built one in a shoe in the late 1970s and proved it worked; in 2004 a trio using a laser scanner and a phone won about £1.3 million from a London casino in two nights.

Legality. Wheel bias and visual ballistics are legal — you're observing a public event. Devices are not: Nevada made it a felony in 1985 and most jurisdictions followed. The 2004 London trio were arrested and released without charge because UK law at the time had no such offence; it does now.

Acceptance. Modern wheels are precision-machined, levelled daily and swapped between tables, and casinos log every spin to detect bias before you can. Dealers are trained to call "no more bets" earlier if anyone bets late with suspicious accuracy. The realistic status is: historically real, practically closed. A wonderful set of stories, not a plan.

The Gent's verdict. Roulette is the most honest game in the casino in one sense — every bet costs the same, and the price is visible in the number of green pockets. Find a single-zero wheel, bet the even-money spots if it offers la partage, and it's a slow, cheap, pleasant evening. Sit at the triple-zero wheel by the entrance and you're paying nearly three times as much for the same spin.
Game 08

Slots: the only game where the price is a secret.

Seventy percent of a modern casino's gaming revenue comes from machines. They're the fastest game in the building, the only one with no published odds, and the one engineered by the best behavioural psychologists money can hire. Everything about a slot is designed to make you not do the arithmetic. So let's do the arithmetic.

Gent's Grade
DC for high-limit · F for pennies
House edge~2–15%, unknowable per machine Decision leverageNone — only denomination Pace & bleed500–900 spins/hr — fastest in the house Trap densityThe whole machine Fun per dollarLow; high on stimulation

How it plays

You put money in, choose a bet (lines × coins × denomination), press a button, and a random number generator picks an outcome the instant you press — the spinning reels are an animation of a decision already made. Matching symbols pay by a table, bonus rounds trigger on certain combinations, and a fraction of every bet may feed a progressive jackpot. Every machine is set to a fixed long-run return — say 92% — chosen by the casino from a menu the manufacturer offers, within limits set by the regulator. That number is not displayed anywhere on the machine. Two identical-looking games side by side can be set to 88% and 96%.

What's published is aggregate: regulators in some jurisdictions report the average hold by denomination. The pattern is consistent everywhere it's reported: penny slots hold the most (often 10–12%), dollar machines less (5–7%), high-limit rooms least (2–4%). The cheap machine is the expensive one. And "penny" is marketing — a typical penny game at 40 lines and max bet is $2–$4 a spin, 600 spins an hour.

The quirks

  • The hourly cost is the number that matters. $2.50 a spin × 600 spins × 10% = $150 an hour in expectation. A $25 blackjack hand at 0.5%, 70 hands an hour, costs about $9. The slot player betting a tenth as much per decision loses fifteen times as much per hour.
  • "Loose" and "tight" are real and invisible. The casino sets the hold. Machines near entrances and aisles are commonly believed to run looser; the honest answer is that nobody outside the slot department knows, and they're not saying.
  • Near-misses are designed. Two jackpot symbols on the payline and a third just above it isn't bad luck; on many machines the reel-stop weighting is tuned so near-misses show far more often than chance, because they make you press again.
  • Losses disguised as wins. Bet $2, "win" $0.60, and the machine plays the celebration sound. Modern multi-line slots return less than the bet on a large share of "winning" spins, and the audio doesn't distinguish.
  • Progressives are a tax on everyone but the winner. The meter is funded by a slice of every bet. A must-hit-by progressive that's close to its ceiling is the one exception — see below.
  • Skill-based and "hold and spin" features still resolve to a fixed return. The skill part is usually worth a fraction of a percent, and the illusion of control is the product.

LEGALTOLERATED, INCREASINGLY DESIGNED OUT

Must-hit-by progressives and banked bonuses

How it works. Some progressives are guaranteed to pay by a stated ceiling ("must hit by $500"). As the meter approaches the ceiling the probability of the hit on any spin rises, and at some point the expected jackpot share exceeds the machine's base house edge — the game becomes positive expectation until it hits. The other family is persistent-state or "banked" bonuses: machines that accumulate progress (collected symbols, filled meters) across players. Someone abandons a machine two symbols from a bonus; the next player inherits their progress. A small professional community scouts floors for these states, sits, and plays until the value is extracted. The edges are real and the hourly rate is modest.

Legality. Entirely legal — you're playing the machine as designed, and the state is displayed on screen.

Acceptance. Mostly tolerated, because the amounts are small and the players are visible customers. The industry response is design: newer games reset the bank when a player cashes out, and must-hit-by meters are less common than they were. Some casinos ban known "vulture" players from the floor. As with video poker, the realistic prize is a break-even hobby with comps, not an income.

The Gent's verdict. Slots are a fine product if you treat them as one: a ticket to two hours of lights and noise with a price you can estimate but never see. What they aren't is a bet. Every other game on this page will tell you what it costs. This one's silence is the answer.
The takeaway

The house always wins. Learn to be the house.

Every game above has the same shape: a fair-ish core bet, surrounded by expensive bets that look more exciting, on a layout designed to make the expensive ones feel like the main event. Read the price before you play — and notice that the one game on this list that doesn't have a house edge is the one this whole site is built around.

The exception: poker

Poker isn't on this list because the casino isn't your opponent — it takes a rake and the other players take everything else. There's no house edge to grade, only a skill edge to earn. That's a different curriculum, and the one twenty years at the table paid for. Coming to Pillar 02 next.
