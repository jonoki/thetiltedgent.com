Game 07

Roulette: the only choice that matters is which wheel.

Pascal was trying to build a perpetual-motion machine and accidentally built the most elegant tax in Europe. Every bet on the layout — a single number, red, a column, a corner — costs exactly the same, because they all pay as if the zero weren't there. The house edge isn't in the bet; it's in the green pocket. Count the green pockets and you know the price.

How it plays

A ball is spun around a wheel with numbered pockets — 1 to 36 in red and black, plus one green zero (European), a zero and a double zero (American), or three zeros (the newest tourist-room special). You bet on where it lands: a single number at 35:1, a split at 17:1, a corner at 8:1, a dozen or column at 2:1, red/black or odd/even or high/low at even money. Every payout is set as though there were 36 pockets. There are 37, 38 or 39. That gap is the edge, and it's identical on every bet on the felt except one.

There is no decision to make except which table to sit at. A single-zero wheel costs 2.70%. A double-zero wheel costs 5.26% — nearly double, for the same game, often in the same room. A triple-zero wheel costs 7.69%, and it was introduced precisely because people don't look. If the table offers la partage or en prison (you lose only half, or your bet stays up, when zero hits on an even-money bet), the even-money bets drop to 1.35%. That's a better bet than anything at the craps table except the odds.

The quirks

  • Betting systems don't work and never will. Martingale (double after every loss) turns a 5.26% edge into the same 5.26% edge with a small chance of catastrophe; the table maximum exists to make sure the catastrophe arrives. No sequence of bets on a negative-edge game produces a positive edge. This is the single most-relearned lesson in gambling history.
  • "Hot" and "due" numbers are the display board's job, not the wheel's. Each spin is independent. The board shows the last 20 results because people bet more when they see patterns.
  • Spreading chips across the layout doesn't diversify anything. Seventeen different bets on a 5.26% wheel is a 5.26% bet with more steps. It does slow you down, which is worth something.
  • Rapid roulette and electronic wheels run 60–80 spins an hour and usually on double zero. Same edge, twice the bleed.
  • The five-number bet (0-00-1-2-3 on an American wheel) pays 6:1 and costs 7.89%. It's the one bet on the layout with a different price, and the difference is worse.

Best bets
  • Even-money bets on a single-zero wheel with la partage. 1.35% for a slow, civilised game — a real bargain.
  • Anything on a single-zero wheel. 2.70% is acceptable entertainment pricing at 40 spins an hour.
Worst bets
  • Anything on a triple-zero wheel. 7.69%, and it's usually the wheel with the lowest minimum, which is how they get you.
  • The five-number bet. The one bet that manages to be worse than its own wheel.
  • Any betting system with a name. The wheel has heard of it.

Biased wheels, ballistics and the laser-scanner trio

How it works. Three historical routes. Wheel bias: a physically imperfect wheel (a loose fret, a worn pocket, a slight tilt) favours certain numbers; a patient player clocks thousands of spins, finds the bias, and bets it. An English engineer did it in Monte Carlo in 1873 and a Spanish family did it across Europe in the 1990s, both for six-figure-plus sums. Visual ballistics: a trained eye estimates where the ball will drop from its speed relative to the wheel and bets late, in the few seconds before "no more bets". Computers: a hidden device does the ballistics for you. A group of physics students built one in a shoe in the late 1970s and proved it worked; in 2004 a trio using a laser scanner and a phone won about £1.3 million from a London casino in two nights.

Legality. Wheel bias and visual ballistics are legal — you're observing a public event. Devices are not: Nevada made it a felony in 1985 and most jurisdictions followed. The 2004 London trio were arrested and released without charge because UK law at the time had no such offence; it does now.

Acceptance. Modern wheels are precision-machined, levelled daily and swapped between tables, and casinos log every spin to detect bias before you can. Dealers are trained to call "no more bets" earlier if anyone bets late with suspicious accuracy. The realistic status is: historically real, practically closed. A wonderful set of stories, not a plan.

The Gent's verdict. Roulette is the most honest game in the casino in one sense — every bet costs the same, and the price is visible in the number of green pockets. Find a single-zero wheel, bet the even-money spots if it offers la partage, and it's a slow, cheap, pleasant evening. Sit at the triple-zero wheel by the entrance and you're paying nearly three times as much for the same spin.
