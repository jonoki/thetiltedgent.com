Game 08

Slots: the only game where the price is a secret.

Seventy percent of a modern casino's gaming revenue comes from machines. They're the fastest game in the building, the only one with no published odds, and the one engineered by the best behavioural psychologists money can hire. Everything about a slot is designed to make you not do the arithmetic. So let's do the arithmetic.

How it plays

You put money in, choose a bet (lines × coins × denomination), press a button, and a random number generator picks an outcome the instant you press — the spinning reels are an animation of a decision already made. Matching symbols pay by a table, bonus rounds trigger on certain combinations, and a fraction of every bet may feed a progressive jackpot. Every machine is set to a fixed long-run return — say 92% — chosen by the casino from a menu the manufacturer offers, within limits set by the regulator. That number is not displayed anywhere on the machine. Two identical-looking games side by side can be set to 88% and 96%.

What's published is aggregate: regulators in some jurisdictions report the average hold by denomination. The pattern is consistent everywhere it's reported: penny slots hold the most (often 10–12%), dollar machines less (5–7%), high-limit rooms least (2–4%). The cheap machine is the expensive one. And "penny" is marketing — a typical penny game at 40 lines and max bet is $2–$4 a spin, 600 spins an hour.

The quirks

  • The hourly cost is the number that matters. $2.50 a spin × 600 spins × 10% = $150 an hour in expectation. A $25 blackjack hand at 0.5%, 70 hands an hour, costs about $9. The slot player betting a tenth as much per decision loses fifteen times as much per hour.
  • "Loose" and "tight" are real and invisible. The casino sets the hold. Machines near entrances and aisles are commonly believed to run looser; the honest answer is that nobody outside the slot department knows, and they're not saying.
  • Near-misses are designed. Two jackpot symbols on the payline and a third just above it isn't bad luck; on many machines the reel-stop weighting is tuned so near-misses show far more often than chance, because they make you press again.
  • Losses disguised as wins. Bet $2, "win" $0.60, and the machine plays the celebration sound. Modern multi-line slots return less than the bet on a large share of "winning" spins, and the audio doesn't distinguish.
  • Progressives are a tax on everyone but the winner. The meter is funded by a slice of every bet. A must-hit-by progressive that's close to its ceiling is the one exception — see below.
  • Skill-based and "hold and spin" features still resolve to a fixed return. The skill part is usually worth a fraction of a percent, and the illusion of control is the product.

Best bets
  • Higher denomination, fewer spins. One $5 spin a minute on a 5% machine costs less per hour than a penny game at max bet.
  • A video poker machine instead. Same seat, same drink service, and the price is printed on the glass.
  • Set a time and money budget before you sit, and treat what you lose as the ticket price. That's not a bet; it's the only strategy there is.
Worst bets
  • Penny slots at max bet. The highest hold on the floor at the fastest pace, dressed as the cheapest game in the room.
  • Anything in an airport or a bar. Captive audience, captive hold.
  • Chasing a "due" machine. The RNG has no memory, and no sense of fairness.
Feel the edge

No simulator for slots — and that's the point.

Slot outcome distributions are not published. Every other game on this site has a simulator because its odds are knowable: the deck, the dice and the wheel are public, and the paytable is printed on the felt or the glass. A slot machine's return and hit frequency are set by the casino from a menu the manufacturer provides, are not displayed anywhere, and vary wildly from one machine to the next — two identical cabinets can be set years apart in expected cost. Any simulation would be a guess dressed up as a chart, which is exactly the trick the machine itself is playing. What we do know is enough: reported holds run from roughly 2–4% in high-limit rooms to 10–15% on penny games and bar tops, at 500–900 spins an hour. At those numbers a slot is the worst bet in the building by a wide margin, and no amount of simulating changes that. If you want to see what a fast, high-edge game does to a bankroll, run the any-seven bet on the craps page at $2 for 1,200 bets — that's a penny slot on a good day.

Must-hit-by progressives and banked bonuses

How it works. Some progressives are guaranteed to pay by a stated ceiling ("must hit by $500"). As the meter approaches the ceiling the probability of the hit on any spin rises, and at some point the expected jackpot share exceeds the machine's base house edge — the game becomes positive expectation until it hits. The other family is persistent-state or "banked" bonuses: machines that accumulate progress (collected symbols, filled meters) across players. Someone abandons a machine two symbols from a bonus; the next player inherits their progress. A small professional community scouts floors for these states, sits, and plays until the value is extracted. The edges are real and the hourly rate is modest.

Legality. Entirely legal — you're playing the machine as designed, and the state is displayed on screen.

Acceptance. Mostly tolerated, because the amounts are small and the players are visible customers. The industry response is design: newer games reset the bank when a player cashes out, and must-hit-by meters are less common than they were. Some casinos ban known "vulture" players from the floor. As with video poker, the realistic prize is a break-even hobby with comps, not an income.

The Gent's verdict. Slots are a fine product if you treat them as one: a ticket to two hours of lights and noise with a price you can estimate but never see. What they aren't is a bet. Every other game on this page will tell you what it costs. This one's silence is the answer.
