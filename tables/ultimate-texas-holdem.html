Game 05

Ultimate Texas Hold'em: poker's costume, the house's rules.

The most popular carnival game of the last twenty years, because it feels like the poker you watch on TV. It isn't. There's no bluffing and no opponent — you're playing your hand against the dealer's, heads-up, with a betting structure that rewards people who commit early and punishes people who wait for certainty. Which, as a lesson, is worth the price of admission.

How it plays

You post two equal bets, Ante and Blind, before seeing anything (plus an optional Trips side bet). You get two hole cards; the dealer gets two face down. Now you decide: raise 4x (or 3x) the ante right now, or check. Three community cards come out; if you checked, you can now raise 2x or check again. The last two board cards come out; if you've still not raised, it's raise 1x or fold. Then the dealer's hand is revealed and the best five-card poker hand wins.

The dealer needs a pair to "qualify"; if they don't, your Ante pushes but your Raise still pays. The Blind pays a bonus only when you win with a straight or better, and pushes on lesser winning hands. The design intent is clear once you see it: the earlier you raise, the more you can bet, and the game is built so that raising 4x with less information beats raising 1x with more. Most players do the opposite — wait to see the flop — and that instinct is where the house makes its money.

The quirks

  • Quote the edge honestly. "2.19% house edge" is measured against the ante alone; you'll actually have about 4.15 antes on the table by the end of an average hand. Against the total money you bet, the cost is 0.53% — the "element of risk". Both numbers are true; the second one is what your bankroll feels.
  • Raise 4x with any pair, any ace, K-Q/K-J/K-T, Q-J/Q-T, J-T suited-ish — the published strategy is a short list you can memorise, and it's worth about 3% versus playing "safe".
  • The dealer qualifying rule cuts both ways. You'll often win a raise but push the ante because the dealer had ace-high; it feels like a rip-off, and it's built into the 2.19%.
  • Trips is a separate game with its own paytable and no decisions: it pays on your final hand regardless of the dealer's. The best paytable runs under 1%; the common ones run 1.9–6.2%. Read the felt.
  • The variance is real. Because you're betting 4x early, a bad hour can cost 30–40 antes. Budget accordingly.

Best bets
  • The 4x raise when the strategy says so. This is the whole game. Any pair, any ace, most kings and big queens: max it and let the board fall.
  • Folding the garbage on the river. Any hand that doesn't beat the board with less than 21 dealer outs against it — fold. Losing 2 units beats losing 3.
  • Trips only on the sub-2% paytable, and only if you enjoy it. It's cheaper than most side bets, which is faint praise.
Worst bets
  • Checking every hand to the river "to see what happens." You've turned a 0.5% game into a 5% one by being careful.
  • Raising 1x with nothing because you hate folding. The Blind and Ante are already gone; don't add a third.
  • Trips on the 6.18% paytable. Six times the cost of the game.

Hole-carding and table collusion

How it works. The dealer's two cards are dealt face down, and a sloppy dealer at a badly designed table will flash one of them to a player sitting at the right angle. Knowing one dealer card in Ultimate Texas Hold'em is worth a large player edge — you can raise 4x when you know you're ahead and fold when you know you're not. The second angle is information sharing: at a full table, seven players' hole cards plus the board account for 19 of 52 cards, and knowing what's already out changes the odds of the dealer's pair. Teams signal their cards to each other.

Legality. Nevada courts have held that simply seeing what the dealer accidentally exposes, with your own eyes and no device, is not cheating; other jurisdictions are less settled. Signalling between players isn't a device either, but it can fall under state conspiracy or "alter the outcome" statutes depending on how it's done, and it's a grey zone you don't want to be the test case for.

Acceptance. Zero. A player who's clearly hole-carding gets the dealer replaced, the table closed, and a walk to the door; teams get photographed and shared across the surveillance network. It's a real, documented edge for a small professional community, and a fast way for a hobbyist to become unwelcome.

The Gent's verdict. The best of the carnival games, and the one with an actual lesson in it: when you have the edge, bet big early; when you don't, get out cheap. Play it with the 4x chart and it's a 0.5% game with real decisions. Play it scared and it's the most expensive way to feel like a poker player without meeting one.
